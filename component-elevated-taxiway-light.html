Elevated Taxiway Light

A standard taxiway guidance fixture mounted on a frangible pole or base plate above the pavement surface. Elevated taxiway lights provide excellent visibility and are the most common configuration for taxiway edge lighting, particularly on grass or soft shoulders where inset fixtures are not practical.

Overview

What Is It?

An Elevated Taxiway Light is a standard taxiway guidance fixture mounted on a frangible pole or base plate above the pavement surface. These lights emit the characteristic blue light that defines taxiway edges, providing pilots with clear visual guidance during ground movement. Elevated taxiway lights are the most common configuration for taxiway edge lighting, particularly on grass or soft shoulders where inset fixtures are not practical. The elevated design offers excellent visibility from a distance and is easy to maintain, making it the preferred choice for most airfield taxiway applications.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The elevated taxiway light exists to give pilots the strongest, most visible lateral reference the AGL system offers, using height and a frangible above-ground mount to project the blue edge signal further and more clearly than a flush fixture can — particularly valuable on grass or soft shoulders where an inset light isn't a practical option at all. Because visibility at a distance matters most where a pilot needs the earliest possible cue to track a taxiway edge, the elevated design is the default choice wherever ground conditions permit it.

The frangible mounting is itself part of the purpose, not just a construction detail: it lets the fixture break away safely if struck by an aircraft or vehicle, protecting both the aircraft and the light from a rigid-obstacle collision, so the airfield gets the visibility benefit of an elevated light without introducing an unacceptable strike hazard.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Elevated taxiway lights are installed on taxiways with grass, gravel, or soft shoulders where inset fixtures are not feasible. They are used on:

  • Taxiways with grass or soft shoulders where elevated mounting is required
  • All paved taxiways as the standard edge lighting configuration
  • Apron edges where elevated lights provide clear boundaries
  • Rapid exit taxiways requiring high visibility edge guidance
  • Military airfields where robust lighting is required
  • General aviation airports with cost-effective lighting needs
Placement

Installation & Location

Elevated taxiway fixtures mount on a frangible coupling atop an L-867-type base can set flush with grade along the taxiway edge or shoulder, used wherever the adjacent surface is grass, gravel, or otherwise unsuited to an inset fixture. The base can foundation is set back at the taxiway's edge safety margin, with fitting height kept low enough to stay clear of aircraft wingtip and engine clearance envelopes. Cable is run in a trench from the base can back to the isolating transformer pit and on to the taxiway lighting series circuit, with setting-out tied to the taxiway centreline survey so the edge row stays parallel to the paved surface along its full length.

Function

How Does It Work?

Elevated taxiway lights operate using standard AGL principles:

  • Power: Powered from the constant current series circuit via an isolating transformer (typically 15W – 45W) buried near the fixture.
  • Light Source: Utilizes blue LED arrays or blue-filtered halogen lamps. The distinctive blue colour is universally recognized as a taxiway edge indicator.
  • Beam Pattern: Projects an omni-directional or uni-directional beam pattern to guide pilots along the taxiway.
  • Mounting: Mounted on a frangible pole or base plate approximately 300-500mm above ground level, with a breakaway design to minimize aircraft damage on impact.
  • Intensity Control: CCR-controlled intensity with 3 to 5 steps, on the standard 6.6A series circuit range (2.8A – 6.6A at full brightness).
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Elevated taxiway lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or (at smaller aerodromes) pilot-controlled lighting input. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (typically one of 3 to 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A, with 6.6A as the full-brightness step). Every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, draws from the same current and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no way to dim or brighten a single fixture independently of the rest of the circuit. Fault detection is likewise loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing. Because the fixture sits on a frangible coupling, a sheared coupling looks electrically identical to any other open-circuit fault on the loop — the ALCMS reports it the same way, and only a field inspection confirms whether the cause was impact damage or a failed component.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture head is a die-cast aluminium alloy housing, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, with a low-profile optical assembly under a toughened-glass or high-strength polycarbonate lens, gasket-sealed with an O-ring for an IP67/IP68 rating. The characteristic blue colour is produced by a filtered lens or coloured LED array rather than a dyed housing, keeping output stable as the fixture ages. The head mounts on a frangible coupling atop an L-867-type base can set flush with grade, the coupling engineered to shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft wingtip, vehicle, or ground-support equipment, minimising damage below the break point. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors, sealed for direct burial and continuous immersion, mating to the isolating transformer's secondary in an adjacent base can, with stainless-steel fasteners used throughout and a drainage provision in the base can to keep the connector joint clear of standing water.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Base Plate Setting & Offset
Civil

Set the pole/base plate at the design offset from the taxiway edge and to grade, holding the mounting height within the 300–500mm tolerance before the fixture is fixed.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay the series circuit duct/cable route to design depth and separation from other services, avoiding sharp bends that could stress the L-823 connector at final termination.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal the primary connector per manufacturer procedure, keeping the splice fully submersible-rated before backfill around the base plate.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer wattage and ratio match the fixture and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Frangible Coupling Fixing
Mechanical

Torque the frangible coupling to the specified value, checking the fitting shears as designed under lateral load and does not bind.

Beam Orientation Setting
Mechanical

Set fixture beam orientation against the design layout so the blue beam is visible from the intended direction of taxiing traffic.

Snow/Jet-Blast Clearance Check
Civil

Confirm the fixture's offset and height keep it clear of snowplough blade paths and within the jet-blast tolerance for the taxiway class it serves.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record base position, cable route, connector seal, and torque values as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the isolating transformer and series loop before the circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity end to end and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at the fixture's isolating transformer matches the rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm the fixture responds correctly at each, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Fixture Alignment & Aim Verification
Final Acceptance

Check the mounting height and beam orientation of each fixture against the design layout, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure blue beam intensity, colour, and beam spread with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances prior to operational handover.

Frangibility & Mounting Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm the frangible coupling and pole/base plate height meet the 300–500mm as-built tolerance before the site is finally backfilled.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify blue beam intensity against the applicable candela step using a calibrated photometer; degraded output usually points to filter or LED ageing.

Lens & Blue Filter Cleaning
Quarterly

Wipe dust, rubber deposits, and film build-up from the lens and blue filter so beam output and colour stay within spec.

Frangible Coupling Check
Annual

Inspect the coupling for brittleness, corrosion, or shear fatigue and replace on schedule, particularly in high-traffic or plough zones, so breakaway performance is not compromised.

Impact Damage Inspection
Semi-annual

Check the fixture body and stake for dents, cracks, or leaning caused by vehicle contact or wingtip strikes, and replace damaged units promptly.

Seal & Gasket Inspection
Annual

Check top-cap O-rings and gaskets for cracking or compression set, and re-torque fasteners to spec to keep the fixture hermetically sealed.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test the isolating transformer and series circuit to confirm insulation resistance stays above the minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a series-circuit fault.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Examine primary and secondary cable connections for corrosion or loose crimps, a common source of intermittent series-circuit faults.

Mounting Height & Alignment
Semi-annual

Confirm the fixture sits within the 300–500mm mounting-height range and its base has not tilted or settled, keeping the beam correctly oriented to the taxiway edge.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated taxiway lights rely on frangible couplings that can fatigue over time due to vibration from aircraft, vehicle traffic, and weather exposure. Regular inspection and replacement of couplings is recommended to ensure breakaway performance.

Impact Damage

Elevated lights are vulnerable to damage from snowploughs, ground vehicles, and aircraft wingtip strikes. Regular inspection and prompt replacement of damaged fixtures is essential for safety.

Blue Filter/LED Degradation

Blue light sources and filters are susceptible to UV degradation and thermal stress. Over time, output can decrease or colour can shift. Regular photometric testing and replacement is essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module/lamp, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop (severed cable, corroded L-823 splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Reduced or Hazy Blue Output
Likely Cause
Hazy or eroded lens, an aging LED array, or the CCR running on a lower brightness step than the operational requirement.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the lens; confirm the CCR is set to the correct intensity step for prevailing conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lens, replace a degraded LED module, or correct the CCR step setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Struck, Leaning, or Damaged
Likely Cause
Contact from a snowplough, ground vehicle, or aircraft wingtip has sheared or fatigued the frangible coupling, or damaged the fixture housing.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically check the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against tolerance; inspect the housing and lens for impact damage.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling or damaged housing/lens, re-level and re-aim the fixture, and re-verify before returning it to service.
Fixture Flickering or Intermittent
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded primary/secondary crimp connections, or a marginal transformer winding under thermal load.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque all cable connections at the fixture base; check crimps for corrosion; monitor secondary voltage for drop under load once the CCR has warmed up.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded connections with new crimps and sealant, or replace the transformer if voltage sag is confirmed under load.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Module / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised LED board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single fixture goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the series circuit continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at the fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or rodent activity, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Vibration from aircraft and vehicle traffic, weather exposure, or corrosion at the coupling threads over the service interval.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or separates entirely from its base, taking the light out of tolerance or out of service.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim/level checks against tolerance.
Impact Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Contact from a snowplough, ground vehicle, or aircraft wingtip, which the fixture's above-ground mounting exposes it to more than an inset fixture.
Effect
Sheared frangible coupling, damaged housing or lens, or a fixture knocked out of aim tolerance.
Detection Method
Routine visual patrol along the taxiway edge, and physical inspection of the coupling and housing after any known impact event.
Blue Filter / Lens Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure and thermal stress causing the blue filter or lens to haze, discolour, or shift in output over time.
Effect
Reduced or colour-shifted blue output that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds for taxiway edge identification.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity and colour testing, and visual inspection of the lens during routine cleaning.
Base Can / Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure allowing water to pool and erode the surrounding fill.
Effect
Fixture tilts out of aim tolerance, cable connections come under mechanical stress, and water can pool around the base can.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of the base can and surrounding grass, gravel, or pavement during annual maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Elevated taxiway lights sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling connectors.

Live Taxiway Coordination
Operational

Taxiways carry continuous mixed aircraft and service-vehicle movement; coordinate any work with airside operations and mark the work area clearly before opening a base can.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; it must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft or ground vehicle.

Wingtip & Engine Clearance
Compliance

Keep fitting height within the design limit and clear of the taxiway's wingtip and engine clearance envelope during and after work, since even a slightly taller replacement fixture can foul a low-slung engine or wing on a live taxi route.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the whole taxiway run.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture dark, leaning, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; verify positional and photometric conformance before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Set Frangible Height for the Vehicles That Actually Use It
Installation

Elevated taxiway lights on aprons and cargo ramps see far more contact from ground service vehicles than the same fixture on a runway-adjacent taxiway; confirm frangible point height and coupling rating against the tallest routine ground vehicle for that specific location, not a generic airfield default.

Blue LED Chromaticity Drifts With Age
Retrofit

LED taxiway edge fixtures can shift perceptibly toward a colder or greener blue over years of service even while still meeting minimum intensity; check chromaticity coordinates against the ICAO blue box at overhaul, not only lumen output, since colour drift is what pilots notice first.

Snow Removal Is a Leading Cause of Elevated Fixture Damage
Mechanical

Plough blade strikes and windrowed snow are a more common cause of elevated taxiway light failure than aircraft-related incidents on many airfields; coordinate marked fixture locations with winter operations staff and check frangible coupling condition specifically after each snow season.

Base Can Ponding at Low Points
Durability

Elevated fixtures installed at low points in the taxiway shoulder grade collect standing water around the base can far more than others on the same circuit; check drainage grading and gasket condition at these specific locations rather than treating every base can as equally exposed.

Balance Loops Sharing One Regulator
Design

Where several taxiway lighting loops share a single CCR, unequal loop lengths can leave one taxiway visibly dimmer than another even though each fixture individually tests within tolerance; balance loop impedance at the design stage rather than after a pilot or ground crew report.

Trend Insulation Resistance on Taxiway Loops Too
Troubleshooting

A slow drift in loop insulation resistance at a connector usually shows up months before a hard fault does; log megger readings across maintenance visits on taxiway loops just as on runway loops, since taxiway circuits are just as prone to gradual moisture ingress.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the elevated taxiway light: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an elevated taxiway light?

It is a standard taxiway guidance fixture mounted on a frangible pole or base plate above the pavement. It emits the blue light that defines taxiway edges and is the most common configuration for taxiway edge lighting, especially on grass or soft shoulders where inset fixtures are impractical.

Q2Where are elevated taxiway lights used?

On taxiways with grass, gravel or soft shoulders, on paved taxiways as the standard edge lighting, at apron edges, on rapid exit taxiways needing high-visibility edge guidance, and at military airfields.

Q3How does an elevated taxiway light work?

It is powered from the constant-current series circuit through a buried isolating transformer, typically 15 W to 45 W. The light source is a blue LED array or a blue-filtered halogen lamp, and intensity is set by the CCR in 3 to 5 steps on the standard 6.6 A circuit.

Q4How high is the fixture and how is it frangible?

It is mounted about 300 mm to 500 mm above ground on a frangible pole or base plate with a breakaway coupling (shear force 250 kg to 450 kg), so it breaks away on aircraft impact to minimise damage.

Q5What is the light intensity and beam pattern?

About 2 cd in the main beam (0° to 6° above horizontal) and at least 0.2 cd in the remaining hemisphere, per FAA L-861T. The pattern is omnidirectional (360°) or uni-directional, with a vertical main beam spread of 2° to 10° above horizontal.

Q6What are the intensity steps?

The 3 to 5 steps correspond to 100%, 30%, 10%, 3% and 1% of rated peak intensity, driven by CCR currents from 2.8 A to 6.6 A. Field intensity tolerance is ±25% of the rated value at each step.

Q7How far apart are the lights spaced?

Typically 15 m to 30 m (50 ft to 100 ft) along taxiway edges.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46F (which covers L-861T-type elevated taxiway lights and supersedes -46E), IEC 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief No. 67D (LED and other non-incandescent sources). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should an elevated taxiway light be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens and blue filter cleaning, semi-annual for impact damage inspection and mounting height and alignment, and annual for photometric intensity check, frangible coupling check, seal and gasket inspection, insulation resistance and cable and connector inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with elevated taxiway lights?

Common problems are LED module or lamp failure, isolating transformer winding failure, connector or splice degradation, frangible coupling fatigue, impact damage, blue filter or lens degradation and base can settlement. A single dark fixture with the rest of the circuit normal usually points to the fixture, transformer or connector rather than the cable.
