Approach Light Conductor/Cable

The specialised electrical cable that carries constant current from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to approach lighting fixtures, forming the backbone of the series circuit. These cables are engineered for high reliability, weather resistance, and durability in the demanding airport environment, ensuring consistent illumination of approach lights in all conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

An Approach Light Conductor/Cable is a specialised electrical cable used to carry power from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to the approach lighting fixtures in a series circuit. These cables are designed to operate in a constant‑current environment (typically 6.6A or 20A) and are engineered to withstand the harsh outdoor conditions of an airfield, including exposure to jet fuel, de‑icing chemicals, UV radiation, moisture, and extreme temperature variations.

The approach light conductor system typically consists of a primary cable (running from the CCR to the isolating transformers) and secondary cables (running from the isolating transformers to the individual light fixtures). The primary cable is the main backbone of the series circuit, while secondary cables provide the final connection to each fixture. Both are available in various conductor sizes (typically 6 mm², 10 mm², or 16 mm² for primary; 1.5 mm² or 2.5 mm² for secondary) and insulation types, depending on the system voltage, current rating, and environmental conditions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The approach light conductor cable's purpose is to carry the CCR's regulated current the full length of the approach lighting array — often hundreds of metres out from the runway threshold — while keeping every fixture along that run receiving the same current and therefore the same intensity, which is what the series-circuit design depends on to work at all. Its insulation and sheathing exist specifically to survive an environment that would degrade an ordinary cable within a few seasons: constant exposure to jet fuel, de-icing chemicals, UV, and temperature extremes, any of which could otherwise cause an insulation failure that either drops a fixture out of the loop or creates a dangerous fault. The distinction between primary and secondary cable sizing reflects this same purpose at two different scales — the primary cable minimising voltage drop and I²R losses over the full loop length, the secondary cable making the short, lower-current connection from transformer to fixture.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Approach light conductors are deployed in all approach lighting systems that require electrical power transmission. They are used in:

  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems – providing power to centreline, crossbar, barrette, and SFL fixtures for CAT I, II, and III precision approaches.
  • MALSF and MALSR systems – supplying power for medium‑intensity approach lighting.
  • Calvert I and II systems – powering barrette and crossbar configurations.
  • Runway edge lighting systems – where similar cable types are used for elevated and inset edge lights.
  • Taxiway lighting systems – using similar cable types for taxiway edge and centreline lights.
  • All airfield lighting circuits – where series circuit constant‑current technology is employed.
Placement

Installation & Location

Conductor cable for the approach lighting system is routed from the CCR or field distribution panel out to the approach light structures, typically in underground ducts or direct-buried trenches beneath the extended runway centreline, then up the frangible structure to each barrette, crossbar, or sequenced flashing light. Underground runs are laid at a depth consistent with the rest of the airfield's series circuit cabling, with junction boxes at intervals to break the run into manageable sections and provide splice or transformer connection points. Where the cable climbs a structure, it is secured and protected along the mast so wind and vibration don't work the conductor loose, and slack is left at the top to allow the frangible coupling to break away without immediately snapping the cable.

Function

How Does It Work?

Approach light conductors operate as an integral part of the series‑connected AGL circuit:

  • Series Circuit Configuration: The primary cable connects all light fixtures in a series loop, with the CCR maintaining a constant current (typically 6.6A) regardless of the number of fixtures or cable length. This ensures all lights have the same brightness.
  • Primary Cable: The main cable runs from the CCR through the airfield, with isolating transformers connected in series along its length. The primary cable carries the full loop current and must have a sufficiently large conductor (typically 6–16 mm²) to minimise voltage drop and I²R losses.
  • Secondary Cable: Short cables (typically 1.5–2.5 mm²) connect the secondary side of each isolating transformer to the individual light fixture. These cables carry the transformed current (also constant) to the lamp or LED module.
  • Insulation and Sheathing: The cables are insulated with robust materials such as cross‑linked polyethylene (XLPE), EPR, or PVC, and are often sheathed with a tough outer layer (e.g., neoprene or PE) for mechanical protection and moisture resistance.
  • Colour Coding: Primary and secondary cables are typically colour‑coded to distinguish them and to identify phase and neutral conductors (e.g., black, red, blue) for ease of installation and maintenance.
  • Connectors: The cables terminate in specialised connectors (e.g., L‑823 connectors) that provide reliable, watertight connections to the isolating transformers and fixtures.
  • Earth/Shield: Many cables include a copper screen or earth conductor to provide protection against electromagnetic interference and ensure electrical safety.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The conductor cable is not a controlled element in its own right — it is the physical layer that every ALCMS/CCR control decision described elsewhere in this system actually travels through. Because the CCR holds the loop at constant current rather than constant voltage, the cable never experiences a variable electrical load; what changes is the voltage the CCR must produce to push that current around the loop, and that voltage is exactly what the control system watches. A healthy cable is electrically invisible to the ALCMS — it simply reports a stable loop voltage. Degradation shows up as a control-system event only once it changes loop impedance: insulation breakdown, a damaged screen, or moisture ingress at a connector raises resistance and therefore loop voltage, which the CCR reports as an open-circuit or high-resistance fault indistinguishable, from the control room, from a failed transformer or lamp. Locating the actual point of cable degradation is done outside the control loop entirely, with insulation-resistance (megger) testing and loop-resistance measurement carried out section by section during scheduled maintenance or after a reported fault.

Construction

Physical Construction

The cable is built from annealed, Class 2 or 5 stranded copper conductors — bare or tinned, depending on soil chemistry at the site — surrounded by a cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) or EPR insulation layer chosen for its wide temperature range, then an outer sheath of neoprene, polyethylene, or PVC formulated for UV stability and resistance to jet fuel, de-icing fluid, and hydraulic fluid. Primary cable is sized at 6, 10, or 16 mm² to carry the full loop current from the CCR to each isolating transformer with minimal voltage drop, while lighter 1.5 or 2.5 mm² secondary cable carries the transformer's stepped-down output the short distance to the fixture. Conductor insulation is colour-coded per FAA/ICAO convention so primary and secondary runs, and different circuits sharing a trench, stay distinguishable during installation and fault-finding years later. Terminations at each end — whether a sealed L-823 splice connector or a transformer lead — are what actually deliver the cable's rated IP67/IP68 water resistance; the cable jacket alone is not a sealed system until it is properly terminated.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Cable Insulation Breakdown

Over time, cable insulation can degrade due to UV exposure, chemical attack, or thermal stress. This can lead to short circuits, earth faults, or reduced insulation resistance. Regular insulation resistance testing (megger testing) is essential to identify deteriorating cables before failure occurs.

Connector Corrosion and Poor Termination

Connectors (L‑823 and others) are a common point of failure. Corrosion from moisture ingress, poor crimping, or loose connections can cause high resistance, leading to heat generation and eventual circuit failure. Regular inspection and re‑termination of connectors are recommended, with dielectric grease applied to prevent corrosion.

Mechanical Damage from Excavation

Approach light cables are buried at shallow depths and are vulnerable to damage from excavation, trenching, or surface works. Marking cable routes and using cable protection covers are essential. Any excavation near cable routes should be preceded by cable location using ground‑penetrating radar or cable detectors.

Water Ingress and Moisture Migration

Moisture ingress into the cable can cause corrosion of the conductor and degradation of the insulation. This is particularly problematic in secondary cables where connectors may not be fully sealed. Using water‑blocking cables, properly sealing connectors, and installing cable glands with appropriate IP ratings are essential.

Earth/Shield Connection Failure

Poor grounding connections can lead to electromagnetic interference (EMI) affecting sensitive control systems, and can also pose a safety hazard. Regular testing of earth continuity and resistance is recommended.

Temperature Cycling and Cable Stress

Extreme temperature fluctuations cause thermal expansion and contraction of the cable, which can stress joints and connectors. Using cables with appropriate temperature ratings and allowing sufficient cable slack at termination points can mitigate this issue.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Insulation Resistance Test Fails or Trends Downward
Likely Cause
UV exposure, chemical attack, or thermal stress has progressively degraded the cable insulation.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a megger (insulation resistance) test on the cable run and compare against the baseline/commissioning value.
Corrective Action
Sectionalise the cable run to isolate the degraded section, and replace it before it progresses to a short circuit or earth fault.
Localized Heating or Circuit Failure at a Connector
Likely Cause
Corrosion from moisture ingress, poor crimping, or a loose connection has raised resistance at an L-823 or similar connector.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform an infrared thermal scan of accessible connectors under load, and inspect suspect connectors for corrosion or looseness.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate the connector with a fresh crimp and dielectric grease applied, and re-seal before backfilling.
Sudden Loss of Circuit Continuity Near Recent Ground Works
Likely Cause
Excavation, trenching, or surface works near the buried cable route has physically damaged the cable.
Diagnostic Steps
Check recent work records against the cable route map, and use ground-penetrating radar or a cable detector to locate the suspected damage point.
Corrective Action
Excavate carefully at the located point, repair or replace the damaged cable section, and update route marking to prevent recurrence.
Corrosion or Reduced Insulation Found in a Secondary Cable
Likely Cause
Moisture has migrated into the cable, often through a connector that was not fully sealed.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the connector seal and cable jacket for signs of moisture ingress; test insulation resistance on the affected secondary run.
Corrective Action
Replace the affected cable section with water-blocking cable, properly seal connectors, and fit cable glands with the correct IP rating.
EMI Affecting Sensitive Control Systems, or a Safety Concern Raised
Likely Cause
A poor or corroded grounding/shield connection on the cable run has degraded the earth path.
Diagnostic Steps
Test earth continuity and resistance along the cable run, checking each grounding/shield connection point.
Corrective Action
Clean and re-terminate corroded grounding connections, and re-test continuity and resistance before returning the circuit to service.
Recurring Joint or Connector Failures at the Same Location
Likely Cause
Extreme temperature cycling is stressing a joint or connector that lacks sufficient cable slack to absorb thermal expansion and contraction.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the joint location for insufficient slack, and confirm the cable's temperature rating matches site conditions.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with adequate slack at the termination point, and use cable rated for the site's temperature range if a mismatch is found.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Insulation Breakdown From Jet Fuel / De-Icing Chemical Exposure
High Severity
Root Cause
Chemical attack on the cable's insulation and sheathing from repeated exposure to jet fuel or de-icing fluid, the specific environment the cable's construction is designed to survive.
Effect
A fixture drops out of the series loop, or a fault develops on the circuit, undermining the equal-current design the whole approach lighting array depends on.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the affected cable segment, sectionalised to locate the degraded run.
UV / Thermal Sheath Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure and temperature extremes along an exposed run degrading the outer sheath over years of service.
Effect
Cracking or brittleness in the sheath that leaves the underlying insulation vulnerable to subsequent moisture or chemical ingress.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of exposed cable runs for sheath cracking or brittleness during scheduled maintenance.
Voltage Drop / I²R Loss Over a Long Cable Run
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Undersized primary cable for the run length, or cumulative resistance increase from ageing conductors over a run that can extend hundreds of metres from the threshold.
Effect
Intensity droop at fixtures furthest from the CCR, undermining the equal-intensity design the series circuit depends on to work correctly.
Detection Method
Voltage drop calculation against the as-installed cable run length and gauge, and comparative photometric testing between near and far fixtures.
Splice / Termination Failure at a Junction Box
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improperly made or corroded splice at a junction box connection point, or thermal-cycling fatigue at the termination over years of service.
Effect
Intermittent operation or an open/ground fault at the specific splice point, affecting the fixture or circuit segment beyond it.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance testing sectionalised to the splice, and visual inspection of the termination during junction box maintenance.
Mechanical Damage From Excavation, Rodents, or Handling
High Severity
Root Cause
Third-party excavation nearby, rodent activity, or damage during handling or installation of adjacent infrastructure.
Effect
Open circuit or ground fault at the damage point, potentially taking a fixture or full circuit segment out of service.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The cable carries constant-current series loop power that can reach several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the cable before cutting, splicing, or terminating it.

Confirm the Correct Cable Run
Operational

On multi-circuit approach lighting arrays, positively identify the correct cable and circuit before isolating work, since cutting into the wrong run can leave live conductors exposed while the intended circuit stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice, repair, or termination, megger test the affected cable run before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the loop.

Correct Conductor Size on Repair
Compliance

Use replacement cable of the correct conductor size and insulation type for the position (primary versus secondary run) rather than a convenient substitute; an undersized conductor will overheat under the loop's constant current.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when splicing or terminating series-circuit cable, and confirm shielding and armouring are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting.

Splice Sealing
Electrical Safety

Seal every splice and termination to the specified standard before burial or reinstatement; moisture ingress at a cable joint is one of the most common causes of an eventual ground fault on the series loop.

FOD Control at the Excavation
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and offcuts accounted for at all times when a cable run is exposed for repair, and reinstate the surrounding ground or duct before leaving the area, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a cable run with an unsealed splice, an undersized repair, or a residual insulation fault after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and correct burial depth before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Insulation Resistance Drift Is the Earliest Sign of Cable Degradation
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at a splice or connector on a series-circuit cable usually shows up first as a slow decline in loop insulation resistance rather than a hard fault; logging megger readings across successive maintenance cycles catches a failing cable section months before it takes the loop down entirely.

De-Icing Fluid Attacks Cable Jackets Faster Than Weather Alone
Durability

Repeated exposure to glycol-based de-icing fluid degrades cable jacket material faster than sun and rain alone, particularly on runs near taxiway and runway intersections that see heavy de-icing traffic; specify jacket materials rated for glycol contact rather than assuming a general outdoor-rated cable is sufficient.

Cable Sizing Affects Loop Balance, Not Just Voltage Drop
Design

Where an approach light series circuit mixes cable runs of different length, undersized conductor on the longer runs can create enough impedance imbalance to produce visibly uneven brightness between fixtures at the same nominal current step, even though every fixture individually tests within tolerance.

Handle Frozen Cable With Extra Care During Cold-Weather Work
Field Practice

Cable jacket and insulation can become significantly less flexible in cold temperatures, and forcing a tight bend radius on frozen cable risks cracking the insulation invisibly; allow extra bend radius margin and, where practical, let cable warm slightly before manipulating it during winter maintenance.

A Splice Kit Is Only as Good as Its Installation, Not Its Rating
Installation

A correctly-specified, moisture-sealed splice kit can still fail prematurely if it's installed with contaminated conductors, an incomplete cure time, or a skipped preparation step; treat splice kit manufacturer instructions as mandatory procedure rather than a general guideline, since most field splice failures trace back to a shortcut in preparation.

Record Actual Installed Cable Route, Not Just the Design Drawing
Documentation

Field routing frequently deviates slightly from the original design drawing to avoid obstructions or existing services; keeping as-built records of actual cable routing prevents future excavation work from unexpectedly striking a live series circuit that the design drawing shows in a different location.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about approach light conductor and cable: primary and secondary cable, sizing, insulation, standards, installation, testing, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an approach light conductor/cable?

It is the specialised electrical cable that carries constant current from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to the approach lighting fixtures in a series circuit, forming the backbone of the circuit. It is designed for a constant-current environment (typically 6.6 A or 20 A) and for the harsh outdoor conditions of an airfield.

Q2What is the difference between primary and secondary cable?

The primary cable runs from the CCR through the isolating transformers in series and carries the full loop current, so it uses a larger conductor of typically 6, 10 or 16 mm² (AWG 10, 8, 6) to limit voltage drop and I²R losses. Secondary cables are short runs of typically 1.5 or 2.5 mm² (AWG 16, 14) from each isolating transformer to its fixture.

Q3Why do approach lights use constant-current cable circuits?

The series-circuit design depends on every fixture receiving the same current, and therefore the same intensity, however long the cable run. The CCR holds the loop at a constant current, typically 6.6 A, regardless of the number of fixtures or cable length, so the cable never sees a variable current load.

Q4What is approach light cable made of?

Typical construction is annealed copper (bare or tinned) in Class 2 or Class 5 stranding, cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE), EPR or PVC insulation, and an outer sheath of neoprene, polyethylene or PVC formulated for UV stability and resistance to jet fuel, de-icing fluid and hydraulic fluid. Temperature rating is -40°C to +90°C for XLPE/EPR, and standard L-824 primary cable is rated 5,000 V. Many cables add a copper screen or earth conductor.

Q5Where is approach light cable used?

It is used in ALSF-1 and ALSF-2 systems, MALSF and MALSR systems, and Calvert I and II systems. Similar cable types are also used in runway edge lighting, taxiway lighting and other airfield circuits that use series constant-current technology.

Q6Which standards apply to approach light cable?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-7 (L-824 underground electrical cable for airport lighting circuits), FAA AC 150/5340-30, ICAO Annex 14, IEC 60228 for conductor construction and sizing, UL 44 for North American installations and BS 6346 for UK/European installations. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q7What are the key installation requirements for approach light cable?

Lay underground runs in duct or direct-buried trenches at a depth consistent with the rest of the airfield series circuit cabling, with route markers and covers. Terminate every cable end at a sealed L-823 connector, confirm primary and secondary conductor sizes for the loop current and voltage drop, keep colour-coded circuits distinguishable, and leave slack at the top of a structure so the frangible coupling can break away safely.

Q8How should approach light cable be tested at commissioning?

Megger-test each run before backfill and energisation (insulation resistance of at least 50 MΩ conductor-to-ground), check end-to-end continuity and polarity, verify burial depth and route, and measure resistance across every splice. Apply a withstand (HiPot) voltage test per the cable rating and measure loaded voltage drop at rated current, then record everything in the commissioning dossier.

Q9How often should approach light cable be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for insulation resistance tests, connector inspection and re-termination, cable route marking verification and earth/shield continuity tests, and semi-annual for cable slack and joint inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What does a falling insulation resistance reading on approach light cable mean?

It usually means UV exposure, chemical attack or thermal stress is progressively degrading the cable insulation. Megger the run and compare against the commissioning baseline, sectionalise to isolate the degraded section, and replace it before it progresses to a short circuit or earth fault.

Q11Why is there localised heating or a circuit failure at a cable connector?

The usual cause is corrosion from moisture ingress, poor crimping or a loose connection raising resistance at an L-823 or similar connector. Scan accessible connectors under load with an infrared camera and inspect for corrosion or looseness, then re-terminate with a fresh crimp and dielectric grease and re-seal before backfilling.

Q12What safety precautions apply when working on approach light cable?

The cable carries series loop power that can reach several kV, so de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy before cutting, splicing or terminating. Positively identify the correct cable on multi-circuit arrays, use replacement cable of the correct conductor size and insulation type, seal every splice before burial, and megger-test the run before restoring power.
