AGL Cables & Connector Engineering

AGL cables and connectors form the electrical pathway between the Constant Current Regulator, isolation transformers and airfield lighting fixtures. Although often treated as simple accessories, their condition and installation quality directly determine circuit reliability — a series circuit can have a healthy CCR and perfectly functioning fixtures, yet still fail because of a cable or connector problem.

Overview

What Is AGL Cable & Connector Engineering?

AGL Cable & Connector Engineering covers the selection, installation, termination, testing and maintenance of the electrical connection system used by an airfield lighting installation — primary and secondary cables, series-circuit connections, isolation transformers, primary and secondary connectors, L-823 plug and receptacle systems where applicable, cable joints, routing, protection, terminations, insulation testing, continuity testing and connector inspection.

The objective is a continuous, electrically sound and environmentally protected circuit. AGL cable and connector engineering therefore requires both electrical design and field installation discipline.

Not a Simple Accessory

Cables and connectors carry the same engineering weight as the CCR or the fixture — a weak link anywhere in the chain can fail the whole circuit.

Built for the Environment

AGL cables operate exposed to moisture, soil, pavement, temperature change, construction activity, vehicle movement, lightning and repeated maintenance.

Design Plus Discipline

A reliable connection system needs sound electrical design and equally disciplined field installation, termination and testing practice.

Why Cables Fail

Why AGL Cables Are Different

A series circuit can have a perfectly functioning fixture and a healthy CCR, yet still fail because of the cable or connector system. The cable and connector system must be designed and installed for the actual airport environment, which can include:

The Two Sides

Primary vs Secondary Connections

Understanding the two sides of the isolation transformer is essential — the connector type, cable arrangement and electrical characteristics must match the applicable equipment on each side.

Primary Side

Connects to the series circuit.

  • CCR output
  • Primary cable
  • Primary connectors
  • Isolation transformer primary winding

Secondary Side

Connects the transformer to the fixture or equipment.

  • Transformer secondary
  • Secondary connector
  • Fixture cable
  • Light fixture
Primary Side vs Secondary Side — Across the Transformer

The isolation transformer is the boundary — everything to its left belongs to the series circuit, everything to its right belongs to the individual fixture

Selection, Rating & Sizing

AGL Cable Selection

Do not select an AGL cable based only on conductor size — the complete cable construction must be suitable for the intended application.

Selection Factors

Consider the complete application, not just the conductor.

  • Voltage & current
  • Circuit configuration
  • Insulation system
  • Cable length
  • Installation environment & temperature
  • Mechanical requirements
  • Applicable airport specifications
  • Connector compatibility
  • Manufacturer requirements

Cable Rating

Key parameters that define whether a cable is fit for purpose.

  • Conductor size & material
  • Insulation type
  • Voltage & temperature rating
  • Outer diameter
  • Flexibility & mechanical strength
  • Environmental resistance

Cable Sizing

Sizing should never be based on current capacity alone.

  • Current carrying capacity
  • Voltage drop
  • Insulation rating
  • Installation conditions
  • Complete circuit configuration
  • CCR operating characteristics
Electrical Calculation

Voltage Drop

Voltage drop can become important on long AGL circuits. Consider circuit length, cable resistance, operating current, the number of circuit elements, transformer characteristics and CCR output.

Basic Electrical Relationship

V = I × R   and   R = ρL / A

V = voltage drop  ·  I = current  ·  R = resistance  ·  ρ = conductor resistivity  ·  L = conductor length  ·  A = conductor cross-sectional area

Actual series-circuit calculations should account for the complete AGL circuit characteristics — voltage drop is not a stand-alone check performed on the cable in isolation.

Field Connections

AGL Connector Engineering

AGL connectors provide the field connection between cables, transformers and lighting equipment. A connector is therefore not merely a plug — it must provide electrical continuity, mechanical connection and environmental protection together.

Primary Connectors

Associated with the series circuit and isolation transformers. Inspect:

  • Connector type & cable compatibility
  • Contact condition
  • Termination & insulation
  • Mechanical locking
  • Water protection
  • Physical damage & identification

Secondary Connectors

Connect the isolation transformer secondary to the lighting equipment. Verify:

  • Correct connector type & mating
  • Correct equipment compatibility
  • Cable termination
  • Contact condition & sealing
  • Mechanical security
  • Insulation

L-823 Plug & Receptacle

Where specified, the plug/receptacle arrangement must be compatible with the applicable cable and equipment. Do not mix connector components solely because they physically appear compatible. Check:

  • Correct connector configuration & cable size
  • Correct termination & mating
  • Contact integrity, insulation, sealing
  • Identification

Field Reliability Risk

Connector Water Ingress & Inspection

Water ingress is a major concern in outdoor AGL installations and can eventually cause insulation deterioration, corrosion, leakage, intermittent operation or complete circuit failure.

Possible Causes

  • Damaged sealing
  • Incorrect termination
  • Poor connector assembly
  • Improper cable entry
  • Damaged connector
  • Flooded installation location
  • Incorrect storage or ageing

What Inspection Should Cover

  • Physical: cracks, damage, deformation, corrosion
  • Electrical: contact condition, continuity, insulation
  • Environmental: water, moisture, contamination, sealing
  • Mechanical: locking, cable support, strain relief
Connector Cross-Section — Sealed vs Compromised

A properly mated and sealed connector keeps water out of the contact zone entirely — a damaged or partially mated seal gives water a direct path to the contacts

Traceability

Cable Identification & Joint Engineering

Every cable should be traceable to its circuit, and every joint should be treated as an engineered electrical connection — not simply a repaired cable.

Cable Identification

Correct identification significantly reduces maintenance errors. Record:

  • Circuit number & cable number
  • From / To
  • Cable type
  • Installation location & date

Cable Joint Engineering

Avoid unnecessary joints in critical AGL circuits. Where permitted by design, joints should be:

  • Properly designed & suitable for the cable
  • Environmentally protected & mechanically secure
  • Electrically tested & identified
  • Accessible where required
Where Failures Concentrate

Critical Interfaces

Two interfaces carry most of the risk in an AGL circuit. A mismatch at either can create repeated failures that look like a fixture or transformer problem.

Cable ↔ Connector ↔ Isolation Transformer

Verify:

  • Correct cable, connector & transformer
  • Correct termination & electrical rating
  • Correct mechanical fit & sealing

Transformer ↔ Connector ↔ Fixture

Verify:

  • Fixture type & transformer compatibility
  • Connector compatibility & cable
  • Termination & polarity where applicable
  • Sealing & mechanical security
Verification

Cable & Connector Testing

The exact test method and acceptance criteria should always follow the applicable project and equipment requirements.

Continuity Testing

Verifies the complete intended circuit path, for example: CCR → Cable → Transformer → Connector → Fixture → Connector → Transformer → Cable → CCR. Record results for each circuit.

Insulation Resistance Testing

Can identify moisture, insulation deterioration, damaged cable, ground leakage, poor termination or connector problems. Sensitive connected equipment should be isolated where required.

Visual & Circuit Verification

Visual inspection checks physical damage; circuit verification confirms the correct circuit connection has actually been made.

Typical Faults

  • Open circuit / short circuit
  • Insulation breakdown / ground fault
  • High resistance
  • Moisture-related leakage
  • Physical damage
  • Incorrect termination or joint
  • Cross-connected circuit

Connector Faults

Typical Faults

  • Poor contact / loose connection
  • Partial mating
  • Corrosion / water ingress
  • Damaged insulation
  • Incorrect or wrong connector
  • Mechanical damage

High-Resistance Connection

Causes

  • Poor termination / loose connection
  • Damaged contact or corrosion
  • Incorrect connector
  • Mechanical damage

Symptoms

  • Abnormal circuit behaviour, heating
  • Voltage drop, intermittent operation
  • Repeated component failure

Faults After Rain

Investigate

  • Fixture → Secondary connector
  • Transformer → Primary connector
  • Cable → Joint → Pit/Chamber

Do not replace the fixture repeatedly without checking the complete connection path.

Cable Basics

Airfield Lighting Cable Working Principle, Types, Functions & Design

Airfield Lighting Cable Working Principle

Airfield lighting cable working principle: the cable carries one constant current from the CCR round the series loop, passing through the primary of every isolating transformer in turn. Light output depends on that current, not on supply voltage.

Airfield Lighting Cable Types

Airfield lighting cable types include single-core primary series cable rated for the circuit voltage (commonly 5 kV class), 600 V secondary leads to the fixtures, bare copper counterpoise or earth conductor, and control or fibre cables.

Airfield Lighting Cable Functions

Airfield lighting cable functions are to carry series current reliably, insulate it from earth and moisture for decades underground, and keep a long loop of lights working as one circuit.

Airfield Lighting Cable Components

Airfield lighting cable components are the copper conductor, insulation, outer sheath or jacket, and any shield or armour, plus joints, terminations and L-823 connectors that complete the circuit.

Airfield Lighting Cable Specification

An airfield lighting cable specification states conductor size, insulation material and thickness, voltage rating, sheath, and test requirements. For FAA-based projects the L-824 cable specification (AC 150/5345-7) is commonly cited.

Airfield Lighting Cable Requirements

Airfield lighting cable requirements cover the voltage and current rating of the circuit, insulation level, mechanical protection, moisture resistance, and the route and installation conditions, taken from the project specification and standards.

Airfield Lighting Cable Design

Airfield lighting cable design sets the conductor size, circuit length and loading, route, depth, ducts and separation, and checks that loop resistance and insulation level suit the CCR. Record it in circuit schedules and drawings.

Sizing & Installation

Airfield Lighting Cable Sizing, Duct Bank, Trench & Pulling Procedure

Airfield Lighting Cable Sizing

Airfield lighting cable sizing checks the conductor against circuit current, loop resistance and the CCR's output voltage capability, plus thermal and fault limits and practical pulling strength. Longer circuits may need a larger conductor.

AGL Duct Bank Installation

AGL duct bank installation uses ducts laid in a prepared trench, often concrete-encased under pavements, with pull pits or manholes at set intervals, spare ducts, correct falls for drainage and sealed duct ends.

Airfield Lighting Cable Trench

An airfield lighting cable trench is excavated to the depth and width on the drawings, with a smooth bed, backfill free of sharp stones, warning tape and route markers. Keep power and control cables separated as specified.

AGL Cable Pulling Procedure

An AGL cable pulling procedure sets maximum pulling tension, minimum bend radius, lubricant, pulling grip and communication between ends. Test insulation before and after pulling to catch damage early.

Airfield Lighting Cable Installation

Airfield lighting cable installation covers route marking, trenching or ducting, pulling or laying, jointing and termination, then insulation testing and as-built recording at each stage.

Airfield Lighting Cable Selection Guide

Airfield lighting cable selection guide: match voltage class and conductor size to the circuit design, choose insulation and sheath for soil, moisture and duct conditions, and confirm compatibility with joint kits and connectors.

Airfield Lighting Cable Technical Guide

Airfield lighting cable technical guide: use this page with the manufacturer datasheet, installation drawings and test records, so each cable's rating, route and condition are known.

Airfield Lighting Cable Standards

Airfield lighting cable standards include FAA AC 150/5345-7 for L-824 cable, FAA AC 150/5340-30 for installation, ICAO Aerodrome Design Manual Part 5 and national requirements. See the Standards & References section.

Connectors & Testing

AGL Connector Installation, Cable Insulation Testing & Maintenance

AGL Connector Installation

AGL connector installation means preparing the cable end cleanly, fitting the L-823 plug or receptacle to the manufacturer's instructions, moulding or sealing as required, and testing the joint before it is buried.

AGL Connector Overheating

AGL connector overheating usually comes from a poor crimp or loose contact, corrosion, water ingress or overloading. De-energise and isolate safely before inspection, then replace any discoloured or damaged connector.

AGL Cable Insulation Testing

AGL cable insulation testing uses an insulation resistance tester at the voltage the specification calls for, measuring conductor to earth, with the circuit isolated. Record results and compare them with earlier readings.

Airfield Lighting Cable Testing Procedure

Airfield lighting cable testing procedure: isolate and discharge the circuit, check continuity and loop resistance, test insulation resistance, and record values against the acceptance criteria in the specification.

Airfield Lighting Cable Commissioning

Airfield lighting cable commissioning confirms insulation and continuity results, correct connections and labelling, and that the circuit runs at its rated current, with all tests recorded in the commissioning dossier.

Airfield Lighting Cable Maintenance

Airfield lighting cable maintenance focuses on trending insulation resistance, inspecting joints and connectors, protecting cable from excavation damage, and keeping route records current.

Airfield Lighting Cable Preventive Maintenance

Airfield lighting cable preventive maintenance is a scheduled programme of insulation tests, connector inspections and route checks, so degrading cable is found and repaired before it causes a circuit fault.

Airfield Lighting Cable Troubleshooting

Airfield lighting cable troubleshooting starts with symptoms such as earth fault alarms or low insulation resistance, then isolates the section by sectionalising the circuit and testing each part until the faulty cable or joint is found.

Common Cable & Connector Engineering Mistakes

01

Selecting cable only by conductor size

The complete cable construction matters, not just the copper.

02

Using an incompatible connector

03

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is AGL cable and connector engineering?

AGL cable and connector engineering covers the selection, installation, termination, testing and maintenance of the electrical connection system in an airfield lighting installation. That includes primary and secondary cables, series-circuit connections, primary and secondary connectors, L-823 plug and receptacle systems where applicable, cable joints, routing, protection, insulation testing, continuity testing and connector inspection. The objective is a continuous, electrically sound and environmentally protected circuit.

Q2Why do cables and connectors matter so much for AGL circuit reliability?

A series circuit can have a healthy CCR and perfectly functioning fixtures and still fail because of a cable or connector problem. Cables and connectors carry the same engineering weight as the CCR or the fixture, and a weak link anywhere in the chain can take down the whole circuit.

Q3What is the AGL electrical connection chain?

A typical series-circuit arrangement runs from the CCR through the primary cable and isolation transformer, across the primary and secondary connectors to the light fixture, and then back to the CCR. The exact arrangement depends on the equipment and project design.

Q4What is the difference between primary and secondary connections?

The isolation transformer is the boundary. The primary side connects to the series circuit: the CCR output, primary cable, primary connectors and the transformer primary winding. The secondary side connects the transformer to the fixture: the transformer secondary, secondary connector, fixture cable and light fixture. Connector type, cable arrangement and electrical characteristics must match the applicable equipment on each side.

Q5How should an AGL cable be selected?

An AGL cable should not be selected on conductor size alone. The complete cable construction must suit the application, considering voltage and current, circuit configuration, insulation system, cable length, installation environment and temperature, mechanical requirements, airport specifications, connector compatibility and manufacturer requirements.

Q6Is AGL cable sizing based only on current capacity?

No. Sizing should also consider voltage drop, insulation rating, installation conditions, the complete circuit configuration and CCR operating characteristics. Voltage drop is not a stand-alone check on the cable in isolation; actual series-circuit calculations should account for the complete AGL circuit characteristics.

Q7Why is cable termination such a critical step?

A poor termination can become a high-resistance or intermittent connection long after the installation appears successful. Termination checks include the correct connector, stripping length, conductor preparation, crimp or termination method, insulation arrangement, sealing and identification.

Q8Can L-823 plug and receptacle components be mixed if they physically fit?

No. Components should not be mixed solely because they appear physically compatible. The configuration, cable size, termination, mating, contact integrity, insulation, sealing and identification must all be checked against approved technical documentation and the applicable equipment.

Q9What damages AGL cables during installation?

Excessive pulling force and sharp bends can damage insulation or internal construction, sometimes without any damage being visible at the time. Good practice includes controlled pulling tension, respecting the manufacturer's minimum bend radius, protecting cable ends, and never assuming a newly delivered cable is defect-free.

Q10How does water ingress affect AGL cables and connectors?

Water ingress is a major concern in outdoor AGL installations. It can eventually cause insulation deterioration, corrosion, leakage, intermittent operation or complete circuit failure, which is why insulation resistance testing and connector inspection matter.

Q11How to test an airfield lighting cable?

Isolate and discharge the circuit, check continuity and loop resistance, then measure insulation resistance from conductor to earth at the test voltage the specification requires. Record the readings and compare them with earlier results to spot deterioration.

Q12How to test L-823 connectors?

Inspect each connector for damage, moisture and correct moulding, then check continuity and insulation resistance of the circuit with the connector in place. Overheating or a rising loop resistance points to a poor contact that should be replaced.

Q13How to size an AGL cable?

Start from the circuit current and total load, calculate loop resistance and the voltage the CCR must supply, then check thermal, fault and pulling limits. Select the smallest standard conductor that meets all of them, with margin for future load.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
The Connector Is Part of the Insulation System
Connectors

A mated connector must restore the cable's insulation integrity and water tightness. Poor moulding, contaminated surfaces or wrong sealing practice are common reasons an apparently good cable fails after energisation.

Water Ingress Often Starts at the Termination, Not the Cable
Water Ingress

Most moisture problems appear at joints, entries and connector interfaces, so inspect and test these points first when insulation resistance trends downward.

Respect Bend Radius and Pulling Tension During Installation
Installation

Damage from over-bending or excessive pulling may not show until after the cable is buried. Control pulling methods, use lubricants where permitted and keep records of the installation conditions.

Mark and Record Every Joint
Documentation

Underground joints are the hardest assets to find later. Record location, jointing method, date and test results in the as-built drawings so future fault tracing starts from facts.

Test Before and After Backfill
Testing

A cable that passes in the drum or trench can be damaged by compaction, stones or later civil work. Repeat insulation tests after backfill and before energising.

Keep Mixed Systems Visible
Compatibility

Mating components from different makes or generations can fit mechanically without being approved to work together. Verify compatibility and keep a clear identification scheme for primary and secondary connections.
