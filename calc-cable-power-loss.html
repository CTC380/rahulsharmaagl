Calculator #22

Cable Power Loss

I²R heat loss along a primary AGL cable run, at each brightness step and over a year of operation.

About this calculator

The cable loses power as heat in proportion to the square of the series current, so it falls steeply at lower brightness steps. Add each cable section, choose the brightness steps and how long the circuit spends on each, and the tool reports the loss in watts, the heat per metre, the share of the load, and the yearly energy.

Method & Reference

How the loss is calculated

Every step is shown so the result can be checked by hand.

1. Loop resistance

Each section's resistance comes from the conductor's nominal Ω/km at 20 °C, corrected for temperature, then the sections are added.

R = Σ ρ₂₀ × [1 + 0.00393 × (T − 20)] × L ÷ 1000

2. Loss at each step

The same current flows through the whole series loop, so each section's loss adds directly.

P = I² × R

Halving the current cuts the loss to a quarter, so the lower steps lose much less than full brightness.

3. Yearly energy and cost

E = Σ P × (hours × share) ÷ 1000    Cost = E × price

Hours on each step are the operating hours multiplied by that step's share.

4. Share of load and heat per metre

Share = P_full ÷ (real load × 1000) × 100    Heat = P_full ÷ L

What this tool does not do

It does not calculate voltage drop (see Calculator #15), include the isolating transformer or fixture losses, connectors, splices or reactance, or cover aluminium conductors. Resistance values are nominal DC figures, so confirm them against your cable data sheet. AWG sizes use nominal stranded-copper values at 20 °C (ASTM B3/B8 basis, about 2 % above the ideal-copper figures); metric sizes use the IEC 60228 Class 2 maximum.

FAQ

Cable Power Loss Calculator: Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers on I squared R heat loss along a primary AGL cable run.

What does the Cable Power Loss calculator do?

It calculates the I squared R heat loss along a primary AGL cable run, at each brightness step and over a year of operation. It reports the loss in watts, the heat per metre, the share of the load and the yearly energy.

How is the cable loss calculated?

The loop resistance is the sum of each section's nominal ohms per km at 20 degrees C, corrected for temperature, times its length. The same current flows through the whole series loop, so the loss is the current squared times the resistance, and each section's loss adds directly. Halving the current cuts the loss to a quarter, so the lower steps lose much less than full brightness.

Can I enter the length one-way or in feet?

Yes. You can enter the total loop length (out and back) or the one-way route length, which is doubled for the loop. The length unit can be metres or feet. Each section can use a listed conductor or a custom conductor in ohms per km at 20 degrees C taken from your cable data sheet.

How do the brightness steps and operating hours work?

Choose a step preset (5-step 6.6 A, 3-step 6.6 A, 5-step 20 A, or a custom table) and enter the share of operating hours on each step. The 4,000 hours and the shares shown are placeholders, so replace them with your own airfield operating profile and check step currents against your CCR data sheet.

How are the yearly energy and cost worked out?

Energy is the sum over the steps of the loss times the hours on that step, divided by 1,000, where the hours are the operating hours times the step's share. Cost is the energy times the price per kWh, in any currency. Leave the price at 0 to skip the cost.

What are the share of load and the heat per metre?

The share of load is the full-brightness loss divided by the real load in watts, times 100. Enter the real power of the lamps and transformers, not including the cable loss, and leave it at 0 to skip. The heat per metre is the full-brightness loss divided by the loop length.

Can you give a worked example?

A #8 AWG loop of 3,000 m at 20 degrees C has a resistance of 6.306 ohms. The loss is 274.7 W at 6.6 A, 170.5 W at 5.2 A and 49.4 W at 2.8 A. At 6.6 A the heat is 0.092 W per metre, the share of a 5 kW load is 5.49 percent, and the energy over 4,000 hours at the default shares is 440.5 kWh.

What does the tool not do?

It does not calculate voltage drop (see Calculator #15), or include isolating transformer or fixture losses, connectors, splices, reactance or aluminium conductors. Resistance is held constant across steps and the cable's own heating is ignored. Resistance values are nominal DC figures, so final design shall be verified against the cable manufacturer's data and the project specification.
