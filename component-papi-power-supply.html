PAPI Power Supply

The electrical source that energizes the PAPI light units — from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) that powers halogen lamps via series circuits, to dedicated LED drivers that provide regulated current to modern LED modules. Reliable power delivery ensures consistent intensity and uninterrupted glide slope guidance in all weather conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Power Supply encompasses the electrical systems that provide energy to the PAPI light units. The specific configuration depends on the type of light source used:

  • Halogen PAPI Systems: Powered by a Constant Current Regulator (CCR) that supplies a regulated 6.6A current to the series circuit. Each PAPI unit is connected via an isolating transformer that steps down the voltage while maintaining the constant current. The CCR receives mains power (typically 115/230 VAC) and converts it to the required series current, with selectable intensity steps.
  • LED PAPI Systems: May use a CCR (if designed for series circuit compatibility) with LED drivers in each unit, or a dedicated low‑voltage DC power supply that feeds the LED modules directly. In some modern systems, a central power supply unit converts mains AC to a regulated DC voltage (e.g., 24V or 48V) and distributes it to the LED units via a parallel or distributed power architecture.
  • Portable PAPI: Uses battery packs (often rechargeable Li‑ion or lead‑acid) with integrated charging circuits, sometimes supplemented by solar panels for extended operation.

The power supply is critical to the performance and reliability of the PAPI system. It must deliver clean, stable power with precise current regulation to maintain the required luminous intensity and ensure that all units in the array produce consistent light output.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The power supply's purpose goes beyond simply keeping the lamps lit — PAPI works by a pilot comparing the relative brightness and colour of four units side by side, so what matters most is that every unit receives exactly the same, stable current as the others. If one unit runs even slightly dimmer because of an underpowered or poorly regulated supply, the red/white balance a pilot relies on to judge glide slope becomes distorted in a way that has nothing to do with the aircraft's actual position. This is why constant-current regulation, rather than simple constant-voltage power, is central to the design: it holds every unit in the array to the same output regardless of cable length or connection resistance differences between units. The same regulation also has to support clean switching between day, twilight, and night intensity steps without one unit lagging or overshooting relative to the others during the transition.

Architecture

Power Supply Configurations

PAPI power supplies come in several configurations depending on the system type and installation environment:

CCR-Based (Halogen)

Constant Current Regulator supplies 6.6A to the series circuit. Isolating transformers at each unit step down voltage. Provides 3 or 5 brightness steps. Robust and proven, but less efficient than LED solutions.

LED Driver (Per Unit)

Each PAPI unit contains an integrated LED driver that converts the 6.6A series current (or a DC input) to the regulated current for the LED array. Often includes PWM dimming for intensity control and status monitoring.

DC Distributed (Modern)

A central rectifier converts mains AC to a regulated DC bus (24V or 48V). The DC is distributed to each PAPI unit, where local DC‑DC converters or linear regulators power the LEDs. Offers higher efficiency and simpler wiring.

Battery / Solar (Portable)

Rechargeable battery packs (Li‑ion or lead‑acid) with solar charging capability. Includes charge controllers, battery management systems, and DC‑DC converters to provide regulated power to the PAPI units.

Hybrid (CCR + LED)

Retrofit systems where a CCR is retained but halogen lamps are replaced with LED modules. The LED modules include specialized drivers that accept the 6.6A series current and provide PWM control, allowing gradual upgrade of existing infrastructure.

UPS / Backup

Uninterruptible Power Supply systems that provide battery backup to the PAPI power supply during mains failure. Critical for CAT II/III precision runways where continuous operation is mandatory.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The PAPI power supply is an essential component of every PAPI installation, with variations for different operational requirements:

  • Precision runways (CAT I, II, III) – CCR-based systems with UPS backup, providing reliable 6.6A series power and multiple intensity steps.
  • Non‑precision runways – simpler CCR or DC power supplies, often with manual intensity control.
  • Heliports – compact power supplies, sometimes with lower power ratings for helicopter PAPI units.
  • Portable or temporary PAPI – battery or solar power supplies for rapid deployment in remote locations.
  • Military airfields – ruggedized power supplies with high reliability and EMI filtering.
  • Retrofit upgrades – LED drivers and power supplies that allow existing halogen PAPI systems to be upgraded to LED without replacing the entire infrastructure.
Placement

Installation & Location

The power supply for a permanent PAPI installation is housed in the CCR room or equipment shelter serving that runway, wired into the series circuit (or DC distribution, depending on topology) that feeds the wing bar, with UPS backup added on precision runways where continuous availability is mandated. Cabling runs from the shelter out to each unit on the bar via underground ducting and the same isolating transformers used elsewhere in the AGL network. On portable and temporary installations, the power supply is instead integrated into or mounted alongside the unit itself — a battery pack, solar panel, or small generator — sited close to the wing bar rather than in a fixed building, so the whole system can be set up and struck without permanent infrastructure. In every case, the supply's location is chosen to keep cable runs to the wing bar within the system's voltage-drop limits.

Function

How Does It Work?

The PAPI power supply performs several critical functions to ensure reliable and consistent illumination:

  • AC to DC/Current Conversion: For CCR‑based systems, the input mains AC is converted to a regulated 6.6A constant current, regardless of load variations. This is achieved using a saturable reactor, thyristor, or IGBT-based regulator. For DC‑distributed systems, the AC is rectified and filtered to produce a stable DC voltage.
  • Intensity Control: The power supply receives brightness commands from the control unit (or ALCMS) and adjusts the output current or PWM duty cycle accordingly. In CCRs, this is done by switching transformer taps or electronically controlling the current setpoint. In LED drivers, it's typically achieved via PWM dimming.
  • Protection: The power supply includes overcurrent, overvoltage, short‑circuit, and thermal protection to prevent damage to the PAPI units and the power supply itself. Faults are reported to the control system.
  • Monitoring: The power supply monitors its own status (input voltage, output current, temperature) and, in advanced systems, the status of each connected PAPI unit. This data is made available to the control unit for diagnostics and maintenance planning.
  • Isolation: In CCR‑based systems, isolating transformers at each PAPI unit provide galvanic isolation between the primary series circuit and the secondary lamp circuit, enhancing safety.
  • Filtering: EMI filters and surge protection devices are integrated to protect the power supply from transients and to prevent electromagnetic interference with other airport systems.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The power supply is where the intensity command is actually converted into current — it receives its brightness setting from the control unit or ALCMS and adjusts either the CCR's output current (legacy series-circuit topology) or a PWM duty cycle (modern DC bus / LED-driver topology) to match. Sitting alongside that command path is a set of protection functions — overcurrent, overvoltage, short-circuit and thermal cutoffs — that run independently of the intensity logic and take precedence over it whenever a limit is exceeded.

Because a PAPI's glide-slope function depends on continuous, correctly-graduated illumination, the supply's monitoring — input voltage, output current, internal temperature, and, on advanced units, per-fixture status — feeds back into the same fault-reporting chain as the rest of the AGL system, so a developing problem is flagged to the control unit and ALCMS well before it becomes a hard outage.

ALCMS → Power Supply/CCR (+ Protection Logic) → Isolating Transformer → PAPI Lamps (status feedback)

The two topologies solve the same problem — even current to every unit — in opposite ways. The CCR path keeps a single current loop and isolates each lamp locally, so one failed transformer doesn't affect the rest of the circuit; the DC bus path pushes regulation out to each unit's own driver, trading that shared-loop resilience for higher efficiency and simpler wiring.
Construction

Physical Construction

A CCR-fed halogen installation places the constant current regulator in an equipment room enclosure, converting mains input into a regulated series current fed out through cable glands to the primary loop, with each PAPI unit's isolating transformer built into its own base can or junction box along that loop. An LED PAPI system built around a central DC power supply instead houses a switch-mode power supply in a cabinet or equipment room, rectifying and regulating mains input down to a low DC voltage that is distributed by parallel wiring — a heavier-gauge cable than a series loop, since parallel distribution carries the full current to every branch point rather than a single shared loop current. Portable PAPI units carry their own sealed rechargeable battery pack and charge controller inside the fixture's own housing or a companion equipment box, with a solar panel, where fitted, mounted above or beside the unit and wired through a charge regulator to trickle-charge the battery during daylight. In every configuration, the power conversion and regulation electronics are housed separately from the light-emitting optics, connected to them only by the cable carrying the regulated current or voltage.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

CCR Tap Switching Failures

In older CCRs, the mechanical tap switches or relays can fail, resulting in incorrect intensity steps. This can cause the PAPI to be too bright or too dim. Regular inspection and replacement of worn contactors is essential. Consider upgrading to solid‑state switching for reliability.

LED Driver Overheating

LED drivers integrated into the PAPI unit can overheat if the heatsink is clogged with dust or if the ambient temperature is high. This can reduce the driver's life or cause thermal shutdown, leading to intermittent operation. Regular cleaning and thermal paste renewal are recommended.

Input Voltage Fluctuations

Mains voltage dips or surges can affect the output of the power supply, especially in CCRs without active regulation. Use a UPS or voltage stabilizer for critical installations, and ensure the CCR has wide input voltage tolerance.

Battery Degradation (Portable)

In battery‑powered PAPI systems, battery capacity decreases over time and with temperature. Regular capacity testing and timely replacement of batteries are essential to ensure the system can operate for the required duration. Use battery management systems that monitor health and provide early warnings.

Communication Interference

In systems where the power supply communicates with the control unit (e.g., for dimming or status monitoring), electrical noise can interfere with communication, causing erratic behavior. Use shielded cables and proper grounding to minimize interference.

Incorrect Series Circuit Loading

If the total load on the series circuit (number of PAPI units, wattage of lamps) is not matched to the CCR rating, the output current may not be stable. Always ensure the CCR is correctly sized for the load and that the circuit is properly terminated.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

High Open-Circuit Series Voltage
Electrical Safety

On CCR-fed halogen or LED PAPI systems, the series loop across the four-unit array can present several kV open-circuit; de-energise and lock out the CCR at its supply, and verify zero energy at any output terminal or isolating transformer connection before touching it.

Residual Stored Charge
Electrical Safety

Internal filter capacitors in a central DC supply configuration can retain a dangerous charge after shutdown; observe the manufacturer's specified discharge time, or confirm discharge with a rated meter, before opening the enclosure.

Hot Surfaces
Personnel Safety

Allow halogen lamp housings, isolating transformers, and CCR heatsinks to cool before handling after active operation; halogen PAPI units in particular retain significant heat.

Portable/Battery Configuration Hazards
Personnel Safety

On portable PAPI configurations fed from a 24/48VDC battery source, treat the battery as a high-current short-circuit hazard even when the unit is switched off; use insulated tools and follow the manufacturer's disconnect sequence.

Glide-Path Guidance Is Safety-Critical — Coordinate Before Isolating
Operational

PAPI provides visual glide-path guidance on final approach; taking any unit or the shared power supply out of service requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM before isolation, since aircraft may be relying on it for approach guidance.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the series loop before restoring power after any cable, connector, or unit work, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or leave one or more units dark or at reduced intensity.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm every unit in the array is illuminated at the correct intensity step and that beam angle/aim has not been disturbed before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify Current Regulation Across the Full Step Range, Not Just at 100%
Electrical

A CCR that regulates cleanly at full brightness can still show poor current stability on its lowest intensity step, where control loop performance is often weakest. Check regulation and ripple across every step used operationally, not just the step the acceptance test happens to default to.

Size Isolating Transformers to the Installed Load, Not the Catalogue Default
Design

When a system migrates from halogen to LED PAPI units on the same series circuit, reusing the original transformer VA rating sized for the old lamp load leaves the transformer significantly oversized for the new draw, which can itself introduce regulation and efficiency issues; re-select based on actual installed load.

Mixed Series and Parallel Architectures Need Separate Fault Logic
Design

Where an LED PAPI array mixes a series-circuit CCR feed with a parallel DC distribution stage for the LED drivers, a single fault-detection scheme borrowed from a pure series or pure parallel design usually misses failure modes unique to the hybrid architecture; verify the monitoring logic was actually designed for the specific topology installed.

Battery Capacity Fade on Portable PAPI Is Non-Linear
Reliability

Rechargeable battery packs on portable PAPI units hold their rated runtime reasonably well for most of their service life and then fall off sharply near end-of-life; a runtime check that looked fine a few months ago isn't a reliable predictor of current capacity, so test runtime directly rather than trusting age-based replacement schedules alone.

Solar Supplement Sizing Assumes Worst-Case, Not Average, Conditions
Design

A solar-supplemented battery system sized against average regional insolation will run short during a stretch of overcast days; size for worst-case consecutive low-sun days for the deployment location and season, since portable PAPI is often needed precisely during poor-visibility periods.

An Intensity Complaint May Be a Power Supply Issue, Not an Optical One
Troubleshooting

A uniform, array-wide intensity shortfall that all units share equally points toward the shared power supply or CCR rather than a coincidental optical fault in every unit; check the supply's actual delivered current before pulling and testing individual optical assemblies.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about PAPI power supplies: CCR and LED configurations, regulation, intensity control, standards, testing, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a PAPI power supply?

The PAPI power supply is the electrical system that energises the PAPI light units. For halogen PAPI it is a Constant Current Regulator (CCR) feeding a series circuit; for LED PAPI it is either a CCR with LED drivers in each unit or a central DC power supply; and portable PAPI uses battery packs, sometimes with solar charging. It must deliver clean, stable power so every unit in the array produces the same light output.

Q2Why does a PAPI need constant current regulation rather than constant voltage?

A pilot judges the glide slope by comparing the relative brightness and colour of the four units, so every unit must receive exactly the same, stable current. If one unit runs slightly dimmer, the red/white balance is distorted in a way that has nothing to do with the aircraft's position. Constant-current regulation holds every unit to the same output regardless of cable length or connection resistance differences between units.

Q3What is the difference between a CCR-fed and a DC-distributed PAPI supply?

A CCR keeps a single series current loop (6.6 A) and isolates each lamp locally with an isolating transformer, so one failed transformer does not affect the rest of the circuit. A DC-distributed system uses a central rectifier to create a regulated 24 V or 48 V DC bus, distributed in parallel to each unit where a local DC-DC converter or driver powers the LEDs. That trades the shared-loop resilience for higher efficiency and simpler wiring.

Q4How is PAPI intensity controlled by the power supply?

The supply receives brightness commands from the control unit or ALCMS. In CCRs this is done by switching transformer taps or electronically controlling the current setpoint, giving 3 or 5 steps between 2.8 A and 6.6 A. In LED drivers it is typically done by PWM dimming from 0 to 100%. Protection functions such as overcurrent, overvoltage, short-circuit and thermal cutoffs run independently and take precedence over the intensity logic.

Q5What are the typical specifications of a PAPI power supply?

A CCR for halogen PAPI typically takes 115/230 VAC ±10% at 50/60 Hz, outputs 6.6 A constant current, handles up to 5 kVA and has an efficiency of 75% to 85%. A per-unit LED driver is typically 50 W to 150 W with 85% to 95% efficiency, and a central DC supply is typically 200 W to 1 kW with 90% to 95% efficiency. Operating temperature and protection class vary by type, from IP20 indoor to IP67 for drivers integrated in the fixture.

Q6Do PAPI power supplies need UPS or battery backup?

UPS or battery backup is added on precision runways (CAT II/III) where continuous operation is mandatory, and it must be sized for the installation and transfer without exceeding the output specification. Non-precision runways often use a simpler CCR or DC supply. Portable or temporary PAPI instead uses its own battery pack, sometimes supplemented by solar panels.

Q7Which standards apply to PAPI power supplies?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-28 (PAPI system power supply specifications), FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program), IEC 61000-6-2 and IEC 61000-6-4 for EMC immunity and emission, and UL 508 for industrial control equipment safety. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How should a PAPI power supply be tested at commissioning?

Confirm the CCR series current, LED driver output current or DC supply voltage matches rated values at each configured step before connecting live PAPI units. Then run a full-load test, simulate overcurrent, short-circuit and overtemperature conditions to confirm protection trips and recovers, spot-check efficiency, verify Modbus, DALI, BACnet or 0-10 V control against the local display, check cooling, and megger-test input and output circuits against earth. Record the results in the commissioning dossier.

Q9How often should a PAPI power supply be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for CCR tap-switching tests, input voltage stability checks, series circuit loading verification and, for portable units, battery checks, and semi-annual for LED driver thermal performance checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why are my PAPI intensity steps incorrect or erratic?

The usual cause in older CCRs is failure of the mechanical tap switches or relays. Inspect them for wear, pitting or failure to actuate, and compare the actual output current against the commanded step. Replace worn contactors, or upgrade to solid-state switching for better reliability. A uniform, array-wide intensity shortfall shared equally by all units also points toward the shared power supply or CCR rather than an optical fault in every unit.

Q11What safety precautions apply when working on a PAPI power supply?

On CCR-fed systems the series loop can present several kV open-circuit, so de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy before touching any output terminal or isolating transformer. Central DC supplies can retain charge in filter capacitors, and battery sources on portable units are a high-current short-circuit hazard. PAPI guidance is safety-critical, so taking a unit or the shared supply out of service requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM, and the series loop should be megger-tested before re-energising.
