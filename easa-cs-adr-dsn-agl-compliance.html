EASA CS-ADR-DSN & AGL COMPLIANCE

A deep dive into European Aerodrome Design Requirements for Airfield Ground Lighting — how EASA CS-ADR-DSN, Certification Specifications and Guidance Material for Aerodrome Design, is translated through approved design, selected equipment, verified installation, electrical performance and monitoring into defensible EASA compliance evidence.

01 · Core Principle

CS-ADR-DSN Is a Certification Framework, Not a Parts List

EASA CS-ADR-DSN, Certification Specifications and Guidance Material for Aerodrome Design, is one of the key European references for aerodrome design. For Airfield Ground Lighting engineers, it is particularly important because the specification contains detailed provisions relating to visual aids, aerodrome lighting, runway/taxiway/approach lighting, PAPI, signs, markings, obstacle lighting, electrical systems, secondary power, lighting system design, monitoring, serviceability and installation-related requirements.

The current EASA publication is CS-ADR-DSN Issue 7, issued through ED Decision 2025/004/R.

02 · Definition

What Is CS-ADR-DSN?

CS-ADR-DSN means Certification Specifications and Guidance Material for Aerodrome Design. It is EASA's aerodrome design certification specification framework.

It addresses the design of aerodrome infrastructure and visual aids required for safe aircraft operations.

03 · Relevance

Why CS-ADR-DSN Matters to AGL

AGL is not an isolated electrical installation — it is part of the aerodrome's visual navigation system. The AGL system must provide appropriate guidance, identification, alignment, position information, runway/taxiway delineation, approach guidance, operational warnings and obstacle identification. AGL design must therefore be considered together with the aerodrome layout and operational purpose.

04 · Current Baseline

Current EASA Baseline & Regulatory Structure

As of 2026, the current published CS-ADR-DSN baseline is Issue 7, published 19 May 2025 and issued through ED Decision 2025/004/R. EASA subsequently replaced the file on 16 June 2025 to incorporate corrections, including a correction identified on page 312. EASA explains that some provisions of operational nature previously contained in CS-ADR-DSN had been transferred to the AMC and GM associated with Part-ADR.OPS, to improve consistency between aerodrome design requirements and operational requirements.

05 · Regulatory Hierarchy

CS vs Regulation vs AMC/GM

This distinction is important, and EASA describes Certification Specifications as non-binding technical standards indicating means to demonstrate compliance with the applicable regulatory framework.

06 · Scope

Not an Equipment Catalogue — Compliance Is System-Based

CS-ADR-DSN should not be treated as a list of AGL products. It establishes aerodrome design requirements; the engineer still needs to determine Requirement → Equipment → Installation → Electrical Interface → Testing.

An AGL system should not be evaluated only by asking "Does this light meet the specification?" The better question is "Does the complete visual aid system satisfy the applicable aerodrome design requirement?"

07 · Chapter M — Visual Aids for Navigation, Lights

General, Elevated & Surface Light Requirements

One of the most important parts of CS-ADR-DSN for AGL engineers is Chapter M, Visual Aids for Navigation, Lights. The March 2026 EASA Easy Access Rules identify Chapter M provisions covering visual navigation lights and associated systems. AGL fixtures must be considered in relation to their operational environment — elevated fixtures, surface fixtures, approach lights, runway lights, taxiway lights and other visual navigation lights — and the installation must preserve the required operational and physical characteristics.

Elevated Lights

EASA requirements address physical characteristics: elevated runway, stopway and taxiway lights are required to be frangible, with height sufficiently low to preserve aircraft clearance. This is a design issue, not simply a product issue.

Surface Lights

Inset fixtures in runways, stopways, taxiways and aprons must be designed and fitted to withstand aircraft wheel loading without damage to the aircraft or the light itself: Fixture → Base → Pavement → Drainage → Installation → Mechanical Loading.

Frangibility

Particularly important for elevated equipment — fixture construction, supporting structure, mounting, height, aircraft clearance, location and structural behaviour should be considered at design review, procurement and installation inspection.

08 · Approach Lighting

Approach Lighting Systems & Categories

Approach lighting is a major component of the EASA visual-aid framework. EASA states that the safety objective of an approach lighting system is to provide alignment and roll guidance and limited distance-to-go information to support a safe approach. The applicable arrangement depends on the runway type and operational category — CS-ADR-DSN provisions address non-instrument runways, non-precision approach runways, Precision Approach Category I, and Precision Approach Categories II and III. The applicable design must be determined from the runway classification and approved aerodrome design.

09 · Runway & Taxiway Lighting

Runway Lighting & the Taxiway Guidance System

Runway Lighting

A core part of the visual navigation system. Engineering considerations include runway edge, runway centreline, threshold, runway end, touchdown zone where applicable, approach lighting, runway designation and markings, and control and monitoring.

Taxiway Lighting

Taxiway visual aids support safe aircraft movement on the aerodrome surface — depending on the applicable design, this can involve centreline lights, edge lights, stop bars, runway guard lights, signs, markings and control systems.

A taxiway should not be evaluated by looking at the centreline lights alone. The operational guidance environment is integrated: Centreline + Edge + Signs + Markings + Stop Bars + Control + Monitoring.

10 · PAPI

PAPI: More Than Selecting a Light Unit

PAPI is another important visual navigation aid. PAPI design involves more than selecting a suitable light unit — engineering considerations include location, elevation, alignment, azimuth, vertical aiming, unit relationship, approach geometry and visual performance.

A PAPI can be electrically functional while still providing incorrect guidance. Therefore: Electrical ON ≠ Correct PAPI Guidance. PAPI verification must consider its optical and geometric performance.

11 · Photometric Performance

Photometry, Colour & Beam Alignment

AGL photometry is fundamental to visual-aid verification. Depending on the applicable visual aid, performance may involve light intensity, beam characteristics, colour, alignment, coverage, uniformity and operational visibility. The applicable requirement must always be identified before establishing the acceptance criteria — a runway centreline light, taxiway light, approach light and PAPI do not automatically have identical photometric requirements.

12 · Chapter S — Electrical Systems

Electrical Systems, Secondary Power & Low Visibility Design

AGL performance depends on the electrical system supporting the visual aids — primary power, secondary power, CCRs, series circuits, cables, isolating transformers, connectors, control systems, monitoring, protection, and earthing and bonding.

CS ADR-DSN.S.885 states that where runway visual range is less than 550 m, the electrical systems for power supply, lighting and control included in the relevant table should be designed so that an equipment failure does not leave the pilot with inadequate visual guidance or misleading information. This is a major AGL engineering principle.

Secondary power is particularly important for critical visual aids — the objective is not simply "Provide a generator." The system must provide the required level of continuity and operational availability. Where duplicate feeders are used for secondary power, the provisions address physical and electrical separation to ensure the required availability and independence — redundancy should not simply mean two cables; it should mean an appropriately engineered independent supply arrangement.

CS ADR-DSN.S.885 also addresses interlocking where a runway forms part of a standard taxi route and runway and taxiway lighting are provided: the lighting systems should be interlocked to prevent simultaneous operation of both forms of lighting. This connects directly with control logic, safety, human factors, ALCMS and fail-safe design.

13 · Chapter S — Monitoring

Monitoring Under CS ADR-DSN.S.890

Monitoring is explicitly addressed by CS ADR-DSN.S.890 — a system of monitoring should indicate the operational status of lighting systems. Where lighting systems are used for aircraft control purposes, the provisions call for automatic monitoring capable of indicating faults that may affect control functions, with the information automatically relayed to the air traffic service unit. This is highly relevant to stop bars, control systems, ALCMS and safety-critical visual aids.

14 · ALCMS Relationship

Field Reality vs Control-System Status

An ALCMS may provide command, status, alarm, fault indication, circuit monitoring, equipment monitoring and event logging. However, the ALCMS display must not automatically be assumed to represent the actual optical condition of every field fixture.

A control system may indicate LIGHTING ON while a field condition may actually be: fixture failed, lens damaged, incorrect colour, beam misaligned, reduced intensity, cable fault, or water ingress. Therefore: Command Status ≠ Complete Visual Performance Verification.

15 · Safety-Critical Visual Aids

Stop Bars, Runway Guard Lights, Centreline Lighting & Signs

Stop Bars

Safety-critical: location, configuration, red light, control, interlocking, monitoring, fail-safe operation, taxiway centreline relationship and ATC interface.

Runway Guard Lights

Provide conspicuous visual warning associated with runway access — effectiveness depends on correct location, configuration, visibility, control, electrical supply, maintenance and operational procedures.

Taxiway Centreline

Guidance along taxi routes requires attention to geometry, fixture spacing, curves, intersections, stop bars, runway crossings, control logic and low-visibility requirements.

Signs

Part of the visual navigation system: message, location, orientation, visibility, illumination, power, control, mounting and maintenance.

Markings and lighting work together — a visual navigation system combines different information channels: Marking defines surface information, Sign provides identification/instruction, Lighting provides visual guidance. The three should be considered together.

16 · Chapter Q — Visual Aids for Denoting Obstacles

Obstacle Marking & Obstacle Lighting

CS-ADR-DSN also contains provisions for visual aids used to denote obstacles. The current EASA material includes Chapter Q, Visual Aids for Denoting Obstacles. The requirements address objects within obstacle limitation surfaces and include provisions for objects near taxiways and other aerodrome movement areas. Exact applicability depends on the object, location, height, aerodrome environment, obstacle limitation surface and operational conditions.

The EASA material also addresses marking of elevated aeronautical ground lights and signs where required to make them conspicuous by day, and specifies that obstacle lights should not be installed on elevated ground lights or signs in the movement area — an important detail that should not be overlooked during obstacle-lighting design.
17 · Compliance Dimensions

Electrical, Visual & Mechanical Compliance Are Different

An AGL installation can be electrically compliant — the circuit operates correctly — but visually non-compliant, with incorrect intensity, colour, alignment or location, or damaged optics. Therefore Electrical Test + Visual/Photometric Verification are complementary, not interchangeable.

Mechanical compliance can involve fixture mounting, frangibility, height, pavement integration, structural integrity, aircraft clearance, fasteners, bases and drainage. A complete compliance review must therefore cover: Electrical + Optical + Mechanical + Geometric.

21 · Two Diagnostic Traps

Electrically ON, Operationally FAIL

A fixture may illuminate, draw current and respond to CCR control, but still fail because the beam is misaligned, intensity is inadequate, colour is incorrect, the fixture is damaged, or installation geometry is incorrect. Therefore: Electrical Function ≠ Complete AGL Compliance.

Similarly, an ALCMS may show normal status while the actual fixture has optical degradation — monitoring should be understood according to what it actually monitors. Command monitoring is not necessarily the same as Photometric performance monitoring.

23 · The Golden Rule

Never Write "EASA Compliant" Without Defining It

The phrase "EASA compliant AGL" is too broad by itself. Instead identify the applicable CS, paragraph, issue/revision, equipment, installation, test, acceptance criterion and evidence.

A stronger engineering statement is: "The applicable EASA aerodrome design requirements were identified, incorporated into the approved design, verified against the installed system, and supported by documented inspection and test evidence."

24 · CS-ADR-DSN in Context

EASA vs ICAO Annex 14 vs FAA vs National Requirements

Manufacturer instructions — installation, torque, cable connection, base, alignment, sealing and maintenance — remain important and should be reviewed together with the applicable aerodrome design requirement. Project specifications can introduce additional requirements: EASA → Authority → Airport → Project Specification → Approved Design → Manufacturer. The project-specific governing framework must always be established.

FrameworkRelationship to CS-ADR-DSN
ICAO Annex 14EASA aerodrome requirements should not simply be treated as a copy of Annex 14 — there are relationships and common aviation objectives, but the documents have different regulatory structures and applicability. For an EASA-regulated project, the applicable EASA regulatory framework must be followed.
FAA AC 150/5340 seriesEASA CS-ADR-DSN ≠ FAA AC 150/5340. FAA Advisory Circulars belong to the FAA regulatory and technical framework; CS-ADR-DSN belongs to the EASA aerodrome certification framework. A project should not mix requirements without identifying why each reference applies.
Indian Projects (DGCA/AAI)For an Indian airport project, EASA CS-ADR-DSN should not automatically be treated as the governing regulatory standard. The applicable framework may instead include DGCA requirements, AAI requirements, ICAO provisions, Indian Standards, project specifications and airport-specific requirements. EASA requirements may be relevant where the project or contract specifically adopts them.
25 · AGL Photometry Under EASA

Photometry Workflow & Report Structure

For a photometry engineer, the correct workflow is: Identify Visual Aid → Identify Applicable CS Requirement → Identify Performance Criteria → Select Measurement Method → Measure → Evaluate → Report. Do not use a generic photometric pass/fail criterion for every visual aid.

Project & Equipment

Airport, runway/taxiway and location; fixture type and manufacturer.

Test Equipment & Requirement

Photometer and calibration information; applicable EASA/project reference.

Measurements & Acceptance

Recorded results, requirement versus measured value, PASS/FAIL, corrective action if required, retest after rectification.

26 · Monitoring & Low-Visibility Verification

Verifying Monitoring & Low-Visibility Readiness

Monitoring should be tested separately from illumination: Field Condition → Detection → Controller → ALCMS → Alarm/Status → ATC/Operator Indication. Create a controlled fault, then verify it occurs, is detected, changes alarm/status, correctly locates, reaches the appropriate interface within the applicable response time, is recorded, and that the system returns to normal after rectification.

For systems supporting low-visibility operations, verify lighting availability, power supply, secondary power, control, monitoring, interlocking, failure response, serviceability and required visual guidance. The objective is to prevent a failure from leaving the pilot with inadequate or misleading visual guidance.

27 · AGL Safety Model

What Makes a Reliable AGL Safety Function

Visual Guidance + Electrical Availability + Control + Monitoring + Installation + Maintenance = Reliable AGL Safety Function. A reliable AGL system requires correct design, correct equipment, correct installation, reliable power, correct control, effective monitoring and proper maintenance.

32 · Change Management

Change Management & Modification Compliance

Any major aerodrome change should trigger a compliance review — runway extension or rehabilitation, taxiway modification, new taxiway, new holding position, PAPI relocation, approach lighting modification, new stop bar, ALCMS modification, secondary power modification, obstacle introduction or new sign.

A modification should follow: Existing Requirement → Proposed Change → Design Review → Approval → Installation → Testing → Operational Verification → Documentation. Do not treat a modification as simply replacing equipment.

33 · Final Principle

The Final Engineering Principle

CS-ADR-DSN should not be treated as simply another AGL equipment standard. It is an aerodrome design certification framework that establishes requirements affecting the environment in which AGL operates.

For AGL engineers, the correct approach is: Understand the Aerodrome → Identify the Visual Aid → Identify the Applicable CS → Check the Current Issue → Design → Select Equipment → Install → Test → Verify → Monitor → Maintain → Document.

The most important question is not "Is this light EASA compliant?" The better question is: "Which EASA aerodrome design requirement applies to this visual aid, how has it been implemented in the approved design, and what objective evidence demonstrates that the installed system satisfies the applicable requirement?"

Q1What is EASA CS-ADR-DSN?

CS-ADR-DSN means Certification Specifications and Guidance Material for Aerodrome Design. It is EASA's aerodrome design certification specification framework, addressing the design of aerodrome infrastructure and visual aids required for safe aircraft operations.

Q2What is the current CS-ADR-DSN baseline according to this page?

It notes that the March 2026 Easy Access Rules for Aerodromes incorporate Issue 7, and that engineers should not rely on an old PDF without checking the current EASA publication.

Q3Why does CS-ADR-DSN matter for airfield ground lighting?

AGL is part of the aerodrome's visual navigation system, and CS-ADR-DSN contains detailed provisions on visual aids, aerodrome lighting, runway, taxiway and approach lighting, PAPI, signs, markings, obstacle lighting, electrical systems, secondary power, monitoring and serviceability. AGL design must be considered together with the aerodrome layout and operational purpose.

Q4Is CS-ADR-DSN a list of approved AGL products?

No. It is a certification framework, not a parts list or equipment catalogue. The better question is not whether a light meets the specification but whether the complete visual aid system satisfies the applicable aerodrome design requirement, following the chain of requirement, equipment, installation, electrical interface and testing.

Q5What is the difference between a Certification Specification, a regulation, AMC and GM?

Regulation provides the legally applicable framework. A Certification Specification provides technical specifications used as a means of demonstrating compliance, and EASA describes CSs as non-binding technical standards. AMC provides acceptable means of compliance, and GM provides guidance and explanatory material.

Q6What is Chapter M of CS-ADR-DSN?

Chapter M, Visual Aids for Navigation, Lights, is described on the page as one of the most important parts of CS-ADR-DSN for AGL engineers. It covers visual navigation lights and associated systems, including elevated fixtures, surface fixtures, approach lights, runway lights and taxiway lights.

Q7What do the requirements say about elevated and inset lights?

Elevated runway, stopway and taxiway lights are required to be frangible, with height sufficiently low to preserve aircraft clearance. Inset fixtures in runways, stopways, taxiways and aprons must be designed and fitted to withstand aircraft wheel loading without damage to the aircraft or the light itself.

Q8How does CS-ADR-DSN treat approach lighting?

EASA states that the safety objective of an approach lighting system is to provide alignment and roll guidance and limited distance-to-go information. The provisions address non-instrument runways, non-precision approach runways, Precision Approach Category I, and Precision Approach Categories II and III, so the applicable design depends on the runway classification and approved aerodrome design.

Q9Does a working PAPI mean the PAPI is compliant?

No. PAPI design involves location, elevation, alignment, azimuth, vertical aiming, unit relationship, approach geometry and visual performance. A PAPI can be electrically functional while still providing incorrect guidance, so electrical ON does not equal correct PAPI guidance and verification must consider optical and geometric performance.

Q10Why were some operational provisions moved out of CS-ADR-DSN?

EASA explains that some provisions of operational nature previously contained in CS-ADR-DSN were transferred to the AMC and GM associated with Part-ADR.OPS, to improve consistency between aerodrome design requirements and operational requirements.
