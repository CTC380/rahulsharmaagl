L‑823 Connector

The industry‑standard two‑pin connector for AGL primary series circuits, providing a reliable, waterproof, and field‑repairable connection between the primary cable and isolating transformers. Designed for submersible use, it ensures secure, low‑resistance electrical joints that withstand the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

The L‑823 Connector is a standardized, two‑pin, waterproof electrical connector used extensively in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems for making primary circuit connections. It is the primary means of connecting the series lighting cable (primary circuit) to isolating transformers, junction boxes, and other inline equipment. The connector is designed to be robust, field‑repairable, and fully submersible (IP68), ensuring reliable performance in the demanding outdoor environment of an airfield.

Originally developed under U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) specification L‑823, this connector has become the de‑facto standard for 6.6A and 20A AGL series circuits worldwide. Its simple yet rugged design features two pins (one for the incoming and one for the outgoing primary cable), a threaded coupling ring for secure mating, and elastomeric seals that prevent moisture ingress even when submerged.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The L-823 connector's broader purpose, beyond any single plug or receptacle, is standardisation: by defining one interface that isolating transformers, junction boxes, and cable terminations from different manufacturers can all use interchangeably, it lets an airport source primary circuit connectors from multiple suppliers without compatibility concerns, and lets maintenance crews repair or replace a connector without matching it to a specific vendor's proprietary design. That standard also has to hold up specifically at the point where AGL circuits are most exposed to water ingress, since primary connectors are routinely buried or submerged in wet conditions between the CCR and each fixture. Its de-facto global adoption for 6.6A and 20A series circuits is what has made the connector, rather than any single manufacturer's version of it, the reference point the rest of this component family is built around.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The L‑823 connector is used throughout the airfield wherever primary circuit connections are required, including:

  • Isolating Transformer Connections: The primary side of every isolating transformer is terminated with an L‑823 connector (or a mating receptacle), allowing quick connection to the primary series cable.
  • Junction Boxes and Splices: Inline splices, tap‑off points, and junction boxes within the primary loop use L‑823 connectors for reliable, waterproof connections.
  • CCR to Field Cable Interface: The output of the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) is typically connected to the field cable using L‑823 connectors at the transition point.
  • Disconnect Points: For maintenance and testing, L‑823 connectors provide a convenient means to isolate sections of the primary circuit.
  • Retrofit and Temporary Installations: L‑823 connectors facilitate rapid deployment and reconfiguration of AGL systems in temporary or mobile airfield setups.

The connector is available in both plug (male) and receptacle (female) versions, with straight and right‑angle configurations to suit various installation needs. It is rated for continuous operation at the full primary current (6.6A or 20A) and can withstand the high transient voltages that occur in inductive series circuits.

Placement

Installation & Location

The connector is installed at every point where a primary series cable meets a piece of inline equipment — most commonly the primary side of an isolating transformer, but also at junction boxes, splice points, and the CCR-to-field cable interface. Plug and receptacle halves are field-terminated to their respective cables using crimped or soldered pins matched to the conductor size, then mated and threaded together so the coupling ring fully compresses the internal O-rings, since a partially threaded connector is a common cause of later water ingress. Once mated, the connector is typically housed within a below-grade pit, handhole, or the base of the equipment it serves, with enough cable slack looped in on both sides to allow disconnection and reconnection during maintenance without disturbing the buried cable runs. Disconnect points intended for periodic testing or isolation are sited so the connector remains reachable at finished grade, rather than buried under permanent backfill.

Function

How Does It Work?

The L‑823 connector provides a secure, low‑resistance electrical joint while maintaining a waterproof seal. Its design incorporates several key features:

  • Two‑Pin Contact System: The connector has two round, gold‑plated or tinned brass pins (or sockets) that mate to carry the primary current. The pins are designed to provide a gas‑tight connection with minimal contact resistance.
  • Threaded Coupling Ring: A large, knurled coupling ring on the plug side threads onto the receptacle, compressing the internal seals and locking the connection against vibration and accidental disconnection.
  • Elastomeric Seals: Multiple O‑rings and gaskets (silicone or EPDM) provide IP68 sealing when the connector is fully mated. The seals are compressed by the coupling ring to form a watertight barrier.
  • Cable Termination: The connector is field‑terminated using a compression‑type gland or a solder/crimp‑type termination inside the connector shell. The cable entry is sealed by a compression gland that prevents moisture ingress along the cable jacket.
  • Environmental Durability: The connector shell is typically made of corrosion‑resistant materials such as brass, bronze, or high‑grade plastic (polyamide) with UV stabilizers. The contacts are corrosion‑resistant, and the overall assembly is designed to withstand temperature extremes, UV exposure, and chemical attack from de‑icing fluids.

When properly installed and torqued, the L‑823 connector provides a connection that can remain submerged in water for extended periods without degradation. Its low resistance ensures minimal voltage drop and heating, critical for maintaining the performance of the constant‑current series circuit.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The L-823 connector sits inline in the series circuit loop, typically at the interface between the primary cable and the isolating transformer's secondary tail, and it carries the full loop current across its two-pin contact system. It is not individually addressable — the ALCMS and CCR regulate the loop as a whole and have no visibility into the connector as a discrete point. When properly mated and torqued, its contact resistance is low enough to be electrically invisible to the control system. As the connector ages, however, contact wear, seal degradation, or partial water ingress at the pin interface raises its resistance, and because the CCR holds current constant rather than voltage, that added resistance surfaces as a rise in overall loop voltage at the commanded current step. If the connector fully fails to mate — a cracked shell, a badly seated pin, a torn O-ring that lets water bridge the contacts — the CCR reports it as an open-circuit or ground-fault loop condition indistinguishable, at the control-system level, from any other break in the loop, and locating it still requires physically checking each connector on the affected section.

Construction

Physical Construction

The connector is built as two mating half‑shells, each a moulded or machined body housing one pin (plug side) or one socket (receptacle side) of gold‑ or tin‑plated brass, chosen for its low, stable contact resistance over repeated mating cycles. A threaded coupling ring on the plug half draws the two shells together and compresses a set of internal elastomeric O‑rings at the mating face, which is what allows the joint to be rated IP68 and to remain sealed even fully submerged. Each half is terminated to its cable via a compression or crimp connection inside the shell, with the cable entry itself sealed by a further O-ring or gland so that no moisture path exists between the cable jacket and the connector body. The shell material — typically a glass‑filled thermoplastic or cast metal — is selected for resistance to fuel, de‑icing fluid, and UV exposure, and the whole assembly is designed to be field‑serviceable, so a damaged connector can be cut off and a new one terminated onto the same cable without replacing the run.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Contacts

Despite plating, contacts can corrode in saltwater or chemically aggressive environments. This increases contact resistance, causing local heating and voltage drop. Regular inspection and cleaning with contact cleaner, along with application of dielectric grease, are recommended.

O‑Ring Degradation

O‑rings can harden, crack, or lose elasticity over time due to UV exposure, temperature cycles, or chemical attack. Replace O‑rings periodically and use silicone grease to prolong their life.

Incomplete Mating & Loose Coupling

If the coupling ring is not fully tightened, the connection may be loose, leading to intermittent contact, arcing, and overheating. Always tighten to the recommended torque and verify that the connection is secure.

Damaged Cable Termination

Poorly stripped or crimped cable terminations can cause high resistance and overheating. Use the proper stripping and crimping tools, and ensure the cable is fully inserted into the termination point.

Over‑Tightening

Excessive torque can damage the coupling ring or strip the threads, making future disconnection difficult. Use a torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value.

Incorrect Cable Gland Selection

Using a gland that does not match the cable diameter will fail to seal properly, allowing water ingress. Always select the correct gland size for the cable being used.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Elevated Contact Resistance / Localized Heating at the Connector
Likely Cause
Corrosion has formed on the plated contacts despite their protective coating, typically from prolonged exposure to saltwater or aggressive chemical environments.
Diagnostic Steps
Disconnect and inspect the contacts for discolouration or pitting; measure contact resistance across the mated pair and compare against acceptable limits.
Corrective Action
Clean the contacts with an approved contact cleaner and apply dielectric grease before re-mating; replace the connector if corrosion has pitted the contact surface.
Water Ingress at a Previously Sealed Connector
Likely Cause
The O-ring has hardened, cracked, or lost elasticity due to UV exposure, temperature cycling, or chemical attack, breaking the environmental seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Disassemble the connector and visually inspect the O-ring for hardness, cracking, or flattening; check for moisture inside the connector body.
Corrective Action
Replace the O-ring with the correct size and material, lubricate with silicone grease, and re-mate and re-seal the connector before backfilling.
Intermittent Contact, Arcing, or Overheating at the Coupling
Likely Cause
The coupling ring was not fully tightened to the specified torque, leaving the mated connector loose under vibration or thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Check coupling ring tightness against the manufacturer's torque specification; look for arc marks or heat discolouration on the contacts.
Corrective Action
Disassemble, inspect contacts for damage, and re-mate the connector, tightening the coupling ring to the correct torque with a torque wrench.
High Resistance or Overheating Traced to the Cable Termination
Likely Cause
The cable was poorly stripped or crimped, or not fully inserted into the termination point, leaving a marginal electrical connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crimp for proper conductor insertion depth and crimp shape; measure resistance across the termination.
Corrective Action
Re-strip and re-crimp the cable termination using the correct tools and manufacturer-specified crimp dimensions; verify full conductor insertion before re-testing.
Coupling Ring Difficult to Disconnect or Threads Feel Damaged
Likely Cause
Excessive torque was applied during a previous mating, damaging the coupling ring or stripping the threads.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coupling ring threads for galling, stripping, or deformation; attempt to mate a known-good connector to confirm the fault is in the ring, not the mating half.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged connector body/coupling ring, and re-terminate using a torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value going forward.
Water Ingress Traced to the Cable Gland Rather Than the O-Ring
Likely Cause
An incorrectly sized cable gland was fitted for the cable diameter in use, preventing a proper seal around the cable jacket.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the gland size against the cable outer diameter specification; inspect for gaps or an incomplete seal around the cable at the gland entry.
Corrective Action
Replace with the correctly sized cable gland for the cable in use and re-seal the entry point before returning the connector to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Mating or Unmating
Electrical Safety

Never connect or disconnect an L-823 connector on a live series circuit; de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy at the pins before separating a mated pair.

Correct Coupling Ring Torque
Workmanship

Tighten the coupling ring to the manufacturer's specified torque; under-torquing leaves the mated pair loose under vibration, while over-torquing can gall or strip the threads.

O-Ring & Gland Inspection
Workmanship

Inspect the O-ring for hardness or cracking and confirm the cable gland matches the actual cable diameter before mating; a compromised seal here is a leading cause of water ingress in the base can.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Check contact condition, coupling torque, and seal integrity before restoring power; a marginal connection found after re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Confined Space
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working inside a base can, and follow confined-space precautions where the can is deep or poorly ventilated.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the connector is exposed, and secure or remove loose material before closing the base can.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a connector unmated, under-torqued, or with a suspect seal; verify the connection and reseal the base can before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Not All L-823 Connectors Are Interchangeable Across Manufacturers
Design

Despite a common standard, coupling ring thread pitch and pin or socket tolerances can vary slightly between manufacturers; mixing brands within one mated pair can seem to mate correctly while leaving marginal contact pressure.

A 'Working' Connector Can Still Be Marginal Under Load
Troubleshooting

A connector can pass a continuity check at zero or low load yet develop resistance heating once carrying full series-circuit current; where nuisance CCR trips recur at one location, check contact resistance under actual load current, not just continuity.

O-Ring Compression Set Happens Even Without Visible Damage
Durability

An O-ring can lose its sealing force through compression set after repeated mate/unmate cycles or years in place, without showing cracking or visible wear; replace the O-ring on schedule at overhaul rather than only when it looks obviously degraded.

Coupling Ring Torque Affects Contact Pressure, Not Just Mechanical Security
Electrical

Under-torquing the coupling ring doesn't just risk the pair working loose under vibration, it can leave contact pressure at the pins below spec even while the connector appears fully mated.

Confirm Gland Torque Independently of Coupling Ring Torque
Workmanship

Technicians sometimes assume that a correctly torqued coupling ring also means the cable gland is sealed; the gland must be checked and torqued to its own specification, since the two seals are independent.

Log Which Base Cans Have Had Recent Connector Work
Compliance

Because L-823 connector faults commonly reappear at the same location shortly after a repair, keeping a simple log of recent connector work by base can location helps a follow-up crew recognize a recurring problem rather than treating each callout as unrelated.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about L-823 connectors: ratings, sealing, standards, installation, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is an L-823 connector?

The L-823 connector is a standardised two-pin waterproof connector used for primary circuit connections in airfield ground lighting. It joins the series lighting cable to isolating transformers, junction boxes and other inline equipment, and is designed to be robust, field-repairable and fully submersible (IP68).

Q2Why is the L-823 interface standardised?

One common interface lets isolating transformers, junction boxes and cable terminations from different manufacturers connect interchangeably. An airport can therefore source connectors from multiple suppliers, and crews can repair or replace a connector in the field.

Q3Where are L-823 connectors used on the airfield?

Wherever a primary series cable meets inline equipment: the primary side of every isolating transformer, junction boxes and inline splices, and the CCR-to-field cable interface. Plug and receptacle halves are field-terminated to their cables and then mated and threaded together.

Q4How does an L-823 connector stay watertight?

Two gold- or tin-plated brass pins mate with sockets, and a threaded coupling ring draws the two shells together and compresses internal O-rings at the mating face. A compression gland with seals closes the cable entry, so the mated connector is rated IP68.

Q5What are the typical ratings of an L-823 connector?

Typical values are 6.6 A or 20 A continuous depending on variant, up to 5 kV transient and 600 V continuous, cable compatibility of 6-16 mm² (AWG 10-6), at least 500 mating cycles with gold-plated contacts, a coupling ring torque of 3.5-5.5 N·m, and an operating temperature of -40°C to +85°C.

Q6What insulation resistance should a mated L-823 connector achieve?

A typical requirement is at least 50 megohms, measured with a megger from the mated connector to ground. Testing before backfill and again annually catches moisture ingress before it becomes a ground fault.

Q7Which standards apply to L-823 connectors?

FAA AC 150/5345-26 is the governing specification for L-823 plug and receptacle cable connectors, and L-823 is the equipment type designation rather than an AC number. Related references are FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program) and ICAO Annex 14.

Q8Can an L-823 connector be connected or disconnected on a live circuit?

No. De-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy at the pins before separating a mated pair. Never mate or unmate the connector on a live series circuit.

Q9How often should L-823 connectors be inspected and maintained?

Annual tasks include cleaning contacts and reapplying dielectric grease, inspecting and replacing O-rings, checking coupling ring torque, checking cable terminations and the gland seal, and megger testing insulation resistance. Harsh saltwater or chemical environments make the contact-cleaning task especially important.

Q10Why does an L-823 connector overheat or cause a CCR ground fault?

Corroded contacts raise contact resistance and cause local heating and voltage drop, while moisture ingress shows up as a CCR ground fault or open circuit. A partially threaded coupling ring is a common cause of later water ingress, so confirm full engagement and correct torque.

Q11Are L-823 connectors from different manufacturers interchangeable?

Despite the common standard, coupling ring thread pitch and pin or socket tolerances can vary slightly between manufacturers. Mixing brands within one mated pair can appear to mate correctly while leaving marginal contact pressure, so a working connection should still be verified by measurement.
