Fault Detection & Status Reporting Comparison

PAPI Monitoring VS Conventional PAPI

A monitored PAPI and a conventional PAPI give the pilot exactly the same visual approach-slope indication. The difference sits entirely behind the scenes — in whether the system can automatically detect faults, report status remotely and feed that information into the airport's AGL control and monitoring system, or whether it relies on periodic manual inspection to find problems.

02 · The Core Point

Same Light, Different Awareness

The main difference between the two is not the optical guidance principle. Both configurations provide the same PAPI visual guidance to the pilot. The difference is the monitoring, fault detection, alarm and status-reporting capability layered on top of the light units.

Conventional PAPI

Consists primarily of the light units, power supply/control equipment and associated cabling. Its main function is simply to provide the correct visual approach-slope indication.

PAPI with Monitoring

Adds a monitoring/control layer that can detect defined equipment faults and provide status/alarm information to maintenance or the airport's lighting control/monitoring system, while giving exactly the same visual indication to the pilot.

03 · What The Pilot Sees

Identical Indication, Either Way

Whichever configuration is installed, the pilot reads the same colour pattern across the four units:

3 White + 1 RedSlightly high

Monitoring is invisible to the pilot. It changes what maintenance and the airport's control room know about the system's condition — it does not change what appears in the cockpit view.

04 · What Can Be Monitored

Typical Monitored Parameters

The exact functions depend on the PAPI manufacturer and system architecture. A project specification should not assume that every monitored PAPI provides all of the following functions.

Light-source condition

Whether the LED module or lamp is on, off, or has failed.

Electrical condition

Whether the power supply is operating normally or shows a fault.

Intensity

Whether light output remains within limit or has drifted into fault.

Control status

Whether the PAPI unit itself reports normal operation or a fault state.

Communication

Whether the link from the PAPI to the monitoring unit and on to the control system is intact.

05 · Maintenance Workflow

Scheduled Inspection vs Fault-Driven Response

With a conventional system, maintenance personnel depend more heavily on visual inspection, functional testing, photometric checks, alignment checks, lamp/LED inspection, electrical testing and scheduled maintenance. A fault may remain undetected until the next inspection unless it is obvious locally.

06 · Local vs Remote

Where the Status Is Seen

Conventional PAPI

Status is effectively known only locally — a technician has to check the equipment directly to know its condition.

PAPI with Monitoring

Status can be pushed to the airport's AGL control and monitoring system, making it visible from a control room or central maintenance point without a site visit — particularly useful at larger airports.

07 · Monitoring ≠ Automatic Alignment

An Important Distinction

A PAPI monitoring system may monitor defined electrical, optical or equipment parameters, but that does not necessarily mean it automatically verifies or corrects the physical aiming angle of the units.

Electrical and light-source status can be monitored, but physical alignment still requires appropriate inspection. Alignment remains a critical maintenance requirement regardless of monitoring capability.

08 · Monitoring vs Control

Two Different Concepts

Control

Means commanding or adjusting equipment operation — for example ON/OFF, intensity selection, or mode selection. In short: "Turn PAPI on."

Monitoring

Means determining and reporting the equipment's actual status — for example ON, OFF, fault, lamp failure, or communication failure. In short: "PAPI is on."

A modern PAPI installation can incorporate both control and monitoring together.

09 · LED and Lamp PAPI

Monitoring Applies to Either Light Source

Monitoring becomes particularly useful with LED PAPI, where possible status information can include LED module status, driver fault, power fault, communication fault and intensity/control status.

Traditional lamp PAPI can also be monitored, though the monitoring architecture differs depending on the lamp arrangement and equipment design. For example, a system may detect a lamp failure without necessarily measuring every aspect of the optical performance.

The exact monitored parameters are manufacturer-specific for both LED and lamp PAPI — this comparison describes typical, not universal, capability.

10 · Typical Architecture

Where Monitoring Sits in the System

Conventional

CCR/power → PAPI control → light units. Status is available only at the equipment itself.

Monitored

CCR/power → PAPI control unit → light units, each feeding a monitoring layer → AGL system → control room.

12 · Main Advantages of Monitoring

Why Airports Add a Monitoring Layer

  • Faster fault detection — faults can be identified without waiting for the next routine inspection.
  • Remote status — maintenance staff can see PAPI status from a central location.
  • Better maintenance planning — the team can respond based on reported faults rather than guesswork.
  • Integration — PAPI can be incorporated into the airport's broader AGL Control and Monitoring System (CMS).
  • Improved operational awareness — the status of the approach-lighting aid can be available to the airport operations/maintenance system.
13 · Limitations

What Monitoring Does Not Remove

Monitoring does not eliminate the need for:

  • PAPI alignment checks
  • Photometric verification
  • Lens cleaning
  • Physical inspection
  • Cable testing
  • Structural inspection
  • Periodic preventive maintenance

Monitored PAPI ≠ maintenance-free PAPI. Monitoring adds fault detection and status reporting on top of the maintenance program — it does not replace it.

14 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: Monitoring changes the optical guidance
Fact

No. Both conventional and monitored PAPI provide the same red/white visual approach-slope indication to the pilot.

Myth: A monitored PAPI is maintenance-free
Fact

No. Monitoring adds fault detection and status reporting, but alignment, photometric checks and routine maintenance are still required.

Myth: Monitoring automatically corrects PAPI aiming angle
Fact

No. Monitoring reports on electrical, optical and equipment status — it does not verify or correct the physical alignment of the units.

Myth: Monitoring and control are the same thing
Fact

No. Control means commanding equipment operation (e.g. ON/OFF); monitoring means reporting the equipment's actual status. A system can do either, both, or neither.

Myth: Only LED PAPI can be monitored
Fact

No. Traditional lamp PAPI can also be monitored, though the monitoring architecture differs from LED installations.

15 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI Monitoring vs Conventional PAPI

Engineering Verdict

Conventional and monitored PAPI are not different visual systems — they are the same visual system with a different level of built-in awareness. A conventional PAPI provides visual approach-slope guidance with basic equipment control and relies on local, periodic maintenance inspection to catch problems.

A monitored PAPI provides that same visual guidance while continuously watching defined equipment parameters, raising alarms on fault, and — where designed for it — reporting status into the airport's AGL Control and Monitoring System. This shortens the gap between a fault occurring and it being addressed, and gives the airport a clearer real-time picture of its visual aids, but it never replaces alignment checks, photometric verification or routine physical maintenance.

ConventionalPilot guidance + basic control, status confirmed only by physical/local inspection.
MonitoredPilot guidance + continuous status + automatic fault alarm, optionally integrated with the AGL CMS.

Monitoring improves fault detection and operational awareness, but it does not replace PAPI alignment, photometric checks or routine maintenance.

16 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a monitored PAPI give a different visual indication than a conventional PAPI?

No. Both provide the same PAPI visual guidance — the difference is entirely in the monitoring, fault detection, alarm and status-reporting layer, not the optical principle.

Does monitoring automatically fix a misaligned PAPI?

No. A monitoring system may track electrical, optical or equipment parameters, but it does not necessarily verify or correct the physical aiming angle. Alignment still requires appropriate inspection.

Is PAPI monitoring the same as PAPI control?

No. Control means commanding or adjusting equipment operation, such as turning the PAPI on or selecting an intensity. Monitoring means determining and reporting the equipment's actual status, such as on, off, or fault.

Can lamp-type PAPI be monitored, or is monitoring only for LED PAPI?

Both can be monitored. Monitoring is particularly useful with LED PAPI, but traditional lamp PAPI can also be monitored, with an architecture that differs depending on the lamp arrangement and equipment design.

Does a monitored PAPI remove the need for routine maintenance?

No. Monitoring does not eliminate PAPI alignment checks, photometric verification, lens cleaning, physical inspection, cable testing, structural inspection or periodic preventive maintenance. Monitored PAPI is not maintenance-free PAPI.

What parameters can a PAPI monitoring system typically track?

Typical parameters may include light-source condition, electrical condition, intensity, control status and communication status, though the exact monitored functions depend on the manufacturer and system architecture.

17 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents and the specific manufacturer's monitoring architecture.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — visual approach slope indicator systems including PAPI, siting and photometric requirements.

FAA AC 150/5345-28, Current Edition

Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) Systems (equipment designations L-880/L-881) — FAA equipment standards for PAPI units, including power/control unit and constant current regulator options, applicable to both conventional and monitored installations.

Airport AGL CMS Documentation

Project- and manufacturer-specific documentation defining the actual communication protocol and interface between PAPI monitoring units and the airport's control and monitoring system.

Final Engineering Note

PAPI monitoring scope and architecture must always be developed from the current governing standard, the approved airport design, and the manufacturer's actual system capability — not from a generic web comparison.

A monitored PAPI gives the airport a faster, clearer picture of its own equipment condition. It does not change what the pilot sees, and it does not remove the need for a disciplined physical maintenance program.
