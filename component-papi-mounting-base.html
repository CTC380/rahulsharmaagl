PAPI Mounting Base

The structural foundation that secures the PAPI light unit to its frangible pole or ground-level base. This component provides precise alignment, vibration damping, and a breakaway interface to ensure safe impact performance while maintaining optical accuracy over years of service in harsh outdoor conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Mounting Base is the mechanical interface between the PAPI light unit and its supporting structure. It is typically a robust, precision‑machined component made of aluminium alloy or stainless steel, designed to:

  • Secure the PAPI unit – firmly attaching it to the frangible pole or a ground‑level base plate.
  • Provide precise alignment – allowing adjustment of the unit’s azimuth (horizontal orientation) and elevation (vertical angle) to achieve the required glide slope transition.
  • Absorb vibration – damping mechanical vibrations from wind, aircraft, or ground traffic to protect the optical assembly.
  • Enable frangible breakaway – incorporating a breakable coupling that shears on impact, minimising aircraft damage.
  • Facilitate maintenance – with accessible fasteners and adjustment mechanisms for easy servicing.

The mounting base is a critical link between the fixture and the airfield infrastructure. Its design directly affects the long‑term stability of the PAPI’s optical alignment, which is essential for accurate glide slope indication.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The mounting base’s purpose is to keep an angle that was calibrated to fractions of a degree from drifting back out of tolerance under everyday physical stress. Wind loading, ground vibration from aircraft and vehicles, and thermal expansion and contraction of the pole and fittings all act continuously on the fixture, and any looseness or flex in the base translates directly into a shift in elevation or azimuth — the very angles the calibration tool was used to set precisely. By providing a rigid, vibration-damped mechanical link, the base is what makes a one-time calibration remain valid for months or years rather than needing constant re-adjustment. Its frangible breakaway feature exists in tension with that same rigidity: strong and stable enough to hold a precise angle under normal conditions, yet designed to shear cleanly on aircraft impact so the fixture doesn’t become a rigid hazard, which is why the base has to satisfy both requirements simultaneously rather than trading one off for the other.

Architecture

Key Components & Construction

A typical PAPI mounting base consists of several integrated elements:

Base Plate

Heavy‑duty plate that attaches to the frangible pole or ground foundation. Provides a stable mounting surface with bolt holes for anchoring.

Azimuth Adjustment

Slotted holes or pivot mechanism allowing horizontal rotation of the PAPI unit to align with the runway centreline (typically ±5° adjustability).

Elevation Adjustment

Precision threaded adjusters or shim system that sets the vertical angle of the unit to achieve the precise glide slope (typically 2.5°–4°).

Frangible Coupling

Breakable joint (e.g., shear pins or scored section) that snaps on impact, allowing the fixture to separate cleanly without damaging the pole or aircraft.

Locking Mechanism

Fasteners (bolts, nuts, locking plates) that secure the adjustment settings once aligned, preventing drift due to vibration.

Corrosion Protection

Surface treatments (anodising, painting, or galvanising) and stainless steel hardware to resist corrosion in aggressive environments.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The PAPI mounting base is a universal component used in every PAPI installation, with variations for different site conditions:

  • Permanent PAPI systems – on precision runways (CAT I, II, III) with frangible poles set in concrete foundations.
  • Helicopter PAPI – similar mounting bases, often with adjustable elevation ranges for steeper angles.
  • Portable PAPI – lightweight, quick‑release bases that clamp to tripods or mobile stands.
  • Elevated vs. ground‑level – bases designed for pole‑mounting (elevated) or direct ground mounting (low‑profile).
  • Retrofit projects – when upgrading old PAPI units, a new mounting base may be required to match the existing pole or foundation.
  • Military and offshore environments – with enhanced corrosion resistance and ruggedised locking mechanisms.
Placement

Installation & Location

The mounting base is set at each wing bar position along the PAPI's lateral offset from the runway edge, on a foundation matched to the installation type — a concrete footing with frangible coupling for permanent runway PAPI, a simpler ground-level or low-profile base for helicopter PAPI, or a quick-release clamp onto a tripod or skid for portable systems. Its position and level are set out from the runway centreline and threshold survey so the four (or two) units in the bar sit at identical height and lateral spacing, since any variation between bases would throw off the sharp glide-angle transition the array is designed to produce. Once fixed, the base is what the fixture head is aimed against during commissioning, so its foundation must hold that alignment against wind, vibration, and (for frangible types) everything short of a designed breakaway impact.

Function

How Does It Work?

The PAPI mounting base performs several critical mechanical functions that ensure the PAPI unit stays aligned and safe:

  • Secure Attachment: The base plate is bolted to the top of the frangible pole (or directly to a concrete pad). The PAPI unit is then attached to the base using heavy‑duty bolts or clamps. This creates a rigid connection that resists wind loads and vibration.
  • Precise Alignment: The base incorporates adjustment mechanisms. Azimuth adjustment allows the unit to be rotated left or right to point precisely along the runway centreline. Elevation adjustment uses threaded adjusters or shims to set the vertical angle. These adjustments are made using a theodolite or specialised alignment tool during installation.
  • Frangible Breakaway: The base includes a frangible coupling—often a scored neck, shear pins, or a breakable flange. In the event of an aircraft strike, this coupling shears at a predetermined force, allowing the PAPI unit to separate from the pole. This minimises damage to the aircraft and the pole structure.
  • Vibration Damping: Some bases incorporate rubber or elastomeric mounts to absorb high‑frequency vibrations from wind or ground traffic, protecting the sensitive optical alignment from gradual drift.
  • Locking & Security: Once aligned, the adjustment mechanisms are locked using nuts, locking plates, or thread‑locking compound to prevent movement from vibration or thermal cycling. Security seals or tamper‑proof fasteners may also be used.
  • Corrosion Resistance: The base is designed to withstand outdoor exposure. Aluminium bases are anodised, steel bases are galvanised or painted, and all exposed fasteners are stainless steel or coated.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The mounting base has no electrical control logic — it's a purely mechanical link in the system, and its function is set once, at installation or during periodic re-calibration, rather than commanded in real time. The azimuth and elevation angles it locks in place run entirely independently of the CCR's brightness-step control and the ALCMS's fault monitoring, both of which continue to operate on the unit exactly the same way regardless of whether the base is perfectly aligned or has drifted.

That independence is also the base's main risk: because alignment isn't an electrical parameter, a base that has shifted from vibration or a near-miss impact doesn't register as a fault anywhere in the control system — the unit will still take its brightness command and light up correctly, just pointed at the wrong angle, which is why periodic physical re-verification, rather than electrical monitoring, is what catches base misalignment.

Base Alignment (mechanical, fixed) + CCR Brightness Control (electrical, ongoing) → Beam at Set Angle

Construction

Physical Construction

The base plate is precision-machined from aluminium alloy or stainless steel and bolts to the top of the frangible pole or a ground-level foundation plate, with the azimuth pivot and elevation adjuster built into the plate stack itself rather than as separate add-on fittings. The azimuth mechanism sits at the bottom of the stack, allowing the whole upper assembly to rotate on its pivot before the setting is locked; the elevation adjuster sits above it, tilting the PAPI unit's own mounting face through its threaded or shimmed range independently of the azimuth setting beneath it. The frangible coupling is built into the base itself as a scored section or shear-pin joint positioned below both adjustment mechanisms, so an impact separates the fixture and base together from the pole rather than disturbing the calibrated azimuth and elevation settings above the break point. All fasteners are stainless steel and the base's exposed surfaces are anodised, painted, or galvanised, since any corrosion-driven play in the stack would show up directly as drift in the PAPI unit's aim.

Civil

Set a concrete footing for permanent runway PAPI, a simpler ground-level or low-profile base for helicopter PAPI, or fix a quick-release clamp onto a tripod or skid for portable systems, matching the foundation to the installation type.

Lateral Offset & Bar Position Set-out
Civil

Set out each wing bar position along the PAPI's lateral offset from the runway edge, from the runway centreline and threshold survey, so all units in the bar sit at identical height and lateral spacing.

Level & Height Consistency
Mechanical

Confirm level and height match precisely across every base in the bar, since any variation between bases throws off the sharp glide-angle transition the array is designed to produce.

Frangible Coupling Fixing
Mechanical

For permanent installations, torque the frangible coupling built into the base to spec, confirming an impact would separate the fixture and base together from the pole without disturbing the azimuth and elevation settings above the break point.

Azimuth & Elevation Mechanism Check
Mechanical

Confirm the azimuth pivot and elevation adjuster move freely through their full range and lock securely, since the base is what the fixture head is aimed against during commissioning.

Structural Load Rating
Mechanical

Confirm the foundation holds alignment against wind and vibration loads, since drift in the base directly shows up as drift in the PAPI unit's aim.

Corrosion Protection Check
Mechanical

Pre-Aiming Sign-off
Documentation

Record base position, level, foundation type, and mechanism condition as-built before the fixture head is aimed and commissioning proceeds.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Breakaway Force Verification

Confirm the frangible coupling's certified breakaway force (2,500–5,000 N) against manufacturer documentation or witness test records before the base is finally installed.

Torque all mounting fasteners to spec and confirm stainless locking nuts are correctly seated, since under-torqued fasteners undermine both stability and frangibility.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Fasteners

Exposed bolts and adjusters can corrode in marine or industrial environments, making it difficult to adjust or remove the unit. Use stainless steel hardware and apply anti‑seize compound during installation. Regular inspection and replacement of corroded fasteners is recommended.

Loose Adjustment Locking

If the locking nuts or plates are not tightened sufficiently, the PAPI unit can drift out of alignment due to vibration or wind. Always use a torque wrench for final tightening and consider using locking wire or thread‑locker on critical bolts.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Repeated wind loading and thermal cycling can weaken the frangible coupling over time, potentially causing it to fail prematurely. Inspect the coupling for cracks or deformation during routine maintenance. Replace if any signs of fatigue are present.

Alignment Drift

Even with locking mechanisms, heavy vibration from aircraft or ground vehicles can cause gradual angular drift. Regular photometric checks and alignment verification using a theodolite are essential to maintain glide slope accuracy.

Base Plate Deformation

If the pole or foundation is not perfectly level, the base plate may be stressed and deform over time, affecting alignment. Ensure proper foundation preparation and use shims to level the base during installation.

Incorrect Mounting Hardware

Using bolts that are too long or too short can compromise the integrity of the mounting. Always use the specified hardware and consult the manufacturer’s installation manual. Over‑torquing can damage threads or deform components.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Adjuster/Bolt Cannot Be Turned
Likely Cause
Corrosion of exposed fasteners in marine or industrial environments.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect bolts and adjusters for rust or seizing, and check whether anti-seize compound was applied at installation.
Corrective Action
Apply penetrating lubricant and free the fastener, or replace with stainless steel hardware and anti-seize compound.
Unit Drifts Out of Alignment Between Checks
Likely Cause
Loose adjustment locking — locking nuts or plates not sufficiently tightened.
Diagnostic Steps
Check locking nut/plate torque against specification and look for the absence of locking wire or thread-locker.
Corrective Action
Re-tighten using a torque wrench to specification, and fit locking wire or thread-locker on critical bolts.
Base Fails or Cracks Under Load
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling fatigue from repeated wind loading and thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coupling for cracks or deformation during routine maintenance.
Corrective Action
Replace the coupling at the first sign of fatigue, before it fails prematurely.
Gradual Angular Drift Despite Locking Mechanisms
Likely Cause
Heavy vibration from aircraft or ground vehicles causing slow angular creep.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric check and an alignment verification using a theodolite, and compare against the last recorded alignment.
Corrective Action
Re-align the unit, re-secure locking mechanisms, and increase the frequency of alignment verification if drift recurs.
Base Plate Shows Deformation or Uneven Seating
Likely Cause
An unlevel pole or foundation stressing the base plate over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the foundation and pole for level, and inspect the base plate for visible deformation or stress marks.
Corrective Action
Correct the foundation level, shim the base as needed, and replace a deformed base plate.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Adjuster / Bolt Seizure
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion binding the adjuster or bolt threads, or dried-out lubricant allowing dirt ingress over years of outdoor exposure.
Effect
Adjuster/bolt cannot be turned, preventing azimuth or elevation correction during a scheduled or corrective alignment visit.
Detection Method
Functional check of adjuster travel during scheduled maintenance, and re-lubrication of threads per the maintenance schedule.
Locking Mechanism Creep
High Severity
Root Cause
A locking mechanism that does not fully re-engage after adjustment, or gradual loosening under wind and thermal cycling loads.
Effect
Unit drifts out of alignment between checks, despite the locking mechanism appearing engaged at each inspection.
Detection Method
Periodic alignment verification against the approved setting, and inspection of the locking mechanism for full engagement.
Base Fracture Under Load
High Severity
Root Cause
Material fatigue accumulated over years of wind and vibration loading, or a casting defect that propagated into a crack over time.
Effect
Base fails or cracks under load, compromising the structural support for the light unit it carries.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for cracking during scheduled maintenance, and load-bearing check where feasible.
Gradual Angular Creep
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Micro-slip at the locking interface under repeated thermal cycling, accumulating into a measurable drift over an extended period.
Effect
Gradual angular drift despite locking mechanisms appearing secure, slowly degrading alignment accuracy between formal checks.
Detection Method
Trend review of alignment readings across successive maintenance visits to catch a slow drift pattern.
Base Plate Deformation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ground settlement or frost heave beneath the base plate, or over-torquing during a prior installation or adjustment.
Effect
Base plate shows deformation or uneven seating, introducing a persistent alignment error that adjustment alone cannot fully correct.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of the base plate and surrounding ground during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Adjustment
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the PAPI unit's supply circuit before loosening or adjusting the mounting base, and verify zero energy before handling cable connectors at the light unit.

Angle Verification After Any Adjustment
Operational

Re-check the elevation angle with a calibrated inclinometer against the commissioned setting after any adjustment, and re-verify the full four-box light unit array reads correctly from the approach; a small angular error here directly shifts the visual glide path pilots fly.

Do Not Force a Seized Adjuster
Mechanical

If an adjuster bolt or mechanism resists movement, apply penetrating fluid and controlled force rather than forcing it; a stripped or sheared adjuster can leave the base unable to hold a precise angle.

Locking Mechanism Verification
Mechanical

Torque the locking mechanism to specification and confirm it is fully engaged after every adjustment; a mechanism left loose or under-torqued is the direct cause of gradual angular creep between inspections.

Airside Work Coordination & NOTAM
Operational

Coordinate with ATC and issue a NOTAM before adjusting a PAPI unit, since the system provides pilots with real-time visual glide path guidance and any period of incorrect or unreliable indication must be reflected in ATC procedures.

Working Near the Light Beam
Personnel Safety

Avoid looking directly into an energised PAPI beam at close range during alignment checks, and use secure footing or a rated platform when working on an elevated mounting base.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Fully Torqued Lock Doesn't Guarantee the Angle Was Right
Troubleshooting

Torque and lock verification confirms the mechanism won't move further, not that it was set to the correct elevation before locking. Always cross-check the actual inclinometer reading independently of confirming the lock is engaged, since a firmly locked base holding the wrong angle fails just as silently as a loose one.

Base Settlement Mimics a Mechanism Fault
Design

Where a unit keeps drifting out of tolerance despite a sound adjuster and lock, check whether the foundation or ground beneath the base itself has settled or heaved, particularly at sites with expansive soils or seasonal frost. Chasing the adjustment mechanism repeatedly without checking the foundation wastes maintenance cycles on the wrong root cause.

Vibration Loosens Locks Gradually, Not Suddenly
Reliability

Continuous low-amplitude vibration from wind loading or nearby vehicle/aircraft traffic can slowly back off a lock that tested correctly torqued at the last visit, without any single dramatic event. Where creep recurs at the same unit despite correct torque each time, consider a thread-locking compound or lock-washer upgrade rather than repeating the same torque procedure indefinitely.

Adjuster Seizure Correlates With Lubrication Interval, Not Age Alone
Maintenance

Two bases of the same age can differ enormously in adjuster stiffness purely based on how consistently they've been re-lubricated and protected from dirt ingress. Track lubrication history per unit and prioritise overdue units for preventive service, rather than assuming seizure risk is uniform across the array by installation date.

Verify the Whole Array After Any Single Base Adjustment
Verification

Correcting one unit's base angle in isolation, even correctly, can leave the four-unit array presenting an inconsistent transition if the other units have quietly drifted since the last check. Make a full-array recheck from an approach-representative position standard practice after any individual base adjustment, not just a spot-check of the corrected unit.

Frangible Coupling Condition Isn't Visible From Torque Alone
Mechanical

A frangible coupling can be correctly torqued and still have degraded shear characteristics from UV or thermal ageing over years of service. Include periodic shear-verification testing in the overhaul cycle for the mounting base's frangible interface, separate from routine adjustment and lock checks.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the PAPI mounting base: adjustment, frangibility, specifications, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a PAPI mounting base?

The mechanical interface between a PAPI light unit and its supporting structure. It is a robust, precision-machined component, typically aluminium alloy or stainless steel, that secures the unit and holds its optical alignment.

Q2Why is the mounting base critical?

The unit's angle is calibrated to fractions of a degree. The base keeps that angle from drifting under wind loading, ground vibration from aircraft and vehicles, and thermal expansion and contraction.

Q3Where is it used?

In every PAPI installation, including permanent systems with frangible poles set in concrete foundations, and helicopter PAPI installations.

Q4How does the base hold alignment and stay safe?

The base plate bolts to the top of the frangible pole or directly to a concrete pad, the PAPI unit attaches to it, and adjusters set azimuth and elevation. A frangible coupling lets the structure break away if struck.

Q5What adjustment range does it offer?

Typically ±5° in azimuth and 2.5°-4.5° in elevation, adjustable in 0.1° increments.

Q6What are the typical specifications?

Aluminium alloy (6061-T6 or 5083), 316L stainless or galvanised steel, a breakaway force of 2,500-5,000 N, a maximum load of 100 kg vertical and 50 kg horizontal wind load, stainless A2 or A4 fasteners, and at least 500 hours salt spray resistance (ASTM B117).

Q7What frangible mechanisms are used?

Shear pins, a scored neck or a breakable flange, designed to ICAO frangibility requirements.

Q8Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-28, FAA AC 150/5220-23A (Frangible Connections), IEC TS 61827 and ASTM B117 (salt spray testing).

Q9How often should the mounting base be inspected?

Semi-annually for azimuth and elevation locking mechanisms, and annually for fastener corrosion, frangible coupling condition and base plate deformation.

Q10Why can't an adjuster or bolt be turned?

Corrosion of exposed fasteners in marine or industrial environments is the usual cause. Check for rust or seizing and whether anti-seize compound was applied at installation.
