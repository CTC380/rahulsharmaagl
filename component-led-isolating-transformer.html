LED Isolating Transformer

An isolating transformer specifically optimised for powering LED‑based airfield ground lighting fixtures. Engineered to deliver stable, low‑ripple power to LED drivers with high efficiency, reduced core losses, and precise voltage regulation across the full dimming range, ensuring consistent light output and extended LED life.

Overview

What Is It?

The LED Isolating Transformer is a variant of the standard AGL isolating transformer, purpose‑built for the unique electrical demands of LED lighting. While retaining the fundamental function of galvanic isolation and constant‑current transfer from the 6.6A (or 20A) primary series circuit, this transformer is optimised with design features that enhance compatibility with LED drivers, improve system efficiency, and address the specific power quality requirements of solid‑state lighting.

Key differentiators include:

  • Optimised Secondary Voltage: Secondary voltages are often standardised to common LED driver input voltages (e.g., 12V, 24V, 48V AC) rather than traditional lamp voltages (6.82V, 9.85V). This reduces the rectification burden on the LED driver and minimises losses.
  • Reduced Core Loss: Using high‑grade grain‑oriented steel with lower hysteresis and eddy‑current losses, the transformer exhibits higher efficiency, especially at low dimming currents, which is critical for LED systems that often operate at reduced brightness for extended periods.
  • Superior Voltage Regulation: Tighter regulation (±3% vs. ±5%) ensures that the LED driver receives a stable input voltage across the entire current range, preventing flicker and colour shift.
  • Lower Leakage Inductance: The winding design is optimised to minimise leakage inductance, reducing voltage spikes and electromagnetic interference (EMI) that can affect sensitive LED driver electronics.
  • Thermal Management: With lower losses and improved heat dissipation, these transformers run cooler, contributing to the overall reliability of the LED system.

The transformer is fully encapsulated (IP68) and available in the same power ratings (15W to 100W) and installation types (buried, elevated, plug‑in) as conventional units, providing a seamless drop‑in replacement for legacy halogen systems when upgrading to LED.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The LED isolating transformer exists because pairing an LED driver with a conventional halogen-era transformer creates real compatibility problems: mismatched secondary voltages, looser regulation, and higher leakage inductance can all degrade the driver's performance or shorten its life, showing up as flicker, colour shift, or premature failure in a fixture that should otherwise be highly reliable. By tailoring the transformer's electrical characteristics to what an LED driver actually needs, this variant protects the reliability gains LED fixtures are supposed to deliver.

Because it's built as a drop-in replacement across the same power ratings and installation types as legacy transformers, it also serves a transition purpose — letting an airfield upgrade individual fixtures or whole circuits to LED without redesigning the underlying series-circuit infrastructure, so the safety and fault-tolerance benefits of the existing loop carry over unchanged.

Application

Where Is It Used?

This transformer is the ideal choice for any AGL installation using LED fixtures, including:

  • Runway Lighting: LED edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, end lights, and touchdown zone lights on precision runways.
  • Taxiway Lighting: LED edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, clearance bars, and guidance signs.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSR, and other approach configurations upgraded to LED.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Low‑, medium‑, and high‑intensity LED obstruction lights on structures of all heights.
  • Illuminated Airfield Signs: LED‑lit mandatory instruction, information, and direction signs.
  • Airport Beacons: LED rotating beacons and heliport beacons.
  • Retrofit Projects: Replacing traditional halogen transformers with LED‑optimised units when upgrading legacy systems to LED.

The transformer is compatible with all major LED driver topologies (constant current, constant voltage, dimmable) and is designed to work seamlessly with the CCR dimming steps (2.8A to 6.6A or 6.6A to 20A), ensuring smooth brightness control without flicker or instability.

Placement

Installation & Location

The LED-optimised transformer is installed in the same handhole, pit, or direct-buried enclosure as its halogen equivalent, adjacent to the fixture it feeds, so it can drop into an existing footprint during a retrofit without new civil works. The primary is spliced into the series loop with a standard L-823 connector, and the secondary is run to the LED fixture's driver input. Because LED drivers are more sensitive to waveform quality than halogen lamps, care is taken during installation to keep secondary cable runs within the manufacturer's specified length and to avoid shared enclosures with sources of electrical noise, ensuring the CCR's dimming steps translate into stable, flicker-free brightness at the fixture.

Function

How Does It Work?

Like all AGL isolating transformers, the LED variant operates on the principle of electromagnetic induction. The primary winding is connected in series with the constant‑current loop, and the secondary winding delivers power to the LED driver. However, the design is refined to address the specific requirements of LED loads:

  • Primary Side: Identical to standard transformers, with L‑823 or compatible connectors, rated for 6.6A (or 20A) primary current and designed to withstand the high transient voltages of the series loop.
  • Secondary Side: The secondary winding is configured to provide a higher voltage (e.g., 12V, 24V, 48V) at the rated primary current. This allows the LED driver to operate at a higher input voltage, reducing I²R losses in the secondary cabling and improving overall efficiency.
  • Core Design: The core is constructed from high‑permeability, low‑loss steel, often with thinner laminations to reduce eddy current losses at higher frequencies (some LED drivers use higher switching frequencies that may introduce harmonics).
  • Winding Technique: Winding geometry is optimised to minimise leakage inductance and inter‑winding capacitance, reducing EMI and voltage ringing that can interfere with driver operation.
  • Regulation Performance: The transformer maintains secondary voltage within ±3% of the nominal value across the full current range (2.8A to 6.6A), ensuring that the LED driver receives a stable input regardless of the selected brightness level.

The transformer is compatible with both leading‑edge and trailing‑edge dimming methods, and its low leakage inductance makes it particularly suitable for drivers with active power factor correction (PFC).

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The LED-optimised transformer occupies the same position in the control chain as any other isolating transformer: an Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects the required brightness step, the CCR regulates the whole loop to the corresponding fixed current, and the transformer has no addressable identity of its own — it converts whatever current the loop is carrying at that moment into the voltage its downstream load needs. The difference is what sits on the other side of that secondary winding: a halogen lamp draws current in rough proportion to voltage regardless of waveform quality, while an LED driver expects a stable, low-ripple supply to translate the CCR's discrete current steps into consistent, flicker-free brightness. This is why waveform quality, not just voltage level, becomes part of the transformer's job here — a unit that would pass a functional test into a resistive halogen load can still produce visible flicker or step-transition instability into an LED driver if its regulation isn't tight enough, which is the reasoning behind the secondary-side design refinements covered next.

Construction

Physical Construction

Mechanically the LED-optimised transformer is built the same way as any potted AGL transformer — windings around a laminated core, fully encapsulated in epoxy or polyurethane for its IP68 rating — but the core and winding design inside that potting is refined for lower loss and tighter regulation: core loss is held to 0.5W or less at rated current and leakage inductance to within 5% of rated inductance, both tighter tolerances than a standard lamp-rated transformer needs, since they translate directly into the low-ripple secondary an LED driver requires. Insulation is specified to Class F or Class H depending on rating, and completed units weigh 1.5–4.0 kg depending on power option and whether the unit is potted for burial or housed for elevated mounting. Leads terminate in connectors sized for 12V, 24V, or 48V AC secondary outputs matched to common LED driver inputs, rather than the wider voltage range a halogen-compatible unit might offer.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Driver Compatibility & Voltage Matching

Not all LED drivers accept the same input voltage. If the transformer's secondary voltage does not match the driver's specified input range, the driver may not function correctly or may be damaged. Always verify driver specifications before ordering.

Harmonics and EMI

Some LED drivers draw non‑sinusoidal current, introducing harmonics that can cause additional core losses and heating in the transformer. Using transformers with low leakage inductance and proper shielding can mitigate this. Consider using transformers with a PFC‑optimised design.

Dimming Instability

At very low dimming currents (e.g., 2.8A), some LED drivers may exhibit flicker or instability if the input voltage drops below the driver's minimum operating threshold. Ensure the transformer's regulation is tight enough to maintain adequate voltage at the lowest current step.

Overloading with High‑Power LED Fixtures

Although LED fixtures consume less power than halogen for the same light output, high‑intensity LED fixtures (e.g., 100W equivalent) still require adequate transformer sizing. Do not exceed the transformer's rated power.

Retrofit Challenges with Legacy Cabling

When replacing halogen transformers with LED‑optimised units, the secondary cabling may need to be upgraded if the voltage is increased (e.g., from 6.82V to 24V). Check cable insulation ratings and current‑carrying capacity.

Secondary Side Protection

LED drivers often require overvoltage and overcurrent protection. Some transformers include built‑in secondary fuses or thermal cut‑outs; if not, external protection should be provided.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

LED Driver Fails to Start or Is Damaged
Likely Cause
The transformer's secondary voltage doesn't match the LED driver's specified input range, either under-powering the driver or exceeding its rating.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the driver's rated input voltage range against the transformer's actual secondary output under load; confirm the driver's specification sheet before further testing.
Corrective Action
Fit a transformer with secondary voltage matched to the driver's input range; verify compatibility before ordering replacement units.
Driver or Transformer Runs Hot / Increased Losses
Likely Cause
The LED driver draws non-sinusoidal current, introducing harmonics that increase core losses and heating in the transformer.
Diagnostic Steps
Check transformer temperature against rating during operation; if test equipment is available, check for harmonic content on the primary or secondary side.
Corrective Action
Use a transformer with low leakage inductance and proper shielding, or a PFC-optimised design better suited to the driver's current waveform.
Flicker or Instability at Low Dimming Steps
Likely Cause
At very low dimming current (e.g., 2.8A), the driver's input voltage may drop below its minimum operating threshold if the transformer's regulation isn't tight enough.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure secondary voltage at the lowest CCR current step under load; compare against the driver's minimum operating voltage.
Corrective Action
Fit a transformer with tighter voltage regulation at low current, or select an LED driver rated for a wider input voltage range.
Transformer Overloaded by High-Intensity LED Fixture
Likely Cause
The connected LED fixture's power draw, even though lower than an equivalent halogen, still exceeds the transformer's rated power.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the fixture's rated power draw against the transformer's nameplate rating.
Corrective Action
Replace the transformer with one correctly rated for the fixture's actual power draw.
Retrofit Cabling Overheats or Shows Voltage Drop
Likely Cause
Existing halogen-era secondary cabling wasn't sized for the LED transformer's different secondary voltage or current, causing overheating or excessive voltage drop.
Diagnostic Steps
Check secondary cable insulation rating and gauge against the new transformer's voltage and current; measure voltage drop under load.
Corrective Action
Upgrade the secondary cable to match the new voltage and current-carrying requirement before returning the circuit to service.
No Overvoltage or Overcurrent Protection on the Secondary
Likely Cause
The transformer lacks a built-in secondary fuse or thermal cut-out, leaving the LED driver unprotected from secondary-side faults.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the transformer's specification for built-in secondary protection; review whether external protection has been fitted.
Corrective Action
Fit external overvoltage/overcurrent protection on the secondary if the transformer doesn't include it, per the driver manufacturer's recommendation.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Primary Winding Open Circuit
High Severity
Root Cause
Wire fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown internal fuse-wire link from a lightning-induced transient.
Effect
Opens the series loop, darkening every fixture downstream of the transformer on that circuit.
Detection Method
CCR loop-continuity/open-circuit indication, and megger or continuity testing at the transformer primary.
Insulation Breakdown from Moisture Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or degraded encapsulation, a failed cable gland or seal, or prolonged submersion in a flooded handhole or pit.
Effect
Reduced insulation resistance leading to a partial or full ground fault that can trip the CCR.
Detection Method
Megger testing against the ≥50 MΩ minimum, and visual inspection of the encapsulation and cable entries.
Secondary Winding Short or Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal insulation failure between secondary turns, often accelerated by sustained overtemperature operation.
Effect
Loss of secondary voltage regulation, fixture dimming or failure, and possible primary overcurrent.
Detection Method
Secondary voltage measurement under load, compared against the transformer's rated output.
Overload / Core Saturation from Rating Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A CCR step or connected fixture load exceeding the transformer's rated primary current or secondary power.
Effect
Increased core losses, overheating, and accelerated insulation ageing.
Detection Method
Verification of the CCR step current against the transformer's rating plate, and thermal inspection where accessible.
L-823 Connector / Termination Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating during installation, gasket failure allowing moisture in, or galvanic corrosion building up at the connector pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent contact, flicker, or a high-resistance joint that generates localised heat.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at scheduled maintenance, and continuity or resistance checks across the joint.
Encapsulation Cracking / Case Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Freeze-thaw cycling, mechanical impact during installation or maintenance, or age-related degradation of the potting compound.
Effect
Exposes the winding to moisture, accelerating insulation breakdown.
Detection Method
Visual inspection during handhole or pit access, and a drop in insulation resistance on megger testing.
Thermal Degradation from Sustained Overcurrent
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated or prolonged operation above rated current, often from an incorrectly set CCR step or a transformer undersized for the installed load.
Effect
Progressive insulation embrittlement that shortens service life and raises the risk of an eventual winding failure.
Detection Method
Rating verification against actual loop current, and insulation resistance trending across successive maintenance cycles.
Output Voltage Drift / Regulation Loss
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual winding degradation or core ageing that shifts the transformer's turns-ratio performance away from its original specification.
Effect
Fixture brightness drifts slightly from the intended intensity step without an outright fault.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity survey and secondary voltage measurement compared against commissioning records.
Core Loss from LED Driver Harmonic / Ripple Current
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The non-linear load presented by LED drivers introducing harmonic and ripple current content the transformer's core and winding weren't originally sized around.
Effect
Elevated operating temperature and accelerated insulation ageing even when average loading stays within the rated power.
Detection Method
Thermal check against a resistive-load baseline, and confirmation that the connected LED driver's harmonic profile matches the transformer's compatibility rating.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The LED isolating transformer sits on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the transformer before opening its housing or handling connectors.

Residual Charge in Driver Circuitry
Electrical Safety

Allow time for any downstream LED driver capacitance to discharge after isolation, and verify zero energy at the transformer output before disconnecting secondary leads, since electronic loads can retain a residual charge briefly after power-off.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Driver Compatibility Verification
Compliance

Confirm the replacement transformer's output waveform and rating are compatible with the connected LED driver before installation; a mismatch can cause driver overheating, flicker, or premature failure even where the loop current itself is correct.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the transformer case and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

FOD Control at the Housing
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the transformer housing is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating it, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Seal Integrity on Reinstatement
Electrical Safety

Renew gaskets and properly torque or seat sealed enclosures on reassembly, since LED driver electronics are more sensitive to moisture ingress than a simple magnetic winding and a poor seal can cause an early repeat failure.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture at reduced intensity, flickering, or otherwise non-compliant after maintenance; verify photometric output and insulation resistance before closing out the work.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the LED isolating transformer: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an LED isolating transformer?

It is a variant of the standard AGL isolating transformer purpose-built for LED lighting. It keeps the same galvanic isolation and constant-current transfer from the 6.6 A (or 20 A) series circuit but is optimised for LED driver compatibility, efficiency and power quality.

Q2How does it differ from a standard isolating transformer?

Secondary voltages are often standardised to common LED driver input voltages (12 V, 24 V or 48 V AC) rather than traditional lamp voltages, which reduces the rectification burden on the driver and minimises losses. It also uses high-grade grain-oriented steel for lower core loss and has minimised leakage inductance for lower EMI.

Q3Where is an LED isolating transformer used?

In any AGL installation with LED fixtures: runway, taxiway, approach lighting (ALSF-1, ALSF-2, MALSR) upgraded to LED, and obstruction lighting.

Q4What are the typical specifications?

Power options of 15 W to 100 W (custom up to 300 W), secondary 12 V, 24 V or 48 V AC, core loss of 0.5 W or less at 6.6 A and 50 Hz, voltage regulation of ±3% across the dimming range and leakage inductance of 5% or less. Protection is IP68 (fully encapsulated) and weight is 1.5 kg to 4.0 kg.

Q5What primary current does it use?

6.6 A nominal with 5-step dimming from 2.8 A to 6.6 A (2.8, 3.4, 4.1, 5.2 and 6.6 A), or 20 A versions.

Q6Is the secondary output regulated?

The secondary provides a regulated AC output matched to the LED driver input, held within ±3% across the dimming range. The secondary stays isolated from the primary loop, and the case is bonded to the airfield earth grid.

Q7Which standards apply?

The main references are IEC 61823, FAA AC 150/5345-47 (L-830, L-831 and L-832 series transformers, including LED-optimised variants), SAE ARP 5553 (LED transformer performance and compatibility) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should it be inspected?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for voltage regulation, secondary voltage and load current verification, and annual for insulation resistance, core loss and efficiency, EMI and leakage inductance, encapsulation integrity and cable and connector inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with LED isolating transformers?

Common problems are driver compatibility and voltage matching, harmonics and EMI, dimming instability, overloading with high-power LED fixtures, retrofit challenges with legacy cabling and lack of secondary-side protection. A driver that fails to start, flicker at low dimming steps or a transformer running hot are typical symptoms.
