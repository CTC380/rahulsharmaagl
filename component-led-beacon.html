LED Beacon

A modern solid‑state rotating or flashing beacon system that uses high‑power LEDs as the light source. Offers significantly longer life, lower power consumption, instant‑on capability, and reduced maintenance compared to traditional halogen or xenon beacons. Available in airport (green/white), heliport (white/yellow), and obstruction (red/white) configurations.

Overview

What Is It?

An LED Beacon is a rotating or flashing beacon system that utilizes high‑power light‑emitting diodes (LEDs) as the light source instead of traditional halogen lamps or xenon flashtubes. It provides the same identification function—alternating colour flashes (green/white, white/yellow, or red/white)—but with the advantages of LED technology: up to 50,000+ hours of service life, instant‑on at full intensity, lower power consumption (typically 50–70% less than halogen), and no warm‑up time. LED beacons are available in rotating (motor‑driven) and static (electronic flashing) configurations, with the latter using multiple LED modules to simulate rotation. They are increasingly the standard for new installations and retrofits at airports, heliports, and obstruction warning systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The LED beacon exists to deliver the identical identification function of a traditional halogen or xenon beacon while removing the specific reliability weaknesses that come with a mechanical light source: filament or flashtube burnout, warm-up delay before reaching full intensity, and the higher power draw of a heated light source. Because the beacon's purpose is continuous, unattended operation, often for years between service visits, an LED source's 50,000+ hour service life and instant-on behaviour directly serve that purpose better than a technology that degrades and requires a warm-up period every time it activates. The static electronic-flashing configuration exists for a related reason: it reproduces the beacon's rotating-beam identification pattern without any moving parts at all, eliminating drive-motor and turntable wear as failure modes entirely, which is precisely why it is becoming the preferred choice for new installations and retrofits.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Used as a replacement for traditional halogen beacons in all aviation applications: airport identification beacons (green/white), heliport beacons (white/yellow), water airport beacons (yellow/white), and obstruction warning beacons (red/white). Installed on control towers, hangar roofs, helipads, offshore platforms, and tall structures. The LED beacon is also used in portable and temporary applications due to its low power consumption and ability to run on battery or solar power.

Placement

Installation & Location

As a like-for-like replacement, the LED beacon mounts into the same base, turntable, or fixed housing as the halogen or xenon unit it replaces, using the existing structural mounting and, in most retrofits, the existing power and control wiring. New installations follow the same siting principles as any rotating or static beacon: an elevated, unobstructed position on a tower, mast, or rooftop with a clear 360° sweep. Because LED beacons draw substantially less power, some installations take advantage of the reduced load to run from a smaller supply, standby battery, or solar-charged system, which is particularly useful at remote or off-grid sites. Static (electronic-flashing) configurations are mounted as a fixed unit with no rotating parts, simplifying the mounting bracket to a rigid fixed mount rather than a bearing-supported turntable, and cabling runs directly to the beacon controller for flash-pattern and photocell control.

Function

How Does It Work?

An LED beacon uses an array of high‑intensity LEDs (typically 5–20 W each) with specific colour outputs (white, red, yellow) or with dichroic filters. In a rotating configuration, the LED array is mounted on a motor‑driven turntable with reflectors to produce a sweeping beam. In a static configuration, multiple LED modules are arranged in a circle and electronically sequenced to simulate a rotating beam (this is known as an "LED strobe" or "electronic beacon"). The intensity is controlled by pulse‑width modulation (PWM) to achieve the required effective intensity. The LED driver provides constant current regulation and includes surge protection. A photocell or timer controls the on/off cycle. The housing is weatherproof with a transparent dome, and the LEDs are mounted on a heat sink to dissipate heat efficiently.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The LED beacon's control architecture is built around its own driver board rather than any shared airfield circuit. Mains or DC power feeds the driver, which performs constant-current regulation and PWM dimming to hold the array at the required effective intensity, and a photocell or timer wired directly into that same board decides when the beacon switches on and off. From there the driver's logic forks depending on configuration: in a rotating unit it feeds a small motor controller that turns the turntable at a fixed speed, while in a static unit it feeds an electronic sequencer that steps current through the ring of LED modules in order, simulating the same sweep with no moving parts. Since both configurations produce the same white/red/yellow identification signal, this driver-level fork is really the only thing distinguishing the two internally. Surge protection and thermal monitoring of the heat sink also live on the driver board, so the beacon can throttle or shut down the array locally in response to overheating or a power surge well before that condition would ever reach a wider monitoring system.

Power In → LED Driver (PWM + Surge/Thermal Protection) → Photocell/Timer → Motor Controller or Sequencer → LED Output

Two Ways to Sweep

Rotating vs. Static LED Configuration

Rotating

LED array mounted on a motor-driven turntable, just like a halogen rotating beacon — the whole assembly physically turns to sweep the beam.

Static

No moving parts — a ring of fixed LED modules is electronically sequenced, switching on and off in order to simulate the same sweeping flash.

Construction

Physical Construction

The beacon body is a weatherproof cast aluminium or moulded composite housing, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, built around a ring or cluster of high-power LED modules rather than a single filament lamp or flashtube. In rotating configurations, the LED cluster and its coloured lens segments are mounted on a low-friction bearing turned by a small, low-power drive motor; in static electronic configurations, multiple fixed LED modules are arranged around the housing and sequentially switched to simulate the sweep of a rotating beam, eliminating moving parts entirely. Each LED module sits behind a coloured (green, white, yellow, or red) polycarbonate or acrylic lens, gasket-sealed to the housing to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating, with the solid-state design allowing a smaller, lighter optical head than equivalent halogen or xenon units. The housing mounts to a mast, rooftop plinth, or tower structure via a flanged base and stainless-steel fasteners, and internal driver electronics, which manage flash timing, dimming, and thermal regulation, are potted or conformal-coated and isolated from the optical chamber, drawing from a 230V AC or 24V DC supply with the reduced power draw of the LED source easing load on backup and solar-powered installations.

Installation Requirements

Mast/Structure Mounting Check
Civil

Confirm the mast, rooftop plinth, or tower structure is rated for the beacon's weight and wind loading before fixing the flanged base.

Bearing/Sequencing Alignment
Mechanical

On rotating configurations, level the base so the bearing tracks true under the drive motor; on static electronic configurations, confirm all LED modules are seated evenly around the housing for a consistent simulated sweep.

Colour Segment Verification
Configuration

Confirm the correct lens colours (green, white, yellow, or red) are fitted to the LED modules in the correct positions for the site's beacon colour sequence.

Driver Electronics Wiring
Electrical

Terminate wiring to the potted or conformal-coated driver electronics per the wiring diagram, keeping the driver compartment isolated from the optical chamber as designed.

Power Supply Termination
Electrical

Terminate the 230V AC or 24V DC supply, confirming the converter (where fitted) matches the installed voltage, especially on backup- or solar-fed installations.

Housing Seal Check
Mechanical

Confirm gaskets around each LED module lens seat correctly to achieve the rated IP66/IP67 protection before the housing is closed and energised.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record mounting position, colour sequence configuration, and wiring termination as-built before the beacon is closed out for service.

Pre-Energisation

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric & Colour Check
Annual

Test intensity and colour output, since LEDs gradually lose brightness and can shift colour; plan module replacement around the 50,000-hour mark.

Heat Sink Cleaning
Semi-annual

Clean heat sink fins and confirm airflow is unobstructed, since blocked or inadequate heat dissipation shortens LED life significantly.

Driver & Surge Protection Check
Annual

Inspect the LED driver and surge protection devices, which are vulnerable to voltage transients and lightning-induced surges.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Wipe the lens free of dust, salt film, or debris that would otherwise reduce measured intensity below spec.

Annual

Check gaskets and housing seams for cracking or compression set to keep moisture out of the driver and LED compartments.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Examine power connections for corrosion or loose crimps, a common cause of intermittent beacon outages.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

LED Degradation and Colour Shift

LEDs gradually lose intensity and can shift colour over time. Regular photometric testing is essential to ensure the beacon still meets intensity and colour requirements. Replacement of LED modules is typically needed after 50,000 hours.

Heat Sink and Thermal Management

LEDs generate heat that must be dissipated to maintain performance and life. Blocked or inadequate heat sinks can cause premature failure. Regular cleaning of heat sink fins and ensuring proper airflow is critical.

Driver Failure and Surge Damage

LED drivers are susceptible to voltage surges and lightning. Use of high‑quality surge protection devices and regular inspection of the driver's condition is recommended to avoid unexpected outages.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Beacon Completely Dark
Likely Cause
Failed driver board, blown fuse or breaker at the supply, an open circuit in the incoming power feed, or a photocell stuck in the "daylight" state and holding the beacon off.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming supply voltage at the beacon base; test driver output with the photocell bypassed or covered; inspect the fuse/breaker; confirm the photocell responds correctly when shaded.
Corrective Action
Replace a failed driver board or photocell, repair the supply wiring fault, or reset/replace the fuse or breaker before re-energising.
Beacon Stuck On or Fails to Switch Off in Daylight
Likely Cause
Photocell failure, dirt or debris shading the sensor lens, or a driver-board timer fault overriding the photocell input.
Diagnostic Steps
Clean the photocell lens and re-test; cover and uncover the sensor to confirm it switches correctly; check the driver's timer/override setting if fitted.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the photocell, and reprogram or replace the driver's timer control if it is not honouring the photocell input.
Reduced Intensity or Dim Flash
Likely Cause
LED array degradation approaching end of rated service life, a hazy or dirty dome, or the driver's PWM duty cycle set below the required effective intensity.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against the applicable ICAO/FAA threshold; inspect and clean the transparent dome; verify the driver's PWM/intensity setting against the design requirement.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded LED module, clean or replace a hazed dome, or correct the driver's PWM setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Rotating Beacon Sweep Stalled or Running Slow
Likely Cause
Worn or seized turntable bearings, debris binding the rotating assembly, or a fault in the motor controller.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the turntable for binding or resistance when turned by hand; measure motor current draw against the rated value; test the motor controller output independently of the motor.
Corrective Action
Lubricate or replace worn bearings, clear any obstruction from the turntable, or replace the motor controller if it is not delivering the correct drive signal.
Static (Electronic) Beacon Sweep Pattern Irregular
Likely Cause
A failed LED module within the sequenced ring, or a timing fault in the electronic sequencer driving the simulated sweep.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe a full sequence cycle to identify which module fails to fire or fires out of order; check the sequencer board for a stepping fault; test the suspect module in isolation.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED module, or replace the sequencer board if the stepping fault persists across modules.
Beacon Overheats or Trips on Thermal Protection
Likely Cause
Blocked or dust-clogged heat sink fins restricting heat dissipation, inadequate ventilation at the mounting location, or an oversensitive driver thermal-protection threshold.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and clean the heat sink fins; confirm adequate airflow around the housing; check the driver's thermal fault indicator or log for repeated trips.
Corrective Action
Clean the heat sink, improve ventilation or shading at the site, or replace the driver board if it continues to trip below its rated thermal limit.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out and verify zero energy at the LED driver before opening the housing; because LEDs are instant-on with no warm-up delay, treat the fixture as live the moment power is present, not just once it appears lit.

Driver Board ESD Precautions
Component Handling

Use anti-static precautions when handling the LED driver or module boards; the low-voltage electronics are more sensitive to static discharge than a traditional halogen lamp or ballast.

Direct Beam Viewing
Personnel Safety

Avoid staring directly into the LED array during functional testing, particularly on high-output rotating or flashing configurations.

Heat Sink Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow the LED module's heat sink to cool after sustained operation before handling; despite lower overall power draw than halogen, the LED junction and heat sink can still run hot.

Outage Coordination
Operational

Notify ATC or the site operator before taking the beacon offline for module replacement or driver work.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for driver-side connections and fall-protection PPE appropriate to the mounting height.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify colour, flash rate, and intensity meet specification before closing out the work, since a like-for-like LED module swap can still shift chromaticity slightly if the wrong part is fitted.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Instant-On Changes the Sequencing Verification, Not Just the Warm-Up
Commissioning

Because an LED source reaches full intensity instantly rather than ramping up like halogen or xenon, colour sequencing verification needs to catch timing errors at the very start of each segment rather than allowing for a brief rise period. Set commissioning acceptance criteria for this fixture around instant full-intensity switching, not the tolerances written for lamp-based beacons.

Heat Sink Cleaning Is the Maintenance Item Lamp-Based Beacons Never Needed
Maintenance

An LED beacon's long service life depends on the driver electronics and LED junctions staying within their thermal design envelope, which a dust- or grime-fouled heat sink quietly undermines long before an actual light-output failure appears. Add heat sink cleaning to the routine schedule even though it has no equivalent on a traditional lamp-based beacon.

Driver Board Failure Looks Different From Lamp Failure
Troubleshooting

A failed LED driver board can produce partial, flickering, or wrong-colour output rather than the clean total darkness a burnt-out halogen lamp gives, which can be misread as a filter or lens problem. Run the driver board's self-test and check surge protection status before assuming an optical fault on an LED beacon showing degraded output.

Surge Protection Matters More Here Than on a Lamp-Based Beacon
Electrical

LED driver electronics are considerably more sensitive to voltage transients than a simple halogen filament circuit, so a beacon retrofitted from lamp to LED without upgrading surge protection is taking on new vulnerability the original design didn't carry. Verify surge protection is sized for the LED driver, not just carried over from the legacy lamp circuit.

Retrofit Power Draw Calculations Need Redoing, Not Assumed
Retrofit

The 50-70% power reduction an LED beacon offers over halogen changes the load seen by the existing supply and any shared circuit protection, which was sized for the old lamp's draw. Recalculate circuit protection and any shared transformer sizing at retrofit rather than assuming the existing electrical infrastructure is simply "more than enough" for the lighter LED load.

Long Rated Life Doesn't Remove the Need for Photometric Checks
Reliability

A 50,000+ hour rated life covers gradual LED lumen depreciation, but doesn't guarantee the beacon stays within chromaticity and intensity limits over that whole span, particularly if individual LEDs within a segment fail unevenly. Keep photometric and colour checks on the normal inspection cycle rather than extending the interval on the strength of the LED life rating alone.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about LED beacons: how they work, colours, power savings, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an LED beacon?

An LED beacon is a rotating or flashing beacon that uses high-power LEDs instead of halogen lamps or xenon flashtubes. It performs the same identification function with alternating colour flashes, but with a service life of 50,000+ hours, instant-on at full intensity and no warm-up time.

Q2What is the difference between a rotating LED beacon and an electronic LED beacon?

A rotating LED beacon mounts the LED array on a motor-driven turntable with reflectors to produce a sweeping beam. A static, or electronic, LED beacon arranges several LED modules in a circle and sequences them electronically to simulate rotation, so it has no moving parts.

Q3What colours and flash rate do LED beacons use?

Typical sequences are green/white for airports, white/yellow for heliports and water airports, and red/white for obstruction warning. The effective flash rate is 24–30 flashes per minute, matching an equivalent rotating beacon.

Q4How bright is an LED beacon?

Effective intensity is up to 50,000 cd for rotating versions and up to 30,000 cd for electronic versions, with peak beam intensity of at least 2,000 cd per colour per ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5345-12. Intensity is controlled by PWM dimming steps selected by the Beacon Controller.

Q5How much power does an LED beacon save compared with halogen?

An LED beacon typically draws 50–150 W compared with 200–400 W for a halogen beacon, a saving of roughly 50–70%. The lower load also allows lighter-gauge supply cable and lets the beacon run from battery or solar power in portable or temporary applications.

Q6Can an LED beacon replace an existing halogen beacon?

Yes. It is designed as a like-for-like replacement that mounts into the same base, turntable or fixed housing and, in most retrofits, reuses the existing structural mounting and power and control wiring. Check compatibility with the Beacon Controller and the flash rate requirement before changeover.

Q7What power supply does an LED beacon use?

A dedicated beacon circuit, typically 230 V AC or 24 V DC with a converter, independent of the airfield series circuit. The Beacon Controller manages colour sequence, flash rate and PWM dimming, and detects LED array or driver failure and loss of sync.

Q8Which standards apply to LED beacons?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-12 (airport rotating beacons, LED version) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED beacon performance and durability. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should an LED beacon be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for heat sink cleaning, and annual for photometric and colour checks, driver and surge protection checks, seal and housing inspection and cable and connector inspection. Plan LED module replacement around the 50,000-hour mark. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is my LED beacon dark or stuck on?

A dark beacon is often a failed driver board, blown fuse or breaker, an open supply circuit, or a photocell stuck in the daylight state. A beacon that stays on in daylight usually has a failed or dirty photocell, or a driver timer fault overriding it. Clean the photocell lens, test its switching by shading it, and check the driver output.
