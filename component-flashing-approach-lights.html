Flashing Approach Lights

A series of white flashing lights that provide enhanced visual guidance to pilots during final approach, particularly in low visibility conditions. Often used in conjunction with steady‑burning approach lights, these flashing beacons improve depth perception and directional awareness, helping pilots maintain the correct glide path and align with the runway centreline.

Overview

What Is It?

Flashing Approach Lights are a set of high‑intensity white lights installed along the approach lighting system that flash at a regular interval (typically 60‑120 flashes per minute) to provide a dynamic visual cue for pilots during final approach. Unlike Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL), which flash in a progressive sequence to create a "rolling" effect, these lights typically flash simultaneously or in a simple alternating pattern, offering a pulsing visual reference that enhances the pilot's awareness of the approach path and helps maintain proper descent angle.

They are often used in systems such as Runway End Identifier Lights (REIL), as part of ALSF configurations, or as standalone flashing beacons at airports where basic flashing guidance is sufficient. Their primary advantage is the increased conspicuity compared to steady‑burning lights, making them particularly valuable in fog, rain, or snow.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

These lights exist because a flashing signal is inherently more conspicuous to the human eye than a steady one at equivalent intensity, particularly when visibility is degraded by fog, rain, or snow and the pilot's attention is divided across multiple approach references. Their purpose is to draw and hold the pilot's attention to the approach path itself during the highest-workload phase of flight, functioning as a supplementary conspicuity layer rather than a replacement for the steady-burning centreline, barrette, and crossbar lights that carry the primary lateral and distance guidance. By reinforcing the approach lighting system's overall visibility rather than adding new geometric information, they serve a purpose distinct from sequenced flashing lights, which are used specifically for directional "rolling" guidance rather than general conspicuity.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Flashing approach lights are installed as part of approach lighting systems at airports that require enhanced visual cues. They are typically used in:

  • Runway End Identifier Lights (REIL) – pairs of synchronized flashing lights at the runway threshold
  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems – as part of the approach lighting configuration
  • MALSF and MALSR systems – providing flashing cues in medium‑intensity systems
  • Simple approach lighting systems where basic flashing guidance is needed
  • Military airfields requiring conspicuous approach markers
  • Heliports and remote airstrips where low‑cost flashing beacons are used
Placement

Installation & Location

Flashing units are integrated into the same pier or pole line as the steady-burning approach lights they augment, typically at the outer end of the approach lighting system and/or paired at the runway threshold as REIL. Approach-lighting-pier units mount the flasher head atop the same frangible pole as the associated centreline or crossbar light, sharing its foundation and cable route back to the approach lighting control cabinet; threshold REIL units mount on their own low frangible masts just clear of the runway strip, one on each side of the runway. Each flasher head is fed from a dedicated flasher control unit rather than the CCR series circuit, so a separate control cable run is required alongside the series-circuit cabling.

Function

How Does It Work?

Flashing approach lights operate on a simple but effective principle:

  • Flashing Pattern: The lights flash at a consistent rate (typically 60‑120 flashes per minute) – either simultaneously (all on/off together) or in a simple alternating pattern between groups.
  • Visual Cue: The pulsing effect draws the pilot's attention and provides a clear contrast against steady‑burning lights, helping to distinguish the approach path from background lighting.
  • Depth Perception: The flashing action helps pilots judge distance and closure rate, as the apparent brightness and flash timing change with distance.
  • Directional Guidance: When placed along the approach centreline, the lights provide a directional reference, especially when combined with steady‑burning barrettes or crossbars.
  • Intensity Control: Like most AGL lighting, the intensity is typically controlled by a CCR with multiple steps, allowing ATC to adjust brightness for visibility conditions – higher intensity in fog, lower in clear conditions.
  • Integration: They are often integrated with the ALCMS for remote control and monitoring.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Flashing approach lights are governed by two independent control layers rather than the single CCR loop that runs a plain edge or centreline light. A dedicated flasher control unit — separate from the constant current series circuit — generates the flash timing (simultaneous or alternating, 60‑120 flashes per minute) and requires its own control cable run back to the approach lighting control cabinet. Brightness, by contrast, is still set the conventional way: the flasher head is fed through an isolating transformer from the CCR series circuit, so the ALCMS-selected intensity step applies to the flash just as it would to a steady-burning fixture. In practice this means the ALCMS issues two separate commands to the same fixture — a brightness step to the CCR loop, and a flash mode/rate to the flasher control unit — and a fault in one layer (e.g., a stuck flasher relay) does not necessarily affect the other.

Unlike Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL), where each fixture along the approach fires a fraction of a second after the last to create a "rolling" motion toward the runway, Flashing Approach Lights hold no fixed sequence — every fixture in a group changes state at the same instant, giving a pulsing rather than travelling visual cue.

Construction

Physical Construction

The flasher head houses a xenon flash tube (or, in newer installations, a high-output LED array driven in pulse mode) behind a toughened-glass or polycarbonate dome, gasket-sealed for an IP65/IP66 rating against the weather exposure typical of an approach lighting site. The flash tube's discharge capacitor and trigger electronics are housed either within the head itself or in a separate weatherproof enclosure at the pole base, since this circuitry is driven by the dedicated flasher control unit rather than the CCR series loop. On approach-lighting piers the head mounts atop the same frangible pole used by the associated centreline or crossbar light, sharing its foundation; threshold REIL units instead mount on their own low frangible masts set just clear of the runway strip. Because the fixture carries two separate feeds — series-circuit power for brightness and a control cable for flash timing — its base enclosure has separate sealed glands for each, along with the isolating transformer for the series-circuit connection.

Installation Requirements

Pole/Mast Setting
Civil

Set the frangible pole or REIL mast to design position and elevation, sharing the pier foundation where the head mounts atop an existing centreline or crossbar pole.

Dual Feed Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay separate duct/cable routes for the series-circuit power feed and the dedicated flasher control cable, since the two do not share the same fault path.

Sealed Gland Termination
Electrical

Terminate the series-circuit and flasher control cables through separate sealed glands at the base enclosure, keeping both splices fully weatherproof before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer wattage and ratio for the series-circuit connection match the fixture and the circuit's rated current step before connection.

Flasher Trigger Electronics Installation
Electrical

Install the discharge capacitor and trigger electronics in the flash head or its weatherproof base enclosure per manufacturer procedure, connecting it to the dedicated flasher control unit.

Frangible Coupling Fixing
Mechanical

Torque the frangible coupling to the specified value, checking the fitting shears as designed under lateral load and does not bind.

Elevation & Aim Setting
Mechanical

Set fixture height and beam aim against the design aiming diagram, keeping elevated units within the frangibility height limit.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record pole/mast position, both cable routes, connector seals, and torque values as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the isolating transformer and series loop before the circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity end to end and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Flasher Control Cable Verification
Pre-Energisation

Check continuity and insulation of the dedicated flasher control cable run separately from the series-circuit loop, since the two feeds share the fixture but not the same fault path.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at the L-823 connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm the flasher head responds correctly at each, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Flash Rate & Pattern Commissioning
Energisation

Confirm each unit fires at the specified 60‑120 flashes/min rate and that the row holds the designed simultaneous or alternating pattern in sync.

Fixture Alignment & Aim Verification
Final Acceptance

Check elevation and azimuth of each flasher head against the design aiming diagram, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure peak flash intensity, colour, and beam spread with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances prior to operational handover.

Frangibility & Mounting Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm the frangible coupling or pole, base can depth, and fixture height meet as-built tolerances before the site is finally backfilled.

ALCMS/Controller Integration Test
Final Acceptance

Confirm brightness-step and flash-mode commands issued from the ALCMS or standalone flashing controller are correctly received and acknowledged at every unit.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, flash timing, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

Flashing lights are exposed to bird droppings, rubber deposits, and de‑icing chemicals. Regular cleaning with approved solvents is essential to maintain high‑intensity output.

Flashing Controller Failure

Failure of the flashing control module can cause lights to stop flashing or flash at incorrect rates. Regular inspection and backup controllers are recommended.

Individual Light Failure

A single failed unit can break the flash pattern, reducing conspicuity. Prompt replacement is essential.

Power Supply and CCR Issues

Flashing systems require stable power; voltage fluctuations can cause erratic flashing. Regular power quality monitoring is recommended.

Misalignment of Elevated Fixtures

Elevated units can be knocked out of alignment by wind or wildlife. Regular checks using a theodolite are essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Unit Not Flashing
Likely Cause
Failed flashtube/lamp, a degraded discharge capacitor, or an open isolating transformer at that unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the unit's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check capacitor charge and flashtube trigger voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the flashtube/lamp or capacitor if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Row Not Flashing / Controller Fault
Likely Cause
Failure of the flashing control module, or loss of the trigger signal distributed to each unit in the row.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the flashing controller and any backup controller for fault codes; verify the trigger signal is present at the first and last units in the row; confirm ALCMS or standalone controller commands are being acknowledged.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the flashing controller, restore the trigger distribution wiring if broken, and re-verify the full row flashes together before returning to service.
Flash Pattern Out of Sync
Likely Cause
One or more units are drifting off the simultaneous or alternating sequence, typically from a marginal trigger circuit or timing drift in a secondary controller.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a flash pattern verification across the full row and identify which unit(s) are out of step; check trigger cable length/routing and controller timing settings for that segment.
Corrective Action
Re-sync or replace the affected unit's trigger circuit, correct controller timing settings, and re-verify the pattern with a follow-up flash sequence check.
Erratic or Irregular Flash Rate
Likely Cause
Supply voltage fluctuations or an unstable CCR intensity step feeding the circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor supply voltage stability at the CCR; cycle through each intensity step and observe flash consistency; check for loading imbalance across the series circuit.
Corrective Action
Stabilise or correct the power supply, adjust the CCR intensity step, and re-confirm consistent flash timing across a full cycle.
Reduced or Uneven Intensity
Likely Cause
Bird droppings, rubber deposits, or de-icing chemical residue on the lens is blocking or scattering the high-intensity flash output.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for contamination; run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds to quantify the loss.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents, and re-verify intensity with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Leaning or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Wind loading or wildlife strike has knocked an elevated frangible-pole unit off its designed aim.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture aim against tolerance using a theodolite; inspect the frangible coupling and pole base for cracking, corrosion, or play.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-aim the fixture, and replace the frangible coupling or pole hardware if fatigued or damaged.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Flashtube / Lamp Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cumulative discharge stress from repeated high-energy flashing, moisture ingress through a compromised seal, or ordinary end-of-life wear.
Effect
A single unit stops flashing while the rest of the row continues, breaking the pattern's continuity and reducing overall conspicuity.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, flash pattern verification across the full row, and capacitor charge/trigger voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Discharge Capacitor Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Repeated charge/discharge cycling over the fixture's service life, or thermal stress accelerating capacitor wear.
Effect
Weak, delayed, or missed flashes at the affected unit as the capacitor fails to reach full charge between trigger pulses.
Detection Method
Capacitor charge testing at the unit, and comparison of flash intensity and timing against a known-good fixture in the row.
Flasher Control Unit / Trigger Circuit Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal fault in the dedicated flasher control module, or loss of the trigger signal distributed along the row's separate control cable run.
Effect
Entire row stops flashing or loses synchronisation, even though the fixtures remain electrically lit at the commanded intensity step from the CCR loop.
Detection Method
Flasher controller fault-code check, trigger signal verification at the first and last units in the row, and flash-sequence testing.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point on the brightness layer's series loop; darkens the single unit's steady output while the flasher's own trigger circuit may remain unaffected.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Lens Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Bird droppings, rubber deposits, and de-icing chemical residue accumulating on the outer lens surface.
Effect
Reduced or uneven flash intensity that undermines the conspicuity advantage the flashing pattern is meant to provide, particularly in fog or rain.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing against ICAO/FAA thresholds, and visual lens inspection during routine cleaning.
Power Supply Instability
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Voltage fluctuation on the incoming supply feed, or a CCR fault affecting the brightness layer that shares the same fixture.
Effect
Erratic or irregular flash rate as the flasher's timing circuit responds to an unstable power source.
Detection Method
Supply voltage monitoring at the CCR, and flash consistency observation while cycling through each intensity step.
Fixture Misalignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wind loading or a wildlife strike knocking an elevated, frangible-pole unit off its designed aim.
Effect
The unit's flash is directed away from the approach path, reducing its effective conspicuity to an inbound pilot.
Detection Method
Periodic alignment check using a theodolite, and visual inspection of the frangible coupling and pole base for cracking or play.
Trigger Cable / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage, corroded splices, or moisture ingress in the separate control cable run feeding the flasher head.
Effect
One or more units drift out of the simultaneous or alternating flash sequence, or lose the trigger signal entirely.
Detection Method
Trigger cable continuity and insulation resistance testing, and flash pattern verification across the affected segment.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Separate Flasher Control Circuit
Electrical Safety

Flasher heads are fed from a dedicated flasher control unit rather than the CCR series loop; isolate and lock out at the flasher control unit as well as the series circuit before climbing the pole or handling connectors, since one being dead does not guarantee the other is.

Flash-Tube Capacitor Discharge Hazard
Electrical Safety

The flasher power supply stores energy in high-voltage capacitors that can remain charged after the unit is switched off; follow the manufacturer's discharge procedure and confirm capacitors are bled down before opening the control unit or handling the flash head.

Elevated Pier or Mast Work
Personnel Safety

Flasher heads mount atop approach lighting poles or their own frangible mast; use fall protection appropriate to the mounting height, and follow the pier structure's own access procedure where it is a raised platform over water.

High-Intensity Flash Exposure
Personnel Safety

These units are designed for high conspicuity from a distance; avoid direct close-range viewing of an energised flash head, and warn nearby personnel before re-energising a unit under test.

Approach Surface Coordination
Operational

Work on approach-line flasher units or threshold REIL-style units requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM for the affected lights, since they sit within the approach protected surface or near the runway threshold.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a unit dark or with a weakened, mistimed flash after maintenance; verify flash rate and intensity against the parent system before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Confirm Flash Rate After Any Controller Change
Configuration

Flash rate for FAL units is normally set at the controller rather than the fixture; after any control-cabinet or firmware update, verify the actual observed rate at the fixture rather than assuming the setpoint applied — a rate outside the certified band changes how pilots perceive the cue.

Trigger Transformer Wear Is a Common Root Cause
Electrical

For xenon-based flashing units, the high-voltage trigger transformer that ignites the flash tube degrades well before the tube itself typically fails; when a unit flashes intermittently rather than not at all, check trigger transformer output before condemning the flash tube.

Manage Glare Complaints From Nearby Residents
Operational

High-intensity flashing units near the approach path are a frequent source of light-nuisance complaints from residents under the extended centreline; log complaint locations and consider certified intensity step-down procedures for night operations rather than treating every complaint as unrelated to the last.

Distinguish a Wiring Fault From a Tube Failure
Troubleshooting

A completely dark FAL unit is as likely to be a feeder or connector fault as a failed flash tube, especially on units furthest from the control cabinet; check circuit continuity to the unit before pulling and replacing the flash tube assembly.

Verify Flash Rate Is Actually Uniform Across the Run
Inspection

Individual FAL units can drift to slightly different flash rates over time even when driven from a common controller, especially where local control modules have aged unevenly; check rate consistency across the full run rather than assuming a shared controller guarantees a shared rate.

Capacitor Bank Life Is Planned Replacement, Not a Failure Mode
Maintenance

The capacitor bank driving a xenon flash unit has a known service life that shortens with flash count and ambient temperature; track cumulative flash cycles and plan capacitor replacement on that basis rather than waiting for an in-service failure.
