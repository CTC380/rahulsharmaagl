Heat Pipe

A high-efficiency passive thermal management device that transfers heat away from high-power LED modules and electronics in AGL fixtures. Using a two-phase cooling cycle, heat pipes maintain optimal junction temperatures, extending LED life and ensuring consistent light output even in demanding airfield environments.

Overview

What Is It?

A Heat Pipe is a passive thermal management component that efficiently transfers heat from a heat source (e.g., an LED module) to a heat sink or ambient environment using the principles of evaporation and condensation. In AGL applications, heat pipes are critical for:

  • Maintaining LED junction temperatures well below its maximum rating — typically 125–150°C for the device, though designed to a much lower working target, because lumen maintenance and colour stability degrade long before the absolute limit is reached.
  • Preventing thermal runaway and premature failure of LED drivers and power electronics.
  • Enabling higher power LED operation in compact fixtures without overheating.
  • Eliminating active cooling components (fans) that have moving parts and are prone to failure.
  • Ensuring consistent photometric performance across the ambient range airfield fixtures are qualified to, normally –40°C to +55°C. The relevant acceptance test is at fixture level, since the standards specify light output at temperature rather than heat pipe performance in isolation.

Heat pipes are used in high-intensity runway edge lights, threshold lights, approach lighting systems, and PAPI units where thermal management is critical for reliability.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The heat pipe's purpose is to move heat away from an LED module or power electronics far more effectively than a solid metal conductor could in the same space, which matters specifically in sealed, high-intensity fixtures where convection cooling is limited by the IP67/IP68 enclosure and there is no room for a fan. By keeping the LED junction temperature below its rated limit even in a compact, fully sealed housing, the heat pipe is what allows a fixture to run at the high output levels modern runway and approach lighting requires without sacrificing service life or drifting in colour — the same reliability problem a heat sink alone addresses, but solved for cases where a heat sink's direct conduction path isn't enough on its own.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Heat pipes are deployed in AGL fixtures where high-power LEDs or electronics generate significant heat and require efficient removal:

  • Runway Edge Lights: High-intensity LED edge lights (10,000+ cd) use heat pipes to manage thermal loads.
  • Runway Centreline and Threshold Lights: Inset fixtures with high LED densities require heat pipes for thermal management.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: ALSF and MALSR barrettes with high-output LEDs.
  • PAPI and VASI Units: Visual approach slope indicators with multiple high-power LED modules.
  • LED Drivers and Power Supplies: Integrated heat pipes in driver enclosures to manage power electronics heat.
  • Smart Fixtures and Sensors: Dense electronic assemblies benefit from heat pipe cooling.

They are especially critical in sealed IP67/IP68 fixtures where convection cooling is limited.

Placement

Installation & Location

The heat pipe is fitted inside the fixture housing between the LED PCB (or module) and the housing's heat sink or outer casing, clamped or soldered at both ends with thermal paste or pad to ensure full contact at each interface, since any air gap at either end defeats the pipe's heat-transfer capability. Orientation is set by the fixture's internal geometry rather than a fixed rule, though the evaporator end (at the LED) is kept lower than or level with the condenser end (at the heat sink) wherever the fixture's mounting orientation allows, since gravity assists the working fluid's return in that configuration. Being a sealed passive component, no cabling or field wiring is involved — installation is purely thermal (clamping, paste, and contact pressure) and is verified by a thermal check rather than an electrical test.

Function

How Does It Work?

A heat pipe operates on a two-phase heat transfer cycle using a working fluid sealed inside a hollow, evacuated tube. The process involves the following steps:

  • Evaporation: The heat pipe's evaporator section is placed in contact with the heat source (LED module). The heat causes the working fluid (typically water, acetone, or methanol) to boil and vaporize at a low saturation temperature due to the internal vacuum.
  • Vapor Transport: The vapor flows along the hollow core of the pipe (at near sonic speed) to the cooler condenser section, driven by the pressure difference.
  • Condensation: At the condenser section, the vapor releases its latent heat to the heat sink or ambient air, condensing back into liquid form.
  • Liquid Return: The condensed liquid returns to the evaporator section through a capillary wick structure lining the inner wall of the pipe (by capillary action), completing the cycle.

This passive, self-sustaining cycle can transfer heat hundreds of times more efficiently than a solid copper rod of the same dimensions. A typical heat pipe used in AGL fixtures can transfer 20-200 watts of heat with a temperature difference of less than 5°C between the evaporator and condenser.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The heat pipe has no control logic and is not part of the fixture's electrical or command architecture in any way — it carries no signal, reports no state, and cannot be commanded or adjusted by the CCR, a driver, or the ALCMS. Its place in the system is purely thermal and entirely passive: it sits between the LED module and the fixture's heat sink as a fixed-capacity thermal conduit, continuously carrying away whatever heat the LED array happens to be generating at the moment, in direct proportion to the current the CCR or constant current module is delivering. This creates an indirect but real link back to the electrical control chain — every time the ALCMS steps a loop up to a higher current for lower-visibility conditions, the LED array dissipates more heat, and the heat pipe's condensation/evaporation cycle simply runs faster to keep pace, with no command, delay, or decision involved on its part. Because the cycle is self-regulating by physics rather than by control logic (a hotter evaporator drives faster boiling and vapor transport, which increases heat rejection at the condenser until the system reaches a new equilibrium), the heat pipe imposes no thermal control decisions on the rest of the architecture; it simply keeps the LED junction temperature within its safe range across the fixture's full range of CCR-commanded intensity steps, up to its rated heat-transfer capacity.

Construction

Physical Construction

The pipe is a hermetically sealed copper tube, typically 4–10mm in diameter and 50–500mm long, with a wick structure — sintered copper powder, machined grooves, or a wire mesh — lining the inner wall to draw condensed fluid back to the evaporator by capillary action. A small charge of working fluid (water, acetone, methanol, or ethanol depending on the target temperature range) is vacuum-sealed inside before the tube is crimped and welded shut, since any air ingress would collapse the two-phase cycle the pipe depends on. The outer copper surface is often nickel- or chrome-plated for corrosion resistance, and the evaporator end presses directly against the LED or driver's hot spot while the condenser end contacts a finned heatsink or the fixture housing itself, with orientation (gravity-assisted vs. gravity-independent) dictated by which wick structure was used.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Wick Dry-out at High Heat Flux

If the heat flux exceeds the capillary limit of the wick structure, the liquid cannot return to the evaporator fast enough, causing dry-out and a rapid temperature spike. Proper heat pipe sizing and selection (using sintered wick for high-power applications) is essential.

Non-Condensable Gas (NCG) Accumulation

Over time, small amounts of gas (hydrogen or air) can permeate through the seals, forming bubbles that block the condenser and reduce heat transfer efficiency. This is often detected by a hot evaporator and cold condenser. High-quality hermetic seals are critical.

Mechanical Damage

Heat pipes are relatively fragile and can be damaged by bending, denting, or excessive vibration. Proper mounting with strain relief and vibration isolation is essential in airfield applications where fixtures are subject to aircraft-induced vibration.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Rapid Temperature Spike at the Evaporator
Likely Cause
Heat flux has exceeded the capillary limit of the wick structure, causing dry-out.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor evaporator temperature against expected operating range under actual load; verify the heat pipe's rated capacity against the fixture's actual heat flux.
Corrective Action
Fit a correctly sized heat pipe (e.g., sintered wick for higher-power applications) matched to the actual thermal load.
Hot Evaporator With a Cold Condenser
Likely Cause
Non-condensable gas has accumulated inside the pipe, blocking part of the condenser section and reducing heat transfer.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure temperature gradient along the pipe length; a cold section near the condenser end with a hot evaporator is characteristic of NCG blockage.
Corrective Action
Replace the affected heat pipe, since NCG accumulation is generally not field-repairable; source replacements with verified hermetic seal quality.
Heat Pipe Shows Dents, Kinks, or Reduced Performance After Handling
Likely Cause
Mechanical damage from bending, denting, or excessive vibration during installation or fixture servicing.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the pipe for deformation along its length; compare thermal performance to baseline if a spec sheet or commissioning record is available.
Corrective Action
Replace a visibly deformed heat pipe, and ensure proper mounting with strain relief and vibration isolation during reinstallation.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Do Not Puncture, Crush, or Over-Bend
Mechanical

Never puncture, crush, or bend a heat pipe beyond its rated radius; the internal working fluid is sealed under vacuum or pressure and mechanical damage permanently disables the pipe's heat-transfer capability.

Working Fluid Hazard Awareness
Personnel Safety

Treat a ruptured or leaking heat pipe as a hazardous fluid release; avoid direct contact with the internal working fluid and follow the manufacturer's safety data sheet for cleanup and disposal.

Thermal Burn Precaution
Personnel Safety

Allow the heat pipe and the LED module or heat sink it serves to cool before handling, since the evaporator end can remain hot for some time after the fixture is de-energised.

Orientation Sensitivity During Installation
Mechanical

Install the heat pipe in the orientation specified by the manufacturer; many designs rely on gravity or wick capillary action that is orientation-dependent, and an incorrect orientation can silently reduce cooling performance.

Disposal / Recycling Compliance
Operational

Dispose of a failed or removed heat pipe through a route that accounts for its sealed working fluid, following local hazardous-material disposal requirements rather than general waste.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Monitor LED Thermal Trends as an Indirect Health Check
Diagnostics

A slow micro-leak in a heat pipe's sealed working fluid charge shows no external symptom until LED junction temperatures start creeping up, and there's no direct way to inspect the sealed charge in the field; track LED thermal performance trends over time as the practical way to catch a degrading heat pipe.

Verify As-Installed Orientation Matches the Design Spec
Installation

Some heat pipe designs depend on mounting orientation relative to gravity for the wick to properly return condensed fluid to the heat source; confirm the as-installed orientation matches the manufacturer's specification, especially after any fixture repositioning or retrofit.

Check Thermal Interface Contact as a Distinct Item
Maintenance

A heat pipe can be functioning perfectly while poor thermal contact, from inadequate thermal paste, corrosion, or a loose mechanical joint, at the LED or heat sink interface bottlenecks the whole thermal path; verify interface quality separately from the heat pipe's own condition rather than assuming a thermal fault means the pipe itself has failed.

Treat Visible Deformation as Grounds for Replacement
Handling

A heat pipe that looks only cosmetically dented from mishandling during fixture servicing can have reduced thermal capacity from internal vapour-flow restriction that isn't visible externally; handle it with the same care as a sealed pressurised component, and replace rather than reuse a pipe with visible kinks or dents.

Reassess Heat Pipe Capacity Before an LED Upgrade
Retrofit

Swapping in a higher-output LED module during a retrofit without reassessing the existing heat pipe's thermal transfer capacity can push it beyond its rated performance; the resulting premature LED degradation can look like an LED quality issue when the real cause is a thermal design mismatch.

Inspect the Sealed End Crimps for Slow Corrosion
Durability

The crimped, sealed ends of a heat pipe are typically the most vulnerable point to long-term corrosion in humid or coastal environments; inspect these end seals specifically during overhaul rather than judging the pipe's condition from its main body alone.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about heat pipes in AGL LED fixtures: how they work, materials, capacity, orientation, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a heat pipe?

A heat pipe is a passive thermal management component that moves heat from a source, such as an LED module, to a heat sink or the ambient environment using evaporation and condensation of a working fluid. It has no moving parts.

Q2Why do AGL LED fixtures use heat pipes?

LED junction temperature must stay well below its maximum rating (typically 125–150 °C), because lumen maintenance and colour stability degrade long before that limit. In sealed IP67/IP68 fixtures with no room for a fan, a heat pipe removes heat far more effectively than a solid metal conductor in the same space, and protects LED drivers and power electronics.

Q3How does a heat pipe work?

The evaporator end at the LED heats a working fluid (typically water, acetone or methanol) that boils at low temperature in the internal vacuum. Vapour flows to the cooler condenser end, condenses and releases its heat, and the liquid returns through the wick to the evaporator to repeat the cycle.

Q4Where are heat pipes used in AGL?

High-intensity runway edge lights (10,000+ cd), inset runway centreline and threshold lights with high LED densities, ALSF and MALSR approach barrettes, PAPI and VASI units with multiple high-power LED modules, and integrated in LED driver and power supply enclosures.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

Heat transfer capacity of 20–200 W, thermal resistance of 0.05–0.3 °C/W, 4–10 mm diameter, 50–500 mm length, copper with nickel or chrome plating and a sintered copper, groove or mesh wick. Life expectancy is 100,000+ hours when hermetically sealed.

Q6Does orientation matter?

It can. Gravity-assisted types need the condenser above the evaporator, while sintered-wick types are gravity-independent. The evaporator end at the LED is kept lower than or level with the condenser where the design requires.

Q7How is a heat pipe installed?

Between the LED PCB or module and the housing's heat sink or casing, clamped or soldered at both ends with thermal paste or pad. Any air gap at either end defeats the pipe's heat-transfer capability.

Q8Which standards apply to heat pipes?

The main references are IPC-7711 (handling and rework), MIL-STD-883 (thermal test methods), the JEDEC JESD51 series, the RoHS Directive, FAA Engineering Brief 67 (LED airfield fixture qualification) and IEC 61827. Heat pipe performance itself is usually verified to manufacturer-specific test methods. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a heat pipe be checked?

Annually: compare hot-end and cold-end temperatures against baseline to catch wick dry-out or non-condensable gas early, inspect for dents or kinks that could impair vapour flow, and confirm good thermal contact at both ends. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is there a rapid temperature spike at the evaporator?

Heat flux has exceeded the wick's capillary limit, so liquid cannot return fast enough and the wick dries out. Verify the pipe's rated capacity against the fixture's actual heat flux and fit a correctly sized pipe, such as a sintered wick for higher-power applications.

Q11Why is the evaporator hot while the condenser stays cold?

Non-condensable gas (hydrogen or air) has built up inside the pipe and is blocking part of the condenser. A cold section near the condenser end with a hot evaporator is characteristic. This generally cannot be repaired in the field, so replace the heat pipe.
