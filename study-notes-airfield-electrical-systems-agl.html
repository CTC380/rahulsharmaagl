AIRFIELD ELECTRICAL SYSTEMS FOR AGL

The lighting fixture is only one part of an Airfield Ground Lighting system. Behind every runway or taxiway light is an electrical system responsible for delivering controlled power to the lighting circuit. One of the most important AGL concepts for electrical and airport engineering students is the series circuit.

1

CONSTANT CURRENT REGULATOR (CCR)

CCR = Constant Current Regulator. It supplies controlled current to the AGL series circuit. FAA currently lists AC 150/5345-10H as the specification for constant-current regulators and regulator monitors used with airport lighting circuits.

Basic function

  • Supplies the series circuit
  • Controls circuit current
  • Provides different lighting intensity levels in applicable systems (typically 3 or 5 brightness steps)
  • Interfaces with airport lighting control arrangements
Typical FAA values (check the applicable specification)
  • Class 1 = 6.6 A output; Class 2 = 20 A output (for specific applications)
  • Style 1 (3 brightness steps), Class 1: 4.8 / 5.5 / 6.6 A
  • Style 2 (5 brightness steps), Class 1: 2.8 / 3.4 / 4.1 / 5.2 / 6.6 A
  • Designation: L-828 = regulator without monitoring; L-829 = regulator with monitoring

More detail: Study Note 07: Constant Current Regulators.

Why is a CCR used in AGL?

Because the series-circuit lighting system is designed to operate with controlled constant current, so every light in the circuit receives the same current.

What are the typical FAA CCR output currents?

Maximum 6.6 A. A 3-step CCR gives 4.8, 5.5 and 6.6 A; a 5-step CCR gives 2.8, 3.4, 4.1, 5.2 and 6.6 A.

2

SERIES CIRCUIT

In a series AGL circuit, the lighting units are connected as part of a continuous circuit supplied by the CCR. A simplified circuit:

CCR out ─► [Transformer 1] ─► [Transformer 2] ─► [Transformer 3] ─► … ─► [Transformer N] ─┐
  ▲               │                  │                  │                       │         │
  │            Light 1            Light 2            Light 3                 Light N      │
  │                                                                                       │
  └───────────────────────  return to CCR: the loop is continuous  ───────────────────────┘

The circuit is a single continuous loop: the same current leaves the CCR, passes through every isolating transformer primary in turn and returns to the CCR. It is not a set of branches. The actual installation uses primary cabling and associated connectors to form the loop.

Why a series circuit?

  • The same current flows through every light, so brightness stays uniform regardless of cable length or the number of lights
  • One CCR controls the intensity of the whole circuit
  • A failed lamp does not open the loop, because each light is fed through an isolating transformer
Never open an energised series circuit. The CCR tries to keep the current constant, so opening the loop can produce a dangerously high voltage. Isolate the CCR and follow your lockout and permit-to-work procedure before breaking the primary circuit at any cable or connector.
Why is a series circuit used for airfield lighting?

The same current flows through every light, so brightness stays uniform regardless of cable length, and the circuit is controlled by one CCR.

Why must an energised series circuit never be opened?

The CCR tries to keep the current constant, so opening the loop can produce a dangerously high voltage. Isolate the CCR and follow lockout and permit-to-work procedure first.

3

ISOLATING TRANSFORMER

An AGL isolating transformer provides electrical isolation between the primary series circuit and the individual light fixture. FAA AC 150/5345-47C specifically covers series-to-series isolation transformers for airport lighting systems.

Why is it important?

  • Electrically isolates the fixture from the primary series circuit
  • Transfers power to the secondary side
  • Allows individual fixtures to connect to the series circuit
  • Forms a key interface between the primary circuit and light fixture
  • Keeps the primary loop closed if a lamp fails, so one dead lamp does not put out the whole circuit
Designations and ratings
  • L-830 = 60 Hz series; L-831 = 50 Hz series (check which applies to your supply frequency)
  • Typical series-circuit rating: 6.6 A primary, with different wattage sizes for different fixtures

More detail: Study Note 08: AGL Isolating Transformers.

Which FAA isolating transformer designation is for 50 Hz systems?

L-831 is the 50 Hz series; L-830 is the 60 Hz series. Check the project specification for the applicable frequency.

4

PRIMARY CABLE

The primary cable forms part of the high-voltage series circuit. FAA's current advisory circular listing identifies AC 150/5345-7F as the specification for L-824 underground electrical cable for airport lighting circuits. L-824 cable is available in 5,000 V and 600 V ratings; series-circuit (primary) cable is the 5,000 V type. The primary circuit can be studied as:

5

PRIMARY CONNECTOR

Primary connectors join lengths of series-circuit cable and connect the cable to the isolating transformer primary and other equipment. In FAA systems these are normally L-823 connectors (see the next section).

Connection quality is important because a connector problem can appear as a circuit or fixture fault.
6

L-823 PLUG & RECEPTACLE

L-823 connectors are used in airport lighting systems for connections involving underground power cables, isolation transformer leads and light fixture leads. AC 150/5345-26E is the current FAA specification for L-823 plug and receptacle cable connectors. L-823 covers both the primary (series-circuit) connectors and the secondary (fixture-lead) connectors.

Exam point
  • L-823 = Airport Lighting Cable Connector

More detail: Study Note 10: L-823 Connectors.

Putting It Together

HOW AN AGL CIRCUIT WORKS

Suppose a runway contains many lights. Instead of supplying every fixture independently from a conventional low-voltage distribution board, the AGL system can use a series circuit supplied by a CCR.

  • The CCR controls the circuit current
  • Each fixture is connected through an isolating transformer
  • The fixture receives the appropriate secondary-side supply
  • The series circuit is isolated from earth by design, so insulation resistance to earth is an important health indicator

This makes the relationship between CCR + Cable + Transformer + Connector + Fixture fundamental to AGL troubleshooting.

Power Supply

SECONDARY POWER & SWITCH-OVER TIME

The power supply at the top of the electrical chain must be reliable. ICAO Annex 14, Volume I, Chapter 8 requires a secondary power supply for precision approach runways (and, with limits, for non-precision runways), arranged to connect automatically if the primary source fails. The key figure is the maximum switch-over time: how long the light output may be interrupted during the change.

Runway typeMaximum switch-over time
Non-precision approach15 seconds for the listed lighting aids
Precision approach CAT I15 seconds for the listed lighting aids
Precision approach CAT II/III1 second for key lights (such as the inner 300 m of the approach lighting, runway centreline, touchdown zone and stop bars); 15 seconds for the other listed lights
Why it matters: a 15-second change-over can be met by a standby generator that starts and stabilises. A 1-second requirement normally needs an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) arrangement. Which lights need which time is set out in Annex 14 Table 8-1; always check the current edition and your aerodrome category.
What switch-over times does ICAO Annex 14 set for secondary power?

The maximum is 15 s for most lights on CAT I runways. For CAT II/III, key lights such as the inner 300 m of the approach lighting, runway centreline, touchdown zone and stop bars need 1 s. Confirm in Annex 14 Table 8-1.

Troubleshooting

AGL FAULT-FINDING LOGIC

Safety first. Treat every primary circuit as hazardous. Before opening any connector, cable or transformer, isolate at the CCR, lock out and tag out, prove the circuit dead and earth it as required by your organisation's procedures and permit to work.

When an AGL fault occurs, avoid immediately replacing the light. Ask:

  1. Is the CCR operating correctly?
  2. Is the series circuit energised?
  3. Is the primary circuit continuous, and is its insulation resistance to earth acceptable?
  4. Are the connectors satisfactory?
  5. Is the isolating transformer healthy?
  6. Is the secondary connection satisfactory?
  7. Is the light fixture working?

This creates a logical troubleshooting sequence.

Example fault: one runway light not operating

Possible causes

  • Fixture fault
  • Secondary connector problem
  • Transformer fault
  • Primary connector problem
  • Local cable issue

Do not assume the lamp or LED module is automatically the fault.

Example fault: several lights in a series circuit not operating

Consider

  • CCR output
  • Primary circuit
  • Cable continuity
  • Connector condition
  • Multiple component failures

The more fixtures affected, the more important it becomes to investigate the common part of the system.

Whole circuit or part of it?
  • Whole circuit dark: suspect the CCR (including its protection) or an open primary loop
  • Only some lights out: the loop is intact, so the fault is local to those fixtures, transformers or connectors
If the whole circuit is dark, where should you look first?

At the CCR (including its protection) and the primary loop for an open circuit. If only some lights are out, the loop is intact and the fault is local to those fixtures.
