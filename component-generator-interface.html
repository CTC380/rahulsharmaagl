Generator Interface

A control and power transfer system that automatically manages the connection between backup generators and AGL power distribution, ensuring seamless transition during mains power failure to maintain continuous airfield lighting operations.

Overview

What Is It?

A Generator Interface (also known as an Automatic Transfer Switch (ATS) controller or generator control panel) is a critical system that orchestrates the connection between emergency backup generators and the AGL power distribution infrastructure. It continuously monitors the status of the utility mains supply, and upon detecting a failure or unacceptable voltage/frequency deviation, it initiates a controlled transfer of the AGL load to the generator.

The interface includes a combination of power switching devices (typically motorised circuit breakers or contactors), control logic (PLC or dedicated ATS controller), and communication interfaces to coordinate with the UPS, generator, and ALCMS. It ensures that the transfer is seamless, synchronised (when applicable), and prioritises critical loads to maintain runway, taxiway, and approach lighting without interruption.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The generator interface exists because a backup generator is only useful if the transfer to it happens automatically, quickly, and correctly — a manual switchover process would introduce a delay measured in minutes at exactly the moment runway lighting cannot afford to go dark, during active night or low-visibility operations. Its continuous monitoring of the mains supply exists specifically to detect a failure or unacceptable deviation the instant it happens, rather than waiting for lights to visibly fail before anyone realizes power has been lost. The load-prioritization logic built into the interface reflects a related purpose: if the generator's capacity is limited, the interface ensures runway, taxiway, and approach lighting are restored first, ahead of less safety-critical loads, so the most operationally essential systems come back first even if everything can't be restored simultaneously.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Generator Interfaces are installed in central electrical rooms, equipment shelters, or dedicated AGL power buildings. They are deployed in:

  • Main AGL Power Centers: Managing the transfer of power to all CCRs, UPS units, and other critical loads during mains failure.
  • Remote Equipment Huts: Smaller interfaces for individual CCRs or small groups, often with local generator sets.
  • Integration with UPS Systems: Coordinating with UPS units to provide a seamless transition — the UPS bridges the gap during the ~10-30 second generator start-up and synchronisation period.
  • Load Shedding Systems: Interfaces that can shed non-critical loads (e.g., apron lighting, floodlights) during generator operation to conserve fuel and prioritise runway lighting.
  • ALCMS Integration: Providing status, alarms, and control capabilities to the central ALCMS for remote monitoring and management.
Placement

Installation & Location

The interface is housed in its own floor-standing cabinet in the main electrical room or AGL power building, positioned between the incoming utility supply, the standby generator feed, and the power distribution panel it serves, so that both source cables and the outgoing feeder can be run with the shortest practical bus connections. Smaller interfaces serving a single remote equipment hut are wall-mounted in an outdoor-rated enclosure close to the local generator set. Control and communication cabling to the generator, the UPS, and the ALCMS is run separately from the power cabling, and the cabinet is bonded to the site earthing system with a rating matched to its current capacity (up to 2000A). Clear access is maintained at the front for breaker operation and at the rear or top for cable entry, per the enclosure's indoor or outdoor protection rating.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Generator Interface operates through a sequence of automated steps to ensure a reliable and safe power transfer:

  • Mains Monitoring: Continuously measures voltage, frequency, and phase of the utility supply. If any parameter falls outside set thresholds (e.g., voltage below 80% or frequency beyond ±5%), the interface initiates the transfer sequence.
  • Generator Start Signal: Sends a start command to the generator set (typically via a dry contact or CAN/Modbus signal). The generator begins its start-up and warm-up cycle.
  • Generator Stabilisation: Once the generator reaches stable voltage and frequency, the interface verifies that the generator output is within acceptable limits before proceeding.
  • Mains Opening: Opens the main circuit breaker or contactor connecting the AGL load to the utility supply.
  • Generator Closing: Closes the generator circuit breaker or contactor, transferring the load to the generator. In systems with UPS, this transfer is typically "make-before-break" to avoid any power interruption to sensitive AGL equipment.
  • Return Transfer: When mains power is restored and stable, the interface automatically reverses the process, transferring the load back to the utility and shutting down the generator after a cool-down period.
  • Communication: Continuously reports status (mains available, generator running, load on generator, fault conditions) to the ALCMS via Modbus, SNMP, or Ethernet/IP.

Construction

Physical Construction

The Generator Interface is typically housed in a floor-standing or wall-mounted steel switchgear enclosure, sized to accommodate the motorised transfer switch or contactors alongside the control section. The power switching devices — automatic transfer switch (ATS) contactors or motorised circuit breakers — occupy the main compartment, interlocked mechanically and electrically to prevent simultaneous connection of the mains and generator supplies. A separate control compartment houses the PLC or dedicated ATS controller, voltage/frequency sensing relays, and an operator interface (indicator lamps, mimic diagram, or small HMI) on the enclosure's front door. Cable entry for the incoming mains, generator, and outgoing load connections is via glanded plates at the top or base, with heavy-duty busbars or cable lugs sized for the AGL system's full load current. Communication wiring to the UPS, generator control panel, and ALCMS terminates at a dedicated terminal strip, and the enclosure is rated for its installation location, whether an indoor switchroom or an outdoor generator-shelter environment.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Contact Wear on Transfer Breakers

Frequent power transfers (especially in areas with unstable mains) cause contact wear on motorised circuit breakers or contactors. Regular inspection and periodic replacement of contacts or entire switching devices is essential to prevent failure during critical transfer events.

Communication Loss with Generator Controller

Interfaces that rely on digital communication (e.g., Modbus) to start/stop generators can experience protocol mismatches or network interruptions. Installing redundant control signals (e.g., dry contact backup) and regular testing of communication links is recommended.

Improper Synchronisation Settings

In closed transition (make-before-break) systems, incorrect phase synchronisation can cause severe mechanical stress and electrical disturbances. Regular calibration of synchronisation parameters and verification of generator governor/AVR response is critical.

Load Shedding Logic Failures

When operating on generator power, load shedding logic must correctly prioritise critical AGL loads. If the shedding logic fails or is incorrectly configured, the generator may trip on overload. Regular testing and validation of load prioritisation schemes is essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Failure to Transfer to Generator Power
Likely Cause
Contact wear on the motorised transfer circuit breaker or contactor, accelerated by frequent transfers on unstable mains.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect transfer breaker/contactor contacts for pitting or wear; command a manual transfer test and observe operation.
Corrective Action
Replace worn contacts or the switching device, and re-verify transfer operation under a live test before relying on it again.
Loss of Communication with Generator Controller
Likely Cause
A digital communication link (e.g., Modbus) between the interface and generator controller has dropped due to a protocol mismatch or network interruption.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the communication link status and configuration; verify a dry-contact backup signal is present and functional if fitted.
Corrective Action
Restore or reconfigure the communication link, and confirm the dry-contact backup can start/stop the generator independently.
Mechanical Stress or Electrical Disturbance During Transfer
Likely Cause
Incorrect phase synchronisation settings in a closed-transition (make-before-break) system have caused the generator and mains to connect out of phase.
Diagnostic Steps
Review synchronisation parameter settings against commissioning values; verify generator governor/AVR response during a monitored transfer.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate synchronisation parameters and confirm the generator's governor/AVR response is within tolerance before returning to closed-transition operation.
Generator Trips on Overload During Transfer
Likely Cause
Load shedding logic has failed or is misconfigured, so non-critical loads were not shed and the generator was overloaded when it picked up the full load.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the load shedding sequence and priority configuration against the site's actual load list; test the shedding logic under a simulated transfer.
Corrective Action
Correct the load shedding configuration to properly prioritise critical AGL loads, and re-test a full transfer to confirm the generator stays within capacity.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Automatic Transfer Switch (ATS) Contactor Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Contact wear or welding from repeated transfer cycles, or a mechanical linkage fault that prevents full contactor travel.
Effect
Failure to transfer to generator power during a utility outage, leaving the AGL circuit without backup supply.
Detection Method
Scheduled functional transfer test under load, and physical inspection of contactor contacts and linkage during maintenance.
Loss of Communication with Generator Controller
High Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
Interface cannot confirm generator readiness or coordinate transfer timing, risking a delayed or failed transfer.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check between the interface and generator controller, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Transfer Timing / Synchronisation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
A misconfigured transfer delay, drifting relay timing, or a sensing circuit that misreads utility or generator voltage phase.
Effect
Mechanical stress on contactors or an electrical disturbance to connected loads during the transfer event.
Detection Method
Timed transfer test with waveform capture to verify the transfer sequence occurs within the specified window.
Control Relay / Logic Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Relay contact wear from repeated cycling, or a control board component failure affecting the transfer logic.
Effect
Erratic or unreliable transfer behaviour, including missed transfers or unwanted transfers back to utility power.
Detection Method
Functional test of the control logic sequence and inspection of relay contacts during scheduled maintenance.
Surge Damage During Transfer
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The switching transient inherent in a make-before-break or break-before-make transfer, worsened by a marginal surge suppression device.
Effect
Damage to the interface's control electronics or to connected downstream equipment sensitive to the transfer transient.
Detection Method
Post-event functional check, and inspection of onboard surge protection devices for visible damage after any transfer.
Enclosure / Environmental Ingress
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set or a cable gland that no longer seals after repeated access for maintenance.
Effect
Corrosion on internal contacts and circuit boards, leading to intermittent operation over time.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of gaskets and seals during scheduled maintenance, and internal humidity check where indicators are fitted.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Dual-Source Isolation
Electrical Safety

The interface switches between two live sources — utility mains and the standby generator; before internal work, isolate and lock out both sides independently and verify zero energy on each, since isolating only one source leaves the other able to back-feed the panel.

Disable Auto-Start Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

Place the generator in manual/off mode or otherwise disable automatic start before working on the interface or the generator itself, since a simulated mains-failure condition can start the generator and initiate a transfer unexpectedly.

Transfer Switch Arc-Flash Risk
Electrical Safety

Motorised breakers or contactors within the ATS can switch substantial load current during a transfer; follow the site's arc-flash procedure and stand clear of the switching mechanism during any manual transfer test.

Generator Exhaust and Fuel Hazards
Personnel Safety

Ensure adequate ventilation and exhaust routing before running the generator for a transfer test, and follow fuel-handling precautions during refuelling or fuel-system maintenance.

Rotating Machinery and Hot Surfaces
Personnel Safety

Keep clear of the generator's rotating shaft, belts, and cooling fan during operation, and allow the engine, exhaust, and alternator to cool before close-contact maintenance.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Testing a transfer with AGL load connected interrupts or briefly dims the circuits it feeds; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before conducting a live transfer test.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm automatic transfer and re-transfer both operate correctly, within the specified time, before closing out the work and returning the interface to automatic mode.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Transfer Time Measurement Beats a Simple Pass/Fail Test
Testing

An interface that successfully transfers the AGL load to the generator can still be drifting toward a slower transfer time than originally commissioned, degrading over years as contactors and control relays age, without ever actually failing a basic transfer test. Measure and log actual transfer time at each test rather than recording only a binary pass/fail result.

Interlock Verification Deserves Its Own Dedicated Check
Safety

The mechanical and electrical interlocks that prevent the generator and mains supply from ever being connected simultaneously are the single most safety-critical function of this equipment, and a general functional test can pass while a specific interlock has quietly failed. Test interlock function explicitly and separately from the overall transfer sequence, not as an assumed side-effect of a successful transfer test.

A Full Transfer Test Is Not the Same as a Load Bank Test
Reliability

A generator that starts and picks up the load successfully at low demand can still fail to hold voltage and frequency under the AGL system's actual peak load, a shortfall that only a genuine load bank test at rated capacity reveals. Run the load bank test at realistic peak demand periodically rather than relying on routine low-load transfer tests alone.

Retransfer and Cooldown Logic Is as Important as the Initial Transfer
Design

A generator interface that transfers load away from the mains cleanly can still cause a nuisance retransfer if mains restoration is detected too quickly, before it's actually stable, bouncing the AGL load back and forth. Verify retransfer delay and cooldown timing are configured to avoid premature switchback on a momentarily restored but unstable mains supply.

Contact Wear on Transfer Breakers Accelerates With Transfer Frequency
Maintenance

A site with frequent mains interruptions puts far more cumulative wear on the transfer breaker contacts than the standard inspection interval assumes, since arc erosion accumulates with the number of transfers rather than purely with calendar time. Track actual transfer count where practical and adjust contact inspection frequency to match rather than a fixed calendar-based schedule.

Communication Link Failure Shouldn't Be Allowed to Block a Mains-Fail Transfer
Design

Where the interface reports status to the ALCMS over a comms link, confirm that a loss of that communication link cannot itself prevent or delay the core mains-fail transfer function, since the primary safety function has to remain independent of the monitoring layer's health.
