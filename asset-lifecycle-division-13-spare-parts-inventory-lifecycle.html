Spare Parts & Inventory Lifecycle

The supply-readiness layer of the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide. Division 13 exists to ensure that the right spare part is available, when required, in the correct quantity, in usable condition, traceable to the correct asset, and held at an economically justified lifecycle cost — connecting directly with asset criticality, procurement, maintenance strategy, asset health, reliability engineering and lifecycle cost management.

Initial Issue

The question this division answers — What spares must be held, where, in what quantity, and why — so that critical AGL assets can be restored within the required timeframe, without carrying unnecessary inventory, obsolete stock or excessive lifecycle cost?

SPR-02 Critical-Spare Identification
Purpose

Not every spare part needs to be stocked at the same level. A critical spare is one where non-availability could create significant consequence.

Non-Availability Could Create
  • Safety impact
  • Operational impact
  • Extended downtime
  • Maintenance delay
  • Regulatory concern
  • Restoration difficulty
Assessment Factors
  • Asset criticality
  • Failure probability
  • Lead time
  • Replacement time
  • Availability of alternatives
  • Consequence of stock-out
  • Number of installed assets using the part
Example

A specialized CCR component with a long manufacturer lead time may require strategic stocking even if annual consumption is very low.

SPR-03 Minimum Stock Level
Purpose

The lowest inventory level that should normally be maintained. When stock approaches this level, replenishment action may be required.

Determine Using
  • Consumption
  • Lead time
  • Criticality
  • Safety stock
  • Supplier reliability
  • Failure frequency
Scope Boundary

The minimum level should not simply be selected as an arbitrary number.

SPR-04 Maximum Stock Level
Purpose

Defines the upper quantity normally justified for inventory.

Excessive Stock Creates
  • Capital tied up
  • Storage requirements
  • Obsolescence risk
  • Shelf-life risk
  • Damage risk
  • Unnecessary inventory
Governing Principle
Optimized Availability at Acceptable Lifecycle Cost

The objective is not maximum availability at any cost.

SPR-05 Reorder Level
Purpose

The inventory point at which replenishment should normally be initiated.

Simplified Concept
Reorder Level = Expected Consumption During Lead Time + Safety Stock

Actual calculation should use approved inventory-management assumptions.

Factors
  • Average consumption
  • Maximum consumption
  • Supplier lead time
  • Criticality
  • Supplier reliability
  • Emergency procurement capability
SPR-06 Safety Stock
Purpose

Safety stock provides protection against uncertainty.

Sources of Uncertainty
  • Unexpected failures
  • Demand variation
  • Supplier delays
  • Transportation delays
  • Long lead times
  • Emergency maintenance
  • Forecast error
Critical AGL Assets

Higher-risk assets may justify higher safety-stock levels where the cost and operational consequence of stock-out are significant.

SPR-08 Spare Failure Analysis
Purpose

Spare consumption and asset failure are related but not identical.

A High Consumption Rate May Indicate
  • High asset failure rate
  • Poor component reliability
  • Incorrect maintenance practice
  • Poor installation
  • Environmental stress
  • Incorrect spare selection
Example Chain
SPARE CONSUMPTION INCREASESFAILURE ANALYSISASSET/CIRCUIT INVESTIGATIONROOT CAUSERELIABILITY IMPROVEMENT

If the same transformer type is repeatedly replaced on the same circuit, this pattern should trigger investigation. This connects Division 13 directly with Division 11.

SPR-09 Spare Shelf-Life Management
Purpose

Some spare parts may deteriorate while sitting in storage.

Monitor
  • Manufacturing date
  • Receipt date
  • Shelf-life
  • Expiry date where applicable
  • Storage conditions
  • Packaging integrity
  • Inspection requirements
  • Preservation requirements
Principle

A spare is not considered available simply because it exists physically in the warehouse. It must also be fit for intended use.

SPR-10 Obsolete Spare Management
Purpose

Obsolescence can create a major lifecycle risk.

Identify
  • Discontinued products
  • End-of-support products
  • Manufacturer withdrawal
  • Unavailable components
  • Legacy control systems
  • Proprietary components
  • Outdated technology
Possible Strategies
  • Last-time purchase
  • Strategic stock
  • Approved alternative
  • Interchangeable replacement
  • System upgrade
  • Equipment replacement
Scope Boundary

Obsolescence should be connected with Division 05 vendor management and Division 12 lifecycle-cost planning.

SPR-11 Long-Lead-Time Spares
Purpose

Long-lead-time parts require special strategic consideration.

Assess
  • Supplier lead time
  • Manufacturing time
  • Transportation
  • Import requirements
  • Minimum order quantity
  • Availability of alternatives
  • Failure consequence
Strategy Trigger

A low-consumption part may still require inventory if failure consequence is high, lead time is long, and an alternative is unavailable.

SPR-12 Strategic Spares
Purpose

Strategic spares are parts held because of their importance to long-term operational continuity.

May Be Characterized By
  • Low consumption
  • High criticality
  • High procurement difficulty
  • Long lead time
  • High consequence of non-availability
Examples Could Include
  • Specialized CCR modules
  • Critical control components
  • Proprietary ALCMS components
  • Specialized PAPI components
  • Obsolete/legacy components with no immediate replacement
Scope Boundary

The exact list should be airport-specific.

SPR-13 Emergency Spares
Purpose

Emergency spares support rapid restoration following unexpected failures.

Emergency-Stock Strategy Should Consider
  • Critical assets
  • Failure frequency
  • Response-time requirement
  • Supplier availability
  • Storage location
  • 24/7 accessibility
  • Shelf life
Scope Boundary

Emergency spares should be clearly identifiable so that they are not inadvertently consumed for routine maintenance without replenishment.

SPR-15 Interchangeable Spares
Purpose

Interchangeability can reduce inventory complexity. Where technically approved, one spare may serve multiple asset models or applications.

Benefits
  • Reduced inventory
  • Lower capital tied up
  • Easier logistics
  • Reduced obsolescence
  • Improved availability
Scope Boundary

Interchangeability must be technically verified and formally approved. Do not assume that two physically similar components are electrically, optically, mechanically or functionally interchangeable.

Consider

A cheap spare with a very short shelf life, high storage requirements and high obsolescence risk may have a greater lifecycle cost than a more expensive but stable component.

Most Important Design Principle

Spare-parts management is not simply warehouse management; it is a reliability, risk, maintenance and lifecycle-cost function. Division 13 should remain the supply-readiness layer of the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide — it decides what spares must be held, where, and in what quantity, so that critical assets can be restored within the required timeframe, while avoiding unnecessary inventory, obsolete stock and excessive lifecycle cost. It does not define the maintenance procedure itself — that remains with the AGL Maintenance Handbook.

ASSET CRITICALITY (Division 03)
FAILURE RISK → FAILURE HISTORY → FAILURE RATE / MTBF (Division 11)
SPARE REQUIREMENT → CRITICAL-SPARE ASSESSMENT → MIN / MAX / SAFETY STOCK
PROCUREMENT & STORAGE (Division 05)
SPARE ISSUE → ASSET REPAIR / REPLACEMENT (Maintenance Handbook)
SPARE CONSUMPTION RECORD → RELIABILITY ANALYSIS (Division 11)
FORECAST UPDATE → INVENTORY STRATEGY OPTIMIZATION (Division 12)

This makes Division 13 the natural supply-readiness engine of the Asset Lifecycle Guide — the loop where failure data determines spare demand, and spare consumption in turn reveals hidden reliability problems, feeding back into asset criticality, maintenance strategy and lifecycle-cost planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Spare Parts & Inventory Lifecycle in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 13 — Spare Parts & Inventory Lifecycle in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 13 is the supply-readiness layer. It covers 20 topics on spare-parts strategy, critical-spare identification, min/max/reorder/safety stock, shelf-life, obsolescence, strategic and emergency spares, lifecycle cost, forecasting and traceability.

Q2What is an AGL spare-parts strategy?

It determines what spares should be held, where, in what quantity and why. Inputs include asset criticality, failure rate, lead time, consequence of stock-out, consumption rate and manufacturer support.

Q3How are critical spares identified?

Not every spare needs to be stocked at the same level. A critical spare is one where non-availability could create significant consequence, such as safety or operational impact, extended downtime or maintenance delay.

Q4What are minimum stock, maximum stock, reorder level and safety stock?

Minimum stock is the lowest level normally maintained. Maximum stock is the upper quantity normally justified, limited by capital tied up, storage, obsolescence, shelf-life and damage risk. Reorder level is the point at which replenishment should normally start, and safety stock protects against uncertainty such as unexpected failures, demand variation and supplier delays.

Q5What is the difference between spare consumption analysis and spare failure analysis?

Consumption analysis reviews the history of parts used, by part number, asset, quantity, date and reason. Spare consumption and asset failure are related but not identical, so failure analysis looks for causes such as high asset failure rate, poor component reliability, incorrect maintenance practice or poor installation.

Q6How are shelf life and warehouse conditions managed?

Some spares deteriorate in storage, so manufacturing date, receipt date, shelf-life, expiry date and storage conditions should be tracked. Inventory quality also depends on warehouse conditions such as temperature, humidity, dust, water ingress and corrosion.

Q7What are strategic, emergency and long-lead-time spares?

Strategic spares are held because of their importance to long-term operational continuity, typically with low consumption and high criticality. Emergency spares support rapid restoration after unexpected failures. Long-lead-time parts need special consideration because of supplier lead time, manufacturing, transport and import requirements.

Q8How do obsolescence, interchangeability, forecasting and traceability affect spares?

Obsolete spares, such as discontinued or end-of-support products, create major lifecycle risk. Where technically approved, interchangeable spares can reduce inventory complexity. Forecasting estimates future needs from consumption, asset population, age and failure rate, and every significant spare should be traceable from procurement to failure or replacement history.
