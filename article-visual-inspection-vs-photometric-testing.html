Why Visual Inspection Cannot Replace Photometric Testing in AGL

Visual inspection is necessary. But it is not sufficient. In Airfield Ground Lighting, what looks "okay" on the ground can still be unsafe for a pilot in the air.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

May 2, 2026 Updated October 27, 2026

Visual inspection and photometric testing serve two different purposes. Visual inspection helps identify obvious physical and operational faults, while photometric testing verifies whether the lighting system is actually performing within the required parameters.

The difference is critical because AGL is ultimately designed for the pilot — not for the person inspecting the light from the ground.

Key Takeaway

Safety is not based on appearance. It is based on performance. A fitting can look correct on the ground and still be outside acceptable limits from the cockpit — which is why photometric testing should not be treated as optional.

01

What We See During Ground Inspection

From the airside or maintenance perspective, visual inspection typically checks whether the light is on, any visible damage, physical alignment, and lens cleanliness. These checks matter — they catch obvious failures and physical defects.

  • Whether the light is ON
  • Visible damage such as cracks, water ingress, or broken glass
  • Physical alignment of fittings
  • Cleanliness of lenses

However, visual inspection only tells part of the story. A light being ON does not necessarily mean it is producing the correct intensity, beam direction, colour, or uniformity required for safe aircraft operations.

02

What the Pilot Actually Sees

From the cockpit, the evaluation is completely different. A pilot depends on the visual information provided by the AGL system to support alignment, landing, taxiing, and decision-making.

  • Correct beam direction
  • Uniform light intensity across the circuit
  • Consistent colour output, especially for threshold and end lights
  • A continuous visual pattern without dark patches

Even small deviations can affect depth perception, alignment, and decision-making during landing or taxiing. A fitting appearing normal on ground inspection does not guarantee it is providing correct visual guidance from the cockpit.

03

The Problem with Human Vision

Human eyes are adaptive. This creates an important limitation when visual inspection is used as the primary method of judging AGL performance.

  • Dim lights may still appear acceptable in low ambient conditions
  • Intensity variations are often unnoticed
  • Slight beam misalignment is difficult to judge from ground level
  • Gradual degradation can go undetected

As a result, visual inspection can create a false sense of compliance. What looks "fine" during inspection may already be outside acceptable limits — this is why visual appearance cannot substitute for measured photometric performance. Night-time checks of the light pattern, from runway level or from an aircraft's perspective, are valuable for spotting gross problems, but they cannot quantify intensity, beam direction or colour.

04

Where Photometric Testing Becomes Critical

Photometric testing removes subjectivity from the evaluation of AGL performance. It provides measurable information that can be compared directly against the applicable ICAO Annex 14, FAA or national requirements.

  • Measured intensity values in candela
  • Beam spread and direction verification
  • Colour compliance validation
  • Uniformity assessment across fittings

The system is not only checked to determine whether it is working — it is tested to determine whether it is performing correctly. Photometric testing provides objective evidence that normal visual inspection alone cannot obtain.

05

Visual Inspection vs. Photometry: The Real Difference

The two methods have different objectives. One is a condition check; the other is a compliance verification.

06

Why Both Are Required

Relying only on visual inspection is risky. At the same time, relying only on photometry is incomplete. A safe and compliant AGL system requires both approaches, and the combination provides a much stronger level of assurance.

07

The Connection Between Appearance and Performance

AGL systems are visual guidance systems. Their effectiveness depends not simply on whether the fittings are illuminated, but on whether they produce the correct visual information for the pilot. A fitting can appear operational while still having incorrect intensity, incorrect beam direction, non-uniform output, inconsistent colour, or performance degradation that is difficult to see from ground level.

Photometric testing converts visual performance into measurable data, allowing deviations to be identified objectively.

08

Safety Is Based on Performance

The fundamental principle is simple: safety is not based on appearance. It is based on performance.

Visual inspection
Keeps the system running by helping maintenance teams identify obvious problems and faults.
Photometric testing
Ensures the system is performing as required and provides objective validation of its optical characteristics.

Both functions are essential because they address different aspects of AGL reliability and safety.

09

Key Takeaways

  • Visual inspection identifies faults; photometric testing identifies performance deviations
  • A light that is ON is not proof that it is performing correctly
  • Human eyes adapt to ambient conditions, which can mask real intensity and beam problems
  • Photometric testing gives objective, measurable evidence compared against ICAO Annex 14, FAA or national requirements
  • A safe and compliant AGL system needs both visual inspection and photometric testing — neither replaces the other

Conclusion

Visual inspection has an important place in AGL maintenance, but it cannot replace photometric testing. Visual inspection identifies visible faults and checks the physical condition of the lighting system. Photometric testing goes further by objectively measuring intensity, beam characteristics, colour, and uniformity.

The distinction can be summarised simply: visual inspection identifies faults, photometric testing identifies performance deviations. A safe and compliant AGL system therefore requires both.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can visual inspection replace photometric testing in AGL?

No. Visual inspection identifies obvious physical and operational faults, while photometric testing verifies whether the lighting system is actually performing within required parameters. Visual inspection is a condition check and photometry is a compliance verification, and neither should be treated as a replacement for the other.

What does visual inspection of AGL typically check?

It checks whether the light is on, visible damage such as cracks, water ingress or broken glass, the physical alignment of fittings and the cleanliness of lenses. A light being on does not mean it is producing the correct intensity, beam direction, colour or uniformity.

What does the pilot actually see that a ground inspection may miss?

A pilot depends on correct beam direction, uniform intensity across the circuit, consistent colour output (especially for threshold and end lights) and a continuous visual pattern without dark patches. A fitting appearing normal on ground inspection does not guarantee it is providing correct visual guidance from the cockpit.

Why is human vision unreliable for judging AGL performance?

Human eyes adapt, so dim lights may still look acceptable in low ambient conditions, intensity variations often go unnoticed, slight beam misalignment is hard to judge from ground level and gradual degradation can go undetected. This can create a false sense of compliance.

What does photometric testing provide that inspection cannot?

It removes subjectivity by providing measured intensity values in candela, verification of beam spread and direction, colour compliance validation and uniformity assessment, compared directly against ICAO Annex 14, FAA or national requirements. It tests whether the system is performing correctly, not just whether it is working.

Are night-time visual checks of the light pattern useful?

Yes, for spotting gross problems, either from runway level or from an aircraft's perspective. However, they cannot quantify intensity, beam direction or colour.

How often are the two methods used?

Visual inspection is frequent and routine and supports quick fault detection, while photometric testing is periodic and event-driven and supports beam characteristic verification and colour and uniformity validation. A safe and compliant AGL system requires both.
