Heliport Beacon

A specialized rotating beacon system that identifies a heliport from the air, producing a repeating sequence of green, yellow, and white flashes. Provides pilots with a clear visual indication of the helipad location, distinguishing it from land airports (white/green) and water airports (white/yellow).

Overview

What Is It?

A Heliport Beacon is a rotating or flashing light system used to identify a heliport to pilots. It produces a repeating sequence of green, yellow, and white flashes to distinguish it from other airport beacons. The beacon is typically mounted on a mast, control tower, or rooftop, and is visible from a distance of several nautical miles. It serves as the primary visual cue for pilots to locate the helipad during night operations and low‑visibility conditions. The colour sequence and flash pattern are specified by ICAO and FAA to ensure unambiguous identification.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The heliport beacon exists to solve a location problem distinct from a fixed-wing airport's: helipads are frequently small, unlit rooftop or ground-level sites with no runway lighting system to guide a pilot in from distance, so the beacon alone often carries the entire burden of making the site findable at night. Its colour sequence is deliberately chosen to be distinguishable from the standard airport beacon pattern, so a pilot searching visually for a helipad isn't misled toward a nearby fixed-wing airport beacon that happens to be in the same general direction. Because helipads are frequently located in urban or otherwise visually cluttered environments, the beacon's purpose is concentrated on cutting through that clutter with an unambiguous, standardized signal rather than relying on the site's surroundings to make it identifiable.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at all heliports that support night operations or low‑visibility approaches. Used on hospital helipads, offshore platforms, corporate heliports, military helipads, and emergency service heliports. Typically mounted on a mast, control tower, or rooftop at an elevated position to ensure maximum visibility. The beacon is often supplemented by a secondary beacon for larger heliports or where obstructions may block the primary beacon's line of sight.

Placement

Installation & Location

The beacon is mounted on a mast, control building, or rooftop at the highest practical point on or near the heliport, positioned so its light is not blocked by nearby buildings, trees, or other obstructions in any direction a pilot might approach from. Siting takes into account the heliport's approach and departure paths, keeping the beacon and its supporting structure clear of the obstacle-free surfaces defined for the site. Where the primary beacon's line of sight could be blocked from certain directions, a secondary beacon is installed at a location that covers the shadowed sector. Power and control cabling runs back to a nearby controller and photocell, sized for the beacon's duty cycle, with the mast or mounting structure itself designed to withstand the site's wind loading and, for rooftop installations, tied into the building's structural grid rather than a lightweight parapet.

Function

How Does It Work?

The heliport beacon operates similarly to an airport beacon but with a different colour sequence. It uses a rotating assembly with lamps (or LEDs) and coloured filters (green, yellow, and white). The rotation speed is typically 12–15 RPM, producing 30–45 flashes per minute. The light source is a high‑intensity halogen lamp or LED array, with a parabolic reflector to produce a narrow, intense beam. The beacon is powered from the heliport's electrical supply with backup battery or UPS to ensure continuous operation. A photocell or timer controls the on/off cycle. Modern beacons use LED technology for longer life, lower power consumption, and instant‑on capability.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Unlike runway or taxiway fixtures wired into a shared constant-current series circuit, a heliport beacon is a standalone, independently powered unit. It draws directly from the heliport's electrical supply, with a battery or UPS holding it through short outages, and a photocell or timer is the entire control loop — sensing ambient light and switching the beacon on at dusk and off at dawn without input from any wider lighting control system. The rotation itself (or, on LED units, the electronic sequencing that simulates it) is driven by the beacon's own motor or driver circuit, running independently of the colour-flash timing used elsewhere on the airfield. Where a heliport uses paired primary and secondary beacons for line-of-sight redundancy, the two units are not electrically slaved together — each runs its own photocell cycle on its own schedule, and their overlapping coverage comes purely from where they're physically mounted, not from any synchronisation between them. On sites with a remote monitoring system, the beacon reports lamp, rotation, or power status upstream for maintenance visibility, but this is a reporting link only: the beacon keeps cycling on its local photocell logic even if that connection is lost.

Heliport Supply + Battery/UPS → Photocell / Timer → Drive Motor or LED Sequencer → Beacon Output

On larger heliports, a single beacon's sightline can be broken by a nearby structure or the terrain itself. A second beacon, positioned so its arc overlaps the blind spot, ensures the green/yellow/white identification signal stays visible from every approach direction.

Construction

Physical Construction

The beacon housing is a weatherproof aluminium or cast alloy enclosure, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, split into an upper optical head and a lower base assembly that carries the drive motor or electronic switching gear. The rotating optical head houses the LED array or halogen/xenon lamp behind coloured (green/yellow/white) glass or dichroic filters, mounted on a precision bearing assembly that allows smooth, low-friction rotation at the required 12-15 RPM; a weatherproof dome or cover, typically borosilicate glass or UV-stabilised polycarbonate, seals the optical chamber to an IP66/IP67 rating while remaining optically clear. The base is bolted to a mast, rooftop plinth, or control tower structure using a flanged mounting ring and stainless-steel fasteners, with the mast itself engineered to hold the beacon at the elevation needed for the required several-nautical-mile visibility. Power and control cabling enters through a sealed gland at the base, feeding the drive motor, lamp circuitry, and photocell or timer controls, with the assembly connected to a UPS or standby generator circuit so the beacon continues operating through mains interruptions.

Installation Requirements

Mast/Structure Mounting Check
Civil

Confirm the mast, rooftop plinth, or control tower structure is rated for the beacon's weight and wind loading, and holds it at the elevation needed for the required nautical-mile visibility.

Sightline Coverage Check
Civil

On larger heliports with a primary and secondary beacon, confirm each unit's position covers the blind spot created by nearby structures or terrain, so the identification signal stays visible from every approach.

Optical Head Levelling
Mechanical

Set the base flanged mounting ring plumb and level so the rotating optical head tracks true on its bearing assembly at the required 12–15 RPM.

Power & Standby Supply Termination
Electrical

Terminate the 230V AC supply and confirm the UPS or standby generator circuit is wired and set to transfer automatically on mains failure before energisation.

Base Cable Gland Termination
Electrical

Seal the cable gland at the base and terminate power/control wiring to the drive motor, lamp circuitry, and photocell or timer controls per the wiring diagram.

Dome/Cover Seal Check
Mechanical

Confirm the weatherproof dome or cover seats correctly against the optical chamber to achieve the rated IP66/IP67 protection before commissioning.

Colour Sequence Configuration
Configuration

Confirm the correct three-colour filter set (green, yellow, and white per ICAO Annex 14 and the FAA AIM) is fitted for the site before commissioning.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record mounting height, bearing/levelling check, cable termination, and colour sequence configuration as-built before the beacon is closed out.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Colour Filter Degradation

Green, yellow, and white filters are susceptible to UV degradation and heat, causing colour shift or fading. Regular photometric inspection and filter replacement is required to maintain the correct identification sequence.

Obstruction of Line of Sight

Heliport beacons are often installed on rooftops or masts that may be shadowed by nearby buildings or structures. Proper siting and periodic obstruction surveys are essential to ensure 360° visibility.

Vibration from Helicopter Operations

Heliport beacons are exposed to significant vibration from hovering and landing helicopters. This can lead to loose connections and premature bearing failure. Regular inspection and secure mounting with vibration‑damping mounts is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Beacon Completely Dark
Likely Cause
Loss of supply to the beacon, a failed lamp/LED array, or a battery/UPS that has failed to hold the load through a supply interruption.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming supply at the beacon; test the battery/UPS output under a simulated outage; check lamp or LED array continuity.
Corrective Action
Repair the supply feed, replace a failed battery/UPS, or replace the lamp or LED array.
Beacon Fails to Switch On/Off at the Correct Time
Likely Cause
Photocell or timer failure, or a stuck manual override, since the beacon's entire control loop runs independently on this local logic.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the photocell's response by shading and exposing it; check timer settings against local dusk/dawn times; confirm no manual override is engaged.
Corrective Action
Replace the photocell or timer, or correct the settings/override state.
Rotation Stalled or Uneven
Likely Cause
Worn or seized motor bearings, an obstruction in the rotating assembly, or a fault in the beacon's own drive/motor circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for binding or resistance when the assembly is turned by hand; measure motor current against the rated draw; test the driver output.
Corrective Action
Lubricate or replace worn bearings, clear the obstruction, or replace the motor/driver circuit.
Incorrect or Faded Colour Sequence
Likely Cause
UV-degraded or heat-damaged colour filter, or a failed LED module in one colour channel.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric/visual check of each colour segment against the required sequence; inspect filters for fading, cracking, or discolouration.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded colour filter or the failed LED module for that channel.
Reduced Visibility from Certain Angles
Likely Cause
New obstruction from a nearby structure shadowing part of the sweep, a misaligned parabolic reflector, or a dirty lens/dome.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct an obstruction survey around the beacon to confirm 360° visibility; check reflector alignment and lens/dome cleanliness.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens/reflector, realign the beacon, or escalate for removal of a newly shadowing structure.
Loose Connections or Intermittent Operation
Likely Cause
Vibration from helicopter hover and landing operations loosening electrical connections or bearing mounts.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque terminal connections; check bearing play and mounting hardware for looseness.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate loose connections, replace worn bearings, and fit vibration-damping mounts.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Battery / UPS Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
A battery or UPS that fails to hold the load through a supply interruption, or a failed lamp/LED array.
Effect
Beacon goes completely dark, either continuously or specifically during a mains interruption.
Detection Method
Incoming supply checks, battery/UPS output testing under a simulated outage, and lamp/LED array continuity testing.
Photocell / Timer Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Photocell or timer failure, or a stuck manual override on the beacon's independent local control logic.
Effect
Beacon fails to switch on or off at the correct time relative to local dusk/dawn.
Detection Method
Photocell response testing by shading and exposing it, timer-setting verification, and manual-override status checks.
Drive Assembly Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Worn or seized motor bearings, an obstruction in the rotating assembly, or a fault in the beacon's own drive/motor circuit.
Effect
Rotation stalls or becomes uneven, reducing the beacon's effective coverage.
Detection Method
Binding/resistance checks when turning the assembly by hand, motor-current measurement against rated draw, and driver output testing.
Colour Filter / LED Channel Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
UV-degraded or heat-damaged colour filter, or a failed LED module in one colour channel.
Effect
Incorrect or faded colour sequence relative to the beacon's required identification pattern.
Detection Method
Photometric/visual checks of each colour segment against the required sequence, and filter inspection for fading or damage.

Medium Severity
Root Cause
A newly erected structure shadowing part of the sweep, a misaligned parabolic reflector, or a dirty lens/dome.
Effect
Reduced visibility of the beacon from certain approach angles.
Detection Method
An obstruction survey confirming 360° visibility, and reflector-alignment and lens/dome-cleanliness checks.
Vibration-Loosened Connections
Low Severity
Root Cause
Vibration from helicopter hover and landing operations loosening electrical connections or bearing mounts over time.
Effect
Loose connections or intermittent operation that can worsen with continued vibration exposure.
Detection Method
Terminal-connection torque checks, and bearing-play and mounting-hardware inspection.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Elevated Access
Fall Protection

The beacon is typically mounted on a mast, tower, or rooftop; use fall-arrest equipment and the facility's access permit before approaching the unit.

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the beacon supply at the controller before opening the lens housing or drive assembly, and verify zero energy before touching internal connections.

Rotating Assembly Pinch Points
Mechanical

Keep hands and tools clear of the turntable and drive train until it is confirmed stationary and electrically isolated.

Direct Beam Viewing
Personnel Safety

Avoid looking directly into the beam at close range during functional testing; the collimated output can cause temporary glare or discomfort.

Heliport Operations Coordination
Operational

Notify the heliport operator and ATC before any planned outage, since the beacon is the primary night-identification cue for arriving helicopters and its loss affects approach planning.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Wear cut-resistant gloves when handling lens/filter segments, insulated tools for electrical work, and fall-protection PPE appropriate to the mounting height.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm correct flash rate, colour sequence, and photometric output before closing out the work and notifying the heliport operator the beacon is back in service.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Colour Sequence Is the Whole Point of This Fixture
Identification

Because a heliport beacon's green, yellow, and white sequence exists purely to distinguish it from an airport beacon, any colour drift is a safety issue here in a way it wouldn't be for a plain obstruction light. Prioritise colour sequence verification over pure intensity checks when servicing this fixture, since a beacon that's bright but wrongly coloured has failed its actual job.

Line-of-Sight Surveys Need Repeating More Often at Rooftop Heliports
Siting

A heliport beacon mounted on a building rooftop is more exposed to new obstructions from adjacent development than one on a dedicated mast at a full-size airport. Repeat the line-of-sight obstruction survey on a shorter cycle for rooftop and urban heliports than the interval used for airfield beacons.

Primary/Secondary Beacon Overlap Deserves a Second Look After Any Change
Design

Where a secondary beacon supplements the primary at a satellite pad or partially obscured approach, a change to either beacon's mounting or intensity can quietly break the overlap coverage that was verified at commissioning. Re-check overlap whenever one beacon in the pair is serviced or replaced, not just the beacon that was actually worked on.

Rotation Speed and Flash Rate Are Two Separate Checks
Diagnostics

On rotating heliport beacons, a correct rotation RPM doesn't guarantee the resulting flash rate is still within spec if the number of beam segments or filter arrangement has changed during a repair. Verify flash rate as its own reading rather than inferring it from rotation speed alone.

Backup Power Is More Likely to Matter Here Than at a Major Airfield
Reliability

Many heliports run on a single mains feed without the redundant power infrastructure a major airport has, so a beacon's backup supply is genuinely the last line of defence rather than a formality. Treat the backup power test as a priority item on this fixture rather than a routine box-tick.

Rooftop Vibration Sources Differ From a Ground Mast
Mechanical

A rooftop-mounted heliport beacon can pick up sustained vibration from adjacent HVAC plant or lift machinery that a ground-mounted airport beacon never experiences, accelerating bearing and mounting-hardware wear. Factor the specific rooftop vibration environment into the inspection interval rather than applying a generic beacon maintenance schedule.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about heliport beacons: colour sequence, flash rate, siting, power, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a heliport beacon?

A heliport beacon is a rotating or flashing light that identifies a heliport to pilots. It is often the primary visual cue for locating a helipad at night or in low visibility, especially at small rooftop or ground-level sites that have no runway lighting.

Q2What colours does a heliport beacon show?

A repeating sequence of green, yellow and white flashes, chosen to be distinguishable from a standard airport beacon. The colour sequence and flash pattern are specified by ICAO and FAA so the heliport can be identified unambiguously.

Q3What is the flash rate of a heliport beacon?

The rotation speed is typically 12–15 RPM, producing 30–45 flashes per minute, with rotation speed held within ±1% of rated RPM to keep the flash rate consistent.

Q4Where are heliport beacons used?

At heliports that support night or low-visibility operations, including hospital helipads, offshore platforms, corporate heliports, military helipads and emergency service heliports. Larger sites or sites with obstructions may add a secondary beacon.

Q5Where should a heliport beacon be mounted?

On a mast, control building or rooftop at the highest practical point on or near the heliport, so nothing blocks the light from any direction a pilot may approach. Typical mounting height is 10–30 m above ground level, and the beacon and its structure must stay clear of the obstacle-free surfaces defined for the site.

Q6How bright is a heliport beacon?

Effective intensity is up to about 30,000 cd, with peak beam intensity of at least 2,000 cd per colour per ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5345-12. A narrow vertical beam of about 2°–3° maximises range at the horizon.

Q7How is a heliport beacon powered?

From a dedicated 230 V AC single-phase circuit with UPS or generator backup, independent of any airfield series circuit. A photocell, astronomical clock or Beacon Controller handles switching, colour sequence and day/night intensity.

Q8Which standards apply to heliport beacons?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-12 (airport and heliport beacons), FAA AC 150/5390-2 (heliport design and siting) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED performance. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a heliport beacon be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for mounting and vibration checks, and annual for photometric intensity, colour filter, line-of-sight obstruction survey and bearing and connection checks. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is a heliport beacon completely dark?

Likely causes are loss of supply, a failed lamp or LED array, or a battery or UPS that failed to hold the load through an interruption. Check incoming supply, test the UPS under a simulated outage and check lamp or LED continuity.
