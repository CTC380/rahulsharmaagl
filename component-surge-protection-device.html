Surge Protection Device (SPD)

A critical protective device that safeguards Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) equipment from transient overvoltages caused by lightning strikes, switching operations, and other electrical disturbances. SPDs are essential for ensuring the reliability and longevity of sensitive AGL electronics, including Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), LED drivers, communication modules, and control systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Surge Protection Device (SPD) is a protective component designed to limit transient overvoltages and divert surge currents to earth, thereby protecting downstream equipment from damage. In AGL systems, SPDs are installed at critical points such as:

  • AC Mains Input: Protecting the entire AGL substation or CCR room from external surges originating from the utility grid.
  • CCR Outputs: Protecting series lighting circuits from lightning‑induced surges on the airfield.
  • Control and Communication Lines: Protecting ALCMS, PLC, and data networks from surges induced on signal cables.
  • LED Fixtures: Many modern AGL luminaires have built‑in SPDs to protect the LED drivers from surges on the secondary circuit.

SPDs are classified into three types (according to IEC 61643‑11): Type 1 (for direct lightning strike protection), Type 2 (for indirect surges), and Type 3 (for sensitive equipment protection). AGL installations typically use a combination of these types in a coordinated cascade.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The surge protection device's purpose is to intercept the transient overvoltages that lightning strikes and grid switching events induce on power and signal lines, clamping them to a safe level before they reach the CCRs, LED drivers, and control electronics that are otherwise vulnerable to this kind of damage. Because these transients typically last only microseconds but carry enough energy to destroy solid-state components instantly, the SPD's role is to react fast enough and absorb enough energy to prevent that damage in the first place, rather than simply detecting or reporting a fault after it has already occurred. Deploying SPDs in a coordinated cascade — a robust Type 1 device at the main supply, progressively more sensitive Type 2 and Type 3 devices closer to the equipment — is what allows each stage to handle the portion of surge energy it is designed for, protecting increasingly delicate electronics the deeper into the system a surge travels.

Application

Where Is It Used?

SPDs are used at multiple levels within the AGL electrical infrastructure:

  • At the Main Distribution Panel: Type 1 or Type 2 SPDs protect the entire AGL power supply from external surges (lightning, grid switching).
  • At CCR Input and Output: SPDs on the input side protect the CCR from mains surges; on the output side, they protect the series loop from induced surges (especially in exposed airfield locations).
  • At Remote Lighting Control Panels: Protecting sub‑distribution circuits feeding individual runway/taxiway sections.
  • At Communication and Data Lines: Protecting Ethernet, fiber optic (with metallic conductors), RS‑485, and other control signals.
  • Within LED Fixtures: Many modern LED AGL lights include built‑in SPDs to protect the LED driver and optics.
Placement

Installation & Location

SPDs are DIN-rail or panel-mounted at the specific point in the circuit they protect, rather than at a single dedicated location — units are fitted at the main distribution panel's incoming feeder, at the input and output terminals of each CCR, in remote lighting control panels, and inline on communication and data cables entering equipment cabinets. Lead lengths to the protected busbar or terminal are kept as short and direct as practical, since excessive lead length reduces clamping effectiveness, and each SPD's earth conductor is bonded to the panel's earth bar with as short and low-impedance a path as the enclosure allows. Where an SPD is built into an LED fixture, it sits inside the fixture housing ahead of the LED driver, requiring no separate installation beyond the fixture itself.

Function

How Does It Work?

An SPD operates by diverting surge energy away from the protected circuit and safely to earth. The operating principles depend on the technology used:

  • Metal Oxide Varistor (MOV): The most common technology. MOVs have a high resistance at normal voltage but become highly conductive when the voltage exceeds the clamping level (breakdown voltage). They absorb surge energy and dissipate it as heat.
  • Gas Discharge Tube (GDT): A spark gap filled with inert gas. At normal voltage, the gas is non‑conductive. When a surge exceeds the breakdown voltage, the gas ionizes and conducts the surge current to ground. GDTs handle very high surges (Type 1) but have a slower response.
  • Transient Voltage Suppression (TVS) Diode: A solid‑state device that clamps voltage to a fixed level. It has a very fast response (< 1 ns) and is used for sensitive electronics (Type 3).
  • Combined SPDs: Often incorporate multiple technologies (e.g., GDT + MOV + TVS) to provide both high energy handling and fast response.
  • Sequential Protection: In practice, a cascade of SPDs (Type 1 at the main panel, Type 2 at sub‑distribution, Type 3 at equipment) ensures that the surge energy is progressively reduced before reaching sensitive electronics.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

An SPD is a passive protective device with no active control loop of its own — its "logic" lies entirely in its clamping characteristic, diverting transient overvoltage to earth once the let-through voltage threshold is exceeded, then resetting to a high-impedance state once the transient passes. Its role in the wider control architecture is as a monitored protective element: most airfield-grade SPDs include a status indicator, a visual flag and/or a dry contact, that changes state once the internal protection element has been consumed by repeated or severe surges. That contact is typically wired back to the alarm panel or a SCADA/monitoring system, so a failed or end-of-life SPD is reported for replacement rather than silently leaving the downstream equipment unprotected.

Transient Event → SPD Clamping → Diverted Energy → Status Contact → Alarm Panel / SCADA

Construction

Physical Construction

The SPD is built as a modular DIN-rail cartridge: a plastic housing contains the protection elements — metal-oxide varistors (MOVs), gas discharge tubes (GDTs), or a hybrid combination depending on the protection type — potted or securely mounted to withstand the thermal and mechanical stress of repeated surge events. The cartridge plugs into a fixed base that remains wired into the circuit, so a degraded module can be swapped without disconnecting the protected conductors, and a mechanical flag or remote contact on the module face indicates end-of-life so a failed unit is visually identifiable during inspection. Terminals are screw-clamp, sized for the current rating of the protected circuit, and the DIN-rail base includes an integral earth connection bar to provide the low-impedance path to ground the device depends on to divert surge current effectively. The housing is rated for the same wide temperature range as other DIN-rail control-cabinet equipment, reflecting its typical installation alongside CCRs, PLCs, and other AGL electronics in the same enclosure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect SPD Type Selection

Using a Type 3 SPD at the main panel (where high energy surges are expected) will result in failure. Always select the appropriate type based on the location and risk level. A cascade approach (Type 1 → Type 2 → Type 3) is recommended.

Poor Earthing System

An SPD is only as effective as the earthing system to which it is connected. High‑impedance earth connections reduce surge diversion effectiveness. Ensure proper earthing (low resistance) and inspect connections regularly.

End‑of‑Life Indicator Ignored

Many SPDs have mechanical or remote indicators that show when the protection element has degraded (e.g., MOVs have a limited number of surge events). Ignoring these indicators can leave equipment unprotected. Replace SPDs when indicated.

Voltage Protection Level (Up) Too High

If the SPD's clamping voltage is higher than the withstand voltage of downstream equipment, the equipment may still be damaged. Always verify that Up is compatible with the protected equipment's insulation rating.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

SPD Fails Shortly After a Surge Event
Likely Cause
Incorrect SPD type selected for the location (e.g., a Type 3 used where high-energy surges occur).
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the installed SPD type against the location's risk level and the cascade design (Type 1 → Type 2 → Type 3).
Corrective Action
Replace with the correct SPD type for that point in the cascade, restoring the intended staged protection scheme.
Poor Surge Diversion Despite a Healthy SPD
Likely Cause
High-impedance or degraded earthing connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure earth resistance at the SPD's earth connection and inspect for corrosion or loose connections.
Corrective Action
Repair or improve the earthing connection to achieve low resistance, and re-test after remediation.
Equipment Remains Unprotected After Multiple Surge Events
Likely Cause
The SPD's protection element has reached end-of-life and the indicator was not acted on.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the mechanical or remote end-of-life indicator and review the surge event history/counter if available.
Corrective Action
Replace the SPD, and put a periodic indicator-check routine in place.
Downstream Equipment Damaged Despite an SPD Being Present
Likely Cause
The SPD's voltage protection level (Up) is too high for the downstream equipment's insulation rating.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the SPD's rated Up against the protected equipment's withstand voltage.
Corrective Action
Replace with an SPD having an appropriately low Up, or add a further protection stage closer to the equipment.
SPD Indicator Shows Fault But the Circuit Still Operates
Likely Cause
One or more internal protection modules have failed, but remaining modules or a bypass are keeping the circuit live.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the SPD's status window/indicator per module, and test with a multimeter if accessible.
Corrective Action
Replace the SPD at the earliest opportunity — do not rely on a partially failed unit for continued surge protection.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

MOV / Protection Element End-of-Life Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Cumulative energy absorption from repeated surge events gradually degrading the metal-oxide varistor or other protection element.
Effect
The SPD fails shortly after a surge event, or provides progressively poorer surge diversion despite still appearing healthy at a glance.
Detection Method
End-of-life indicator check and a status indicator verification as part of scheduled maintenance.
Earthing System Continuity Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion, a loose connection, or physical damage to the earth bonding conductor between the SPD and the earthing system.
Effect
Poor surge diversion despite a healthy SPD, since the device has nowhere effective to route the diverted energy.
Detection Method
Earth bond continuity test and an earthing system continuity check against the design resistance value.
Incorrect SPD Type / Rating Selection
Medium Severity
Root Cause
An SPD Type or discharge current rating specified or installed that doesn't match the actual exposure risk at that point in the circuit.
Effect
Downstream equipment is damaged despite an SPD being present, because the device's let-through voltage or energy handling is inadequate.
Detection Method
SPD type and rating verification against the circuit's exposure category and the coordination study.
Cascade Coordination Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
SPDs at different points in the same circuit not properly coordinated in energy let-through and response time.
Effect
Equipment remains unprotected after multiple surge events even though every individual SPD appears to be functioning.
Detection Method
Cascade coordination check across all SPD stages protecting the circuit.
Repeated Surge Event Cumulative Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A site with frequent lightning activity or switching transients subjecting the SPD to more surge events than its design life assumes.
Effect
Faster-than-expected degradation, shortening the practical replacement interval below the manufacturer's nominal rating.
Detection Method
Remote contact/SCADA signal monitoring to log surge events, correlated against the end-of-life indicator trend.
End-of-Life Indicator Ignored
Low Severity
Root Cause
A maintenance gap where the SPD's visual or remote fault indicator has tripped but the device hasn't been inspected or replaced.
Effect
The SPD indicator shows a fault but the circuit still operates normally, masking the fact that surge protection has already been lost.
Detection Method
Scheduled end-of-life indicator checks built into the routine maintenance cycle, not left to reactive inspection.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate the Protected Circuit Before Replacement
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the circuit the SPD is connected to before removing or replacing it, and verify zero energy at the terminals, since the device sits directly across live conductors.

Degraded SPD May Be Hot or Have Vented
Personnel Safety

Treat an SPD that has tripped its end-of-life indicator or absorbed a major surge as potentially hot or internally damaged; handle it as a failed electrical component, not a routine part, and avoid assuming it's safe to touch immediately after a fault.

Confirm Correct Replacement Rating
Compliance

Replace a failed SPD with one matched to the same nominal voltage, discharge current, and protection class as specified for that point in the system; an undersized or mismatched replacement leaves the downstream equipment under-protected.

Earthing Continuity Verification
Electrical Safety

Confirm the SPD's earth connection has low, continuous resistance before and after replacement, since a surge-protection device is only effective if its path to earth is intact.

Minimise Time Without Protection
Operational

Where the SPD protects a CCR room, substation, or series-circuit output, coordinate the replacement to minimise the time the protected equipment is left without surge protection, particularly during storm season.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify the new or repaired SPD's status indicator shows healthy and the protected circuit is functioning normally before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Degradation Without Failure Is the Normal Life Cycle
Reliability

An SPD's varistor or gas-discharge element degrades incrementally with every surge event it absorbs, whether or not that surge was large enough to trip the end-of-life indicator; treat SPDs on circuits with a known lightning-exposure history as candidates for proactive replacement on a service-life schedule rather than waiting for the indicator to trip.

A Cluster of Trips After One Storm Is a Data Point, Not Noise
Reliability

Where several SPDs across a site trip in the same storm event, record which positions were involved rather than just replacing and moving on; a repeated pattern at the same locations points to a systemic earthing or lightning-protection weakness at those points, not simple bad luck.

Earth Impedance, Not Just Continuity, Determines Effectiveness
Electrical

A simple continuity check confirms the SPD's earth path exists but says nothing about its impedance under a fast-rising surge current; where surge activity is frequent, periodic earth resistance testing gives a much better indication of actual protection effectiveness than continuity alone.

Coordinate Protection Stages, Don't Just Match Ratings Locally
Design

Where SPDs are deployed in stages (service entrance, distribution, equipment level), replacing one stage with a device that doesn't coordinate properly with the others can leave a let-through voltage gap that no single device catches; check the manufacturer's coordination requirements across stages, not just the rating of the unit being replaced.

Thermal Runaway Risk Rises With Age, Not Just Surge Count
Failure Mode

An aged varistor can enter thermal runaway from sustained overvoltage that a fresh unit would handle without incident, since its leakage current increases as it degrades; don't assume an SPD's age alone is benign just because it hasn't tripped its indicator or visibly failed.

Keep a Replacement Log Tied to Storm Events
Maintenance

Recording the date and approximate storm severity alongside each SPD replacement builds a usable history for identifying which circuits or positions are chronically under-protected; a replacement log with no context beyond "replaced" loses this diagnostic value over time.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about surge protection devices: types, placement, ratings, earthing, standards and selection.

Q1What is a surge protection device (SPD) in AGL?

An SPD limits transient overvoltages and diverts surge currents to earth, protecting downstream equipment such as Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), LED drivers, communication modules and control systems. Transients from lightning and switching typically last only microseconds but carry enough energy to destroy solid-state components.

Q2What are the types of SPD?

IEC 61643-11 defines Type 1 (direct lightning strike protection), Type 2 (indirect surges) and Type 3 (sensitive equipment protection). AGL installations typically use a coordinated cascade of all three: Type 1 at the main panel, Type 2 at sub-distribution and Type 3 at the equipment.

Q3Where are SPDs installed in an AGL system?

At the main distribution panel's incoming feeder, at CCR inputs and outputs, at remote lighting control panels, and on communication and data lines such as Ethernet, RS-485 and fibre with metallic conductors. Many modern LED AGL luminaires also have built-in SPDs protecting the LED driver.

Q4How does an SPD work?

It diverts surge energy away from the protected circuit to earth. Common technologies are the metal oxide varistor (MOV), gas discharge tube (GDT) and transient voltage suppression (TVS) diode. Combined SPDs use several (for example GDT + MOV + TVS) to get both high energy handling and fast response.

Q5What are typical SPD ratings?

Typical nominal voltage is 230 V or 400 V AC. Surge current capacity is about 10 kA (10/350 µs) for Type 1, 20 kA (8/20 µs) for Type 2 and 3 kA (8/20 µs) for Type 3. Voltage protection level (Up) is at or below 1.5 kV for equipment protection and at or below 2.5 kV for main protection. Modular units mount on 35 mm DIN rail.

Q6Why does earthing matter so much for an SPD?

An SPD is only as effective as the earthing system it connects to. A high-impedance earth connection reduces surge diversion. Keep connecting leads short and straight, and bond the SPD to the main earthing busbar with a short, low-impedance path.

Q7How do I know when an SPD needs replacing?

Many SPDs have a mechanical flag or remote signal contact showing end of life, which can be wired to the ALCMS or BMS for alerts. MOVs have a limited number of surge events, so ignoring the indicator leaves equipment unprotected. A degraded SPD may be hot or have vented, so isolate the circuit before touching it.

Q8What backup protection does an SPD need?

A backup fuse or breaker sized to the manufacturer's coordination table, so a failed SPD is cleared without nuisance-tripping the main breaker.

Q9Which standards apply to SPDs?

The main references are IEC 61643-11, UL 1449 and IEEE C62.41. FAA equipment ACs such as AC 150/5345-10 (CCRs) and AC 150/5345-54 (PCUs) require lightning and surge arresters tested to IEEE C62.41, and the FAA has no standalone SPD advisory circular. Always verify the current edition.

Q10What are the common SPD selection and installation mistakes?

The common mistakes are using the wrong type (for example a Type 3 SPD at the main panel, where it will fail), poor earthing, ignoring the end-of-life indicator, and a voltage protection level (Up) higher than the withstand voltage of the downstream equipment.
