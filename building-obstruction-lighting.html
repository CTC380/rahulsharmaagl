AGL Visual Encyclopedia ENTRY 8 · OBSTRUCTION LIGHTING

How are buildings lit?

A light on the highest point, lights along the roof edges, and every light kept clear of parapets and plant so it can be seen from all sides.

top light, on the highest point
roof edge and mid-level light
clear view from every direction
The Fact ICAO Annex 14

A building is an obstacle for aircraft only when it is tall enough, or close enough to an aerodrome, to stand above or near the protected airspace. Where the regulator decides it must be lit, it is lit like any other fixed object: the light class follows the height, from fixed red low-intensity lights on lower buildings to medium-intensity lights on taller ones.

A light goes on the highest point, which on a building is often rooftop plant, a lift overrun, a water tank or an antenna. More lights go on the roof corners and edges so the extent of the building is clear, spaced along a wide roof within the limit for the light type. A tall building adds mid-level lights, spaced as equally as practicable down the sides.

What is different about a building is its setting. It stands in a city full of other lights, and its own parapets and roof clutter can hide a light from low angles. Lights are therefore set clear of and above them. A lower building beside a taller lit one may need no light at all if the study finds it is shielded.

How a Building Is Lit
1
Check it is an obstacle

Compare its height and position with the protected airspace around the aerodrome. Many buildings are not obstacles and need no light.

2
Light the highest point

Fit the top light on the highest part, plant and antennas included, so the full height of the building is shown.

3
Mark the roof edges

Add lights at the corners and along the edges, at equal spacing within the limit for the light type, so the extent is clear.

4
Add levels, keep clear

On a tall building add mid levels, and place every light clear of parapets and plant so it is seen from every direction.

What Makes Buildings Different
City backdrop

Obstacle lights compete with facade, signage and street lighting, so they must stay recognisable and separate from decorative light.

Roof clutter

Parapets, plant, tanks and antennas can hide a light, so lights go on the highest items and clear of anything that shields them.

Access to maintain

Roof lights must be reached safely to change a lamp or lens. Fit them where they can be serviced, with guarded access and fall protection.

Power and alarms

The lights need a reliable supply, usually with a standby source, and a way to raise an alarm when a light fails.

  Important Distinction

Three kinds of light on a building are easily confused:

  • Obstacle Lighting→ the aviation lights that show the building to pilots, at night and, for white lights, by day
  • Facade and Architectural Lighting→ decorative or advertising light, which is not an aviation signal and must not be mistaken for one or dazzle pilots
  • Rooftop Heliport Lighting→ a separate set of lights for a helipad on the roof, with its own rules

Also note that height alone does not decide it. The same building may need lights beside an airport and none in a city centre well away from any flight path.

Key Points

ICAO Building Obstruction Lighting

ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, Chapter 6, treats a building as a fixed object. The light class follows the height, from fixed red low-intensity lights on lower objects to medium-intensity lights from about 45 m and high-intensity lights above about 150 m. One or more lights are placed as close as practicable to the top, arranged to show at least the points or edges of the object that are highest in relation to the obstacle limitation surface. Where two or more edges are at the same height, the edge nearest the aerodrome is included, and on an extensive object the lights show its general extent. Which objects count as obstacles at all, including shielding by an existing object, is covered in Chapter 4 of the same Annex. Take the light types, number and layout from the regulator's determination and the current edition of the standard.

FAA Building Obstruction Lighting

The FAA covers structures in AC 70/7460-1, Obstruction Marking and Lighting, and a building is judged through the FAA notice and determination process. Where lighting is required, the styles use fixtures such as the L-810 (steady red), L-864 (flashing red), L-865 (flashing white, medium intensity) and L-856 (flashing white, high intensity). The number of light levels and the placement at each level follow the height of the building and the determination. Confirm the current edition of the advisory circular, the lighting style the FAA determination requires for your building, and any local rules on facade lighting.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1Do all buildings need obstruction lights?

No. A building needs lights only when the regulator's study finds it is an obstacle, because it stands above or near the protected airspace around an aerodrome or its flight paths. Most buildings are well clear of it, and a lower building beside a taller lit one may be treated as shielded. The decision comes from the aeronautical study, not from height alone.

Q2Where are obstruction lights placed on a building?

On the highest point, including rooftop plant, lift overruns, water tanks and antennas, and along the roof corners and edges so the full extent of the building is clear from every direction. On a tall building further lights are added at mid levels, spaced as equally as practicable. The exact arrangement comes from the regulator's determination.

Q3Why are roof lights set above the parapet?

A parapet, plant room or tank can hide a light from low angles, which is where a pilot normally sees it. Lights are placed clear of and above these items so they can be seen from every direction in azimuth, and an extra light is added where one would be shielded.

Q4Can decorative or facade lighting replace obstruction lights?

No. Decorative light is not an aviation signal and does not stand in for obstacle lights. It can also confuse or dazzle pilots, which is why non-aeronautical lights near an aerodrome are controlled. Keep facade lighting separate and check that it neither hides nor mimics the red obstacle lights.

Q5Who decides whether a building needs lights?

The aviation regulator, through an aeronautical study or height-clearance process. In the US that is the FAA notice and determination process, and in India it is the AAI height-clearance (NOC) process for buildings near airports. The result sets whether lights are needed and their type, number and positions, so take it from the determination for your building.
