01 · Overview

What Is AGL Photometry?

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) photometry is the measurement and evaluation of the optical performance of aerodrome lighting on runways, taxiways and approaches, and of visual approach guidance systems such as PAPI.

A lighting system may be electrically operational and still fail to provide the required optical performance. A lamp can illuminate, an LED fitting can turn on and a CCR can supply the correct current, yet the actual beam intensity, aiming, colour, coverage or configuration may be outside the applicable requirements. That is why AGL photometric testing matters for commissioning, periodic inspection, maintenance, troubleshooting and performance verification.

In India, AAI tender documents show that in-field photometric testing is actively used for AGL facilities. Recent AAI specifications call for testing of runway, approach, threshold, PAPI and other AGL fittings, with measurements covering colour, coverage, configuration and chromaticity.[1] ICAO's aerodrome visual-aids documentation also addresses the characteristics and performance of approach, runway and taxiway lighting.[2]

The testing process can include
Luminous intensityPhotometric distributionBeam coverageAiming angleHorizontal and vertical alignmentColourChromaticityFixture configurationLight-unit conditionPAPI optical performanceApproach-light performanceRunway-light performanceTaxiway-light performance

The purpose is not simply to confirm that a fitting is ON. It is to determine whether the fitting produces the correct optical output in the intended direction, and whether the installed system performs consistently across the airfield.

Airport lighting is a safety-critical visual system; pilots depend on runway, taxiway, approach and PAPI cues, especially at night and in reduced visibility. This is particularly important for LED AGL: long service life does not prevent optical performance being affected by contamination, damaged optics, incorrect aiming, mechanical movement, ageing, lens deterioration, internal component failure or installation errors.

02 · 4C Testing

AGL Photometry and the 4Cs: Colour, Coverage, Configuration, Chromaticity

AAI specifications for AGL in-field photometric testing have used the framework of the 4Cs.[1] These are not a simple lamp-function test.

Colour

The visible colour must be correct for the fitting's function. Incorrect colour can come from incorrect optical components, damaged lenses, LED degradation, contamination, incorrect installation, wrong replacement components or electrical/electronic failures.

Coverage

Whether emitted light gives the intended angular and spatial distribution. Poor coverage can result from incorrect aiming, fixture displacement, damaged optics, incorrect mounting, pavement settlement, mechanical movement or internal optical deterioration.

Configuration

Whether the installation is arranged correctly within the overall visual aid system — for example incorrect fixture spacing, a missing fitting, incorrect orientation, wrong fixture type, incorrect light sequence or a displaced or misinstalled unit.

Chromaticity

An objective way of evaluating colour characteristics rather than relying only on visual judgement. The measurement method and limits should come from the governing specification, not an arbitrary generic value.

AGL Photometry Equipment: Vehicle-Mounted and Hand-Held Testing

AAI specifications have called for both vehicle-mounted mobile testing equipment and hand-held devices for in-field AGL photometry at Indian airports.[3]

AAI specifications for hand-portable equipment also describe measurement of photometric diagrams, mean intensity, aiming angles and other parameters for AGL and PAPI systems.[4]

Photometric Testing Equipment Airport

Photometric testing equipment for an airport is chosen by the testing scope: vehicle-mounted systems for large runway and taxiway surveys, and hand-held equipment for PAPI, individual fittings and spot checks. Either type should provide calibrated photometric sensors, angular and aiming-angle measurement, colour / chromaticity measurement, data acquisition with reporting software, and calibration documentation. Confirm suitability for the systems to be tested, including PAPI, before mobilising.

Vehicle-mounted testing

Testing every fitting with a manually positioned instrument is slow on large airports. A vehicle-mounted system measures systematically while travelling along a runway or taxiway. Useful for runway edge and centreline surveys, approach lighting, threshold and end lights, taxiway surveys, acceptance testing, periodic maintenance testing and trend monitoring. AAI has tendered in-field AGL photometric testing using vehicle-mounted devices.[3]

Hand-held testing

Useful where a vehicle system is impractical or an individual fitting needs detailed investigation: PAPI measurements, defective-fitting verification, isolated runway or taxiway lights, post-maintenance verification, field alignment checks, troubleshooting, spot measurements and laboratory or workshop evaluation. Portable systems complement, rather than necessarily replace, a vehicle-based survey.

05 · Runway, Taxiway, Approach & PAPI

Runway, Taxiway, Approach Lighting and PAPI Photometry

Runway lighting

Edge lights: verify output and orientation (low or inconsistent intensity, incorrect aiming, tilted elevated fixtures, damaged inset fittings, dirty optics, wrong colour, dead or weak units). Threshold and end lights: re-check after maintenance, resurfacing, fixture replacement, civil works or rehabilitation. Centreline lights: identify weak fittings, defective LEDs, wrong colour, optical degradation, damaged inset units and circuit inconsistencies. Touchdown zone: reveals deterioration not obvious in a night visual inspection.

Taxiway lighting

Edge and centreline lights, stop bars, runway guard lights, holding-position and special guidance lighting. Attention to colour, beam direction, fixture orientation, damaged or sunken fittings, contamination, intensity variation and incorrect replacement fixtures. A taxiway fitting may be electrically functional but optically misaligned; photometry provides objective evidence.

Approach Lighting Photometric Testing

Arrays of visual aids give alignment and distance cues. Assessment covers intensity, colour, configuration, aiming, spacing-related installation condition, elevated support alignment, inset light performance and defective or missing fittings. AAI specifications explicitly include approach lights among fittings subject to in-field photometric testing.[1]

PAPI

Depends on both intensity and the correct relationship between white and red sectors. Testing covers luminous intensity, colour, beam transition, unit aiming, horizontal alignment, elevation angle, optical consistency between units and system configuration. A unit can illuminate normally while incorrectly aimed, so visual inspection alone is not enough. Use suitable equipment and the applicable aerodrome specification and authority-approved criteria.

06 · Procedure

AGL Photometric Testing Procedure

A professional field photometry programme follows a controlled sequence.

1. Document review

AGL layout drawings, runway and taxiway configuration, fitting inventory, fixture type, circuit identification, project specifications, previous photometric reports, maintenance records, PAPI documentation and applicable criteria — establishing what is supposed to be measured.

2. Airfield inspection

Look for damaged, missing or displaced fittings, dirty lenses, broken housings, incorrect orientation, damaged cables, standing water, damaged mountings and signs of pavement movement, so installation problems are not mistaken for measurement anomalies.

3. Calibration verification

Current calibration or verification documentation appropriate to the project. A photometric report is only meaningful when the measurement system itself is controlled.

4. Equipment setup

Configure per the manufacturer's procedure and applicable methodology. Vehicle: vehicle → measurement frame → sensor → data acquisition → analysis software. Portable: operator → portable photometer → fixture → measurement → report.

5. Circuit preparation

Energize the circuit at the appropriate operating condition. Where several brightness stages exist, the test plan must say which are required — on CCR-fed series circuits, measured performance can depend on the operating current level.

6. Measurement

Record the applicable parameters: intensity, angular distribution, aiming angle, colour, chromaticity, photometric curve, fixture position, unit identification and pass/fail status.

7. Data analysis

Process raw data against the applicable requirements and classify results as Pass, Marginal / investigation required or Fail, linked to project criteria rather than a generic industry number.

8. Defect identification

Locate the problem, for example: Runway 09 → edge light no. 47 → low intensity; Taxiway A → centreline unit 18 → incorrect aiming; PAPI 27 → unit 3 → abnormal beam transition. More useful than an overall pass percentage.

9. Corrective maintenance

Cleaning, aiming correction, fixture, optical-component or LED-module replacement, cable/connector repair, transformer replacement, mechanical realignment, housing repair, replacement of damaged fittings and correction of installation configuration.

10. Retesting

Retest corrected fittings where required to confirm the action restored the required optical performance.

09 · Photometry vs Electrical Testing

Photometry vs Electrical Testing

The two are different disciplines that should be considered together; a complete AGL maintenance programme uses both.

Electrical testingPhotometric testing
Checks circuit continuityChecks optical performance
Measures insulation conditionMeasures light output
Checks transformer conditionChecks beam characteristics
Evaluates CCR / circuit operationEvaluates intensity and distribution
Identifies electrical faultsIdentifies optical / aiming faults
Electrical measurementOptical measurement

A fitting with low measured intensity could have a healthy transformer, correct circuit continuity and acceptable insulation resistance — and still have a defective optical assembly.

Photometry vs visual inspection

Visual inspection finds lights that are off, obviously damaged, wrong-coloured, missing or visibly misaligned, but cannot reliably quantify optical performance. Photometry gives measurement → evidence → comparison → defect identification → corrective action → retest, which is far more valuable for engineering acceptance and asset management.

10 · Applications

Photometry in Commissioning, Existing Airports, Compliance, LED and CAT II/III

Commissioning

Sequence: installation → electrical testing → functional testing → energization → photometric testing → corrections → retest → final acceptance → documentation. At new runways, extensions, taxiway construction, rehabilitation, LED conversion, approach modifications, PAPI relocation, CAT II/III improvements and major upgrades, it identifies installation defects before handover.

Existing airports

Existing systems may mix legacy lamps, LED fittings, mixed fixture types, older transformers, new and old circuits and replacement fixtures from different manufacturers. Photometry establishes actual field condition. AAI has awarded and tendered dedicated photometric work at operational airports, including testing of all AGL at Hyderabad Airport, Begumpet, and other airport-specific photometric surveys.[5]

DGCA compliance

An AAI 2026 tender for Ranchi Airport sought in-field photometric testing of AGL using vehicle-mounted and hand-held equipment as part of compliance with DGCA observations.[3] Photometry is not only a design or commissioning activity — it can be an operational compliance and corrective-maintenance activity. Acceptance criteria must come from the applicable DGCA requirements, ICAO provisions, airport specifications and approved documents.

LED airport lighting

LED removes many lamp-related problems but not optical ageing, lens contamination, damaged optics, thermal degradation, electronic faults, incorrect replacement assemblies, alignment errors or incorrect installation. Periodic photometry works as a performance-monitoring tool: initial test → annual test → trend comparison → reduced performance → corrective maintenance. Historical data becomes part of asset management.

Preventive maintenance

Photometric data supports condition-based maintenance: spot gradual intensity reduction, repeated failures at one location, systematic aiming problems, colour deterioration, recurring circuit abnormalities and fixture types with higher failure rates — directing resources to assets that need attention.

CAT II / III operations

A performance programme for low-visibility operations may include regular visual inspections, functional checks, photometric testing, fixture condition monitoring, PAPI verification, circuit and CCR checks, corrective maintenance and documented retesting. Frequency and acceptance requirements come from the operational approval, regulations, airport procedures and approved maintenance programme.

ICAO and Indian Requirements for AGL Photometry

Photometry should always be performed against the correct governing requirements.

ICAO Annex 14 Volume I is a core international reference for aerodrome design and operations; the ICAO Store lists the 9th Edition (July 2022) with Amendment 18 dated 4 August 2025 and subsequent corrigenda issued in 2026.[8] ICAO's Aerodrome Design Manual Part 4, Visual Aids, addresses visual-aid characteristics including approach, runway and taxiway lighting; the store lists it as the 5th Edition, 2021.[2]

For Indian projects, the applicable DGCA requirements, AAI technical specifications, airport procedures and contract documents must also be considered. AAI field-testing specifications illustrate how ICAO/DGCA requirements are incorporated into actual Indian airport work packages.[1]

Acceptance criteria vary with the project and operational category, so this page deliberately does not publish universal numerical pass/fail values. Always take them from the exact applicable standard or project specification.

15 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is AGL photometry?
AGL photometry is the field measurement and evaluation of the optical performance of Airfield Ground Lighting, including parameters such as intensity, colour, chromaticity, coverage and aiming, depending on the lighting system and applicable requirements.
Why is photometric testing required when all the runway lights are working?
Electrical operation does not prove optical compliance. A fitting may turn on while having low intensity, incorrect aiming, wrong colour or inadequate coverage.
What AGL systems can be tested?
Depending on the test equipment and project scope, testing can cover runway edge, threshold, end, centreline, touchdown-zone, approach, taxiway, stop-bar, runway guard and PAPI systems.
Can PAPI be tested using AGL photometry equipment?
Yes, provided the equipment is suitable for PAPI and the testing methodology covers the required optical and aiming parameters. AAI specifications for portable photometric equipment explicitly include AGL and PAPI measurements.
What is 4C AGL testing?
In AAI technical specifications, 4C testing refers to Colour, Coverage, Configuration and Chromaticity.
Is vehicle-mounted photometry better than hand-held testing?
Neither is universally better. Vehicle-mounted systems can be efficient for large runway and taxiway surveys, while hand-held equipment is valuable for PAPI, individual fitting investigations and detailed field verification.
How often should AGL photometry be performed?
The required frequency depends on the airport's approved maintenance programme, applicable regulations, operational requirements, project specifications and authority instructions. It should not be assigned a universal interval without reference to the governing documentation.
Does photometry replace electrical testing?
No. Photometry and electrical testing evaluate different aspects of the system and should normally be considered complementary.
Can photometry be used after LED conversion?
Yes. Photometry is particularly useful after LED conversion to verify installed optical performance and to establish a new baseline for future monitoring.
What should an AGL photometric report contain?
At minimum, traceable information on the airport/test location, test equipment, measurement conditions, individual fittings or locations, relevant measured parameters, identified defects and final test status, with exact reporting requirements determined by the contract and applicable standards.
