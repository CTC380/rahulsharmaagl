Overview

What Is an Airport Approach Lighting System?

An Approach Lighting System (ALS) is a pattern of fixed and, where specified, flashing lights extending from the runway threshold into the approach area. It helps pilots move from instrument flight to visual flight for landing. Its geometry, colour, intensity, alignment and pattern give visual references for runway alignment, roll guidance, height perception and limited distance-to-go information.

ICAO treats approach lighting as part of the visual aids required for different runway types and operating conditions. The framework is Annex 14 Volume I (9th Edition, July 2022, with Amendment 18), and the Aerodrome Design Manual Part 4 is the principal design guidance.

Configuration

    Engineering notes

      Geometry follows the ICAO/EASA-style configuration. Actual installed systems may be shorter because of terrain, land, obstacles or approved limitations, so check the airport’s approved design and AIP data.
      Purpose

      What an ALS Provides and Where It Is Used

      Principal functions

      • Runway alignment information
      • Roll reference
      • Visual indication of the approach direction
      • Limited distance-to-go information
      • Visual transition from instrument references
      • Added guidance in reduced-visibility approaches

      Typical applications

      • Non-instrument and non-precision runways
      • Precision approach Category I, II and III
      • Runways supporting low-visibility operations
      • Runways needing improved visual alignment
      • Airports where terrain or obstacles make approach guidance important

      Choose the system for the operational objective, not the runway length. Current Indian AIP data show SALS at 420 m, shorter SALS, CAT I at 720 m or 900 m and CAT II/III precision approach lighting. Use the approved aerodrome design and AIP data, not an assumed standard length.

      Categories

      CAT I, CAT II and CAT III Approach Lighting

      SystemGeometry (ICAO/EASA-style)Key points
      Simple (SALS)Centreline to 420 m where possible, crossbar at 300 m (18 m or 30 m)Non-instrument and non-precision runways.
      Precision CAT ICentreline, standard length 900 m wherever possible, crossbar at 300 mAlignment, roll reference, limited distance-to-go, visual acquisition. May be shorter at some airports. Details vary by standard, including centreline arrangements, barrettes and sequenced flashing lights.
      Precision CAT II/IIICentreline 900 m where possible, two side rows (270 m, or 240 m with demonstrated serviceability), crossbars at 150 m and 300 m.       CAT II vs CAT III

      ICAO expresses the physical lighting category as precision approach Category II and III, not a separate CAT III visual-aid configuration. Some national systems and manufacturers use separate labels such as CAT II, CAT III or CAT IIIB, so assess the installation against the applicable State regulations, approved runway operation and certification basis.

      CAT III is not a lighting-only capability

      It depends on ILS performance, AGL, RVR systems, secondary power, taxiway and runway lighting, monitoring, low-visibility procedures, aircraft and operator capability and aerodrome certification. AAI eAIP entries for CAT II/III link the approach lighting to ILS, AGL, RVR and standby power.

      For CAT II/III, watch the inner portion. Indian low-visibility procedures can set specific restrictions when a percentage of the approach lights in the inner 450 m is unserviceable. The thresholds are airport- and procedure-specific.

      Layout

      Approach Lighting Configuration and Functional Zones

      An ALS configuration is described by the geometric arrangement of centreline lights, crossbars, side rows, barrettes, threshold lights, sequenced flashing lights and supporting structures. It helps to divide the approach area into functional zones. The pattern must stay visually coherent when viewed from the intended approach path.

      Threshold zone

      The transition between the approach lighting and the runway visual aids.

      Inner approach zone

      Close-in centreline and lateral visual reference. Especially important for low-visibility operations.

      Middle approach zone

      Crossbars and centreline lighting give pattern recognition and roll and alignment information.

      Outer approach zone

      Helps the pilot acquire the runway environment and establish the approach alignment earlier.

      FAA Systems

      FAA Configurations and Terminology

      FAA names include ALSF-1, ALSF-2, MALS, MALSF, MALSR, SSALS, SSALF, SSALR and ODALS. FAA describes ALSF-2 as a high-intensity system used for Category II/III approaches, and systems such as MALSR as medium-intensity. FAA describes ALSF-1 as a Category I configuration. MALSR combines steady-burning light bars with sequenced flashers.

      Do not use FAA system names as though they were ICAO classifications. For an Indian airport, the DGCA and AAI requirements and the current AIP/eAIP take precedence. Use FAA documents where an FAA-standard installation is specifically being designed or evaluated.
      Components

      Light Units, Barrettes and Flashers

      Approach light units

      Single sources, multi-lamp barrettes, elevated units, threshold units, crossbar units, sequenced flashers and specialized fixtures. Each must give the required colour, intensity, angular distribution, optical alignment, environmental protection and physical integrity.

      Barrettes

      A linear group of sources that reads as one visual element, used in centrelines, crossbars, side rows and CAT II/III systems. A strong linear reference beats an isolated point. A partial failure can change the look even while the bar is still lit.

      Sequenced flashers

      Create a moving effect toward the runway. FAA describes it as a ball of light moving toward the threshold. Verify sequence, direction, timing, synchronization, intensity, aim, circuit independence where specified and control operation.

      FAA and EASA material commonly describes the sequence running from the outermost light toward the threshold at about two flashes per second for the applicable configurations. Accept against the governing system specification, not one timing value for every ALS.

      Flasher electrical system. Depending on the design: CCR output, isolating transformers, flasher control equipment, high-voltage series connections, trigger or control wiring, individual flasher switching, synchronization circuits and monitoring. Diagnose a flasher fault at both the light-unit and the control/electrical level.

      Structures

      Towers, Masts, Frangible Structures and Foundations

      Towers

      Needed where the ground profile requires elevated lights to keep the approach-light plane. They carry centreline, crossbar and side-row lights, flashers and barrette assemblies. Design for structural load, wind, foundation, corrosion, cable routing, maintenance access, frangibility or low-impact requirements, the approach-light plane, obstacle clearance and aircraft safety.

      Masts

      A mast is not only a structural support. Its height sets the vertical geometry. Design it from the light’s station + elevation + vertical aiming angle + photometric coverage. A structurally adequate mast in the wrong position or at the wrong elevation breaks the approved pattern.

      Frangible and low-impact structures

      Supports sit where an aircraft could contact them, so they must not become an unacceptable hazard. ICAO’s Aerodrome Design Manual Part 6 covers frangibility and says required navigation and safety equipment in specified areas must be frangible and mounted as low as possible. FAA uses Low-Impact Resistant (LIR) structures, with an active AC 150/5345-45C for approach lighting supports.

      Foundations

      Check coordinates, elevation, orientation, anchors, structural load, concrete, drainage, water ingress, corrosion, cable entry and maintainability. Settlement or movement can cause an aiming error even when the fixture is unchanged.

      Frangible and low-impact resistant are not automatically interchangeable. They are standard-specific concepts, so specify the applicable requirement instead of “weak mast”.

      Wiring

      Approach Lighting Wiring and Transformers

      Electrical chain

      An ALS normally forms part of the series-circuit system: CCR → AGL cable → isolating transformer → secondary connector → light unit. Specialized flashers use: CCR/control system → circuit → flasher control/switching → isolating transformer → flasher unit. FAA AC 150/5340-30J gives installation guidance for visual aids and is on FAA’s current list.

      Cable design

      Consider circuit configuration, voltage rating, insulation, current, length, voltage drop, connector compatibility, transformer rating, circuit impedance, fault behaviour, electromagnetic environment, mechanical protection and maintainability. Coordinate underground cable with ducts, pits, junction boxes, transformer housings, light bases, drainage and access. Protect cable and allow for movement at mast and tower interfaces.

      Isolating transformers

      Inspect primary and secondary connectors, housing, insulation, moisture, corrosion, leads, physical damage and connection integrity.

      Geometry

      Aiming, Elevation, Azimuth and the Approach-Light Plane

      Each light must be aligned so that its photometric distribution gives the required pattern from the intended approach path. A fixture can be electrically healthy and still be functionally defective because it is mis-aimed.

      Aiming

      Vertical angle, horizontal direction, fixture orientation, centreline alignment, crossbar orientation, elevation relationship and beam coverage.

      Elevation

      Elevation sets the light’s relation to the approach-light plane. A design survey fixes threshold elevation, light station, unit elevation, structure height, terrain profile, the plane and vertical aiming. EASA guidance notes lights may need poles of appropriate height to compensate for ground undulations beyond the stopway or clearway.

      Azimuth

      The horizontal direction of aim. Centreline lights follow the extended runway centreline, crossbars are perpendicular to it, side rows keep the approved lateral geometry and each unit covers the intended approach sector. Errors cause apparent lateral displacement, uneven pattern, weak centreline guidance and coverage failure.

      Horizontal and vertical alignment

      Horizontal: centreline station, lateral offset, crossbar perpendicularity, side-row gauge, fixture and structure orientation. Vertical: light elevation, mast height, fixture tilt, beam aim, relation to the plane and terrain. A settled, tilted or struck light needs re-survey and re-aiming. Total stations and GNSS survey can be used, and survey and photometry should complement each other at acceptance.

      Approach-light plane. The design should keep installed lights on the approved profile without misleading perspective. EASA guidance stresses avoiding objects that protrude through the plane. Watch uneven terrain, roads, drainage channels, embankments, bridges, railways, buildings, temporary construction, cable structures and elevated service equipment.

      Acceptance

      Acceptance Testing

      Do not accept an ALS because “all lights are ON”. Acceptance should demonstrate physical, electrical, optical and structural compliance and the documentation.

      Physical and structural

      • Correct location, stationing, elevation, structure, configuration and fixture type
      • Mast and tower condition, foundation, fasteners
      • Frangibility or low-impact arrangement
      • Cable support, corrosion protection

      Electrical

      Test with calibrated instruments. Take methods and acceptance values from the electrical design, authority specification and manufacturer. Do not present one universal resistance value for every ALS.

      • Cable continuity and insulation
      • Transformer connection and circuit current
      • CCR operation, brightness stages, control operation
      • Earth checks, flasher circuit checks

      Optical

      A fitting that passes an electrical test can still fail photometric acceptance.

      • Intensity, colour, beam spread
      • Aiming and coverage
      • Flashing sequence where applicable

      Documentation

      • As-built information, equipment identification
      • Calibration certificates, test results
      • Non-conformance report and corrective-action record

      Barrette test

      Verify complete operation, uniform output, correct colour and intensity, orientation, no failed elements and correct beam coverage.

      Flasher acceptance test

      Every flasher operates, with correct sequence, direction, timing and synchronization, appropriate intensity, no unintended simultaneous flashing and correct control status. Take the flash rate from the governing specification.

      Colour verification

      Check colour with intensity, since filters, LEDs and optics affect both. Installations may include white approach lights, green threshold lighting and red components in precision configurations.

      Aiming test

      Design coordinate → installed coordinate → physical orientation → photometric result. Survey the fixture, verify elevation, establish the centreline, set horizontal and vertical aim, energize, measure, fine-adjust, repeat and record the final setting.

      Maintenance

      Maintenance and Low-Visibility Operations

      Daily / weekly

      • Light availability and obvious failures
      • Flashing operation
      • Physical damage and abnormal pattern
      • Light units, lenses, structures, flashers
      • Visible cable sections, corrosion, foundation condition

      Monthly, periodic and annual

      • Transformers, connectors, circuit condition, CCR
      • Structures, mounting, cable entry, control equipment
      • Full physical and structural inspection
      • Electrical testing, survey and alignment
      • Photometry and flasher testing
      • Cable investigation and grounding
      • Control-system verification

      For CAT II/III, link maintenance to the airport’s Low Visibility Procedures. A current Indian example sets separate serviceability thresholds for the inner 450 m and the portion beyond it. A failed outer light and a failed inner light can have different operational consequences, so do not treat all approach lights as equally significant. Unserviceability of specified portions can suspend CAT II/III operations.

      Failure Modes

      Common Approach Lighting Failures

      Click a failure to see typical causes.

      Failed approach light

      Check the chain from source to fixture:

      • Lamp or LED failure
      • Driver failure
      • Transformer failure
      • Connector or cable fault
      • CCR problem

      Incorrect light alignment

      Check what moved:

      • Foundation settlement
      • Mast movement
      • Vehicle impact
      • Structural deformation
      • Incorrect original installation

      Uneven light output

      Compare against the photometric baseline:

      • LED degradation or lamp ageing
      • Optical contamination
      • Incorrect current
      • Damaged optics
      • Wrong intensity setting

      Flasher not sequencing

      Check control and the unit:

      • Controller failure
      • Trigger wiring
      • Power supply
      • Individual flasher failure
      • Synchronization fault

      Water ingress

      Find the cause, not only the water:

      • Failed seals
      • Damaged housing
      • Wrong cable entry
      • Inadequate drainage
      • Damaged connector

      Inspect in detail after new installation, runway reconstruction, threshold relocation or displacement, approach-light extension, pavement rehabilitation near foundations, mast or cable replacement, major structural work, ILS approach geometry changes, PAPI relocation and significant terrain or obstruction changes.

      Related Aids

      ALS, PAPI and ILS

      ALS and PAPI

      ALS gives runway alignment, roll reference, visual runway acquisition, the approach pattern and limited distance-to-go. PAPI gives vertical approach-path guidance. They are complementary, not substitutes.

      ALS and ILS

      ILS is a radio-navigation system and ALS is a visual aid. They are designed to work together, because the aircraft transitions from instrument guidance to visual references in the final stages. The ALS must be geometrically compatible with the runway centreline, threshold and approved approach path. It is not an ordinary electrical lighting project.

      India

      Approach Lighting in India

      Check detailed ALS design and status against DGCA requirements, AAI requirements where applicable, the current AAI eAIP, approved airport drawings, project specifications, runway category, Low Visibility Procedures and manufacturer instructions. Current AAI eAIP publications show CAT I systems of 900 m and 720 m, SALS of 420 m, shorter or abridged SALS, and CAT II/III precision approach lighting. For CAT II/III airports, approach-light availability is tied to LVP serviceability and operational restrictions.

      Indian airport details were not individually re-checked against the current eAIP editions.

      Design photometry in from the start. Fix required intensity, intensity control, beam distribution, colour, orientation, source arrangement, measurement points and tolerances. ICAO’s Aerodrome Design Manual Part 4 covers approach lighting systems and the light characteristics of precision approach runways. The ICAO publication is the 5th Edition (2021) with Amendment 2 dated 15 September 2025.

      Electrical architecture

      Main and standby supply → AGL substation → CCR → series circuit → transformers → ALS fixtures, with ALCMS/ILCMS control and status feedback. For CAT II/III, secondary power is particularly important. Indian AIP material identifies standby power for AGL as part of CAT II/III capability.

      Survey and design tools

      CAD, GIS, survey software, photometric modelling, coordinate transformation, lighting calculation, terrain modelling, obstacle assessment, cable calculation, circuit simulation and as-built databases. Major CAT II/III projects integrate civil, electrical, structural, photometric and aeronautical requirements.

      02

      Applicable documents

      Approved drawings and specification, applicable ICAO provisions, DGCA and AAI requirements, manufacturer instructions, inspection and test plan, and the FAA specification only where the project uses FAA standards.

      03

      Equipment details

      ALS type, light manufacturer, model and source, flasher manufacturer, CCR, transformer, cable and control system.

      04

      Mistakes

      Common ALS Design and Installation Errors

      1

      Treating the ALS as ordinary lighting

      It is an aeronautical visual-aid system.

      2

      Incorrect centreline coordinates

      A small survey error propagates over a long installation.

      3

      Incorrect elevation

      Wrong mast elevation affects the approach-light plane and optical geometry.

      4

      Poor azimuth alignment

      The fixture works but gives wrong lateral guidance.

      5

      Inadequate structural design

      A mast can meet lighting needs yet fail the frangibility requirement.

      6

      Incorrect cable architecture

      Improper series-circuit connections cause repeated failures.

      7

      No photometric acceptance

      Electrical operation does not show optical compliance.

      8

      No as-built survey

      Without final coordinates and elevations, maintenance gets harder.

      9

      Mixing ICAO and FAA terminology

      ALSF-2, MALSR and ODALS are FAA names. ICAO has its own classification.

      10

      Using generic CAT II/III criteria

      Acceptance and serviceability come from the applicable authority and approved conditions.

      Final Engineering Perspective

      An approach light is acceptable only when its location, elevation, structure, electrical performance, optical performance and alignment all conform to the approved design and applicable requirements.

      FAQ

      Approach Lighting FAQ

      Q1What is an airport approach lighting system?

      A visual lighting installation extending from a runway threshold into the approach area. It helps pilots establish runway alignment and visual reference during the transition from instrument flight to visual landing guidance.

      Q2What is ALS in aviation?

      ALS means Approach Light System or Approach Lighting System, one of the major visual aids for runway approach guidance.

      Q3What is the difference between ALS and PAPI?

      ALS mainly gives runway alignment, roll reference and visual acquisition of the runway environment. PAPI gives vertical approach-slope guidance. They do different jobs and are often installed together.

      Q4What is a CAT I approach lighting system?

      A precision approach lighting system for Category I approaches. The ICAO/EASA configuration uses a runway-centreline arrangement with a 300 m crossbar and a full design length of 900 m wherever possible.

      Q5What is a CAT II/III approach lighting system?

      The precision approach lighting configuration for Category II and III operations, with a centreline, side rows and crossbars, and particular importance on the inner approach area and system serviceability.

      Q6Is CAT III approach lighting different from CAT II?

      Under ICAO classification they are grouped as one precision approach Category II and III lighting system. CAT III operation needs a wider set of aerodrome systems and capabilities beyond ALS.

      Q7How long is an approach lighting system?

      A simple system is commonly 420 m where practicable, and precision CAT I and CAT II/III systems are based on 900 m wherever possible. Installed systems may be shorter, so check the approved airport configuration.

      Q8What are ALSF-1 and ALSF-2?

      FAA approach-lighting designations. FAA describes ALSF-1 as a Category I configuration and ALSF-2 as a Category II configuration that may also support Category III operations.

      Q9What is MALSR?

      The FAA Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System with Runway Alignment Indicator Lights. It combines steady-burning light bars with sequenced flashers.

      Q10What are approach lighting flashers?

      Sequenced flashing light units that create a moving visual effect toward the runway and strengthen long-range runway identification and alignment.

      Q11Why are approach lighting masts frangible?

      They stand where aircraft could potentially make contact, so applicable requirements address frangibility or low-impact construction so the support is not an unacceptable hazard. ICAO provides guidance through Aerodrome Design Manual Part 6.

      Q12Why is approach lighting photometry required?

      Electrical operation does not prove optical compliance. A light can be ON with the wrong intensity, colour, beam spread or aiming.

      Q13What tests are required for an ALS?

      Typical commissioning tests include survey verification, physical inspection, electrical tests, CCR tests, transformer and connector checks, optical aiming, colour and intensity verification, photometry, flasher sequencing, control-system testing and documentation review.

      Q14What should an approach lighting test report contain?

      The airport and runway, ALS configuration, equipment, approved criteria, survey results, electrical tests, photometric measurements, flasher results, structural inspection, defects, corrective actions and final acceptance status.
