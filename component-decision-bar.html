Decision Bar

A critical visual reference in precision approach lighting systems, the Decision Bar sits at a fixed 1,000 ft (300 m) from the threshold across virtually all US approach lighting system types. It gives pilots a large, unmistakable visual horizon during the transition from instrument to visual flight around the decision-height region of the approach, helping confirm the runway environment is in sight before the go/no-go call.

Overview

What Is It?

The Decision Bar (also known as the Decision Height Bar) is a row of lights installed perpendicular to the approach centreline at a specific distance from the runway threshold. It marks the point on the approach path where the pilot must make the go/no-go decision — if the runway environment is not visible at this point, a missed approach must be initiated. The Decision Bar is a key component of precision approach lighting systems (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, and other CAT II/III configurations), providing a clear visual reference that enhances safety during low visibility operations.

The Decision Bar is fixed at 1,000 ft (≈300 m) from the threshold on virtually all US approach lighting system types, regardless of the runway's certified decision height. It does not mark the physical point where the aircraft reaches DH — on a standard 3° glide slope, a CAT I 200 ft DH is actually reached several thousand feet farther out, well beyond the approach lighting system itself. Instead, the bar is positioned so that, on a normal glide path, it passes beneath the aircraft at roughly 100 ft AGL, giving the pilot a large, fixed visual horizon to reference during the transition from instrument to visual flight around the decision-height area — not a literal marker of the decision altitude itself. The bar consists of high‑intensity white lights, steady‑burning or flashing depending on system type.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The Decision Bar exists to give the pilot a large, fixed visual horizon during the most consequential moment of an instrument approach: the transition from instrument reference to visual flight around decision height, when the runway environment must be in sight or the pilot must commit to a missed approach. Rather than being surveyed to the exact distance that corresponds to each runway's certified decision height (which varies by category and glide slope, and in most cases falls well beyond the approach lighting system itself), the bar is sited at a single standard distance — 1,000 ft (300 m) from the threshold — used consistently across US approach lighting system configurations. Its value comes from being a large, conspicuous, and reliably positioned reference the pilot can use to judge attitude and closure during that critical phase, not from marking the decision altitude itself.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The Decision Bar is an essential component of precision approach lighting systems and is used on:

  • CAT I precision approach runways (ALSF‑1 configuration) – provides a clear decision point for approaches with 200 ft decision height.
  • CAT II and III precision approach runways (ALSF‑2 configuration) – critical for low visibility operations with decision heights as low as 100 ft or less.
  • Runways with low visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) – ensures pilots have a visual reference at the decision height.
  • Major international airports – where high traffic volumes and adverse weather demand reliable approach guidance.
  • Military airfields – requiring precision approach capability for all‑weather operations.
Placement

Installation & Location

The Decision Bar is erected on its own row of piers set perpendicular to the extended runway centreline at the 300 m (≈1,000 ft) point from the threshold — a fixed siting used across US approach lighting system layouts, including ALSF‑1, rather than a position calculated from any particular runway's certified decision height. Poles either side of the centreline are tied to a common foundation line so the bar presents as a single unbroken span at the same elevation as the centreline barrettes it crosses. Where the approach passes over water or uneven ground, the bar's piers are raised on a platform or bridge structure matching the adjacent approach lighting piers. Cabling loops along the bar from pole to pole back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with an isolating transformer at each pole base, and the whole assembly is set out from the ILS/runway centreline survey to keep the bar square to the approach.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Decision Bar operates as a key visual cue within the approach lighting system:

  • Positioning: The bar is installed at a fixed distance of 1,000 ft (300 m) from the threshold, a standard siting used across US approach lighting configurations rather than one recalculated per runway's glide slope or decision height. It is placed perpendicular to the centreline, spanning the approach corridor.
  • Lighting: The bar comprises multiple high‑intensity white lights (usually 5–7 per side, or a full row across the approach path). They are steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by the CCR (5‑step). In some systems, the bar may flash in sequence to draw attention.
  • Guidance: As the aircraft reaches the decision height, the pilot looks for the Decision Bar. If the bar is clearly visible and the runway environment is acquired, the pilot may continue the approach. If not, a missed approach is executed. The bar provides a positive visual reference that confirms the aircraft is on the correct glide path and at the correct position.
  • Integration: The Decision Bar is integrated with other approach lighting components – centreline barrettes, crossbars, side row bars, and sequenced flashing lights – to form a comprehensive visual guidance system. It is typically the last bar before the threshold, marking the critical decision point.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Decision Bar is not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the approach lighting series circuit, the same as the crossbars and barrettes it crosses. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR or ATC instruction. This command is passed to the approach lighting CCR, which regulates the entire bar's loop to the corresponding fixed current (5-step). Every lamp across the bar, via its own isolating transformer at the base of its pole, draws from the same current and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no way to brighten one section of the bar to draw more attention than another; visual prominence at decision height comes from the bar's position and lamp count, not from any per-fixture control. Fault detection is loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact lamp without further sectionalised testing. Given the bar's role as the last positive visual reference before the go/no-go decision, any reported loop fault on this circuit is treated as high priority for field verification ahead of CAT I operations.

Construction

Physical Construction

The Decision Bar is built like a crossbar: a row of high-intensity lamp heads, each a die-cast aluminium housing with a toughened-glass or polycarbonate lens gasket-sealed for an IP65/IP66 rating, mounted across several frangible poles tied to a common foundation line and set to the same elevation as the centreline barrettes it crosses. Because the bar marks a safety-critical decision point, lamp heads are typically higher-output units (steady-burning or flashing) than the plain centreline barrettes, but share the same housing and lens construction. Each pole incorporates a frangible coupling near its base to shear cleanly on impact, and where the approach crosses water or uneven ground the bar is carried on a raised platform or bridge structure matching the adjacent piers. Cabling loops pole to pole back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with an isolating transformer at each pole base, and stainless-steel fasteners and sealed junction boxes used throughout.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Common Foundation Line Setting
Civil

Set foundations for every pole across the bar to a common line and to the same elevation as the centreline barrettes it crosses, holding tolerance before pole erection.

Frangible Pole Erection
Mechanical

Erect each frangible pole across the bar at matching height, torquing the frangible coupling near the base of every pole so it shears cleanly on impact.

Pole-to-Pole Cable Looping
Electrical

Route series-circuit cable pole to pole across the bar back to the approach lighting control cabinet, avoiding sharp bends that could stress connectors at final termination.

Isolating Transformer Installation
Electrical

Install and confirm the isolating transformer at each pole base matches the higher-output lamp head wattage and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Lamp Head Wiring & Sealing
Electrical

Wire each high-intensity lamp head across the bar and seal its junction box to the fixture's IP65/IP66 rating, keeping all splices weatherproof before energisation.

Elevation Setout Against Centreline
Mechanical

Set the bar's elevation and crossbar level against the adjacent centreline barrettes before final fixing, since the decision point depends on consistent alignment across both.

Raised Platform / Bridge Structure Check
Civil

Where the approach crosses water or uneven ground, confirm the raised platform or bridge structure is structurally complete and matches the adjacent piers before bar installation.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record pole positions, cable routing, transformer ratings, and elevation setout as-built before the bar circuit is energised.

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test each isolating transformer and the approach lighting series loop before the circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity across the bar end to end and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the approach lighting CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each L-823 connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm every lamp on the bar responds uniformly, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Elevation & Alignment Verification
Final Acceptance

Check the pole height, crossbar level, and beam elevation/azimuth of each lamp against the design aiming diagram, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity, colour, and beam spread with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances, confirming the bar is visible from the design range for the approach lighting system it belongs to.

Frangibility & Mounting Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm the frangible coupling, base can depth, and pole/fixture height meet as-built tolerances before the site is finally backfilled.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Precise Distance Calibration

The Decision Bar must be installed at its standard 1,000 ft (300 m) distance from the threshold — this siting is fixed by the approach lighting system design, not recalculated per runway decision height. Inaccurate positioning can mislead pilots and compromise safety. Regular surveys using precision GPS or theodolites are essential, especially after pavement resurfacing or threshold displacement.

Light Obscuration by Fog or Rain

In low visibility conditions, the bar's light output must be sufficient to penetrate fog and rain. Lens contamination or reduced intensity can obscure the bar at the critical moment. Regular photometric testing and cleaning are vital to maintain performance.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated Decision Bar lights are subject to wind loading and thermal cycling, which can fatigue frangible couplings. Regular inspection and replacement per manufacturer recommendations ensure breakaway performance on impact.

Power Supply and Cable Integrity

The Decision Bar is part of the series circuit; a single fault can affect the entire bar. Cable damage, connector corrosion, or transformer failure can cause partial or complete outage. Regular insulation testing and visual inspection of underground cables are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Bar Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module/lamp, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the connectors before backfill.
Entire Decision Bar Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop feeding the bar (severed cable, corroded splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked lenses, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Reduced Light Penetration in Fog or Rain
Likely Cause
Lens contamination or an aging LED array has reduced the bar's intensity below what is needed to remain visible through low-visibility conditions.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against the required output for the applicable CCR step; inspect and clean the lens; compare output against a known-good fixture.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens or replace the degraded LED array/lamp, and re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading before the next low-visibility period.
Bar Positioned Incorrectly Relative to Decision Height
Likely Cause
Survey error, pavement resurfacing, or ground movement has shifted the bar from its calibrated distance from the threshold.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-survey the bar's position against its standard 1,000 ft (300 m) design distance from the threshold using precision GPS or a theodolite.
Corrective Action
Reposition the bar to the correct calibrated distance and re-verify against the survey before returning it to service.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling has fatigued from wind loading and thermal cycling, or the base structure has settled.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically check the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against tolerance; inspect the base for settlement.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling on schedule if fatigued, re-level and re-aim the fixture, and address any base settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Module / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised LED board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
A single fixture within the bar goes dark or drops below minimum intensity, reducing lamp count at the single most consequential reference point on the approach.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken a single lamp or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire decision bar span.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Series Cable / Connector Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or cable jacket damage from age or nearby excavation.
Effect
Intermittent operation, a partial ground fault degrading insulation resistance, or a full open circuit that darkens the whole bar — since the bar is a single series circuit, one fault can affect the entire span.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each pole base during maintenance.
Lens Contamination / Optical Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits, de-icing fluid residue, and prolonged UV/weather exposure building up on the outer lens surface.
Effect
Reduced light penetration through fog or rain right at the point where the pilot must positively acquire the runway environment, undermining the bar's role as the final go/no-go reference.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing against the required output for the applicable CCR step, and visual lens inspection during routine cleaning.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading on the elevated pole and thermal cycling of the coupling material over the service interval.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or separates from its base, taking the light out of tolerance or out of service.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim/level checks against tolerance.
Bar Position Drift Relative to Decision Height
High Severity
Root Cause
Survey error at installation, pavement resurfacing, or ground movement shifting the bar's foundation line from its calibrated distance from the threshold.
Effect
The bar no longer sits at its standard 1,000 ft (300 m) design distance from the threshold, weakening the visual-horizon cue it is meant to provide during the transition to visual flight.
Detection Method
Precision GPS or theodolite re-survey of the bar's distance from the threshold, referenced against its standard 1,000 ft (300 m) design distance.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step or failure to hold regulated current across the bar's loop, right at the fixture where visibility matters most for the decision call.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Pier / Base Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure on approaches over water or uneven ground.
Effect
One or more poles along the bar tilt or shift, breaking the bar's presentation as a single unbroken span at the correct elevation.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of pier foundations during annual maintenance, and a full-span alignment survey against the design pattern.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Decision Bar fixtures sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the pole base before climbing or handling connectors.

Elevated Multi-Pole Work
Personnel Safety

The bar spans several poles at once; confirm every pole feeding it is isolated, not just the one being worked on, and use fall protection appropriate to pole height, especially where piers are carried on a raised platform or bridge over water.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible pole with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; each pole must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft on a low approach or go-around.

Critical Reference — Minimise Outage
Operational

This bar marks the pilot's last visual reference before the go/no-go decision on a CAT II/III approach; schedule work for low-traffic periods, keep any planned outage as short as possible, and always cover the work with ATC coordination and a NOTAM.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the entire decision reference.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave any light in the bar dark, misaligned, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; verify the full bar's photometric conformance before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM, given how safety-critical this reference is.

Verify Position Against the Published Decision Height
Installation

The Decision Bar's value depends entirely on sitting at its correct, fixed 1,000 ft (300 m) distance from the threshold; resurvey its position after any ALS reconfiguration or threshold displacement rather than assuming the original placement still holds.

Steady vs Flashing Mode Must Match the ALS Configuration
Configuration

Whether the Decision Bar burns steady or flashes depends on the specific ALSF-1/ALSF-2/CAT II-III configuration in use; after any control-cabinet or firmware change, confirm the bar is actually operating in the mode the approach procedure assumes, not just that it is illuminated.

Line-of-Sight Obstruction Grows Slowly
Siting

Vegetation growth or new construction near the approach path can gradually degrade the Decision Bar's visibility from the cockpit long before anyone files a complaint; include a sightline check from the nominal approach path in the periodic inspection, not just a fixture-level photometric test.

Treat It as a Critical Circuit, Not Just Another Bar
Electrical

Because the Decision Bar is the last visual cue before a mandatory go/no-go call, verify it sits on the same protected, monitored circuit tier as threshold and touchdown zone lighting — not a lower-priority branch where a fault could go unnoticed until reported.

Match Intensity Steps With the Rest of the ALS
Retrofit

An LED Decision Bar retrofit needs its brightness-step curve validated against the rest of the approach lighting system, not just its own datasheet; a bar that reads noticeably brighter or dimmer than the crossbars around it undercuts the visual cue it exists to provide.

Trend Loop Readings on the Approach Feeder
Troubleshooting

The Decision Bar often sits far down a long approach lighting feeder run; log insulation resistance trends on that circuit specifically, since a slow-developing fault far out on the run can look identical to a fixture problem until it's isolated.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the decision bar: role in precision approach lighting, siting, photometrics, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a decision bar?

The decision bar, also called the decision height bar, is a row of lights installed perpendicular to the approach centreline at a specific distance from the runway threshold. It marks the point on the approach where the pilot makes the go/no-go decision.

Q2Why is the decision bar important?

It gives the pilot a large, fixed visual horizon at the most consequential moment of an instrument approach: the transition from instrument reference to visual flight around decision height, when the runway environment must be in sight.

Q3Where is the decision bar used?

On precision approach lighting systems, including CAT I runways (ALSF-1) for approaches with a 200 ft decision height, and CAT II and III runways.

Q4Where is the decision bar positioned?

At a fixed distance of 1,000 ft (300 m) from the threshold for a 3° glide slope, as specified by the approach design.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

An LED array or 45/65 W halogen source, white light (steady or flashing) of 10,000-20,000 cd, 5-7 lights per side or a full row depending on the system, unidirectional toward approaching aircraft, and elevated frangible or flush mounting rated IP67/IP68.

Q6How is the decision bar powered?

From a CCR-fed constant current series circuit at 6.6 A with a 5-step schedule, with a 45/65 W isolating transformer per light, L-824 cable and L-823 connectors.

Q7What photometric performance is expected?

A main beam of 10,000-20,000 cd white, a horizontal spread of ±4.5° either side of the centreline plane spanning the bar, a ±25% tolerance at each CCR step, and chromaticity within the ICAO Annex 14 white boundary.

Q8Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46, FAA AC 150/5340-30 (ALSF-1/ALSF-2 layout and configuration) and IEC TS 61827.

Q9How often should a decision bar be maintained?

Quarterly lens cleaning, and annually distance calibration surveys (especially after pavement resurfacing), photometric checks, frangible coupling checks and cable and connector inspection.

Q10What if a single decision bar fixture is dark?

A failed LED module or lamp, or an open isolating transformer. Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary.
