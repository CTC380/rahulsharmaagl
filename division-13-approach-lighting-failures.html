Approach Lighting Failures

The dedicated failure classification for approach-lighting systems — covering the complete chain from individual approach-light fixture failure through electrical, structural, optical, alignment, control and weather-related failures. Approach lighting differs from ordinary runway/taxiway lighting because failures can involve elevated structures, crossbars, poles, foundations and frangible components, in addition to the lighting/electrical system. Do not record an approach light simply as “failed.” Identify the failure mode, the affected component or structure, the technical mechanism, the operational consequence and the corrective action.

Initial Issue

Key principle — Division 13 distinguishes component failure, structural failure, lighting-service failure and system-level failure. A failed light should never simply be logged as “approach light failed” — every record should trace the actual technical mechanism: what failed → how did it fail → why did it fail → what evidence confirmed it → how was it corrected → how can recurrence be prevented?

ALF-19 Multiple Approach-Light Failure
Failure Definition

Failure of multiple approach-light fixtures or sections during the same incident or related failure event.

Possible Causes
  • Circuit failure
  • CCR failure
  • Power loss
  • Common cable failure
  • Control failure
  • Severe weather
  • Structural event
Important Investigation Principle

Look for the common cause, rather than treating every failed fixture independently. For example:

COMMON CIRCUIT FAULTMULTIPLE TRANSFORMERS UNAVAILABLEMULTIPLE APPROACH LIGHTS OUT
Severity

Multiple approach-light failures should normally receive greater investigation priority than an isolated fixture failure because they may indicate a common-mode system vulnerability.

F. Failure Severity

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging:

Critical / Safety-Critical — immediate operational or safety risk

Major / Operationally Critical — significant availability impact

Moderate — reduced performance, not yet critical

Minor / Non-Critical — limited or no operational effect

Degradation / Intermittent / Latent — developing or hidden fault

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about approach-lighting failures, as covered in Division 13 of the AGL Failure Database.

Q1What is Division 13 — Approach Lighting Failures in the AGL Failure Database?

Division 13 covers failures in approach-lighting systems. It contains 20 published topics (ALF-01 to ALF-20) spanning fixture, lamp, LED, driver, cable, connector and transformer failures, structural and frangible-component damage, misalignment, optical degradation, moisture, corrosion, circuit and control failures, and weather-related damage.

Q2Which approach-lighting failures does Division 13 cover?

Approach fixture failure, lamp, LED, driver, cable, connector and transformer failure, structure damage, crossbar damage, pole damage, foundation failure, frangible-component failure, misalignment, optical degradation, moisture ingress, corrosion, circuit failure, control failure, multiple approach-light failure and weather-related damage.

Q3How are the 20 approach-lighting topics grouped?

They fall into six groups: Lighting & Component Failures (7 topics), Structural & Mechanical Failures (5), Optical & Alignment Failures (2), Environmental Failures (2), Circuit & Control Failures (2) and System-Level Failures (2).

Q4What approach-lighting failure chains does Division 13 capture?

In the individual-fixture chain, an LED or lamp failure leads to approach fixture failure, an individual approach-light outage and approach-lighting pattern degradation. In the circuit chain, a cable failure leads to circuit failure and multiple approach-light failure.

Q5Where should the investigation start for an approach-lighting failure?

The diagnostic matrix assigns a primary investigation to each topic. Approach fixture failure starts with complete fixture condition, lamp failure with lamp output, LED failure with LED/module condition, driver failure with driver input and output, cable failure with continuity and insulation, connector failure with contact and connection, transformer failure with transformer electrical condition, and structure damage with structural integrity.

Q6How does an approach-lighting failure become engineering intelligence?

Every failure follows a loop: failure event, failure mode, failure mechanism, root cause, corrective action, preventive action and post-repair test. For example, a multiple approach-light failure is traced to a common circuit fault, then to cable insulation breakdown caused by moisture ingress, leading to cable replacement, a review of sealing and drainage, and a post-repair test.

Q7How are failure severity and operational impact classified?

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging. The classes are Critical / Safety-Critical (immediate operational or safety risk), Major / Operationally Critical (significant availability impact), Moderate (reduced performance, not yet critical), Minor / Non-Critical (limited or no operational effect), and Degradation / Intermittent / Latent (a developing or hidden fault).

Q8How does the Failure Database differ from the Troubleshooting Atlas?

The Troubleshooting Atlas answers “I have a fault now — how do I diagnose and fix it?” The Failure Database answers “this failure happened — what happened, why, what evidence proves it, what was done, and how do we stop it recurring?” Kept strictly separate, then linked end to end, the two form an AGL Failure Intelligence Engine rather than a simple fault list.
