Direction Sign

A yellow sign with black text and directional arrows, guiding pilots and vehicle drivers to taxiways, runways, aprons, and other airfield locations. Provides essential navigational information for safe and efficient surface movements.

Overview

What Is It?

A Direction Sign is a yellow sign with black text and arrows that indicates the direction to specific airfield locations, such as taxiways, runways, aprons, and other facilities. These signs are a key component of the airfield surface movement guidance system, helping pilots and vehicle drivers navigate the complex network of taxiways and aprons, especially during low‑visibility conditions. The signs use a standardized format with arrows pointing left, right, or ahead, and text indicating the destination (e.g., "A", "B", "RWY 09", "CARGO"). They are typically mounted on frangible supports adjacent to taxiways.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The direction sign's purpose is to resolve a taxiway routing choice before the pilot reaches the intersection where that choice has to be acted on, rather than leaving them to decide in the moment. By pairing an arrow with a destination identifier ahead of a junction, the sign lets the crew commit to the correct turn early and smoothly, which matters on a taxiway network where a late or incorrect turn can mean crossing into an area not covered by the current clearance. This anticipatory placement is what separates it from the destination sign, which confirms arrival rather than guiding the approach to a decision point.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at taxiway intersections, holding points, and complex junction points where pilots need guidance on which route to take. They are also used at the entrances to aprons, cargo areas, and maintenance facilities. Direction signs are placed on the side of the taxiway, typically at a height of 1.5 to 2 metres above the pavement, and are positioned to be easily readable from the cockpit. They are used at all airfields with multiple taxiways and are especially critical at large international airports.

Placement

Installation & Location

Posts are founded on the shoulder just before the junction or holding point the sign describes, set back clear of wingtip clearance for the design aircraft, with the panel oriented so the arrow reads correctly for a pilot approaching from the expected direction rather than square to the local pavement edge. At multi-branch junctions, several direction sign posts may be grouped on a common foundation so the full set of arrows is read together rather than encountered one at a time. Illuminated panels are cabled from the nearest taxiway lighting distribution point; retroreflective panels need only the post foundation and fixing, with setout tied to the junction geometry survey rather than a fixed offset.

Function

How Does It Work?

The sign displays black text and arrows on a yellow background, following ICAO Annex 14 and FAA standards. The arrows indicate the direction of travel to the destination, with text providing the name or designation of the location (e.g., "TAXIWAY A", "RUNWAY 27", "APRON 2"). The sign may be non‑illuminated (using retroreflective sheeting) or internally illuminated (LED or fluorescent) for nighttime visibility. The design ensures legibility from a distance and allows pilots to make timely routing decisions. The sign is mounted on a frangible or breakaway support to minimise aircraft damage in case of collision.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

An illuminated direction sign draws from the constant current series circuit at the nearest taxiway lighting distribution point, via its own isolating transformer, and follows the same loop-level control as every other AGL fixture on that circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects the brightness step for the loop based on prevailing RVR or ATC instruction, the CCR regulates the whole loop to that fixed current, and the sign brightens or dims together with the taxiway edge lights and any other fixtures sharing the same circuit — there is no independent control of the sign's illumination, and no electrical distinction between its left, straight, and right arrow segments; all illuminate at the same commanded intensity together. Fault detection is loop-based: a voltage rise at constant current signals an open-circuit lamp or failed transformer somewhere on the loop, reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to the sign without further sectionalised testing. Because a direction sign is read upstream of the decision point it supports, a reported fault on its loop is generally treated with more urgency than a fault on a destination sign further along the route, since a pilot approaching the junction may be relying on it to choose the correct branch. A non-illuminated, retroreflective direction panel has no relationship to this circuit at all — it needs no cable or transformer and remains legible purely through reflected aircraft and vehicle light.

Construction

Physical Construction

The face is built the same way as the destination sign it commonly sits beside — an extruded aluminium frame around a Class 1/2 retroreflective panel or, for illuminated versions, a translucent acrylic panel backlit by an internal LED module — but the film or screen-printed legend adds the standard directional arrow (left, right, or straight) alongside the black taxiway designation on the yellow ground. The panel is gasket-sealed and bolted to a frangible coupling on a below-grade base plate so it shears away cleanly on impact. Because the arrow has to read correctly at a glance during the turn itself, the arrow artwork is oversized relative to the text and positioned to match the physical direction of the junction rather than a generic layout, so panels are effectively junction-specific even where the text content repeats elsewhere on the airfield.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Setting-Out & Siting Survey
Civil

Survey and mark the sign position from the design/siting drawing before excavation, confirming clearance from the taxiway/runway edge safety area and any frangibility height limit.

Foundation / Base Plate Installation
Civil

Excavate and set the base plate or footing to design depth, keeping the finished mount level and within the frangible height limit for the sign's location.

Frangible Mount Fitting
Mechanical

Fit the frangible post or breakaway coupling and torque to the specified value, confirming it shears as designed under lateral load and does not bind.

Cable Route & Connector Termination
Electrical

Where the sign is internally illuminated, lay the duct/cable route to the panel and terminate the L-823 connector per manufacturer procedure, keeping the splice fully submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Where the sign is internally illuminated, confirm the isolating transformer wattage and ratio match the panel and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Panel Legend & Content Setting
Quality

Before the panel is fixed and the position closed out, confirm the arrow orientation and destination text match the taxiway layout diagram, and that the arrow reads unambiguously from the approach angle of a taxiing pilot or vehicle driver.

Retroreflective Sheeting Application
Mechanical

Apply and inspect the retroreflective sheeting for correct grade, alignment, and freedom from bubbles or edge lifting before the panel is mounted.

Pre-Backfill Sign-off
Documentation

Record base/footing position, frangible coupling torque, cable route, and panel content as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Where the sign is internally illuminated, megger-test the isolating transformer and internal wiring before the circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

For an illuminated installation, verify series loop continuity through the sign and record loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at the illuminated sign's L-823 connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm an illuminated panel responds correctly at each, ruling out a marginal transformer or a loose connection.

Retroreflectivity / Luminance Verification
Final Acceptance

Measure sheeting retroreflectivity with a retroreflectometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning minimums; where the panel is internally illuminated, also verify luminance and colour uniformity with a luminance meter.

Legend & Content Verification
Final Acceptance

Confirm the arrow orientation and destination text match the taxiway layout diagram, and that the arrow reads unambiguously from the approach angle of a taxiing pilot or vehicle driver.

Frangible Coupling & Mounting Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm the frangible coupling, base depth, and panel height meet as-built tolerances before the site is finally backfilled.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record retroreflectivity/luminance readings, and (where illuminated) insulation, continuity, and voltage readings, alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Retroreflectivity Check
Annual

Measure sheeting retroreflectivity with a retroreflectometer against ICAO / FAA minimums, and budget for sheeting replacement roughly every 5–7 years.

Illumination System Test
Annual

Test internal LED or fluorescent illumination for uniform, even lighting so the sign remains legible at night, especially ahead of low-visibility seasons.

Frangible Coupling Inspection
Annual

Check the coupling or post for brittleness or corrosion and replace per manufacturer schedule to preserve breakaway performance on impact.

Seal & Cable Gland Check
Annual

Inspect housing seals and cable glands for degradation, since moisture ingress through damaged seals is a leading cause of corrosion and electrical faults.

Sign Face Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean dirt, de-icing fluid residue, and rubber deposits off the sign face to keep both reflectivity and legibility within spec.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test internal wiring and any isolating transformer to confirm insulation resistance stays above minimum, catching moisture ingress early.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Retroreflective Sheeting Degradation

UV exposure, weathering, and abrasion can reduce reflectivity, making signs hard to read at night. Regular inspection and replacement (typically every 5–7 years) is recommended.

Internal Illumination Failure

LED or fluorescent lighting can fail, reducing nighttime legibility. Regular testing of the illumination system and replacement of failed components is essential.

Frangible Support Fatigue

Breakaway couplings can become brittle over time. Regular inspection and replacement as per manufacturer guidelines are required to ensure breakaway performance.

Moisture Ingress

Water ingress through seals can cause corrosion and electrical faults. Routine seal checks and use of desiccants are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Legend or Arrow Unreadable at Night, Fine by Day
Likely Cause
Retroreflective sheeting has degraded past its useful reflectivity, or (on an illuminated sign) the internal lighting has failed or dimmed.
Diagnostic Steps
Take a night or low-light reflectivity/luminance reading against the applicable ICAO/FAA minimum; on illuminated signs, confirm supply voltage is present at the fixture before condemning the lamp or LED module.
Corrective Action
Replace the retroreflective face panel if sheeting is below minimum, or swap the LED module/lamp if illumination has failed; re-verify with a follow-up reading.
Illuminated Sign Dark or Flickering
Likely Cause
Failed LED module or fluorescent lamp, a loose/corroded cable connection at the base can, or an upstream circuit fault feeding the sign.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for supply voltage at the sign's connector; inspect and re-torque cable terminations; if voltage is present but the sign stays dark, isolate the lamp/LED module.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp or LED module, or re-terminate corroded connections with fresh crimps and sealant; confirm the upstream circuit if no voltage reaches the sign.
Moisture or Condensation Inside Housing
Likely Cause
Failed gasket or seal at the frame or cable entry, allowing water ingress and risking corrosion of internal electrical contacts.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect gaskets and cable glands for cracking or compression set; check for visible condensation or staining inside the housing; verify desiccant (if fitted) hasn't saturated.
Corrective Action
Replace failed gaskets and cable glands, dry out the housing, refresh or add desiccant, and re-check for recurrence at the next inspection interval.
Arrow or Text Appears to Point the Wrong Way
Likely Cause
The sign has rotated on a fatigued or partially sheared frangible coupling, or was reinstalled/re-aimed incorrectly after prior maintenance.
Diagnostic Steps
Sight along the arrow from the intended decision point to confirm it still points to the correct taxiway or apron; check the frangible coupling for play and compare orientation against the layout drawing.
Corrective Action
Re-orient and re-secure the sign to the correct heading, replace the frangible coupling if it is the cause of the rotation, and record the corrected orientation for future reference.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Panel / Internal Light Source Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or driver-circuit fault in the LED panel of an illuminated unit, or a manufacturing defect in the light source.
Effect
Sign face goes dark or dim on the illuminated variant, removing the sign's night/low-visibility conspicuity while the panel itself remains structurally intact.
Detection Method
Night or low-light visual check against the panel's expected output, and driver-circuit voltage check at the housing.
Retroreflective Sheeting Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure, surface abrasion, or accumulated grime/rubber film reducing the sheeting's coefficient of retroreflection over time.
Effect
Reduced legibility under aircraft or vehicle landing-light illumination at night, most critical for non-illuminated units that depend entirely on retroreflectivity for visibility.
Detection Method
Scheduled retroreflectivity measurement against ICAO Annex 14 / FAA minimums, and visual inspection for sheeting delamination or contamination.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Impact Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading fatiguing the frangible coupling over time, or a direct strike from a vehicle, aircraft component, or ground equipment.
Effect
Sign leans, misaligns, or separates from its post, degrading legibility and, if debris remains in the movement area, creating a foreign-object hazard.
Detection Method
Post alignment and coupling condition check during scheduled inspection, and immediate post-incident inspection following any reported strike.
Power/Circuit Connector Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress at the base can, gasket, or connector feeding an illuminated unit, or a corroded/loose termination from age.
Effect
Intermittent illumination or a partial ground fault on the feeding circuit, affecting the illuminated unit's reliability independent of the panel itself.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the feeder circuit, and visual inspection of the base can, gasket, and connector during maintenance.
Legend/Arrow Mismatch After Junction Reconfiguration
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Directional arrow or taxiway designation on the sign face not updated to match a subsequent junction or routing reconfiguration.
Effect
Sign points pilots toward a route that no longer matches the current junction layout, creating a wrong-turn risk at a complex intersection.
Detection Method
Cross-check of direction and arrow legends against the current taxiway routing chart whenever a junction is reconfigured.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit Isolation Before Access
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the feeder circuit at the CCR or panel, then verify zero energy at the base can before opening the housing or handling cable connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near the movement area requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the sign will be dark, obscured, or out of tolerance, with vehicle and personnel movement kept clear of the taxiway or runway strip per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

FOD Control at the Base
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the base can or housing is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the sign, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or panel work, megger test the affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the feeder circuit or damage healthy units on the same loop.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on circuit connections, and confirm the sign housing and base can are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the circuit.

Legend & Arrow Accuracy Verification
Operational

After any junction reconfiguration, cross-check the sign's directional arrows and taxiway designations against the current routing chart before signing off the work as complete.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Arrow Angle Should Match the Actual Turn, Not a Catalogue Symbol
Design

At non-standard junction geometries, verify the mounted arrow angle reflects the real turn rather than defaulting to a standard 90° or 45° symbol from the sign catalogue — a technically-present arrow that doesn't match the geometry is worse than no arrow.

A Junction Change Usually Touches More Than One Sign
Operational

Reconfiguring a junction often requires updating direction signs on multiple approaches, not just the one nearest the physical change. Walk every approach to the junction before closing out the work.

A Partially-Lit Panel Can Look Like a Different Instruction
Troubleshooting

A direction sign with one failed LED segment at night can appear to show a different arrow-and-destination combination than intended. Verify full-panel illumination during night checks, not just “is it lit.”

Read-Distance Depends on Height and Setback More Than Legend Size
Design

A pilot's ability to react in time depends heavily on mounting height and setback from the decision point. A legibility complaint sometimes needs a position review before a face reprint solves anything.

Verify Legibility at Both the Roll-Through Speed and the Turn-In Speed
Inspection

A sign set out for a rolling read at taxi speed may need different sizing or setback where the same junction also serves a slower turn-in movement. Check both use cases, not just one.

Recurring Confusion at One Junction May Not Be a Sign Problem
Troubleshooting

A pattern of pilot confusion at one location, even when the legend and arrows check out individually, can point to a junction geometry or lighting issue the sign alone can't fix. Escalate to a layout review rather than repeatedly re-verifying the sign.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the direction sign: purpose, siting, power, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a direction sign?

A direction sign is a yellow sign with black text and arrows that indicates the direction to specific airfield locations such as taxiways, runways, aprons and other facilities. It is a key part of the airfield surface movement guidance system, especially in low visibility.

Q2What does a direction sign display?

Black text and arrows on a yellow background, following ICAO Annex 14 and FAA standards. Arrows point left, right or ahead, and the text names the destination, for example A, B, TAXIWAY A, RWY 09, RUNWAY 27, APRON 2 or CARGO.

Q3Where are direction signs installed?

At taxiway intersections, holding points and complex junctions where pilots need guidance on which route to take, and at the entrances to aprons, cargo areas and maintenance facilities. They stand at the side of the taxiway, typically 1.5 to 2 metres above the pavement, positioned to be easily readable from the cockpit.

Q4How does a direction sign differ from a taxiway location sign?

A direction sign shows where to go, with arrows on a yellow background. A taxiway location sign shows which taxiway the aircraft is currently on, as yellow text on a black background. They are commonly installed together.

Q5How is an illuminated direction sign powered?

An illuminated direction sign is wired onto the constant current series circuit for its section of the airfield and fed through its own isolating transformer rather than directly from the loop. The Constant Current Regulator (CCR) holds the loop at a fixed current (up to 6.6 A, with steps of 2.8, 3.4, 4.1, 5.2 and 6.6 A) and the ALCMS selects the brightness step, so the sign burns at the same intensity as the other fixtures on that loop. The non-illuminated variant is a passive retroreflective panel with no electrical supply.

Q6Which standards apply to direction signs?

ICAO Annex 14 covers colours, dimensions and placement, and FAA AC 150/5340-18 covers siting, design and colour convention. FAA AC 150/5345-44 is the equipment specification for the sign unit, ASTM D4956 sets retroreflective sheeting performance, and IEC 60598 covers luminaires for illuminated signs.

Q7What are the photometric requirements for a direction sign?

On the illuminated variant the yellow background should average at least 30 cd/m² (uniformity no worse than 10:1) with an opaque black legend and arrow giving a contrast ratio of at least 5:1. The non-illuminated variant needs a retroreflective coefficient of at least 20 cd/lux/m² for yellow per ASTM D4956 Class 1 or 2.

Q8Why is a direction sign mounted on a frangible support?

The panel sits on a frangible post or breakaway coupling so that it breaks away if an aircraft strikes it, limiting damage to the aircraft. The coupling can turn brittle from weather and vibration, so it is inspected annually and replaced to the manufacturer's schedule to preserve its breakaway performance.

Q9How often should direction signs be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cleaning dirt, de-icing fluid residue and rubber deposits off the sign face, and annual for retroreflectivity measurement, illumination testing, frangible coupling inspection, seal and cable gland checks, and insulation resistance (megger) testing. Retroreflective sheeting is usually budgeted for replacement every 5 to 7 years.

Q10What are the common field issues with direction signs?

Four issues recur: retroreflective sheeting degradation from UV, weathering and abrasion (replacement is typically due every 5 to 7 years); failure of the internal LED illumination, which cuts night visibility; frangible support fatigue, as breakaway couplings turn brittle with weather and vibration; and moisture ingress through damaged seals or cable glands, which causes corrosion and electrical faults. Routine seal checks and desiccants are recommended. After a junction is reconfigured, a legend or arrow mismatch is also a listed failure mode, and an arrow or text that appears to point the wrong way is a listed troubleshooting case.
