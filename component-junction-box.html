Junction Box

A protective enclosure used to house and protect electrical connections, splices, and terminations in AGL systems. Junction boxes provide a secure, weatherproof environment for cable joints, isolating transformer connections, and circuit branching, ensuring reliable and safe power distribution across the airfield.

Overview

What Is It?

A Junction Box (also called a connection box or distribution box) is an enclosure used to protect and organise electrical connections, splices, and terminations in AGL power and control circuits. In airfield ground lighting systems, junction boxes are critical for:

  • Housing cable joints and splices for primary and secondary AGL cables
  • Providing termination points for isolating transformers and light fixtures
  • Enabling branch circuits and tap connections in the series loop
  • Offering a protected interface for testing, maintenance, and troubleshooting

Junction boxes are available in various sizes, materials, and ingress protection ratings to suit different installation environments — from above-ground equipment shelters to underground manholes and direct burial applications.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The junction box's purpose is to give the many cable joints, splices, and taps in an AGL circuit a protected, accessible home rather than leaving them exposed underground or inside conduit where a fault could go undetected and unreachable. Because AGL series circuits run for kilometres and connect to hundreds of individual isolating transformers, the connections between primary cable, transformer, and fixture are inevitable — the junction box's sealed, strain-relieved enclosure is what keeps those connections from becoming the weak point in an otherwise reliable circuit, protecting them from moisture ingress and mechanical damage while still allowing a technician to open the box, test the joint, and re-seal it during maintenance or troubleshooting.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Junction boxes are deployed throughout the AGL infrastructure, including:

  • Runway and Taxiway Lighting Circuits: At the interface between primary cables and isolating transformers, and between transformers and fixtures.
  • Power Distribution Systems: In CCR rooms and substations to distribute power to multiple circuits.
  • Control and Monitoring Networks: In ALCMS cabinets and remote I/O panels for signal and communication wiring.
  • Underground Networks: In manholes and handholes for cable splices and branch connections.
  • Equipment Shelters: Inside electrical rooms to terminate incoming and outgoing cables.
Placement

Installation & Location

Junction boxes are installed wherever cables need to be spliced, tapped, or transitioned along the AGL network — between primary cable and isolating transformer, between transformer and fixture, in manholes and handholes along underground runs, and inside CCR rooms and equipment shelters to terminate incoming and outgoing circuits. Field-buried boxes are set in a handhole or pit at a depth consistent with the surrounding cable trench, with gland entries sealed against water ingress and enough internal space to make and insulate all splices without crowding. Indoor units are wall- or frame-mounted at a height and position that keeps terminations accessible, with the box location chosen so cable runs on either side stay within a straight, well-supported path rather than adding unnecessary bends or slack.

Function

How Does It Work?

A junction box provides a safe and accessible enclosure for electrical connections. Cables enter through cable glands or conduit entries and are terminated inside using terminal blocks, splice connectors, or direct soldering. The box is designed to:

  • Protect connections from moisture, dust, and mechanical damage (IP rated).
  • Organise wiring with sufficient space for cable bending and labelling.
  • Allow access for routine inspection, testing, and maintenance via removable covers.
  • Provide strain relief through secure cable entry points, preventing pull-out forces from damaging connections.

In AGL series circuits, junction boxes are often used to splice the loop conductor through in series to successive isolating transformers, or to tap off the loop for a local fixture connection.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A junction box has no control logic — it is a passive electrical node whose only architectural role is to preserve continuity of the circuit passing through it while providing a sealed, accessible tap-off point. In an AGL series circuit, the loop conductor typically runs straight through the box, entering and leaving through separate glanded ports, while a spliced tap inside feeds the isolating transformer or fixture local to that box; this means a fault at the junction box (a failed seal allowing moisture ingress, for example) shows up to the CCR as a loop-impedance anomaly rather than as a report from the box itself, since the box has no way to communicate its own condition. Detecting a faulty junction box is therefore a matter of loop-level fault indication from the CCR narrowing the search, followed by physical inspection of the boxes on that loop — the box's role in the architecture is purely to be found and opened, not to report.

Construction

Physical Construction

The enclosure body is moulded or cast in one of several materials depending on where it sits in the field — UV-stabilised thermoplastic (polycarbonate or polyamide) for lightweight above-ground boxes, cast aluminium or stainless steel for heavier-duty or corrosive environments, and glass-reinforced polyester (GRP) where chemical resistance is a priority — each finished to achieve the box's rated ingress and impact protection. A gasketed lid, secured with captive stainless-steel screws or a quarter-turn latch, seals the enclosure against water and dust while remaining quick to open for cable work. Cable entries are formed as knockouts or pre-drilled and threaded holes sized to metric (M20–M63) or imperial (PG9–PG48) glands, with blanking plugs fitted to any entries left unused so the enclosure's IP rating isn't compromised. Internally, a mounting plate or DIN rail carries terminal blocks, tap-off connectors, or splice kits, and buried boxes sit on a bed of compacted stone or sand with a surrounding drainage layer so water doesn't pool against the enclosure or its cable glands.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test all circuits terminated in the box before backfill or final closure, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Cable Entry Seal & Gland Torque Check
Pre-Energisation

Confirm each cable gland is correctly sized, sealed, and torqued to the manufacturer's specification to maintain the box's rated ingress protection.

Loop Tap-Off Continuity Test
Energisation

Verify series loop continuity through the box's tap-off terminations, confirming the splice has not introduced added resistance into the circuit.

Terminal & Connector Torque Verification
Energisation

Check and re-torque all terminal block or connector fixings to specification, a common source of intermittent faults if left loose.

Ingress Protection (IP) Verification
Final Acceptance

Confirm the closed enclosure meets its rated IP66/IP67/IP68 ingress protection class, checking lid gaskets and unused knockouts are properly sealed.

Enclosure & Mounting Verification
Final Acceptance

Check the enclosure is correctly mounted (surface, flush, or pole) and that impact rating (IK07–IK10) is appropriate for its location and exposure.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, and IP verification results alongside as-built box position and circuit schedule in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Water Ingress Through Cable Glands

Improperly tightened or damaged cable glands are a primary cause of water ingress, leading to corrosion and short circuits. Regular inspection and replacement of worn glands, along with proper torque application, is essential.

Corrosion of Internal Connections

Moisture and contaminants can cause oxidation of terminal blocks and splice connectors, increasing contact resistance. Using anti-oxidant compounds and sealed terminal blocks mitigates this risk.

Overheating Due to Poor Ventilation

Junction boxes with high-current connections can overheat if not adequately ventilated or if the enclosure is too small. Proper sizing and heat dissipation measures (e.g., larger enclosures, venting) are important for reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Moisture or Water Found Inside the Box
Likely Cause
An improperly tightened or damaged cable gland has allowed water ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect each cable gland for correct torque, cracking, or missing seals; check the enclosure lid gasket for damage.
Corrective Action
Replace worn glands and gaskets, re-torque to the correct specification, and dry out the enclosure before re-energising.
High Resistance or Intermittent Connection at a Terminal
Likely Cause
Oxidation on terminal blocks or splice connectors from moisture and contaminant exposure.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect terminals for discoloration or corrosion; measure resistance across suspect connections.
Corrective Action
Clean and re-terminate the connection with anti-oxidant compound, and use sealed terminal blocks where recurring corrosion is an issue.
Enclosure or Cables Feel Warm to the Touch
Likely Cause
Overheating from poor ventilation or an undersized enclosure for the current carried through high-current connections.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure internal temperature and compare against ambient; check enclosure sizing and venting against the connected load.
Corrective Action
Upgrade to a larger or better-ventilated enclosure, and re-check temperature under full load after the change.
Circuit Downstream of the Box Not Working
Likely Cause
A loose, corroded, or open splice inside the junction box has broken circuit continuity.
Diagnostic Steps
Open the box and visually inspect all splices; test continuity across each connection point.
Corrective Action
Re-make the faulted splice with a sealed connector, and re-test continuity before closing and re-sealing the box.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit Isolation Before Opening
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR before opening a junction box, then verify zero energy at the terminals before handling cable splices or connections inside it.

Confirm the Correct Box on Multi-Circuit Sites
Operational

Where several circuits pass through nearby junction boxes, positively identify the correct box before isolating work, since opening the wrong one can leave live conductors exposed while the intended circuit stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice or termination work, megger test the affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy equipment on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on live-capable connections, and confirm the box and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting.

Seal Integrity on Reinstatement
Electrical Safety

Renew gaskets and properly seat cable glands on reassembly, since a junction box's moisture seal is critical to preventing insulation breakdown at the splices it protects.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the box is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Buried or In-Pavement Boxes
Operational

Follow the aerodrome's permit-to-dig and pit-access procedures where the junction box is buried or set below pavement level, and check for water or debris accumulation before opening it.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a junction box with a loose connection, an unsealed gland, or a mis-seated cover after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and secure closure before closing out the work order.

Quick Reference

Airport Junction Box

An airport junction box is the sealed enclosure that holds cable joints, splices and terminations in an airfield lighting circuit. It gives a protected point for connecting primary and secondary cables, for branch connections and for testing and fault isolation.

Choose the box by its location, load class, sealing rating, size for the cables and joints it must hold, and the voltage of the circuit. For a box used on approach lighting lines, see the approach light junction box, and for PAPI circuits the PAPI junction box.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Failed Gland Reads Like a Cable Fault
Diagnostics

Moisture ingress through a degraded cable gland often shows up first as a nuisance insulation resistance fault on that circuit, easily mistaken for a failing cable itself. Check gland condition and re-seal before condemning the cable, since replacing the gland alone frequently resolves it.

Loose Terminations Show Up as Heat Before They Show Up as Faults
Preventive

A loose termination inside a junction box generates localised heating well before it produces an outright open circuit or fault. Where thermal imaging is available, a periodic scan of junction boxes can catch a developing loose connection long before it fails in service.

Don't Mix Incompatible Conductor Materials at a Termination
Installation

Terminating dissimilar conductor materials directly together in a junction box without the correct connector can set up galvanic corrosion at the joint over time, degrading the connection long after installation looked sound. Use connectors rated for the specific material combination present.

Check the Gasket Every Time the Lid Comes Off
Durability

A gasket that looked fine at the last inspection can take permanent compression set after repeated lid removal and re-torquing, silently reducing the ingress protection rating. Inspect and, where needed, replace the gasket at every entry rather than assuming it's still good from last time.

Unused Cable Entries Need Proper Blanking, Not Tape
Installation

An unused cable entry sealed with tape or an improvised plug instead of a rated blanking gland is a common, easily missed route for moisture and insect ingress. Verify every entry point on the box, not just the ones with cables currently terminated.

Record Which Box on Which Circuit, Not Just "Junction Box #4"
Process

On a site with many similar junction boxes, tie maintenance records to the specific circuit and cable run passing through the box, not just a generic box number. This makes it possible to trace a recurring insulation resistance issue back to a specific run when it resurfaces months later.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL junction boxes: IP ratings, materials, direct-burial use, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is an AGL junction box?

A junction box, also called a connection or distribution box, is an enclosure that protects and organises electrical connections, splices and terminations in AGL power and control circuits.

Q2Why are junction boxes used?

They give the many cable joints, splices and taps in an AGL circuit a protected, accessible home instead of leaving them exposed underground or in conduit, where a fault could go undetected and unreachable.

Q3Where are junction boxes used?

At the interface between primary cables and isolating transformers and between transformers and fixtures on runway and taxiway circuits, and in power distribution systems such as CCR rooms.

Q4How does a junction box work?

Cables enter through glands or conduit entries and are terminated inside using terminal blocks, splice connectors or soldered joints, inside a sealed enclosure.

Q5What materials and IP ratings are typical?

Thermoplastic (PC, PA), cast aluminium, stainless steel or GRP, with IP66, IP67 or IP68 protection depending on the application and impact ratings of IK07-IK10.

Q6What sizes and cable entries are available?

Sizes from about 100x100x60 mm to 400x300x150 mm with 2 to 12 or more entries (PG9-PG48 or M20-M63). Mounting is surface, flush or pole, and colours include grey, black and airfield-specific orange.

Q7Which standards apply?

FAA AC 150/5345-42 (Airport Light Bases, Transformer Housings, Junction Boxes, and Accessories), IEC 60529 (IP code), UL 50E and BS EN 62208.

Q8How often should junction boxes be inspected?

Semi-annually for thermal checks (discolouration or heat damage at connections) and annually for cable gland seals, internal connections and enclosure and lid seal condition.

Q9What if water is found inside a junction box?

An improperly tightened or damaged cable gland has allowed ingress. Check each gland for torque, cracks or missing seals and inspect the lid gasket.
