LED power-draw and CCR re-sizing after retrofit

LEDs cut the fixture load, but not the cable loss. Add up the load again with the new figures, then decide whether the CCR still fits or needs re-sizing.

fixture power
transformer loss
cable loss
The Fact IEC 61820-3-2 · FAA AC 150/5345-10 · ICAO Doc 9157 Part 5

Fitting LEDs cuts the fixture power sharply, but it does not cut the circuit load by the same share. The loop current is unchanged, so the cable loss stays, and each transformer still has its own losses. Re-sizing means adding up the load again with the new figures, then deciding whether the CCR still fits.

1What changes, and what does not

The fixture power falls from tens of watts to about 10 W per light in this example. The transformer loss falls in watts, but a transformer carrying a fraction of its rating is less efficient. The cable loss is the loop current squared times the loop resistance, and the CCR still drives the same current at the top step, so it does not change. The output voltage, which is the load divided by the current, falls with the load. In the example the fixture load falls by about 78% and the circuit load by about 61%.

2Re-add the load, do not scale it

3When the CCR is now too large

A CCR works between a minimum and a maximum load. At 19% of its rating the circuit may sit below the manufacturer’s minimum loading, with a lower power factor and less accurate regulation. There are four ways to deal with it, and the choice is a design decision made with the CCR and fixture manufacturers.

Schematic and illustrative figures only. The 60 fixtures, the 45 W and 10 W draws, the 90% and 78% transformer efficiencies, the 15.4 Ω loop and the CCR sizes are examples, not measured values. Loads are real power in watts, and the output voltage shown assumes a power factor near 1. A lightly loaded loop has a lower power factor, so the real voltage and apparent power (kVA) are higher; check both against the CCR data. Real figures depend on the fixtures, transformers, cable and CCR. Follow the manufacturers’ data, the design drawings and the applicable specification, and confirm against the editions in force.
Checks

What to check when re-sizing

The real fixture draw

Use the manufacturer’s data for the installed fixture, driver included, at the loop current, not the label wattage of the old lamp.

Transformer loading

Compare each transformer rating with the new fixture load, and decide whether to keep it or fit a smaller one.

Cable loss

Recalculate it from the loop resistance at operating temperature and the top-step current. It is now a much larger share of the load.

CCR limits

Compare the load with the rating and the manufacturer’s minimum loading, and see how far it falls as fixtures fail.

Every step

After any change, measure the series current and the light output at every brightness step.

Supply and records

Recheck input kVA and any generator or UPS loading, then update the circuit schedule with the new load and the CCR rating.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1Why does the circuit load fall less than the fixture load after an LED retrofit?

The fixture power falls a lot, but the cable loss depends on the loop current and the loop resistance, and neither changes. The transformer losses also fall by less than the fixture power. In the worked example the fixture load falls by about 78% and the circuit load by about 61%.

Q2How is the new CCR load calculated?

In the same way as the original: fixture power, plus the transformer input power at the new, lighter loading, plus the cable loss at the top step. Use the manufacturers' data for the installed fixtures and transformers, then compare the total with the CCR rating and its minimum loading.

Q3Does the CCR have to be replaced after an LED retrofit?

Not always. If the CCR manufacturer states that it is approved for the lower load, and its regulation, power factor and output waveform check out at every step, it can stay. If the load falls below the minimum loading or the performance suffers, the options are a balancing load, combining circuits or a smaller CCR.

Q4What is a balancing load, and when is it used?

It is an extra load added to the circuit to bring the total back into the CCR’s working range. It is a quick fix that keeps the existing CCR, but it turns part of the saving into heat, needs space and cooling, and is one more item to maintain. It is a design decision to make with the CCR manufacturer.

Q5How much margin should be allowed when choosing a smaller CCR?

The same approach applies as for any CCR: work out the load at the highest step, add margin for growth, ageing and tolerance, and take the next standard size up. Then check that the result is not too large again. The amount of margin comes from the site design basis and the manufacturer.

Q6What changes on the supply side after the retrofit?

The circuit draws less real power, but a lightly loaded CCR can have a lower power factor, so the supply kVA does not fall as far as the kW. Recheck the loading of any generator or UPS on kVA as well as kW, and do not downsize standby power until the future load is settled.
