Flasher Synchronization Unit

A precision timing device that ensures multiple Sequenced Flashing Light (SFL) systems or individual flash units operate in perfect synchronisation. This unit provides a master timing reference — often derived from a GPS or atomic clock — to coordinate flash sequences across multiple approach ends, redundant systems, or adjacent airfields, ensuring a consistent and seamless visual effect for pilots during final approach.

Overview

What Is It?

A Flasher Synchronization Unit (also known as a Master Timing Unit or Sync Generator) is an electronic device that provides a highly accurate, stable timing reference to synchronise the operation of Sequenced Flashing Light (SFL) systems. In complex airport environments with multiple approach lighting systems (e.g., for both runway ends or parallel runways), it is essential that the SFL sequences are synchronised to avoid confusing visual patterns and to maintain a consistent "rolling" effect for pilots. The unit generates a master clock signal that is distributed to all Flasher Control Units, ensuring that each SFL unit flashes at precisely the same rate and phase.

Modern Synchronization Units often incorporate GPS receivers or other precise time sources (e.g., NTP, IEEE 1588 PTP) to achieve microsecond‑level accuracy, eliminating cumulative timing drift. They are a critical component for airports with CAT II/III operations, where the reliability and consistency of the SFL effect are paramount. Many units also include redundant timing sources and automatic failover for maximum reliability.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The flasher synchronization unit's purpose is to make sure that when an airport runs sequenced flashing lights on more than one approach — both ends of a runway, or parallel runways — those systems don't accidentally interfere with each other's visual rhythm and create a confusing pattern for a pilot trying to read the flash sequence as a directional cue. Because the SFL effect depends entirely on flashes occurring in the correct order and timing to create the impression of motion toward the runway, even a small drift between two systems' internal clocks could blur that effect or, worse, create a visually contradictory pattern across a nearby approach. By distributing a single, GPS- or PTP-disciplined master clock to every Flasher Control Unit, the synchronization unit ensures the rolling effect stays coherent and unambiguous across the entire airfield, which matters most in exactly the low-visibility CAT II/III conditions where pilots depend on it most.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Flasher Synchronization Units are deployed in approach lighting systems where multiple SFL systems need to operate in harmony. They are used in:

  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems – synchronising SFL sequences for both runway ends or parallel runways.
  • MALSF and MALSR systems – coordinating SFL timing across multiple approach ends.
  • Calvert II systems – ensuring consistent SFL timing for British approach configurations.
  • Airports with parallel runways – where SFL sequences must be synchronised to avoid cross‑runway visual confusion.
  • Major international airports – with multiple approach lighting systems requiring centralised timing.
  • Military airbases – with multiple approach ends and the need for precise timing across all systems.
  • Airports with redundant SFL systems – where a backup system must synchronise seamlessly with the primary.
Placement

Installation & Location

The synchronization unit is rack-mounted in the same equipment shelter as the flasher control units it coordinates, wired into each SFL system's control circuit via a shared timing bus or link cable. Where it synchronises systems at both ends of the runway or across parallel runways, its location is chosen to keep the interconnecting timing cable run as short and direct as practical, since signal delay along that link is exactly what the unit exists to correct for. Installation includes bonding the unit's chassis to the shelter's earth reference alongside the other lighting control equipment, and its front-panel status indicators are kept accessible for technicians confirming synchronisation during routine checks.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Flasher Synchronization Unit operates through a combination of precise time reference generation and signal distribution:

  • Time Reference Acquisition: The unit acquires a highly accurate time reference from an external source, such as a GPS receiver (providing UTC‑synchronised PPS signals), an atomic clock, or a network time protocol (NTP) server. Some units also support IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) for sub‑microsecond accuracy over Ethernet.
  • Master Clock Generation: Using the reference time, the unit generates a master clock signal with a stable frequency and phase. This clock defines the base timing for all SFL sequences, including the flash rate (e.g., 60 or 120 flashes per minute) and the relative phase (start time) of the sequence.
  • Signal Distribution: The master clock signal is distributed to all connected Flasher Control Units via dedicated synchronisation cables (e.g., twisted‑pair, coaxial, or fibre optic) or over a network using time‑synchronised protocols (e.g., PTP). The control units use this signal to align their internal timing, ensuring that all SFL units flash in perfect unison.
  • Phase Adjustment: The Synchronization Unit may also allow for phase offset adjustments, enabling the synchronisation of sequences that need to be offset (e.g., when two parallel runways have offset thresholds) to avoid visual interference.
  • Monitoring and Diagnostics: The unit continuously monitors the integrity of the timing reference and the synchronisation signals. It can detect loss of GPS lock, cable breaks, or timing errors, and report faults to the ALCMS or local alarm panel. Many units also provide a status output (e.g., a "sync OK" relay contact) for external monitoring.
  • Redundancy: Critical installations often use dual Synchronization Units with automatic failover (e.g., a primary GPS‑based unit and a backup internal oscillator unit) to ensure continuous timing even if the primary reference fails.
  • Integration with ALCMS: The unit typically communicates with the Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) via serial, Ethernet, or fibre optic links, allowing remote monitoring, configuration, and alarm management.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Flasher Synchronization Unit sits above the individual Flasher Control Units it serves, supplying the common time base they all lock to rather than commanding any SFL directly. It acquires its own reference from GPS, an atomic clock, NTP, or PTP over Ethernet, and generates a stable master clock defining the flash rate and start phase for every sequence downstream. That clock is fanned out to each connected Flasher Control Unit, which uses it purely for timing alignment — the control unit still owns its own stored delay sequence and still issues the actual per-SFL triggers; the Synchronization Unit never talks to an SFL or a Flasher Power Supply directly. This separation matters most across a large approach lighting array or between adjacent systems (e.g. offset thresholds on parallel runways), where even a few milliseconds of drift between control units would be visible as a ragged, non-simultaneous flash rather than a clean rolling sequence. Loss of GPS lock, a broken sync cable, or timing drift is detected locally and reported to the ALCMS or a local alarm panel, and a dual-unit installation fails over automatically — typically to a backup internal oscillator — so downstream control units keep a stable (if less externally verified) time base rather than losing sync entirely.

Construction

Physical Construction

The Synchronization Unit is typically a compact 19-inch rack-mount or DIN-rail module housed within the equipment room, built around a precision oscillator (often GPS-disciplined) and a microprocessor-based timing distribution board. A GPS antenna connector on the rear panel feeds an internal or remote GPS receiver module, while a rear-panel terminal block or set of coaxial/fibre outputs distributes the master clock signal to the Flasher Control Units it serves. Redundant timing sources — a secondary GPS receiver, an internal high-stability oscillator as holdover, or an NTP/PTP network input — are typically built into the same chassis with automatic failover circuitry, and the front panel carries status LEDs for GPS lock, sync distribution, and fault conditions. The compact size and low power dissipation of the unit generally allow natural convection cooling within its rack-mount enclosure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

GPS Signal Loss or Degradation

The Synchronization Unit relies on GPS or other external time references. Signal loss due to antenna blockage, atmospheric conditions, or GPS jamming can cause the unit to revert to its internal oscillator, which may drift over time. Regular checking of GPS antenna integrity and signal strength, and installation of backup timing sources, are essential.

Cable and Network Latency

The synchronisation signals must reach all Flasher Control Units with minimal and consistent latency. Cable length differences or network delays can introduce phase errors between units. Proper cable routing, impedance matching, and use of precision time protocols (PTP) with delay compensation are recommended.

Internal Oscillator Ageing

The internal high‑stability oscillator (OCXO or TCXO) will age over time, causing frequency drift. If the GPS reference is lost for an extended period, the drift may become significant enough to affect SFL timing. Regular calibration and periodic verification against GPS are recommended.

Power Supply Transients

Voltage spikes or dips can cause the Synchronization Unit to reset or lose its lock. Use of UPS and surge protection devices (SPDs) is essential to maintain stable operation.

Configuration Errors

Incorrect programming of phase offsets or output assignments can cause SFL sequences to flash in the wrong order or at the wrong time. Careful configuration management and verification during commissioning and maintenance are critical.

Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)

High‑power transmitters, radar, or other RF sources can interfere with the GPS receiver or the synchronisation signals. Proper shielding, filtering, and antenna placement are essential to ensure reliable operation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Timing Drifts After Extended Operation
Likely Cause
GPS signal loss or degradation, from antenna blockage, atmospheric conditions, or jamming, has caused the unit to fall back on its internal oscillator.
Diagnostic Steps
Check GPS antenna integrity and signal strength; review logs for recent loss-of-lock events.
Corrective Action
Clear antenna obstructions or repair antenna cabling, and confirm the unit re-acquires GPS lock; verify a backup timing source is in place.
Phase Errors Between Flasher Control Units
Likely Cause
Cable length differences or network delays are introducing inconsistent latency in the synchronisation signal to different units.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure signal latency to each Flasher Control Unit and compare for consistency; check cable routing and lengths.
Corrective Action
Correct cable routing/impedance matching issues, or implement precision time protocol (PTP) with delay compensation.
Drift Following Extended GPS Outage
Likely Cause
The internal high-stability oscillator (OCXO/TCXO) has aged and drifted while the GPS reference was unavailable.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the unit's timing against GPS once reacquired to quantify the drift that occurred during the outage.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the oscillator and verify periodic calibration against GPS is scheduled going forward.
Unit Resets or Loses Lock Unexpectedly
Likely Cause
Voltage spikes or dips on the power supply are causing the unit to reset.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor input power quality; check for recent transient events correlating with reset/loss-of-lock timestamps.
Corrective Action
Install or verify UPS and surge protection devices (SPDs) on the unit's supply.
SFL Sequence Flashes in the Wrong Order
Likely Cause
Incorrect programming of phase offsets or output assignments in the unit's configuration.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the current phase offset and output assignment configuration against the design sequence.
Corrective Action
Correct the configuration and verify the full sequence matches the design plan before returning to service.
Intermittent Loss of GPS Lock or Signal Errors
Likely Cause
Electromagnetic interference from high-power transmitters, radar, or other RF sources near the antenna or synchronisation cabling.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for known nearby RF sources; inspect antenna shielding, filtering, and placement.
Corrective Action
Improve shielding/filtering or relocate the antenna away from interference sources, then confirm stable lock is maintained.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
GPS Antenna Placement Affects Lock Reliability More Than Receiver Quality
Installation

A high-quality GPS timing receiver with poor sky visibility from its antenna location will still suffer intermittent loss of lock, especially near structures or dense mast clutter; site the antenna for a clear sky view first, since no amount of receiver quality compensates for a poor site.

Confirm Holdover Accuracy, Not Just Steady-State Accuracy
Reliability

Steady-state timing accuracy with a locked GPS reference is easy to verify and usually excellent; what matters more operationally is how much the local oscillator drifts during a holdover period after a GPS outage, since that's the condition under which synchronisation actually has a chance to visibly degrade. Test and document holdover drift specifically.

Distribution Cable Length Differences Introduce Their Own Phase Offset
Design

Even a perfectly synchronised master clock signal picks up a small propagation delay that varies with distribution cable length to each Flasher Control Unit; where multiple approach systems are fed from one synchronisation unit with unequal cable runs, that length difference should be accounted for in the timing budget, not assumed negligible.

Verify Redundant Timing Sources Actually Disagree Gracefully
Testing

A redundant timing source setup should be tested for what happens when the two sources disagree slightly rather than failing outright, since a small persistent disagreement that never triggers a hard failover can still degrade synchronisation quality indefinitely without an alarm ever firing.

NTP and PTP Timing Have Different Failure Signatures on a Congested Network
Network

A unit relying on network-based timing (NTP or PTP) rather than GPS is vulnerable to accuracy degradation from network congestion or asymmetric routing in a way that isn't always obvious from a simple link-up status; monitor actual achieved timing accuracy over the network path, not just link connectivity.

A Timing Fault Looks Like a Sequence Fault at First Glance
Troubleshooting

Two approach systems sharing a runway or parallel runways whose rolling effect appears to drift out of step with each other can point to a synchronisation unit issue rather than a fault in either individual Flasher Control Unit; check the shared timing reference before troubleshooting each approach system independently.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about flasher synchronisation units: what they do, time references, accuracy, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a flasher synchronisation unit?

A flasher synchronisation unit, also called a master timing unit or sync generator, provides an accurate, stable timing reference to synchronise sequenced flashing light systems. It generates a master clock signal sent to all flasher control units so every SFL flashes at the same rate and phase.

Q2Why do SFL systems need synchronising?

Where an airport runs SFL on more than one approach, such as both runway ends or parallel runways, unsynchronised systems can interfere with each other's visual rhythm and give a confusing pattern. The rolling effect depends on flashes occurring in the correct order and timing.

Q3Where do time references come from?

GPS (PPS), an atomic clock, NTP or IEEE 1588 PTP, with an internal high-stability oscillator (OCXO or TCXO) as fallback. Accuracy is ±1 µs with GPS or PTP and ±1 ms on the internal oscillator.

Q4What outputs does it provide?

4–16 sync output channels (TTL, RS-422 or fibre optic) with programmable phase offsets of 0–360° per output. Supported protocols include PPS, IRIG-B, NMEA, Modbus/TCP and PTP.

Q5Which approach lighting systems use it?

ALSF-1 and ALSF-2 (both runway ends or parallel runways), MALSF and MALSR, Calvert II systems, airports with parallel runways and major airports with multiple approach lighting systems.

Q6Where is a synchronisation unit installed?

Rack-mounted in the same equipment shelter as the flasher control units it coordinates, wired to each SFL system through a shared timing bus or link cable. The location keeps the timing cable run short, since signal delay on that link is exactly what the unit exists to manage.

Q7What power and backup does it have?

120/230 V AC or 24/48 V DC at typically under 50 W, with battery or UPS backup to preserve timing through brief mains interruptions. Dual power inputs or dual units with automatic failover are available on some models.

Q8Which standards apply to flasher synchronisation units?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-51, FAA Order 6850.2, IEEE 1588 (PTP) and RTCA DO-160 for GPS receiver compatibility. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a synchronisation unit be maintained?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for GPS signal checks and network latency tests, and annual for oscillator accuracy checks, power supply checks and EMI and shielding inspection. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does timing drift after extended operation?

GPS signal loss or degradation from antenna blockage, atmospheric conditions or jamming has made the unit fall back on its internal oscillator. Check antenna integrity and signal strength, review loss-of-lock logs and confirm a backup timing source.

Q11Why are there phase errors between flasher control units?

Cable length differences or network delays are giving different latency to different units. Measure latency to each unit, correct cable routing or impedance matching, or use PTP to compensate.
