Reflector

The precision mirror that captures and redirects light from the source into a controlled beam. Using parabolic, ellipsoidal, or faceted geometries, reflectors maximise optical efficiency and ensure accurate photometric performance for runway, taxiway, and approach lighting.

Overview

What Is It?

A Reflector is a critical optical component in AGL fixtures that captures light emitted from the source (LED or halogen lamp) and redirects it into a defined beam pattern. Unlike a lens which transmits light, a reflector works by reflection — using highly polished metallic surfaces or dielectric coatings to achieve near-total internal reflection with minimal absorption. Reflectors are designed with precise geometries — parabolic, ellipsoidal, or faceted — to produce the required beam spread, intensity distribution, and cut-off angles. They are typically made from aluminium with a protective anodized or dichroic coating, or from glass with a silver or aluminum reflective layer.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The reflector's purpose, like the prism's, is to convert light radiating in many directions from the source into the tightly defined beam pattern a fixture is certified to produce — but it does so by redirecting light through reflection rather than bending it through refraction, which makes it well suited to fixtures needing very narrow, long-throw beams (parabolic geometry) or wide, even coverage (faceted geometry). Its high-reflectivity coating exists to capture as much of the source's output as possible and put it where it is useful, rather than letting it scatter or be absorbed — a direct driver of how efficiently the fixture converts electrical power into the intensity a pilot actually sees at range. Because beam shape is what allows a pilot to judge distance, alignment, or approach angle from the fixture's characteristic light pattern, the reflector's geometry is engineered as precisely as any other safety-critical optical component in the fixture.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Reflectors are used in a wide range of AGL fixtures where precise beam control is required:

  • Runway Edge & Centreline Lights: Parabolic reflectors for narrow, long-range beams.
  • Threshold & End Lights: Ellipsoidal reflectors to project a uni-directional beam.
  • Taxiway Lights: Faceted reflectors for wide, uniform illumination.
  • Approach Lighting: High-efficiency reflectors for barrettes and crossbars.
  • PAPI & VASI: Specialised reflectors that create the red/white colour transition.
  • Obstruction Lights: Omnidirectional reflectors for 360° coverage.
Placement

Installation & Location

The reflector is fitted behind and around the lamp or LED module, secured to the housing so the light source sits precisely at (or very near) the reflector's focal point; a source mounted even a few millimetres off-focus will produce a beam that's wider, narrower, or misaimed compared to the fixture's rated photometrics. It's held in place by locating tabs, a bracket, or the lamp holder assembly itself, and its reflective surface is protected from scratching or fingerprints during fitting, since surface damage scatters light and reduces beam intensity. Once seated, it's enclosed behind the lens and top cover, and its position is checked whenever the lamp, LED module, or holder is serviced, since disturbing any of those parts can shift the reflector's alignment relative to the source.

Function

How Does It Work?

A reflector works by reflecting light from the source according to the laws of reflection. The geometry of the reflector surface determines the beam shape:

  • Parabolic: Reflects rays parallel to the axis, producing a narrow, collimated beam ideal for long-range illumination.
  • Ellipsoidal: Focuses light to a second focal point, allowing control over beam divergence.
  • Faceted: Uses multiple flat or curved segments to create a specific beam pattern, often used for wide distribution.

The reflective surface is coated with a highly reflective material — typically aluminium, silver, or a dielectric stack — to achieve reflectivity >95%. The reflector is designed to minimise light loss due to absorption and scattering, ensuring maximum photometric efficiency.

Construction

Physical Construction

Metal reflectors are typically spun, stamped, or die-formed from aluminium sheet into the required parabolic, ellipsoidal, or faceted profile, then polished and finished with a vacuum-deposited or anodised reflective layer to maximise reflectivity while resisting oxidation. Glass reflectors instead start as a moulded or ground glass substrate onto which a silver or aluminium reflective layer is deposited and protected by a back coating, offering higher reflectivity at the cost of greater fragility and weight. In either case, the reflector's exact geometry is held to close manufacturing tolerance, since the surface shape — not just its reflectivity — is what concentrates the light source's output into the required beam spread and cut-off angle. The finished reflector seats in the housing on locating features that fix it precisely around the light source, typically retained by clips, screws, or a bonded joint, so its optical axis stays aligned with the lamp or LED module through vibration and thermal cycling in service.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Reflectivity Degradation

Over time, the reflective coating can oxidise, tarnish, or become contaminated with dust and pollutants, reducing reflectivity and light output. Regular cleaning and periodic replacement are recommended.

Thermal Stress Cracking

Glass reflectors can crack due to thermal shock from rapid temperature changes. Use of borosilicate glass or proper thermal management in the fixture design is essential.

Misalignment During Maintenance

If the reflector is disturbed or removed during lamp replacement, the beam pattern can be misaligned. Always use alignment jigs and follow manufacturer's specifications to restore the correct optical orientation.

Corrosion of Aluminium Reflectors

In coastal or high-humidity environments, aluminium reflectors can corrode, especially if the anodized coating is damaged. Use of marine-grade anodizing or stainless steel alternatives is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced Light Output
Likely Cause
Reflective coating has oxidised, tarnished, or accumulated dust/pollutant contamination, reducing overall reflectivity.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the reflective surface for dullness, tarnish, or contamination; run a photometric intensity check against the rated output.
Corrective Action
Clean with approved methods if contamination is light; replace the reflector if tarnish or coating oxidation has permanently reduced reflectivity.
Cracked Glass Reflector
Likely Cause
Thermal shock from rapid temperature change acting on a reflector not made of borosilicate glass.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crack pattern for a thermal-shock signature; confirm the reflector material specification against what is actually fitted.
Corrective Action
Replace with a borosilicate glass reflector as specified, and review the fixture's thermal management if cracking recurs.
Beam Pattern Off-Aim After Maintenance
Likely Cause
Reflector disturbed or removed during lamp replacement and reinstalled without correct orientation.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare current beam aim against the reference orientation using an alignment jig; check for orientation markings on the reflector mount.
Corrective Action
Reseat the reflector to its correct orientation per the manufacturer's specification and re-verify beam pattern with a photometric check.
Visible Pitting or White Corrosion on an Aluminium Reflector
Likely Cause
Damaged anodized coating exposing bare aluminium to coastal salt air or high humidity.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the anodized coating for scratches or wear exposing bare metal; assess the extent of pitting or corrosion product on the surface.
Corrective Action
Replace with a marine-grade anodized or stainless steel reflector for coastal/high-humidity sites, since pitted reflective surfaces cannot be reliably restored by cleaning.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Removal
Electrical Safety

Isolate the fixture and allow the lamp to cool before removing the reflector; a live circuit or hot lamp behind the optic is a shock and burn hazard during service.

Preserve Orientation on Reinstall
Workmanship

Note the reflector's orientation markings before removal and reinstall it exactly as found; a misoriented reflector throws the beam off-aim even though the optic itself is undamaged.

Thermal Shock Avoidance
Handling

Do not apply a cold cleaning solution to a hot reflector or reinstall immediately after a lamp change; allow the fixture to reach ambient temperature to avoid thermal-shock cracking on glass units.

Coating-Safe Cleaning
Workmanship

Clean the reflective surface with a soft cloth and manufacturer-approved cleaner rather than an abrasive pad; scratching or wearing through the anodized coating exposes bare aluminium to pitting corrosion, which is irreversible.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for the removed reflector, gasket, and fasteners at all times during handling, and secure or remove any loose material before returning the fixture to service to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Verify Beam Aim After Service
Compliance

Check beam aim against the alignment jig or reference orientation after any reflector work before closing out the task, since orientation errors are not visually obvious.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return a fixture to service with a cracked, pitted, or misoriented reflector; confirm the optic is fully seated and correctly aligned before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Orientation Marks Wear Off Before the Reflector Does
Workmanship

A factory orientation mark on a reflector can fade or rub off well before the optic itself needs replacement; re-mark the orientation in a durable way at every service if the original marking is hard to read, rather than guessing from memory.

Rapid Cooling Cracks Glass Reflectors the Same Way Rapid Heating Does
Handling

A hot glass reflector exposed to cold night air or a draft during changeover can crack from rapid cooling just as readily as from a cold cleaner applied to a hot unit; let ambient conditions equalize from both directions, not just after removing a heat source.

A Dimming Beam Can Be Coating Pitting, Not Lamp Aging
Troubleshooting

A gradually dimming or scattered beam is often assumed to be lamp aging first; check for anodized coating pitting or corrosion on the reflective surface before replacing the light source, since a degraded reflector produces the same symptom.

Verify Beam Aim With an Instrument, Not by Eye
Compliance

Orientation and aim errors from reflector work are not reliably detectable by eye, especially at night; use an alignment jig or photometric check after any reflector service rather than judging the beam visually.

Gasket Compression Set Outlasts the Reflector's Visual Condition
Durability

A reflector gasket can lose sealing force well before the reflector itself shows any wear, so an optic that looks fine on removal doesn't mean its gasket is still doing its job; replace the gasket at every reflector service as routine.

A Single Fingerprint Can Start Irreversible Pitting
Handling

Oils from bare-hand handling on an anodized reflective surface can create a localized weak point in the coating that accelerates corrosion under humidity cycling, even after the fingerprint itself is cleaned off; handle reflectors by the edges or mounting tabs from the first touch.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the reflector: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a reflector in an AGL fixture?

A reflector is an optical component that captures light from the source (LED or halogen lamp) and redirects it into a defined beam pattern by reflection, using highly polished metallic surfaces or dielectric coatings. Geometry sets the beam spread, intensity distribution and cut-off angles.

Q2What types of reflector geometry are used?

Parabolic reflectors produce a narrow, collimated beam for long range. Ellipsoidal reflectors focus light to a second focal point to control beam divergence. Faceted reflectors use multiple flat or curved segments for a specific pattern, often a wide distribution.

Q3How reflective is an AGL reflector?

Reflectivity is typically above 95% in the visible spectrum. Surfaces are optically polished (Ra below 0.05 µm) and coated with anodising, a dichroic stack, or silver with a protective overcoat.

Q4What materials and temperatures are involved?

Typical materials are anodised aluminium, glass with a silver or aluminium coating, or plastic with a dielectric coating. The temperature range is -40°C to +150°C for high-temperature lamps, with UV-stabilised coatings.

Q5Where are reflectors used?

In runway edge and centreline lights (parabolic), threshold and end lights (ellipsoidal), taxiway lights (faceted), approach lighting barrettes and crossbars, and PAPI and VASI units.

Q6What is the difference between a reflector and a lens?

A lens transmits light, while a reflector works by reflection from a polished or coated surface, minimising absorption. Typical reflector beam angles are 2° to 12° depending on the application.

Q7Which standards apply to reflectors?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46, FAA Engineering Brief 67 (light sources other than incandescent and xenon, covering LED retrofit optics) and IEC TS 61827. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a reflector be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cleaning, semi-annual for alignment verification, and annual for reflectivity checks and surface and corrosion inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with reflectors?

Common problems are reflectivity degradation from coating oxidation or contamination, thermal stress cracking of glass reflectors, misalignment during maintenance, and pitting or white corrosion on aluminium reflectors. Reduced light output is the typical symptom.
