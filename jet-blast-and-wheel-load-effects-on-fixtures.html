How do jet blast and wheel loads damage fixtures?

Airfield lights live where aircraft operate. Blast pushes sideways on elevated lights, and wheels press down on every inset light. Pick a condition to see what each does.

What happens

The elevated light stands upright on its frangible coupling and the inset light sits flush and seated in its base can. Both keep their aim, seal and output.

What to do

Use the as-installed height, alignment and fixing condition as the baseline for later inspections.

The Fact Aircraft operations

Jet blast pushes sideways on elevated lights, signs and their stems at high engine power, and throws grit across lenses. Wheel loads press down, and sideways when turning or braking, on every inset light in the pavement.

Elevated lights are kept light and low and sit on frangible couplings, so an aircraft strike breaks them away. The coupling is for impact, not for routine blast. Inset lights carry the load instead: window, flange, bolts and base can are rated for aircraft wheel loads, and the fixture must stay seated.

Damage often starts small: a slight tilt, a loosened fixing, a chipped window, a flange standing proud. Each one changes aim or output, and a failed seal lets water into the base can.

Elevated light

Takes jet blast sideways. Watch for tilt, loose fixings, scoured lenses and lost aim.

Inset light

Takes wheel load downward. Watch for cracks, a raised flange, bolt torque and seal condition.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1Why does jet blast matter for airfield lights?

Exhaust at breakaway or taxi power pushes sideways on elevated lights, signs and their stems and throws grit across lenses. Repeated blast can loosen fixings, tilt a head and shift its aim.

Q2Are inset lights affected by jet blast?

Much less than elevated lights, because they sit in the pavement. Blast can still throw grit over the window, but wheel load is the main mechanical stress on an inset fixture.

Q3How do inset lights carry aircraft wheel loads?

The window, body and flange are built to take the load, which passes into the base can and the pavement beneath. The fixture must be rated for the aircraft loads on that pavement and stay properly seated and torqued.

Q4Why are elevated lights mounted on frangible couplings?

So an aircraft that strikes one breaks it away rather than being damaged. The coupling is designed for impact, not routine blast, so a light that tilts or loosens in normal operation points to a mounting or fixing problem.

Q5What damage should be looked for?

Tilt or misaim, loose fixings, a scoured lens, a cracked or chipped window, a raised or sunken fixture, damaged seals, and water in the base can.

Q6Why does a raised or loose inset fixture need quick attention?

It can be struck by wheels or clearing equipment, its seal no longer keeps water out of the base can, and it no longer holds its photometric aim. It should be repaired rather than left.
