Structure & Mast Lighting

What It Is

Structure and mast lighting systems provide coordinated visual marking of towers, masts and other tall structures that require aviation identification.

Precision AGL Solutions supports complete obstruction-lighting configurations based on structure height, geometry, location, applicable aviation requirements and project specifications.

Integrated Solution

These systems can combine multiple lighting levels, beacon arrangements, control equipment and monitoring functions to provide an integrated aviation obstacle-marking solution.

Lighting Levels

Low, Medium, High and Combination Beacons

Different structures may require more than one type or intensity of obstruction light. The appropriate configuration depends on the approved obstruction-lighting design.

Low-intensity

Used for applicable portions of structures requiring lower-intensity visual identification.

Medium-intensity

Used where enhanced conspicuity is required for structure marking.

High-intensity

Used for applications requiring high-intensity obstacle identification, subject to the applicable requirements.

Dual / combination beacons

Used where more than one lighting mode or intensity arrangement is required within a coordinated beacon assembly.

LevelICAO Annex 14 TypesTypical Characteristic
Low-intensityTypes A and BSteady red
Medium-intensityTypes A, B and CType A flashing white; Type B flashing red; Type C steady red
High-intensityTypes A and BFlashing white, with intensity controlled according to ambient light
The lighting level, number of lights, vertical spacing between levels and steady or flashing mode follow the structure height and the aviation authority's requirements or approval. Confirm them for every structure before ordering. FAA obstruction-lighting types and height thresholds differ from ICAO (for example L-810, L-864, L-865 and L-856 under AC 150/5345-43J), so apply the system the aviation authority has specified for the structure.

Placement Principles (ICAO Annex 14, Chapter 6)

  • At least one light is placed as close as practicable to the top of the structure. On a chimney or similar structure, the top light sits slightly below the top to limit smoke contamination.
  • Further lights are placed at intermediate levels so the full extent of the structure is indicated.
  • The number and arrangement of lights at each level must show the structure from every direction in azimuth.
  • Annex 14 links the lighting level to height: low-intensity lights suit smaller structures (typically below about 45 m), medium-intensity lights suit structures of about 45 m to 150 m, and high-intensity lights are used above about 150 m. Treat these figures as indicative and take the binding values from the aviation authority approval.

FAQ

Structure & Mast Lighting — Frequently Asked Questions

What is structure and mast lighting?

Structure and mast lighting systems provide coordinated visual marking of towers, masts and other tall structures that require aviation identification. They can combine multiple lighting levels, beacon arrangements, control equipment and monitoring functions into an integrated aviation obstacle-marking solution, based on structure height, geometry, location, applicable aviation requirements and project specifications.

Which structures need obstruction lighting?

Typical applications are communication towers, telecommunications masts, airport structures, industrial masts, tall buildings and rooftops, utility structures and transmission towers, construction and specialist structures, and other aviation obstacles needing visual marking. The final arrangement should be determined from the approved obstruction-lighting assessment and applicable aviation authority requirements.

What can a structure-lighting system include?

A complete system may incorporate low-, medium- and high-intensity obstruction lights, dual or combination beacons, aviation red obstacle lights, flashing beacon systems, steady-burning lights, photocell or automatic day/night control, central control equipment, remote monitoring and alarm interfaces, mounting brackets and support hardware, and cabling and associated electrical equipment.

What are the low-, medium- and high-intensity levels?

Under ICAO Annex 14, low-intensity lights (Types A and B) are steady red. Medium-intensity lights (Types A, B and C) are flashing white for Type A, flashing red for Type B and steady red for Type C. High-intensity lights (Types A and B) are flashing white, with intensity controlled according to ambient light. Annex 14 links the level to height: low-intensity lights suit smaller structures (typically below about 45 m), medium-intensity suit about 45 m to 150 m, and high-intensity are used above about 150 m, but treat these figures as indicative and take the binding values from the aviation authority approval. FAA types and height thresholds differ from ICAO (for example L-810, L-864, L-865 and L-856 under AC 150/5345-43J).

How are lights placed on a structure?

Under ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 6, at least one light is placed as close as practicable to the top of the structure, and on a chimney or similar structure the top light sits slightly below the top to limit smoke contamination. Further lights are placed at intermediate levels so the full extent of the structure is indicated, and the number and arrangement of lights at each level must show the structure from every direction in azimuth. The lighting level, number of lights, vertical spacing and mode follow the structure height and the aviation authority's requirements or approval.

What information is needed to assess a structure for lighting?

Important information includes the overall structure height and geometry, platform and obstruction levels, structure location, nearby airport or heliport operations, surrounding terrain, existing obstruction lighting, the applicable aviation authority, the required lighting classification, electrical supply, control and monitoring requirements and environmental conditions. For tall or complex structures, develop the lighting arrangement as a complete system rather than selecting individual lights independently.

How are the lights mounted and cabled?

Typical mounting arrangements are mast-top, platform, tower-leg, cross-arm, building rooftop, structural-bracket and dedicated beacon support mounting, providing secure attachment, the required optical orientation and practical access for inspection and maintenance. Electrical considerations include supply voltage, AC/DC requirements, circuit and surge protection, earthing, junction boxes and controllers, and for large structures cable routing and voltage drop should be reviewed as part of the system design. Cable routing should also account for vertical runs, structural movement, wind-induced vibration, cable support, weather exposure and lightning protection.

How do control, monitoring and flash synchronisation work?

Control and monitoring can include day/night switching, photocell control, flash control, lighting-level selection, system status indication, individual light monitoring, controller fault indication, remote alarm outputs and communication with site monitoring systems. Flashing lights on one structure normally flash simultaneously unless the applicable standard specifies otherwise, so the system may require a defined flash frequency, synchronised operation, coordinated beacon groups or controller-based sequencing.

What is checked at commissioning?

Typical checks are mechanical inspection, mounting verification, cable and termination inspection, supply-voltage verification, light-function testing, flash-pattern verification, synchronisation testing where applicable, day/night control testing, monitoring and alarm testing, remote communication testing and final system-status verification. Retain commissioning records as part of the project documentation.

How is a structure lighting system maintained?

Typical activities are visual inspection of lights, cleaning optical surfaces, checking mounting hardware and cable supports, checking electrical connections, functional testing, testing control equipment, checking monitoring and alarms, inspecting corrosion and enclosure condition, and replacing failed or degraded components. Maintenance frequency should reflect the structure environment, manufacturer recommendations and site procedures.

Can an existing structure lighting system be upgraded?

Yes. Systems can be supplied for new installations as well as replacement and modernization projects. Upgrade considerations include the existing light types, mounting positions, cable infrastructure, available power supply, control system, monitoring interface, required intensity, current aviation requirements and access and maintenance arrangements. LED-based replacement systems may also reduce power consumption and maintenance requirements, subject to project suitability.

Which standards apply to structure and mast lighting?

References include ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I Chapter 6 (visual aids for denoting obstacles), ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4, FAA AC 150/5345-43J (obstruction lighting equipment; confirm the current revision letter), FAA AC 70/7460-1N (obstruction marking and lighting, which replaces 70/7460-1M, so check which edition your project or FAA determination cites), and DGCA India requirements with the aviation-authority approval or NOC conditions for the structure. Verify the requirements before procurement and installation, particularly for structure height, lighting intensity, flash characteristics, mounting levels and monitoring.
