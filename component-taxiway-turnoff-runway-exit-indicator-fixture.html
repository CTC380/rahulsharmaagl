Taxiway Turnoff/Runway Exit Indicator Fixture

A combined guidance fixture that integrates both turnoff indication and runway exit marking into a single, cohesive lighting solution. These fixtures use a distinctive pattern of green and yellow lights to clearly define the runway exit point and guide pilots through the transition from runway to taxiway, enhancing safety and efficiency during the critical post-landing phase.

Overview

What Is It?

A Taxiway Turnoff/Runway Exit Indicator Fixture is a combined guidance fixture that integrates both turnoff indication and runway exit marking into a single, cohesive lighting solution. These fixtures use a distinctive pattern of green and yellow lights to clearly define the runway exit point and guide pilots through the transition from runway to taxiway, enhancing safety and efficiency during the critical post-landing phase. The fixture typically incorporates multiple light sources in a single housing, providing both the green taxiway centreline guidance and yellow cautionary indication for the exit point. This integrated design reduces installation complexity, improves reliability, and provides a consistent visual cue for pilots.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

This combined fixture exists to handle two related but distinct messages at exactly the moment a pilot needs both simultaneously: the runway exit is here, and proceed with the appropriate caution as you make that transition. Rather than requiring a pilot to process two separate fixtures at slightly different points during the highest-workload phase right after touchdown, integrating both cues into one cohesive signal reduces the chance of missing either message during that critical transition.

Combining turnoff guidance and exit marking into a single housing also reduces the physical and electrical complexity of the installation at what is often a geometrically awkward point on the airfield, while still delivering the same clear green/yellow visual language pilots already recognise from standard taxiway and caution lighting elsewhere on the field.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Taxiway turnoff/runway exit indicator fixtures are installed at runway exit points where taxiways connect to the runway, particularly at busy airports with complex ground movement patterns. They are used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where efficient and safe runway exit is critical
  • Rapid exit taxiways where high-speed exits require clear visual cues
  • Busy international airports with high traffic volumes and complex taxiway layouts
  • Low visibility operations where integrated guidance is essential
  • Military airfields requiring robust and reliable ground movement guidance
  • Airports with multiple intersecting taxiways where exit point confusion is a risk
Placement

Installation & Location

The combined fixture is installed at the runway exit point in place of what would otherwise be two separate fixtures, set into the pavement at the surveyed boundary between the runway and the exit taxiway so both the green centreline guidance and the yellow exit caution are presented from the same physical location. Because the unit integrates multiple light sources into a single housing, its base can and cable entry are sized accordingly, with the internal wiring between light sources and terminal block completed at manufacture rather than requiring several individual connections to be made up in the field. The fixture's single housing simplifies the pit and duct layout at this location compared to installing separate turnoff and exit fixtures, though the two lighting functions within it are still typically wired to preserve independent circuit assignment where the site's design calls for it. Positioning is tied to the specific rapid-exit or standard-exit geometry of the runway it serves, matching the fixture to the same surveyed reference used for the runway edge and threshold lighting it sits alongside.

Function

How Does It Work?

Taxiway turnoff/runway exit indicator fixtures operate as an integrated guidance system:

  • Integrated Design: The fixture combines multiple light sources in a single housing, typically including green lights for the taxiway centreline and yellow lights for cautionary indication at the exit point.
  • Guidance Pattern: The fixture may use a combination of steady and flashing lights to provide clear directional cues. Green lights indicate the correct path onto the taxiway, while yellow lights provide a cautionary alert for the turnoff.
  • Positioning: The fixture is installed at the runway exit point, aligned with the taxiway centreline, providing a clear visual path from the runway onto the taxiway.
  • Integration: The fixture is integrated with runway edge lights, taxiway centreline lights, lead-off lights, stop bars, and other AGL systems to provide seamless transition guidance.
  • Control: The fixture is controlled by the CCR and ALCMS, with intensity adjustment to match prevailing visibility conditions.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Although the green and yellow elements share one housing, they are not one controlled object — each colour is typically wired into its own series circuit (green onto the taxiway centreline loop, yellow onto a separate cautionary or guard-light circuit) with its own CCR and its own loop-level fault detection. The ALCMS commands the two independently, which is why a fixture can show green without yellow, or vice versa, depending on which circuit is energised at that moment. This also means the fixture reports as two separate points in the control architecture despite being one physical unit: a fault on the yellow element's circuit raises a loop fault on that circuit alone, with the green element and every other fixture on the centreline loop unaffected, and a technician diagnosing a "half-lit" fixture is really diagnosing one of its two independent circuits, not the housing as a whole.

Diagram

One Housing, Two Signals

The distinguishing feature of this fixture is consolidation: rather than mounting a separate green taxiway-entry light and a separate yellow caution light at the exit point — each with its own base, isolating transformer, and series-circuit tap — both signals share a single housing, base, and transformer connection. That halves the installation footprint and the number of failure points at a location that already sees concentrated aircraft wheel loading.

Construction

Physical Construction

Unlike a turnoff light built from separate green and yellow fixtures, this component integrates both LED arrays or filtered halogen lamp sources into a single die-cast aluminium housing, anodised or powder-coated against fuel and de-icing fluid exposure. Each light source keeps its own optical prism and internal leads within the shared body, gasket- and O-ring-sealed to an IP67/IP68 rating as one unit. The fixture is typically flush-mounted in an L-868 base can rated for a 100,000 kg (220,000 lb) aircraft wheel load, though elevated versions on a frangible coupling in an L-867 base can are also used. Despite sharing a housing, the green and yellow circuits remain electrically separate all the way to two independent sealed L-823 splice connectors, each feeding its own isolating transformer — a design that trades the installation simplicity of one housing for the maintenance complexity of testing two circuits within it, which is the main consideration when choosing this fixture over separate turnoff and caution lights.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Integrated Optical Alignment

The integrated design requires precise alignment of both green and yellow optical systems. Misalignment can cause incorrect colour projection or reduced visibility in one direction. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are essential.

Lens Contamination

The fixture is exposed to rubber deposits from aircraft tyres, de-icing fluids, and dirt. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and colour visibility for both green and yellow light sources.

LED Driver and Circuit Complexity

Integrated fixtures with multiple light sources require more complex LED driver and circuit arrangements. Faults in one circuit can affect the other, potentially compromising guidance. Regular system testing and diagnostics are essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Incorrect Colour Projection or Reduced Directional Visibility
Likely Cause
Precise alignment of the integrated green and yellow optical systems has drifted, causing one colour to project incorrectly or be less visible from one direction.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the alignment of both optical systems separately using a digital level or specialized alignment jig, comparing each against its design aim point.
Corrective Action
Re-align each optical system independently to the design tolerance, and re-verify from both approach directions before closing the fault.
Reduced Light Output from Either Colour Channel
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits, de-icing fluid, or dirt have contaminated the shared lens, affecting both the green and yellow light sources.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for contamination; take photometric readings on both colour channels and compare against expected output.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush, then re-verify both channels' output after cleaning.
Fault in One Colour Circuit Affecting the Other
Likely Cause
The integrated fixture's more complex LED driver/circuit arrangement has developed a fault that is cross-affecting both light sources.
Diagnostic Steps
Run system diagnostics on both circuits independently to isolate which channel originated the fault; check for shared power or control wiring issues.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted driver/circuit component, then re-test both channels together to confirm the fault has not migrated back.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Integrated Housing Fault Taking Out Both Colour Functions Simultaneously
High Severity
Root Cause
A housing crack, moisture ingress, or connector fault at the single integrated body shared by the green and yellow modules, since this fixture combines both colours in one housing rather than the separate two-housing, two-base, two-transformer-tap approach used elsewhere.
Effect
Both the green taxiway-centreline-continuation cue and the yellow turnoff-flag cue are lost together from a single physical fault, a common-mode failure the two-housing alternative is specifically designed to avoid.
Detection Method
Functional test of both colour modules together whenever any fault is reported at this fixture, rather than assuming one module's failure leaves the other unaffected.
Shared Transformer Tap Fault Affecting Both Modules Together
High Severity
Root Cause
A fault at the shared electrical connection point feeding both the green and yellow modules from within the single integrated housing.
Effect
Both modules lose power or show correlated intensity issues together, rather than the independent per-module behaviour the two-transformer-tap alternative provides.
Detection Method
Voltage and continuity check at the shared connection point specifically, to distinguish a shared-feed fault from an individual module fault.
Green Module LED / Driver Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or a manufacturing defect in the green module's LED array or driver, independent of the yellow module.
Effect
Loss of the green taxiway-centreline-continuation cue while the yellow turnoff-flag cue continues operating normally.
Detection Method
Test activation of the green module specifically, isolated from the yellow module's circuit where the design allows.
Yellow Module LED / Driver Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or a manufacturing defect in the yellow module's LED array or driver, independent of the green module.
Effect
Loss of the yellow turnoff-flag cue while the green taxiway-centreline-continuation cue continues operating normally.
Detection Method
Test activation of the yellow module specifically, isolated from the green module's circuit where the design allows.
Connector / Base Moisture Ingress
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress at the fixture's single base and connector, feeding both modules from the one integrated point.
Effect
Intermittent operation of one or both modules, or a partial ground fault affecting the shared base.
Detection Method
Megger testing of the circuit and visual inspection of the base can seal and connector during maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate All Sources in the Combined Housing
Electrical Safety

Because the fixture integrates multiple light sources and circuits in one housing, de-energise and lock out every CCR feeding it and verify zero energy at each source before opening the base can.

Colour/Function Verification Before Restoration
Compliance

Confirm the green centreline element and yellow exit-caution element are reconnected to their correct sources before re-energising; the integrated design means a wiring error is easy to miss on visual inspection alone.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

This fixture marks the runway exit during the critical post-landing phase; coordinate with ATC, raise a NOTAM for any outage, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the runway and exit path.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for while the base can is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test each circuit feeding the fixture before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can affect one or both integrated functions.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE on series-circuit connections, and confirm the base can is bonded/grounded before reconnecting.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify both the centreline guidance and exit-caution functions operate correctly and at full intensity before closing out the work and cancelling the NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Integrated Housings Hide Wiring Errors From Visual Inspection
Compliance

Because this fixture combines the centreline and exit-caution functions in one housing, a wiring error between the two functions doesn't show up as an obviously wrong-looking connection the way it might in a fixture with separated, visible circuits; verify function-by-function against the wiring diagram rather than by appearance.

Test Both Integrated Functions Independently
Troubleshooting

Confirming the centreline element lights doesn't confirm the exit-caution element is correctly wired to its own source; megger and function-test each source separately even though they share one housing.

Flush Fit Matters as Much as Photometrics
Mechanical

An inset fixture sitting even slightly proud of grade becomes a snag or FOD risk for aircraft and ground vehicles well before it fails photometrically; verify flush seating against the surrounding pavement grade at every reinstall, not just light output.

Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED fixture is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual LED load rather than assuming the existing transformer is "close enough."

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven brightness even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

De-Icing Fluid Ages Seals Faster Than UV Alone
Durability

Repeated exposure to glycol-based de-icing fluid degrades gaskets and lens seals faster than sun and weather alone at a fixture positioned right at the runway exit. Specify seal materials rated for glycol contact and inspect gasket condition at overhaul, not only at initial install.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about taxiway turnoff/runway exit indicator fixtures: combined guidance, colours, control, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a taxiway turnoff/runway exit indicator fixture?

It is a combined guidance fixture that integrates turnoff indication and runway exit marking in one housing, using green and yellow light sources at the runway exit point.

Q2What do the green and yellow lights mean?

Green shows the correct path onto the taxiway, aligned with taxiway centreline guidance, and yellow or amber gives a cautionary indication at the exit point. Steady and flashing patterns may be used for directional cues.

Q3Can the green and yellow elements be switched separately?

Yes. They are switched independently so only the applicable indication is shown.

Q4Why combine both functions in one fixture?

At runway exits pilots need both messages at once: the exit is here, and proceed with caution. One housing also reduces physical and electrical complexity at an often awkward point on the airfield.

Q5Where are these fixtures installed?

At runway exit points where taxiways connect to the runway, especially rapid exit taxiways, CAT II/III precision runways and busy airports with complex ground movement.

Q6How is the fixture controlled?

By the CCR and the ALCMS, with intensity adjusted to match prevailing visibility. It integrates with runway edge lights, taxiway centreline lights, lead-off lights and stop bars.

Q7Which standards apply to these fixtures?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46, IEC TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief 67. Always verify the current edition.

Q8What maintenance and faults should I expect?

Typical maintenance is quarterly lens cleaning, semi-annual dual-colour alignment checks and annual LED driver, circuit and photometric checks. Common faults are optical misalignment, lens contamination and faults in one colour circuit affecting the other.
