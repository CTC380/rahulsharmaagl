MARKINGS VS LIGHTING COMPLETE NOTES

1

THE SIMPLEST DIFFERENCE

Runway markings and runway lighting are both visual aids, but they are fundamentally different systems. Markings are patterns applied to the pavement and are seen by reflected or ambient light. Lighting is light-emitting equipment that produces or directs light itself.

Together they give runway identification, alignment, threshold and runway-end identification, lateral and longitudinal guidance and touchdown-area guidance.

Memory
  • MARKING = SURFACE INFORMATION
  • LIGHTING = ILLUMINATED INFORMATION
  • Marking = passive · Lighting = active

The governing FAA documents are AC 150/5340-1M (airport markings) and AC 150/5340-30J (design and installation details for visual aids, including runway lighting).

Neither replaces the other. Markings cannot give controlled intensity, directional colour, flashing sequence, CCR brightness steps or beam distribution. Lighting cannot give painted runway numbers, surface aiming-point geometry or permanent pavement coding. Lighting also does not make poor markings acceptable.

3

WHY BOTH ARE REQUIRED

ConditionRole
DaylightMarkings give strong surface references; lighting may still operate depending on category, weather and control setting
NightLighting is critical; markings are seen by reflected light
Low visibilityContrast falls and markings are harder to read; lighting and specialised systems give illuminated references. The whole visual-aid system becomes critical

Runway visual aids fall into three groups: markings (what is painted), lighting (what emits light) and signs (coded information by illuminated or reflective characters). Do not confuse them.

4

RUNWAY MARKINGS

FAA marking requirements depend on runway category: visual, nonprecision instrument or precision instrument. A precision runway has the fullest set.

MarkingPurpose and pattern
DesignationRunway number from magnetic azimuth, e.g. heading ≈ 090° gives 09
CenterlineLongitudinal alignment; dashed/segmented line
ThresholdStart of the landing portion; longitudinal bars
Aiming pointVisual reference for the touchdown area; large rectangles
Touchdown zonePairs of rectangular bars about the centerline, coded in 500 ft (150 m) increments
Side stripesContinuous white lines along each side giving contrast against terrain or shoulders
Shoulder stripesYellow; pavement not intended for aircraft use

Written with two digits (09) as in ICAO practice; FAA practice for single-digit runways differs. The FAA touchdown-zone coding above is also FAA-specific: ICAO sets the number of touchdown-zone marking pairs (from one to six) from the landing distance available, so check the Annex 14 table for Indian runways.

Runway markings are generally white; taxiway and holding-position markings and areas not for aircraft use are generally yellow. Marking geometry covers location, dimensions, stripe width, length, spacing, alignment and pavement relationship.

5

RUNWAY LIGHTING

LightFunction and colour
EdgeLateral limits. White, with yellow in the caution zone on instrument runways (FAA): the last 2,000 ft (600 m) or half the runway, whichever is less, as seen by a landing aircraft. The same lights show white to aircraft landing from the other end
Threshold / runway endGreen as seen by an aircraft approaching to land (threshold); red as seen by an aircraft on the runway looking toward the end (runway end)
CenterlineIlluminated longitudinal guidance; colour changes near the runway end (FAA and ICAO use different distances, see Notes 18 and 19)
Touchdown zoneIlluminated longitudinal guidance in the touchdown area
Approach lightsBars, rows and flash sequences
PAPIFour or two units giving red/white combinations
Guard and specialised lightsOther safety functions

AC 150/5340-30J puts edge lights on a line parallel to the centerline, generally not less than 2 ft (0.6 m) nor more than 10 ft (3 m) from the edge of full-strength pavement, with longitudinal spacing normally not above 200 ft (61 m). That is a lighting installation parameter, not a marking parameter. Lighting geometry covers location, spacing, mounting, elevation, azimuth, beam angle, intensity, colour and distribution.

Verify spacing and offset figures against AC 150/5340-30J before relying on them.
ICAO and Indian practice. This note quotes the FAA ACs. Indian aerodromes work to ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I and DGCA requirements, which differ in places. ICAO Annex 14 (5.3.9.7) allows the yellow edge-light section to be the last 600 m or one-third of the runway length, whichever is less, and ties it to the end remote from where the take-off run starts, so how it is applied for landings needs checking against the aerodrome's approved design. ICAO edge-light spacing is also not more than 60 m. Quote the source (FAA or ICAO) whenever you give a distance.

7

COLOUR, DIRECTION AND PATTERN

Paint uses a simple scheme. Lighting uses many colours to separate threshold, runway end, caution zones, taxiway edge, stop bars and approach guidance, and a fixture can show different colours by viewing direction: one threshold/end light shows green one way and red the other. Paint cannot do this.

ColourLighting usePaint use
WhiteRunway edge, centerlineRunway markings
YellowEdge caution zoneTaxiway, holding position, non-aircraft areas
GreenThreshold, approached from outside; taxiway centerline lightsNot an equivalent function
RedRunway end seen toward runway; stop bars; PAPI lower beamNot a runway-end signal
BlueTaxiway edge—
Same colour does not mean same meaning. Meaning = colour + location + pattern + direction + function. "Red always means runway end" is incomplete. Treat the table as a simplified memory aid and verify against the applicable standard.

Marking communicates by shape, position, colour and pattern. Lighting adds intensity, direction and beam distribution.

8

ACTIVE VS PASSIVE: THE ELECTRICAL CHAIN

Airport powerAGL controlCCRPrimary series cableIsolation transformerSecondary cableConnectorRunway light

A marking needs none of this: no CCR, transformer, primary or secondary cable or L-823 connector. Its chain is pavement → surface preparation → marking material → layout. Lighting can be switched, dimmed, monitored and remotely controlled (control panel, CCR, ALCMS, SCADA), and a series-circuit system uses CCR current steps for intensity; markings cannot be dimmed. See Note 09 and Note 10.

PAPI (Note 11) is the clearest contrast: units are positioned, vertically aimed and optically calibrated to give 4W → 3W1R → 2W2R → 1W3R → 4R, which paint cannot do.

9

PERFORMANCE, WEAR AND WEATHER

Photometry

Lighting is assessed on intensity, distribution, colour, beam angle and coverage. Markings are assessed on colour, contrast, visibility, wear and surface condition.

"On" is not "compliant"

A lamp can be on with wrong intensity, colour, distribution or aiming. Paint can be present yet lack contrast.

Marking wear

Traffic, rubber deposits, cleaning, weather, UV, pavement work, chemicals.

Lighting wear

Lamp or LED ageing, contaminated or damaged lens, water ingress, cable, transformer and connector faults, mechanical movement.

Rain

Wet pavement reduces contrast and adds glare; water can add reflections and, in defective equipment, affect insulation.

Rubber deposits

Reduce marking contrast around touchdown areas; pavement cleaning matters.

Worn centerline paint is not fixed by replacing a light; failed edge lights are not fixed by painting. If a pilot says the runway edge is hard to see at night, check both: side stripe, contrast and pavement; and edge-light operation, intensity, spacing, colour, lens contamination and photometry. Do not assume the problem is only electrical. If both are degraded, assess the whole system against the applicable operational and maintenance criteria.

11

INSPECTION CHECKLISTS

Markings

Designation correct · centerline · threshold · aiming point · TDZ where applicable · side stripes · shoulders where required · colour · rubber and wear · geometry · contrast.

Lighting

Edge, threshold, runway-end, centerline, TDZ and approach lights · PAPI indication · brightness level · colour · lenses · water ingress · fixtures · connectors · transformers · CCR · control and monitoring · photometry.

Field identification questions

What am I seeing, where is it, what colour, from which direction, what does it communicate, how is it powered, does it meet its optical requirement, and which standard governs it? A white painted line is marking; a white luminous point along the edge is lighting. At night, white paint and white lights are different systems that can together define the edge.

A drawing also differs: marking drawings show dimensions, stripe widths, spacing and positions; lighting drawings show fixture locations, circuit numbers, cable routes, transformers, CCR circuits and control wiring.

12

COMMON MISTAKES

Treating them as one visual aid

Passive surface vs active optical system.

Assuming all guidance comes from AGL

Markings and signs contribute too.

A painted line replaces centerline lights

It cannot give controlled intensity or direction.

A working light is compliant

Check photometry.

Ignoring marking condition on an AGL inspection

Both systems share the runway environment.

Same colour logic for paint and lights

Meaning comes from the standardised application.

Ignoring viewing direction

The same fixture can show green or red.

Changing lighting without checking marking layout

Coordinate the two disciplines.

Exam & Interview

EXAM / INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

What is the difference between runway marking and runway lighting?

Markings are visual information applied to the pavement; lighting is illuminated equipment giving visual guidance.

Are runway markings electrically powered?

No.

Does runway lighting need electrical power?

Yes.

What is the main runway marking colour in the FAA system?

White.

What colour are taxiway and holding-position markings?

Yellow.

What colour are normal runway edge lights?

White, with a specified yellow caution zone on applicable instrument runways.

What colour marks the landing threshold toward an approaching aircraft?

Green.

What colour marks the runway end toward an aircraft on the runway?

Red.

What is the centerline marking? The centerline light?

A pavement marking giving longitudinal alignment; an illuminated longitudinal guidance system.

Does a marking use a CCR or isolation transformer?

No.

Can runway lighting use a CCR?

Yes, in conventional series-circuit AGL systems.

Why is photometry associated mainly with lighting?

Lighting depends on measurable intensity, colour and beam distribution.

Can a light be on but fail photometric requirements?

Yes.

Can a marking exist but perform poorly?

Yes: wear, contamination or poor contrast degrade it.

What is the difference between a passive and an active visual aid?

A passive aid such as a marking generates no light; an active aid such as lighting generates or directs light.

Why can threshold lighting show green one way and red the other?

The fixture is optically configured for different directional outputs.

Why can't a painted threshold do the same?

It cannot give directionally controlled luminous output.

Why does lighting support night operations better?

It emits controlled light instead of relying on reflected ambient light.

Why maintain both?

They give complementary visual information.

Final Study Takeaway

FINAL STUDY TAKEAWAY

Markings tell the pilot through the pavement surface; lighting tells the pilot through controlled optical signals. The complete system is marking + lighting + signs + control + maintenance + compliance.

Complete visual aid = marking + lighting + signs + correct maintenance.

Passive and Active Aids Fail Differently

Concept

Markings wear and fade; lights fail electrically. Inspect each in the way that suits it.

Check Both at Night and in Wet Conditions

Inspection

Painted markings behave very differently from lights in rain and darkness.

Keep Them Consistent With Each Other

Integration

Markings, signs and lights must tell the same story. Review them together after any layout change.

Attribute Faults to the Right System

Fault Attribution
