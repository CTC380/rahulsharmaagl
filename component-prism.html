Prism

The precision optical heart of AGL lighting fixtures. A prism refracts and directs light into the required beam pattern, ensuring accurate photometric performance for runways, taxiways, and approach lighting systems. Made from high-grade glass or optical acrylic with precisely calculated surfaces.

Overview

What Is It?

A Prism is a precision optical component used in AGL fixtures to refract, reflect, and direct light from the source (LED or halogen lamp) into the specific beam pattern required by ICAO and FAA standards. The prism's geometry is precisely calculated — typically using faceted or curved surfaces — to achieve the correct vertical and horizontal beam angles, intensity distribution, and cut-off characteristics. Prisms are made from high-transparency optical glass or acrylic, with coatings to minimize reflection losses and maintain photometric performance over the fixture's lifespan.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The prism's purpose is to take the comparatively unfocused light from an LED or halogen source and shape it into the precise beam pattern — the specific vertical and horizontal angles, intensity distribution, and cut-off — that ICAO and FAA photometric standards require for that fixture type. This matters because a runway edge light and a threshold light are only interpretable to a pilot if each delivers its light at the correct angles and doesn't spill into neighbouring zones with the wrong meaning; the prism's precisely calculated geometry is what turns a generic light source into a fixture that actually meets its certified beam specification. Anti-reflective coatings and optical-quality polishing serve the same purpose from a different angle: minimising the light lost inside the prism so the fixture achieves the required intensity without needing a disproportionately powerful (and less efficient) light source.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Prisms are used in virtually every AGL lighting fixture that requires a controlled light beam:

  • Runway Edge Lights: Symmetric or asymmetric prisms to define runway boundaries.
  • Runway Centreline Lights: Bi-directional prisms for 360° visibility along the centreline.
  • Threshold & End Lights: Uni-directional prisms for approach-side visibility.
  • Taxiway Lights: Blue or green prisms for edge and centreline guidance.
  • Approach Lighting: Precision prisms for barrettes, crossbars, and flashing lights.
  • PAPI & VASI: Specialised prisms that create the red/white colour transition for glide slope indication.
Placement

Installation & Location

The prism is seated in a dedicated recess or bracket directly in front of the light source, positioned at the manufacturer's specified distance and angle so the emitter sits exactly at the prism's optical design point; getting this distance wrong reshapes the beam the fixture produces even though every other component is correct. It's fitted with a gasket or seal at its mounting face to keep the optical chamber weathertight, and its rotational orientation is set to an index mark or keyway, critical for asymmetric or uni-directional prisms, so the beam is aimed the correct way (toward the approach direction, for instance) rather than simply somewhere in the right general plane. Once positioned, it's protected behind the lens and top cover, and its alignment is re-checked any time the fixture is opened for lamp or LED module replacement.

Function

How Does It Work?

A prism works on the principle of refraction and total internal reflection. When light enters the prism from the source, it bends (refracts) at the entry surface, travels through the optical medium, and is directed (either by refraction or reflection) at the exit surface to form the required beam. The prism's geometry — including angles, curvature, and surface finish — is designed using ray-tracing software to meet specific photometric requirements. Some prisms are made of multiple elements to achieve complex beam patterns. The optical surfaces are precision-polished to optical quality (typically better than 1/4 wavelength) and coated with anti-reflective coatings to maximize light transmission.

Construction

Physical Construction

Prisms are moulded or precision-ground from high-transparency optical glass or acrylic, with the working surfaces cut as a series of calculated facets or continuous curves rather than a single flat face, since the exact surface geometry is what produces the required vertical and horizontal beam angles. Surface finish and facet angle tolerances are held tightly during manufacture, because even small deviations shift the beam pattern enough to fall outside the ICAO or FAA photometric envelope the fixture must certify to. An anti-reflective coating is typically applied to minimise Fresnel losses at each optical surface, preserving output efficiency across multiple internal reflections. The finished prism is bonded, clamped, or seated in a machined recess within the housing or top cover, positioned against locating features that fix its rotational and axial alignment relative to the light source, so the beam pattern stays correct without field adjustment after assembly.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Surface Degradation & Scratching

Prism surfaces can become scratched or hazed from abrasive cleaning, bird droppings, and airborne contaminants. This reduces light transmission and can scatter light outside the required beam pattern. Use only approved, non-abrasive cleaning methods.

UV Degradation of Acrylic Prisms

Acrylic (PMMA) prisms are susceptible to UV degradation over time, leading to yellowing, surface cracking, and reduced transmission. In high-UV environments, glass prisms or UV-stabilised acrylic are recommended.

Thermal Stress Cracking

Rapid temperature changes (e.g., hot lamp start in cold weather) can cause thermal stress in glass prisms, leading to cracking. Proper thermal management and the use of heat-resistant optical glass are essential for reliable long-term performance.

Misalignment During Maintenance

If the prism is removed or disturbed during lamp replacement, the beam pattern can be misaligned. Always use alignment jigs and follow manufacturer's specifications to restore the correct optical orientation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Beam Pattern Off-Aim After Maintenance
Likely Cause
Prism disturbed or reinstalled without correct orientation during a lamp replacement or other service work.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare current beam aim against the reference orientation using an alignment jig; check for orientation markings on the prism mount.
Corrective Action
Reseat the prism to its correct orientation using the alignment jig and manufacturer's specification; re-verify beam pattern with a photometric check.
Reduced Light Output / Scattered Beam
Likely Cause
Surface scratching or haze from abrasive cleaning, bird droppings, or airborne contaminants degrading the optical surface.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the prism surface for scratching or haze; run a photometric intensity/beam-pattern check against rated values.
Corrective Action
Clean using only approved, non-abrasive methods; replace the prism if scratching or haze has progressed beyond what cleaning can restore.
Yellowing or Cracking on an Acrylic Prism
Likely Cause
UV degradation of standard acrylic (PMMA) material over time in a high-UV environment.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect for yellowing, surface crazing, or fine cracking; check whether the fitted material is UV-stabilised acrylic or standard grade.
Corrective Action
Replace with a UV-stabilised acrylic or glass prism for sites with high UV exposure.
Cracked Glass Prism
Likely Cause
Thermal stress from a rapid hot-lamp start in cold weather, especially if the prism is not heat-resistant optical glass.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crack pattern for a thermal-shock signature radiating from the lamp-facing surface; confirm the glass type against specification.
Corrective Action
Replace with heat-resistant optical glass as specified, and review the fixture's thermal management if cracking recurs.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Removal
Electrical Safety

Isolate the fixture and allow the lamp to cool before removing the prism; a live circuit or hot lamp behind the optic is a shock and burn hazard during service.

Preserve Orientation on Reinstall
Workmanship

Note the prism's orientation markings before removal and reinstall it exactly as found; a misoriented prism throws the beam off-aim even though the optic itself is undamaged.

Thermal Shock Avoidance
Handling

Do not apply a cold cleaning solution to a hot glass prism or reinstall immediately after a lamp change; allow the fixture to reach ambient temperature to avoid thermal-shock cracking.

Cleaning Method
Workmanship

Clean the prism with a soft cloth and manufacturer-approved cleaner rather than an abrasive pad; abrasive cleaning scratches or hazes the surface and scatters the beam.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for the removed prism, gasket, and fasteners at all times during handling, and secure or remove any loose material before returning the fixture to service to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Verify Beam Aim After Service
Compliance

Check beam aim against the alignment jig or reference orientation after any prism work before closing out the task, since orientation errors are not visually obvious.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return a fixture to service with a cracked, crazed, or misoriented prism; confirm the optic is fully seated and correctly aligned before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Orientation Marks Wear Off Before the Prism Does
Workmanship

A factory orientation mark on a prism can fade or rub off well before the optic itself needs replacement; re-mark the orientation in a durable way at every service if the original marking is hard to read, rather than guessing from memory.

Rapid Cooling Cracks Glass Optics the Same Way Rapid Heating Does
Handling

A hot prism exposed to cold night air or a draft during changeover can crack from rapid cooling just as readily as from a cold cleaner applied to a hot prism; let ambient conditions equalize from both directions, not just after removing a heat source.

A Beam Pattern Fault Can Be the Prism, Not the Lamp
Troubleshooting

A scattered or off-aim beam is often assumed to be a lamp alignment issue first; check for a hazed, chipped, or misoriented prism before re-aiming or replacing the light source, since a degraded prism produces the same symptom.

Verify Beam Aim With an Instrument, Not by Eye
Compliance

Orientation and aim errors from prism work are not reliably detectable by eye, especially at night; use an alignment jig or photometric check after any prism service rather than judging the beam visually.

Gasket Compression Set Outlasts the Prism's Visual Condition
Durability

A prism gasket can lose sealing force well before the prism itself shows any wear, so an optic that looks fine on removal doesn't mean its gasket is still doing its job; replace the gasket at every prism service as routine.

FOD Risk From a Cracked Prism Starts Before It Fully Fails
Operational

A prism with a hairline crack found during inspection is already at risk of shedding fragments under vibration or thermal cycling, well before it fails completely; treat a cracked prism as an immediate replacement rather than a monitor-and-revisit item.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the prism: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a prism in an AGL fixture?

A prism is a precision optical component that refracts, reflects and directs light from the source (LED or halogen lamp) into the beam pattern required by ICAO and FAA standards. Its faceted or curved geometry sets the vertical and horizontal beam angles, intensity distribution and cut-off.

Q2How does an AGL prism work?

It works by refraction and total internal reflection. Light bends at the entry surface, travels through the optical medium and is directed at the exit surface to form the required beam. The geometry is designed with ray-tracing software, and the surfaces are optically polished and typically anti-reflective coated.

Q3What is a prism made of?

Typical materials are optical glass (BK7 or fused silica) or PMMA acrylic. Refractive index ranges from about 1.49 for acrylic to 1.52 for BK7, and the operating range is -40°C to +80°C.

Q4What beam angles do prisms produce?

Typical vertical beam angle is 3° to 6° and horizontal 6° to 12°, depending on the application, in symmetric, asymmetric, bi-directional or uni-directional patterns.

Q5Where are prisms used?

In virtually every AGL fixture that needs a controlled beam: runway edge lights, runway centreline lights (bi-directional), threshold and end lights (uni-directional), taxiway lights, and approach lighting.

Q6What is the difference between a prism and a reflector?

A prism directs light through a transparent optical medium using refraction and total internal reflection. A reflector redirects light from a polished metallic or coated surface instead of transmitting it through a lens or prism.

Q7Which standards apply to AGL prisms?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 Attachment A (photometric requirements the prism must deliver), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (the qualified fixture's optics are tested as part of the fixture) and IEC TS 61827. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a prism be cleaned and inspected?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cleaning, semi-annual for surface condition and alignment verification, and annual for UV degradation inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with prisms?

Common problems are surface degradation and scratching, UV yellowing of acrylic prisms, thermal stress cracking (especially glass) and misalignment during maintenance. A beam that is off-aim after service usually points to misorientation of the prism.
