MALS

A medium‑intensity approach lighting system for non‑precision runways, typically featuring a configuration of light bars extending 427 metres (1,400 ft) from the runway threshold. MALS provides enhanced visual guidance compared to basic SALS/SSALS, offering improved lateral and distance reference for pilots during night and marginal weather operations, while remaining simpler and more cost‑effective than precision systems like ALSF.

Overview

What Is It?

MALS (Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System) is a non‑precision approach lighting system that provides enhanced visual guidance for pilots during final approach. It typically consists of seven five-light bars spaced at 61m (200 ft) intervals extending 427 metres (1,400 ft) from the runway threshold, with a single crossbar (two wing bars) at the 305m (1,000 ft) point. MALS offers greater intensity and improved visibility compared to basic SALS, making it suitable for airports with higher traffic volumes or more demanding weather conditions. Unlike precision systems (ALSF‑1/2), MALS does not include sequenced flashing lights, but it provides a robust visual centreline and distance reference for night and marginal VFR operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

MALS exists to give airports with meaningful traffic volume or more demanding weather a stronger visual approach reference than a basic single row of lights can provide, without introducing the sequenced flashing components that come with additional cost and complexity. Its optional crossbars at intermediate points add a depth and lateral reference along the row, letting a pilot judge distance and alignment more precisely than a plain unbroken line would allow, while stopping short of the dynamic directional cues that RAIL or SFL provide in the MALSR/MALSF variants. Its purpose sits deliberately between the most basic SALS-class systems and the flashing-augmented MALSR/MALSF configurations, offering airports a middle-tier upgrade path matched to their actual traffic and visibility demands.

Application

Where Is It Used?

MALS systems are installed at airports that support non‑precision approaches but require enhanced visual guidance. They are typically used on:

  • Non‑precision runways with higher traffic volumes or more demanding weather conditions
  • Regional airports serving commercial turboprop and regional jet operations
  • Military airfields where basic approach lighting is insufficient
  • Runways with moderate approach speeds where longer lead‑in is beneficial
  • Airfields transitioning from basic to enhanced lighting without the cost of precision systems
  • Locations where terrain or obstacles require improved visual reference
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out on lightweight frangible stands along the extended runway centreline at the standard 61 m (200 ft) spacing, running from the threshold out to 427 m (1,400 ft). A single crossbar stand pair is placed laterally at the 305 m (1,000 ft) point. Because MALS carries no sequenced flashing lights, the array is typically fed from a single steady-burning series circuit, with cable run in duct from each stand back to the regulator vault. Stands are sized for the lower loads of a medium-intensity system, and elevated piers are used only where the extended centreline crosses a road, ditch, or other minor obstacle. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline extended and threshold survey.

Function

How Does It Work?

The MALS system provides enhanced visual guidance through a combination of features:

  • Single Row of Lights: Seven five-light bars, steady‑burning white, spaced at 61m (200 ft) intervals, extend from the runway threshold out to 427 metres (1,400 ft), providing a visual centreline reference.
  • Crossbar Lights (Optional): A single crossbar (two five-light wing bars, one either side of the centreline bar) is located 305m (1,000 ft) from the threshold to provide distance reference and improve depth perception.
  • Medium Intensity: Higher intensity than basic SALS, with better visibility in marginal weather and brighter ambient light conditions.
  • Lateral Guidance: The row of lights helps the pilot align the aircraft with the runway centreline during the final approach segment.
  • Distance Reference: The spacing of the lights and optional crossbars provides a rough distance reference, helping the pilot judge the approach path.
  • Intensity Control: The lights are typically controlled by a CCR with intensity steps (usually 5‑step), allowing the ATC to adjust brightness for visibility conditions.
  • Simplicity: The system has no flashing lights or complex sequencing, making it simpler and more reliable than precision systems.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

MALS runs on a single series circuit loop, with no sequencer or flasher stage to coordinate — the centreline row and, where fitted, the crossbar lights are all wired into the same loop and therefore switch and dim together as one system. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step; this command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire MALS loop to the corresponding fixed current. Every fixture on the loop, centreline or crossbar, draws from the same current via its own isolating transformer and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no independent control of the crossbars or of any individual light along the row. Because MALS has no flashing stage, its control logic is the simplest of the approach lighting family: one command, one loop, one intensity for the whole array. Fault detection follows the same loop-based principle used across the AGL series circuit family: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing.

ALCMS → CCR → MALS Series Circuit Loop (Row + Crossbar) → Isolating Transformer → Fixture (with loop-level feedback)

Engineering View

Row & Crossbar Overview

MALS carries seven steady-white light bars locked to a flat 61m (200 ft) spacing over the full 427m (1,400 ft) FAA length, rather than a single continuous row. The real functional addition is the single crossbar at 305m (1,000 ft) — two five-light wing bars either side of the centreline bar that give the pilot a depth-perception reference the row alone can't provide.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each light stand is a frangible aluminium or composite pole bolted to a shallow concrete footing or ground screw anchor, sized to break away cleanly if struck while still holding the fixture head steady in wind. The fixture head is a die-cast aluminium housing containing an LED array or 100–200W halogen lamp behind a toughened lens, gasket-sealed to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating, mounted so the beam projects uni-directionally toward the approach. Where the optional crossbar is fitted, three to five fixture heads are ganged on a rigid horizontal bar carried on its own pair of frangible stands, wired as a branch off the same series circuit as the centreline row. Internal wiring runs to L-823 primary connectors sealed for direct burial, terminating at the isolating transformer housed at the base of each stand or in a nearby ground box, with cable run in duct back to the regulator vault. Stainless-steel fasteners and corrosion-resistant coatings are used throughout to withstand prolonged outdoor exposure over unprepared or unpaved approach terrain.

Installation Requirements

Frangible Stand Footing
Civil

Set each centreline stand footing at the standard 61 m (200 ft) spacing out to 427 m (1,400 ft), sized for the lighter loads of a medium-intensity system.

Elevated Piers at Minor Obstacles
Civil

Install piers only where the extended centreline crosses a road, ditch, or other minor obstacle, since most of the run needs no special foundation work.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay the duct run from each stand back to the regulator vault, sized for a single steady-burning series circuit with no separate flasher loop to route.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal the primary connector at each stand per manufacturer procedure, keeping every splice fully submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm each fixture's isolating transformer wattage matches the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Stand & Optional Crossbar Assembly
Mechanical

Fix the fixture head to each frangible stand, and where included, assemble the crossbar on its own pair of stands, torquing all fittings to spec.

Setting-Out to Extended Centreline
Mechanical

Tie stand positions to the extended runway centreline and threshold survey so the row stays true from the threshold out to 427 m (1,400 ft).

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record stand positions, cable route, connector seals, and torque values as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the steady-burning series circuit before backfill, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of first energisation.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity and record total loop resistance for every light along the full 427m (1,400 ft) run, including the crossbar, before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each light's isolating transformer matches the rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm the row and any crossbars respond correctly, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Crossbar Alignment Verification
Final Acceptance

Where crossbars are fitted at 150m, 300m, 450m, or 600m, check their lateral extent and alignment against the design layout, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Light Row Alignment & Aim Verification
Final Acceptance

Check spacing, elevation, and azimuth of each light along the 30m-interval row against the design aiming diagram.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity and colour with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances for non-precision approach lighting.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, and photometric readings alongside as-built light positions in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify beam intensity against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer at representative points along the array; degraded output usually points to lens haze or an aging light source.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean environmental pollutants, bird droppings, and de-icing residue from every fixture, particularly the more distant lights and crossbars where visibility matters most.

Alignment & Crossbar Check
Semi-annual

Verify fixture aim and crossbar positioning using a theodolite or laser alignment system, since wind, wildlife, or vehicle strikes can knock elevated fixtures out of tolerance.

Site Survey & Vegetation Clearance
Semi-annual

Walk the approach line to confirm no vegetation or structures have grown up to obstruct the light output since the last inspection.

CCR & Power Quality Check
Annual

Monitor voltage stability and CCR step response across the array, since MALS systems draw significant power and are sensitive to fluctuations that cause inconsistent brightness.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test isolation transformers and series circuits to confirm insulation resistance stays above the minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a series-circuit fault.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Examine primary and secondary cable connections for corrosion or loose crimps, a common source of intermittent series-circuit faults across the array.

Frangible Coupling & Base Inspection
Annual

Inspect elevated fixture couplings and base housings for corrosion or fatigue to ensure breakaway performance and structural stability along the full 427m (1,400 ft) run.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

MALS components are exposed to environmental pollutants, bird droppings, and de‑icing chemicals. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is essential to maintain light output and visibility, especially for the distant lights and crossbars.

Misalignment of Elevated Fixtures

Elevated approach lights and crossbars can be knocked out of alignment by wind, wildlife, or maintenance vehicles. Regular alignment checks using a theodolite or laser alignment system are essential to maintain the correct beam direction and crossbar positioning.

Power Supply and CCR Issues

MALS systems draw significant power. Voltage fluctuations, CCR faults, or cable damage can cause inconsistent brightness or total failure. Regular power quality monitoring and cable testing are recommended.

Crossbar Light Failures

If crossbar lights are included, individual failures can disrupt the distance reference pattern. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty crossbar lights are essential to maintain the system's effectiveness.

Vegetation and Obstruction Growth

At smaller airfields, vegetation or structures can grow up around approach lights and crossbars, obstructing the light output. Regular site inspections and vegetation management are essential to maintain clear lines of sight.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Light/Bar Dark, Rest of Array Normal
Likely Cause
Failed lamp/LED source, or an open isolating transformer at that fixture (blown primary link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp/LED source if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Array Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop feeding the approach lights (severed cable, corroded splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs along the approach centreline to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
Crossbar Light Failure / Distance Pattern Disrupted
Likely Cause
An individual crossbar light has failed (lamp/LED or transformer), disrupting the distance-reference pattern the crossbar provides.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the crossbar fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp/LED if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal connectors before backfill.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Elevated Fixture or Crossbar Misaligned
Likely Cause
Wind loading, wildlife strike, or vehicle contact has knocked an elevated fixture or crossbar off its aiming tolerance.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-check fixture and crossbar aim with a theodolite or laser alignment system; inspect the frangible coupling and mounting stake for cracking or play.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-aim the fixture or crossbar, replace the frangible coupling if fatigued, and address any base or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reduced or Hazy Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from environmental pollutants or de-icing residue, or an aging light source.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the lens; confirm the CCR is set to the correct intensity step for prevailing conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lens, replace a degraded light source, or correct the CCR step setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Light Source (Lamp/LED) Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised fixture seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single light or bar within the approach array goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the array continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol along the approach centreline, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire array.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or wildlife activity, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop feeding the approach lights.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Elevated Fixture / Crossbar Frangible Coupling Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading, wildlife strike, or contact from a maintenance vehicle, combined with ordinary material fatigue over the service interval.
Effect
Fixture or crossbar light leans, loosens, or separates from its mounting stake, pulling it out of aim tolerance along the approach centreline.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim checks with a theodolite or laser alignment system.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on an otherwise healthy circuit.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Lens Contamination / Reduced Intensity
Low Severity
Root Cause
Environmental pollutants, de-icing residue, or airborne particulate building up on the lens surface, combined with ordinary UV-driven material degradation over time.
Effect
Reduced or hazy light output that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds despite the light source itself being healthy.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of the lens during routine cleaning.
Crossbar Circuit Fault / Distance Pattern Disrupted
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A cable, connector, or transformer fault isolated to the crossbar branch circuit, or one or more crossbar lights drifting out of aim.
Effect
The distance-to-threshold cue provided by the crossbar becomes incomplete or misleading, even though the inline steady-burn bars remain fully lit.
Detection Method
Isolate and test the crossbar branch separately from the inline bar circuit, and visually verify all crossbar lights are lit and correctly aimed.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

MALS bar and crossbar circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR, then verify zero energy at the tower base or bar before opening any enclosure.

Elevated Tower & Off-Runway Access
Personnel Safety

Light bar towers extend along the extended centreline over terrain that may be uneven or off the paved movement area; use fall protection when climbing frangible towers and assess ground conditions before positioning vehicles or ladders.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible coupling or tower base with a rigid fitting, and do not over-torque connections during installation or repair; the structure must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Work on approach lighting affects an active runway's usable minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any bar or crossbar is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

Hot Lamp / Housing Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the fixture housing and lamp/LED module to cool before handling after an active period; incandescent bar lamps in particular retain significant heat and can cause burns during lens or lamp replacement.

FOD Control Along the Approach
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every tower worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work on a bar or crossbar circuit, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a bar out of aim tolerance or at reduced intensity; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Crossbar Aim Errors Are Easy to Miss at a Glance
Structural

A crossbar can look correctly positioned from ground level while its individual light aim has drifted enough to weaken the lateral cue at typical approach range. Verify aim with survey instruments rather than visual inspection alone.

Don't Assume MALS Wiring Mirrors Precision System Practice
Electrical

Because MALS lacks SFL control electronics, its loop topology is often simpler than a precision system and shouldn't be assumed to follow the same segmentation conventions when troubleshooting a fault or planning an upgrade — verify the as-built circuit directly.

Distant Lights Get Less Attention Than They Need
Durability

The lights furthest from the threshold are both the hardest to reach for maintenance and the most exposed to drainage runoff and wildlife activity; without a deliberate schedule weighting, they tend to accumulate contamination and misalignment faster than the near-threshold lights that get checked more often.

Track Intensity Trend Even Without an SFL Component
Photometric

It's tempting to under-inspect a steady-burning-only system relative to a precision array, but MALS still provides the primary visual reference for the approaches it supports; trend intensity data on the same rigor as a more complex system.

A Single Dark Segment Can Look Like a CCR Fault When It's a Connector
Troubleshooting

Because MALS loops can run long distances with several intermediate connectors, a single degraded connector can present as a segment-wide dimming that looks like a CCR problem. Check connector condition along the affected segment before assuming the regulator has failed.

Document Crossbar Positions Before Any Runway Work Nearby
Upgrade Planning

If pavement or drainage work is planned near the approach area, survey and record existing crossbar and centreline positions beforehand so post-work verification has a clear baseline to check against.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about MALS (Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System): layout, length, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is MALS?

MALS (Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System) is an approach lighting system that gives pilots visual guidance during final approach. It uses seven five-light bars of steady-burning white lights along the extended runway centreline.

Q2How long is a MALS installation?

427 metres (1,400 ft) from the runway threshold, with the bars spaced at 61 m (200 ft) intervals.

Q3Does MALS have flashing lights or a crossbar?

No flashing lights, it is steady-burning only. The page describes an optional single crossbar of two five-light wing bars at 305 m (1,000 ft) from the threshold, which improves distance reference and depth perception.

Q4Where is MALS used?

On runways with higher traffic or more demanding weather, regional airports serving turboprop and regional jet operations, and military airfields where basic approach lighting is not enough.

Q5What light sources does MALS use?

LED arrays or halogen lamps of about 100 W to 200 W, white and steady-burning, on elevated frangible fixtures fed from the series circuit.

Q6Which standards apply to MALS?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (light fixtures), IEC TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief 67 for LED components. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q7How often should MALS be maintained?

Typically quarterly lens cleaning, semi-annual alignment and crossbar checks with a theodolite or laser system, and annual photometric intensity checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q8What are the common problems with MALS?

Lens contamination from pollutants, bird droppings and de-icing residue, misalignment of elevated fixtures, power supply and CCR issues, and individual crossbar light failures that upset the distance cue.
