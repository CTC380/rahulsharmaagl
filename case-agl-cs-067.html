AGL-CS-067 — Individual Light Fails but Average Passes

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A photometric acceptance testing case where the average intensity passed the acceptance criterion, but one individual fixture failed — from measurement verification through statistical analysis, optical inspection, and corrective retesting.

Case ID

AGL-CS-067

Category

AGL Photometric Acceptance Testing & ICAO Compliance

Level

Expert

Case Type

Engineering Simulation

Primary Skill

Interpretation of individual photometric failures during acceptance testing and maintenance evaluation

Important: This is an engineering simulation created for training. It is not presented as an actual airport incident. All readings, values, dates and outcomes are illustrative, and no airport, organisation, person or manufacturer is identified or implied.

1Case Study Overview

During a scheduled runway lighting photometric acceptance test, twenty runway edge lights were measured using approved AGL photometric equipment.

The overall test results showed that the average measured intensity exceeded the required acceptance value, and the contractor initially claimed that the lighting system had passed.

However, one runway edge light produced only 58% of the required minimum intensity, while the remaining fixtures produced substantially higher values.

The average intensity remained acceptable because several lights significantly exceeded the minimum requirement.

The engineering question was:

Engineering Question

Can a runway lighting system be considered compliant when the average photometric result passes but an individual light fails the minimum photometric requirement?

This case demonstrates one of the most important principles in ICAO photometric acceptance testing: average compliance does not eliminate individual non-compliance.

2Learning Objectives

After completing this case study, the learner should be able to:

  • Interpret individual and average photometric results.
  • Understand ICAO acceptance philosophy.
  • Identify non-compliant individual fixtures.
  • Evaluate statistical masking effects.
  • Assess operational safety implications.
  • Make correct acceptance and maintenance decisions.
  • Document photometric non-conformities correctly.

3System Background

Photometric testing evaluates:

  • Peak intensity.
  • Beam distribution.
  • Chromaticity.
  • Beam alignment.
  • Individual fixture performance.
  • System uniformity.

Acceptance testing may involve:

  • Individual fixture measurements.
  • Sampling plans.
  • Average intensity calculations.
  • Minimum intensity verification.
  • Compliance evaluation.

A common misunderstanding is that high-performing fixtures can compensate for a weak fixture.

10Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The runway edge lighting system contained one photometrically non-compliant fixture, whose performance was masked by several higher-than-average fixtures.

The average intensity passed because:

  • Most fixtures exceeded the minimum requirement.
  • The statistical mean remained high.
  • One severely deficient fixture had limited effect on the average.

The failed fixture remained individually non-compliant.

11Why the Average Was Misleading

Consider:

Nine fixtures = 110%

One fixture = 58%

Average:

(9 × 110 + 58) / 10 = 104.8%

The average passes comfortably.

The failed fixture remains unacceptable.

This is a classic example of statistical masking.

12ICAO Compliance Principle

Photometric acceptance normally requires evaluation of:

  • Individual fixture performance.
  • Beam distribution.
  • Chromaticity.
  • System uniformity.

A deficient individual fixture cannot automatically be accepted because:

  • It creates a weak visual reference.
  • It reduces pattern continuity.
  • It may affect pilot visual acquisition.
  • It may indicate progressive optical deterioration.

13Corrective Action

The maintenance team:

  1. Removed the failed fixture.
  2. Replaced the optical module.
  3. Restored beam alignment.
  4. Performed photometric testing.
  5. Verified individual compliance.
  6. Recalculated system uniformity.
  7. Updated photometric records.
  8. Closed the non-conformity.

16Why This Case Was Important

Statistical masking can lead to:

  • Incorrect acceptance decisions.
  • Hidden maintenance problems.
  • Reduced visual guidance quality.
  • Progressive system deterioration.
  • ICAO non-compliance.

Photometric evaluation requires both statistical and engineering judgment.

18Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: Average runway edge light intensity = 106%. One fixture = 58%. All measurements verified. What should you do?

A. Accept the system because the average passes.
B. Ignore the individual failure.
C. Reject the individual fixture, investigate the root cause, replace or repair it, and repeat the photometric test before final acceptance.
D. Increase CCR current.
Correct answer: C. A single failed fixture is not acceptable even if the average passes. The root cause must be investigated and corrected before the system can be considered fully compliant.

21Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about average vs individual photometric compliance in acceptance testing.

Q1What is AGL-CS-067?

AGL-CS-067 is an engineering training case study. It covers a runway edge light acceptance test where the average intensity passed but one individual light failed the minimum requirement. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2Can a runway lighting system be compliant if the average passes but one light fails?

No. The case demonstrates that average compliance does not eliminate individual non-compliance. Each runway light is an independent visual reference, so a deficient fixture cannot simply be accepted.

Q3What were the results in AGL-CS-067?

Twenty runway edge lights were measured. The average intensity was 106%, but one fixture produced only 58% of the required minimum while the others were substantially higher.

Q4Why did the average hide the failed fixture?

This is statistical masking. Several lights well above the minimum pulled the average up, so one severely deficient fixture had little effect on it. Nine fixtures at 110% and one at 58% average about 104.8%.

Q5How was the failure confirmed as genuine?

The fixture was measured repeatedly (57%, 59%, 58%), neighbouring fixtures measured 110% and 114%, and inspection found lens contamination, reflector deterioration, LED optical aging and minor alignment error.

Q6Why must individual fixture performance be evaluated?

A deficient light creates a weak visual reference, reduces pattern continuity, may affect pilot visual acquisition, and may indicate progressive optical deterioration.

Q7What caused the failed fixture in AGL-CS-067?

Optical degradation: lens contamination, reflector deterioration, LED optical aging and minor alignment error, which gave reduced peak intensity, beam narrowing and vertical beam depression.

Q8How was the non-conformity corrected?

The failed fixture was removed, its optical module replaced and beam alignment restored. The team then retested, verified individual compliance, recalculated system uniformity, updated records and closed the non-conformity.

Q9What is the key lesson from this case?

Never accept a lighting system on the average alone. Evaluate each fixture against the minimum requirement, along with beam distribution, chromaticity and system uniformity.

22Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

A runway lighting system cannot be considered fully compliant when an individual light fails the minimum photometric requirement, even if the average intensity exceeds the acceptance value, because each fixture provides a critical independent visual reference for flight operations.

Systematic individual photometric verification, beam-distribution assessment, optical inspection, statistical analysis, and corrective retesting can prevent statistical masking from concealing operationally significant photometric defects.

In AGL engineering, pilots do not land on an average light; they land using every individual light that forms the runway visual pattern.
