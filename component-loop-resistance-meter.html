Loop Resistance Meter

A specialized low-resistance ohmmeter used to measure the total resistance of AGL series lighting circuits. The loop resistance meter is essential for verifying circuit integrity, detecting high-resistance connections, and ensuring that the series loop is within acceptable limits for proper Constant Current Regulator (CCR) operation.

Overview

What Is It?

A Loop Resistance Meter (also known as a low-resistance ohmmeter or micro-ohmmeter) is a precision test instrument designed to measure very low resistances in AGL series circuits. It is specifically used to:

  • Measure total loop resistance of the series circuit from the CCR output to the far end and back.
  • Detect high-resistance connections at splices, terminal blocks, or isolating transformer connections.
  • Verify circuit continuity and identify open circuits or broken conductors.
  • Calculate voltage drop across the series loop for proper CCR sizing and operation.
  • Trend resistance changes over time to detect gradual degradation due to corrosion or loose connections.

Unlike standard multimeters, loop resistance meters use a high test current (typically 1A to 10A) to overcome contact resistance effects and provide accurate measurements in the milliohm range. They are essential tools for commissioning, maintenance, and troubleshooting of AGL series circuits.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The loop resistance meter exists because a standard multimeter can't reliably detect the kind of subtle degradation that actually threatens an AGL series circuit — a slightly corroded splice or a loosening terminal connection that adds only a fraction of an ohm of resistance, but which will keep getting worse until it eventually causes a voltage drop severe enough to affect fixture brightness or trip the CCR. Using a higher test current lets this instrument see past contact-resistance noise and measure that small but meaningful change accurately.

Because it can trend resistance over time, its real value lies in catching a developing fault before it becomes an actual outage — a rising loop resistance reading during routine maintenance is a warning that a connection is degrading, giving technicians the chance to repair it proactively rather than waiting for a circuit to fail during live operations.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Loop resistance meters are used across all phases of AGL circuit management:

  • Installation Commissioning: Measuring loop resistance after cable installation to verify that connections are correct and within specification.
  • Preventive Maintenance: Periodic measurement of loop resistance to detect increasing resistance due to corrosion, loose connections, or cable degradation.
  • Fault Diagnosis: Locating high-resistance points in the loop that cause voltage drops, light intensity variations, or CCR instability.
  • After Splice Repairs: Verifying that repairs have restored the loop to acceptable resistance values.
  • Transformer Testing: Measuring the contribution of isolating transformers to the total loop resistance.

Testing is typically performed at the CCR output terminals with the circuit de-energized and all fixtures isolated (or with transformers in circuit, depending on the measurement objective).

Care

Storage & Handling

The meter's four-terminal (Kelvin) leads are handled carefully and stored uncoiled or loosely coiled, since the accuracy of a milliohm-range measurement depends on clean, undamaged lead connections that a tightly wound or kinked lead can compromise over time. Test clips and probes are inspected for wear or corrosion before each use, given that even a small increase in contact resistance at the probe tip can be mistaken for a fault in the circuit being tested. The instrument is stored in its case within its rated temperature range, with batteries or the internal test-current source maintained per the manufacturer's guidance so the meter is ready to deliver its rated test current when next used. Because this instrument is often used to trend resistance values over months or years, it's kept on a strict calibration schedule so a reading taken today can be meaningfully compared against one taken at the last inspection.

Function

How Does It Work?

A loop resistance meter operates on the four-wire (Kelvin) measurement principle to eliminate lead and contact resistance errors. The instrument:

  • Applies a known DC test current (typically 1A to 10A) through the series loop using two "current" leads.
  • Measures the voltage drop across the loop using two separate "potential" leads connected at the same points.
  • Calculates resistance using Ohm's Law (R = V/I) with high accuracy down to micro-ohm levels.
  • Displays resistance in milliohms (mΩ) or micro-ohms (µΩ) on a digital screen.

The four-wire method ensures that lead resistance and contact resistance do not affect the measurement, providing true loop resistance. Modern instruments include automatic ranging, data logging, and temperature compensation features. Some models can measure inductive components to evaluate transformer contributions.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The instrument's internal architecture exists mainly to keep the current-carrying and voltage-sensing paths electrically separate, since that separation is what the four-wire (Kelvin) method depends on. A constant-current source drives a known test current through the outer pair of leads (C1/C2) and into the loop under test, while a pair of dedicated potential leads (P1/P2), connected inside the current leads at the same physical points, feed a high-input-impedance differential amplifier that sees only the voltage drop across the loop itself — not the drop across the current leads' own resistance or their contact points. The MCU digitises this differential voltage, calculates R = V/I, and applies a temperature-compensation factor referenced to a standard conductor temperature (commonly 20°C) so results from different ambient conditions can be compared directly. On instruments that also report an inductive (reactive) component, the MCU drives the test current at more than one frequency and compares the phase and magnitude of the response to separate the loop's resistive and inductive contributions — relevant on AGL series circuits where an isolating transformer adds measurable inductance to what would otherwise be a pure resistance reading.

Separating the current and sense leads at the loop itself — not just at the meter — is what makes the four-wire reading immune to lead and contact resistance.
Operating Principle

Why Four Wires: Separating Current from Sensing

Splitting current injection (C1/C2) from voltage sensing (P1/P2) is the whole trick: the potential leads sit inboard of the current contacts and draw negligible current, so whatever resistance is sitting at the current-lead connections never gets added into the reading.
Construction

Physical Construction

A loop resistance meter is built as a rugged hand-held instrument in an impact-resistant polymer case, with internal circuitry using a four-wire (Kelvin) measurement technique to eliminate lead and contact resistance from the reading, essential given the very low resistances the instrument is designed to measure. The test current source, typically a regulated constant-current circuit capable of delivering several amps, is housed alongside precision measurement electronics isolated from the current-carrying path to maintain accuracy across the instrument's wide auto-ranging span. Heavy-gauge test leads with Kelvin clip or probe terminations carry the current and sense connections separately to the circuit under test, and the front panel houses a backlit digital display along with controls for test current selection and data logging functions. A rechargeable battery pack powers the unit, and the case is built with a carrying strap, protective boot, or holster for use in the field around airfield cable pits and connector housings, with connection terminals arranged to resist dust and moisture ingress during outdoor testing.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lead and Contact Resistance Errors

Using two-wire (instead of four-wire) connections or poor test lead contacts can introduce significant errors. Always use the Kelvin (four-wire) method and ensure clean, tight connections at the test points.

Thermal EMF Effects

Thermoelectric voltages generated at dissimilar metal junctions can affect low-resistance measurements. Using AC test current or allowing thermal equilibrium before measurement can mitigate this.

Inductive Effects from Transformers

The presence of isolating transformers in the loop can cause inductive reactance that affects resistance readings. Using DC test current with appropriate settling time or using specialized instruments with inductive compensation can provide accurate results.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Loop Resistance Reading Higher Than Expected
Likely Cause
A two-wire connection was used instead of the four-wire (Kelvin) method, or test lead contacts are loose or corroded, adding extra resistance to the reading.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the meter is connected using the four-wire Kelvin method; inspect test lead clamps and contact points for corrosion or looseness.
Corrective Action
Reconnect using the correct four-wire method with clean, tight contacts, and repeat the measurement.
Readings Drift or Are Inconsistent Between Repeated Tests
Likely Cause
Thermoelectric voltages generated at junctions of dissimilar metals in the loop are affecting the low-resistance measurement.
Diagnostic Steps
Check whether the meter is using DC test current without thermal EMF compensation; allow the circuit to reach thermal equilibrium before retesting.
Corrective Action
Switch to AC test current if available, or allow adequate settling time before measurement, and repeat the test for a stable reading.
Reading Appears Abnormally High on a Circuit with Isolating Transformers
Likely Cause
Inductive reactance from the isolating transformers in the loop is being picked up by the meter, skewing the resistance result.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the test current type in use (DC vs AC) and check the meter's specification for inductive compensation on transformer-fed loops.
Corrective Action
Use a DC test current with appropriate settling time, or use an instrument with inductive compensation designed for transformer-isolated series circuits.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Calibration Drift
High Severity
Root Cause
Gradual ageing of the meter's internal reference or measurement circuitry shifting readings away from a traceable calibration standard over repeated use.
Effect
A resistance reading that no longer reflects the true state of the circuit under test, risking a missed developing fault or a false alarm on a splice, terminal block, or transformer connection that's actually sound.
Detection Method
Periodic calibration against a certified reference resistance per the manufacturer's schedule, with calibration certificates checked before commissioning or troubleshooting work.
Test Lead or Probe Contact Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wear, corrosion, or damage to the test leads or probe tips that carry the meter's high test current (typically 1A to 10A) to the circuit under test.
Effect
Added contact resistance at the meter's own leads gets folded into the reading, masking or exaggerating the true resistance of the splice or connection actually being tested.
Detection Method
Test lead resistance check (leads shorted together) before each measurement session, as a standard zeroing step.
Battery / Test Current Source Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing battery or degraded internal current source reducing the meter's ability to sustain its rated high test current.
Effect
Reduced test current fails to overcome contact resistance effects the way the instrument is designed to, undermining the milliohm-range accuracy the higher current exists to provide.
Detection Method
Battery condition check and test current verification against a known load before critical commissioning or trending measurements.
Display or Data-Logging Fault
Low Severity
Root Cause
Internal electronics fault affecting the readout display or, on units with data-logging, the storage of trended resistance readings over time.
Effect
A technician loses the ability to trend resistance changes over time, undermining the meter's core value of catching a developing fault before it becomes an outage.
Detection Method
Functional check of display and data-logging against a known test resistance during scheduled instrument maintenance.
Housing Seal Failure Allowing Moisture Ingress
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Degraded case seal from repeated field use in outdoor conditions around AGL series circuits.
Effect
Moisture reaching the internal measurement electronics, causing erratic readings or a complete instrument fault mid-survey.
Detection Method
Housing seal inspection during scheduled instrument maintenance, alongside the standard calibration check.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit De-Energisation Before Testing
Electrical Safety

Confirm the loop under test is de-energised and isolated from its supply before connecting the meter, to protect both the instrument and the operator.

Calibration Currency Verification
Operational

Check the meter's calibration due date before use and do not rely on results from an instrument that is out of calibration for pass/fail compliance decisions.

Weatherproofing of Field Connections
Operational

Protect test leads and probe connections from rain and standing water during field use, since moisture at the test point can produce a false low-resistance reading.

PPE / Insulated Test Leads
Personnel Safety

Use insulated test leads and appropriate PPE when connecting to field circuits, and treat every loop as potentially energised until isolation has been confirmed.

Battery Safety During Field Use
Operational

Check battery condition before extended field testing and carry spares; a low battery can produce an inaccurate reading before the instrument indicates a fault.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Always Use Four-Wire Kelvin Connections, Never Two-Wire
Methodology

Two-wire measurement includes the test lead and contact resistance in the result, which can dwarf the actual milliohm-range loop resistance being measured. Confirm four-wire Kelvin connections are used and that all four contact points are clean and tight before trusting a reading.

Let the Circuit Reach Thermal Equilibrium Before Testing
Methodology

Thermoelectric voltages from dissimilar metal junctions in the loop can skew low-resistance readings if testing happens immediately after the circuit has been under load or exposed to rapid temperature change; allow settling time or use an instrument with thermal EMF compensation.

Isolating Transformers in the Loop Can Distort DC Readings if Not Accounted For
Instrumentation

The inductive reactance from transformers in a series loop can introduce measurement error with a standard DC test unless the instrument allows sufficient settling time or has inductive compensation; confirm the meter and method are appropriate for loops containing isolating transformers before trusting the result.

A Slowly Rising Baseline Is More Actionable Than a Single High Reading
Trending

Loop resistance typically climbs gradually as connections corrode or loosen, well before it causes an obvious fault; trend measurements over successive maintenance visits to catch degradation early rather than waiting for a reading that clearly exceeds a threshold.

Use Sectional Measurements to Localise a High-Resistance Connection
Fault Isolation

A single end-to-end loop reading tells you something is wrong but not where; taking resistance measurements at intermediate splice points or terminal blocks narrows down the specific connection responsible far faster than working from the whole-loop number alone.

Record Test Current and Ambient Temperature With Every Measurement
Documentation

A milliohm reading without its test current and ambient temperature noted is hard to compare reliably against a future measurement taken under different conditions; log the full test parameters as standard practice, not just the resistance value.
