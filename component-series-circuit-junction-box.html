Series‑Circuit Junction Box

A robust, waterproof enclosure used to house cable splices, terminations, and test points in the AGL primary and secondary series circuits. Designed for buried, handhole, or surface‑mount installation, it provides mechanical protection, environmental sealing, and safe access for maintenance and testing of the constant‑current series loop.

Overview

What Is It?

The Series‑Circuit Junction Box is a robust, weatherproof enclosure used in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems to house cable splices, terminations, test points, and transitions in the primary and secondary series circuits. It provides a protected environment for electrical connections, ensuring long‑term reliability and providing safe access for maintenance, testing, and fault diagnosis.

Key characteristics of series‑circuit junction boxes include:

  • Waterproof Construction: IP68‑rated enclosures that protect internal connections from moisture, dirt, and corrosion in buried or handhole installations.
  • Durable Materials: Manufactured from high‑strength materials such as glass‑reinforced polyester, polycarbonate, brass, or stainless steel to withstand mechanical loads, UV exposure, and chemical attack.
  • Multiple Cable Entries: Equipped with cable glands, knockouts, or compression seals to accommodate primary (L‑824) and secondary cables of various sizes.
  • Internal Mounting: Includes mounting rails, terminal blocks, earth bars, and cable tie points for organising and securing internal connections.
  • Accessibility: Designed with removable covers or hinged lids for easy access during installation, inspection, and maintenance.

Junction boxes are installed at strategic points along the series circuit, including at cable splices, circuit transitions, test points, and equipment interfaces. They are essential for maintaining the integrity of the AGL system and enabling efficient maintenance and fault diagnosis.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The junction box's purpose is to turn what would otherwise be a bare, buried cable splice into an accessible, protected node that can be tested, inspected, and repaired without excavating the surrounding trench. Because a series circuit's reliability depends on every splice and termination along its length staying watertight and mechanically sound, concentrating those connections inside a sealed, organised enclosure rather than leaving them wrapped in the open ground is what allows a fault to be isolated to a specific section of cable during troubleshooting instead of requiring the entire loop to be dug up to find it. Positioning boxes at cable splices, transitions, and test points also means routine circuit testing can happen at known, accessible locations, rather than needing to locate a splice point from as-built drawings before any diagnostic work can begin.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Series‑circuit junction boxes are used throughout the airfield at key points in the AGL system, including:

  • Cable Splice Locations: Housing splices where primary or secondary cables are joined to extend the circuit length or repair damage.
  • Test Points: Providing accessible test terminals for measuring current, voltage, and insulation resistance at regular intervals along the series loop.
  • Circuit Transition Points: At the interface between underground and elevated cable sections, providing a protected environment for cable transitions.
  • Transformer Connections: Some junction boxes are used to house the primary and secondary connections to isolating transformers, especially in underground transformer installations.
  • Tap‑Off and Branch Points: Where a branch circuit is taken from the main series loop (e.g., to feed a separate lighting segment).
  • CCR Output Interface: At the output of the Constant Current Regulator, where primary cables are connected to the field distribution system.
  • Equipment Interfaces: At the connection points between the AGL system and other equipment such as control panels, monitoring systems, and alarm panels.

Junction boxes are available in various sizes and configurations to suit the specific application, from small boxes for single splices to large enclosures with multiple cable entries and internal mounting provisions for complex circuits.

Placement

Installation & Location

These junction boxes are installed at whichever point in the series loop needs a splice, test point, or transition — buried in a handhole or pit at cable joins and transformer connections, set into the ground at regular intervals along the loop to provide test access, or mounted where an underground run transitions to an elevated structure or building entry. Box size and configuration are chosen to match the application, from a small single-splice enclosure buried flush with grade to a larger multi-entry box at a CCR output or equipment interface. Placement is tied into the same cable route and survey used for the rest of the circuit, keeping the box accessible for testing and maintenance without needing to trace buried cable to locate it.

Function

How Does It Work?

A series‑circuit junction box provides a protected environment for electrical connections, ensuring reliability and enabling maintenance. Its design incorporates several functional elements:

  • Enclosure: The rigid housing protects internal connections from physical damage, moisture, and contaminants. The IP68 rating ensures that the enclosure remains watertight even when submerged in water or buried in wet soil.
  • Cable Entries: Cable glands or compression seals provide watertight entry for cables, preventing moisture from tracking along the cable jacket into the enclosure. Glands are sized to match the cable diameter.
  • Internal Mounting: Mounting rails or plates allow the installation of terminal blocks, earth bars, and cable ties to organise and secure internal connections. This prevents strain on the connections and facilitates maintenance.
  • Earthing/Bonding: An internal earth bar provides a common grounding point for cable screens, equipment earthing, and the enclosure itself, ensuring electrical safety and EMI protection.
  • Test Access: Many junction boxes include test points or probe ports that allow connection of test instruments without opening cable splices or disturbing terminations.
  • Identification: Clear marking of the enclosure, cable entries, and internal connections aids maintenance and reduces the risk of errors during work.

When properly installed and sealed, the junction box provides a reliable, long‑life housing for AGL circuit connections, withstanding the harsh airfield environment including temperature extremes, moisture, chemical exposure, and mechanical loads.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A series-circuit junction box has no control logic of its own — like a connector or a transformer housing, its role in the system's architecture is structural rather than active. It sits inside the same series circuit the CCR regulates and monitors, providing a protected point where that circuit can be spliced, branched, transitioned between underground and elevated runs, or accessed for testing, without ever itself issuing a command or making a decision about the circuit's state.

What the box does contribute to the system's control architecture is accessibility: because test points are placed at known, documented locations rather than left as bare splices, a technician can verify current, voltage, or insulation resistance at that exact point in the loop — closing a manual diagnostic loop around a specific circuit section — while the CCR's own automatic fault detection continues to watch the whole loop for the same voltage-versus-current deviation it would flag anywhere else.

Construction

Physical Construction

The enclosure is typically a glass-reinforced polymer, cast resin, or corrosion-treated metal box, sized to give working clearance around the splices it houses and rated to at least IP67 given its frequent below-grade or in-pit installation. Cable entries are formed as sealed glands or a gel-filled entry plate on each face, sized for the primary and secondary conductor diameters and positioned so cables enter without sharp bends that could stress the termination. Splices and terminations inside are made with insulated, moisture-resistant connectors — often resin-encapsulated or heat-shrink sealed for the primary series-circuit joint specifically — mounted on a terminal rail or potted directly into the box body for extra security against vibration and settlement. The lid seats on a full-perimeter gasket and is secured with corrosion-resistant fasteners or a locking mechanism, and where the box also serves as a test point, dedicated test terminals are brought to an accessible face so current and insulation-resistance testing can be carried out without disturbing the sealed splices themselves.

Civil

Excavate and set the box to grade for a buried or in-pit installation, or fix the mounting bracket for a structure-transition box, sizing the position for the splice count and cable diameters it will carry.

Position Tied to Cable Survey
Civil

Site the box on the same cable route and survey used for the rest of the circuit, keeping it accessible for testing and maintenance without needing to trace buried cable to locate it.

Cable Entry & Bend Radius
Electrical

Route primary and secondary cables through the sealed glands or gel-filled entry plate so each enters without sharp bends that could stress the termination.

Splice & Termination
Electrical

Test Terminal Provision

Lid Gasket Seal

Pre-Closure Sign-off

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Annual

Check the lid gasket and cable seals for cracking or compression set, and re-fasten quick-release fittings to spec to keep the box watertight to its IP68 rating.

Terminal Block & Earth Bar Check
Annual

Re-torque terminal block and earth bar connections to spec, since a loose splice inside the box is a common source of series-circuit faults.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test connections inside the box to confirm insulation resistance stays above the minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a loop fault.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Water Ingress Through Cable Glands

If cable glands are not properly tightened or are the wrong size for the cable, moisture can enter the enclosure. Regularly inspect and tighten glands, and ensure they are correctly sized for the cable diameter.

Corrosion of Enclosure and Components

In aggressive environments (coastal, industrial), the enclosure and internal components can corrode. Use corrosion‑resistant materials (stainless steel, brass, or GRP) and apply protective coatings where necessary.

Damaged or Missing Gaskets

If the lid gasket is damaged or missing, the enclosure will not maintain its IP rating. Inspect gaskets regularly and replace if cracked, compressed, or degraded.

Inadequate Cable Strain Relief

If cables are not properly secured inside the enclosure, tension on the cables can pull on internal terminations, causing high‑resistance connections. Use cable ties and strain relief brackets to secure cables.

Insufficient Internal Space

Overcrowded enclosures make maintenance difficult and increase the risk of damage to cables and connections. Always select an enclosure size that provides adequate space for the number of cables and terminations.

Loose or High‑Resistance Connections

Terminal connections inside the junction box can loosen due to vibration or thermal cycling. Periodically check and tighten all connections as part of routine maintenance.

Inaccessible Location

Junction boxes that are buried too deep, covered by asphalt, or obstructed by equipment can be difficult to access for maintenance. Ensure boxes are installed at accessible depths and that their locations are clearly marked.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Corrosion or Moisture Found Inside the Box
Likely Cause
A cable gland was not properly tightened or sized for the cable, allowing water to track into the enclosure.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect all glands for tightness and correct sizing against the cable diameter; check for condensation or standing water inside.
Corrective Action
Re-tighten or replace undersized/damaged glands, dry out the enclosure, and megger the terminations before closing up.
Enclosure Fails to Hold Its IP Rating
Likely Cause
The lid gasket has cracked, compressed, or degraded, breaking the enclosure's environmental seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the gasket for visible damage or compression set; perform a hose or pressure test if available.
Corrective Action
Replace the gasket and re-torque the lid fasteners evenly to restore the seal.
Intermittent Fault or High-Resistance Reading at a Termination
Likely Cause
Vibration or thermal cycling has loosened a terminal connection inside the box, or cable tension without proper strain relief has pulled on a termination.
Diagnostic Steps
Check all terminal connections for tightness; inspect strain relief and cable ties for adequacy.
Corrective Action
Re-tighten loose terminals to spec, and fit or improve cable ties/strain relief brackets to remove tension from the terminations.
Corrosion of Enclosure Body or Fittings
Likely Cause
Aggressive soil or atmospheric conditions (coastal, industrial) are attacking non-resistant enclosure materials.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the enclosure body, hinges, and fasteners for pitting or corrosion product.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded fittings with corrosion-resistant equivalents (stainless steel, brass, or GRP), and apply protective coating where the enclosure itself is affected.
Box Difficult to Locate or Access for Maintenance
Likely Cause
The box was installed too deep, or has become covered by asphalt or obstructing equipment since installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check installed depth and surface marking against as-built records; confirm accessibility at the recorded location.
Corrective Action
Expose and re-mark the box location, and raise or relocate it if buried too deep to be reasonably accessed.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Gasket Seal Degradation / Water Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set over years of thermal cycling, or a lid that no longer closes flush after repeated access for maintenance splicing.
Effect
Corrosion or moisture found inside the box, degrading insulation resistance at the splice and risking a ground fault on the series circuit.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at each opening, and insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit as part of scheduled maintenance.
IP Rating Failure at Cable Entries
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or improperly torqued cable glands, or a conduit fitting that was never properly sealed at installation.
Effect
Enclosure fails to hold its rated IP ingress protection, allowing water and debris to reach the internal splices.
Detection Method
Ingress test or visual check of glands/fittings during maintenance, and inspection for standing water inside the box after rain.
High-Resistance Termination
High Severity
Root Cause
A splice connector that was under-crimped or under-torqued at installation, or corrosion building up on the conductor at the termination point over time.
Effect
Intermittent fault or a measurable voltage/resistance rise at the termination, which can progress to a localised hot spot.
Detection Method
Loop resistance testing across the circuit, and thermal imaging of terminations under load to catch a developing hot spot early.
Enclosure Body / Fitting Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Galvanic corrosion between dissimilar metals, or prolonged exposure to moisture and de-icing chemicals in the surrounding pavement or turf.
Effect
Weakening of the enclosure body or fittings, eventually compromising the seal or structural support for the lid and cable entries.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the enclosure body and fittings for corrosion during scheduled maintenance.
Buried or Obstructed Box
Low Severity
Root Cause
Turf growth, pavement resurfacing, or drifted fill gradually covering the box location without an accurate as-built record.
Effect
Box becomes difficult to locate or access for maintenance, extending outage time when a fault needs to be traced.
Detection Method
GPS/as-built survey verification against the field location, and periodic clearing of vegetation or fill around the marked position.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The box houses splices and test points carrying constant-current series loop power that can reach several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the box before opening it or handling connections inside.

Confirm the Correct Box Before Isolating
Operational

On circuits with several junction boxes in series, positively identify the correct box before isolating work, since opening the wrong one can leave live conductors exposed while the intended section stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice, termination, or test-point work, megger test the affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the box and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting.

Maintain IP68 Integrity
Electrical Safety

Renew gaskets and properly seat cable glands and the cover on reassembly to preserve the enclosure's IP68 rating, particularly on buried or handhole installations where a compromised seal is hard to detect until a fault develops.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the box is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Buried or Handhole Access
Operational

Follow the aerodrome's permit-to-dig and pit-access procedures where the box is buried or set in a handhole, and check for water or debris accumulation before opening it.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a junction box with a loose connection, an unsealed gland, or a mis-seated cover after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and secure closure before closing out the work order.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Identical-Looking Boxes Along a Loop Are a Mis-Isolation Risk
Operational

On a long series circuit with multiple similar junction boxes, relying on memory or a rough count to find "the right one" invites opening a live box while the intended section stays dark; mark box identifiers clearly and cross-check against as-built records before isolating.

IP68 Failure Is Progressive, Not Binary
Durability

A gasket or gland that's slightly degraded doesn't fail all at once; it allows gradual moisture ingress that can take months to manifest as an insulation fault, so a box that's been open longer than a quick visit deserves a fresh gasket rather than reuse of the old one on principle.

Trend Insulation Readings at Each Box Independently
Troubleshooting

A long series loop with several junction boxes can have one box slowly developing a fault while the loop as a whole still tests within tolerance at the CCR; testing at each box individually, and trending those readings over time, localises a developing problem long before it takes the loop down.

Handhole Boxes Need a Standing-Water Check Before Opening
Environmental

Opening a handhole box that has accumulated standing water without first checking and pumping it down risks water entering the box the moment the lid comes off; always check and clear standing water in the pit before breaking the seal, not just before working inside.

Splice Point Density Along a Loop Affects Fault-Finding Time
Design

A loop with many closely spaced junction boxes is faster to isolate a fault within but has more total seal points at risk; when adding a new tap or splice, weigh this trade-off rather than defaulting to the nearest convenient box.

Record Box-Specific Insulation History, Not Just Circuit-Wide Results
Compliance

A circuit-wide megger reading taken at the CCR end can mask which specific box is trending toward failure; keep insulation resistance records by individual box location so a developing fault can be traced to a specific point rather than the whole loop.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about series-circuit junction boxes: purpose, construction, ratings, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a series-circuit junction box?

It is a robust, waterproof enclosure that houses cable splices, terminations, test points and transitions in AGL primary and secondary series circuits. It can be buried, installed in a handhole or surface-mounted, and gives protected, accessible connections for maintenance, testing and fault diagnosis.

Q2What is the purpose of a series-circuit junction box?

It turns a bare buried splice into a protected node that can be tested, inspected and repaired without excavating the trench. Because a series circuit depends on every splice staying watertight and sound, concentrating connections in a sealed enclosure lets a fault be isolated to a specific cable section.

Q3Where are series-circuit junction boxes used?

At cable splices, test points along the loop, transitions between underground and elevated cable, underground transformer connections, tap-off and branch points, the CCR output interface and equipment interfaces with control, monitoring and alarm panels.

Q4What are junction boxes made of, and how are they sealed?

Common materials are glass-reinforced polyester (GRP), polycarbonate, brass or stainless steel. The enclosure is IP68 (submersible, continuous), with compression glands, knockouts or cable seals at the entries and a removable or hinged lid with a gasket.

Q5What is inside a series-circuit junction box?

Mounting rails or plates, terminal blocks, an earth bar bonded to the airfield earth grid, and cable tie points for securing connections. Many boxes also have test points or probe ports so instruments can connect without opening splices, and clear labelling of circuits and cable entries.

Q6What are typical junction box specifications?

Insulation is rated 5 kV for primary and 600 V for secondary, with a series circuit current rating of 6.6 A or 20 A. Cable compatibility is primary 6 AWG to 2 AWG and secondary 1.5 mm² to 6 mm². Typical sizes range from 150×150×100 mm to 600×400×200 mm.

Q7Does a junction box have its own protection?

No. Isolation for maintenance is through the associated CCR, and the box itself has no independent protective device. Insulation resistance measured cable-to-ground at the terminal blocks should be at least 50 MΩ.

Q8Which standards apply to series-circuit junction boxes?

The main references are IEC 60529 (IP code), IEEE 404 (shielded power-cable joints, relevant where a splice is rated above the series-circuit range), FAA AC 150/5345-7 and -26 (L-824 cable and L-823 connectors) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition.

Q9How often should a junction box be inspected?

Annually: seal and gasket inspection, terminal block and earth bar torque, insulation resistance test, gland checks, corrosion checks, pit surround condition and label legibility. Semi-annually, open the box to check for standing water. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common junction box problems?

Water ingress through loose or wrongly sized glands, corrosion in aggressive environments, damaged or missing gaskets, inadequate cable strain relief, overcrowded enclosures, loose or high-resistance connections from vibration or thermal cycling, and boxes buried too deep or covered by asphalt.

Q11What safety steps apply when working in a junction box?

The box can carry constant-current series loop power reaching several kV, so de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy. On circuits with several boxes in series, positively identify the correct one first, follow permit-to-dig and pit-access procedures, and megger test the circuit before re-energising. Renew gaskets and seat glands and the cover properly to preserve IP68.
