Overview

What Is Redundancy in AGL Electrical Architecture?

Redundancy in Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) means designing the electrical and control system so that a single equipment or circuit failure does not unnecessarily remove the required airport visual guidance. It matters most for precision approach runways, CAT II/III operations, low-visibility procedures, runway centreline, edge and touchdown-zone lighting, stop bars, critical approach lighting and essential taxiway lighting.

A redundant AGL system is not simply a system with “two of everything.” The engineering objective is to maintain the required lighting service after a defined single failure, with the required continuity and without creating misleading or conflicting indications.

Layers

The Layers of AGL Redundancy

Source redundancy

More than one power source.

Distribution redundancy

More than one independent feeder or bus.

CCR redundancy

Multiple regulators serving independent or appropriately distributed circuits.

Circuit redundancy

Two or more AGL circuits serve a lighting system.

Spatial redundancy

Lights are distributed between circuits instead of grouping adjacent lights on one circuit.

Equipment redundancy

Critical control or monitoring equipment has standby units.

Control redundancy

Failure of a processor or network component does not necessarily mean total loss of control.

Monitoring redundancy

Faults stay detectable even when part of the monitoring architecture fails.

A five-layer way to explain it

1. Power: dual utility / UPS / DG
2. CCR: multiple regulators
3. Field circuit: dual / interleaved
4. Equipment: individual transformers and lights
5. Control: ALCMS, network, field control
Each layer addresses a different failure mechanism. Strength at one layer does not compensate for a single point of failure at another.

Principles

Electrical vs Operational Redundancy, Single Points of Failure and Common Cause

Electrical redundancy

An alternate electrical path exists.

Operational redundancy

The airport can continue the required operation after the defined failure. Two CCRs do not guarantee this: the alternate CCR may be connected to the wrong part of the circuit, or a common cable section may still be a single point of failure.

The right design question is: what lighting function remains available after the defined failure?

Potential AGL single points of failure

One incoming feederOne main switchboardOne transformerOne CCROne common circuit sectionOne field controllerOne communication switchOne ALCMS serverOne UPSOne generatorOne common cable route

Common-cause failures that defeat apparent redundancy

ExampleWhy it defeats redundancy
One flood affects both circuitsBoth circuits share the same exposure
One chamber fire damages both cablesPhysical proximity
One switchboard feeds both CCRsShared electrical source
One network switch controls both systemsShared control path
One software server controls everythingShared logic
One grounding failure affects both circuitsShared reference and protection path
Redundancy needs independence analysis, not just duplication: physical, electrical and logical independence.

Redundant vs fail-safe

Redundant means a backup exists. Fail-safe means that when something fails, the system moves to or stays in a state that minimizes safety risk. Two controllers sending conflicting commands are redundant but not necessarily safe.

Redundancy vs resilience

Redundancy is an alternate path or capacity. Resilience is the airport’s wider ability to withstand and recover from disruption: redundancy plus protection, monitoring, maintenance, procedures, spares and recovery.

Independent vs dependent interleaved circuits

Independent interleaved

Each circuit has an electrical path sufficiently separated from the other, as set by the design. Stronger redundancy.

Dependent interleaved

Lights are interleaved physically but the circuits share some infrastructure or dependency. The consequence of losing one circuit differs.

AAI’s eAIP entries show this difference at specific airports: some runways are described with independent interleaved circuits and others with dependent ones, with different consequences when one circuit fails. These are airport-specific examples. Check the current eAIP and AIP supplements for any airport you rely on.

Redundancy is not the same as switch-over time

Redundancy

Can the system continue after a component failure?

Switch-over time

How quickly is service restored after the source fails? Two generators can still have an unacceptable transfer time, while an online UPS gives very fast continuity with a single generator behind it.

Utility fails
UPS: immediate support
DG starts and takes over
Sustained supply
ICAO calls for automatic connection of required secondary-powered facilities after failure of the normal source, with category-dependent maximum switch-over times for visual aids (Annex 14, secondary power provisions). Study the restoration of actual AGL, not just generator start.

Generator sizing

Do not size from average AGL load. Use the critical-load schedule: which CCRs run together, starting currents, UPS charging, other essential loads, generator failure scenarios, expansion and load shedding.

UPS sizing

Reflect actual AGL load, control load, ALCMS, A-SMGCS interfaces, autonomy, battery ageing, growth and the chosen redundancy philosophy, assessed as a complete system.

Airport Lighting Power Calculation

An airport lighting power calculation starts from the critical-load schedule, not the average AGL load. Add up the loads that run together: the CCRs for each lighting circuit, control and ALCMS load, A-SMGCS interfaces and other essential loads. Then allow for CCR starting currents, UPS charging, expansion and load shedding. The same schedule feeds generator sizing, UPS sizing and the N+1 or 2N decision, so the result should be checked against each failure scenario rather than the normal case alone. For CCR and circuit-level loading, see the AGL Toolkit

Spare capacity is not redundancy. A 500 kVA generator carrying 300 kVA has spare capacity; a second generator able to carry the critical load provides redundancy.

Field Equipment

CCRs, Cable Routes, Transformers and Surge Protection

CCR redundancy

Two CCRs on two circuits give some redundancy, but two CCRs feeding one common cable still leave a single point of failure. Link the CCR study to circuit and cable topology, and prefer an architecture where a CCR failure affects one circuit, not a whole visual-aid system.

CCR failure modes

Power-electronics fault, controller failure, input breaker failure, output fault, cooling failure, communication failure and internal protection trip.

Physical separation

Two circuits in one duct remain exposed to excavation damage, flooding and fire. Plan route separation where feasible and review crossings, duct capacity, drainage, chambers, access and construction exposure.

Transformers and connectors

Individual isolation transformers mean a transformer failure normally removes one light, a natural form of distributed redundancy. Avoid one connector or chamber affecting several lights, and keep moisture ingress from propagating across circuits.

Redundant circuits must survive the same environment. Two circuits routed together and hit by one lightning or surge event may fail together, so coordinate redundancy with lightning protection, counterpoise, bonding, surge protection and cable routing.

Control

ALCMS, Network, Field Controllers, A-SMGCS and Follow the Greens

ALCMS and network

A robust design may use a primary and standby server, two networks and two field controllers where the operational requirement justifies it. Separate lighting power from the control application so a server failure has a defined response. Field equipment may hold its last state, move to a default state or allow local or manual control. Test whichever is specified.

Field controllers

Controllers may drive CCRs, taxiway segments, stop bars, signs and Follow the Greens functions. A controller failure should give a defined safe response and an alarm.

For a safety-related lighting system the question is: what should the pilot see when the system is degraded? The answer should be unambiguous. Redundancy needs interlocking, monitoring and fail-safe logic with it.

Operations

CAT II/III, Serviceability and Maintenance

Low-visibility operations

Redundancy becomes an active operational condition. LVP readiness can depend on circuit redundancy, power redundancy, equipment serviceability and control availability together. AAI’s airport procedures link specific circuit and light failures to restrictions on CAT II/III operations; those limits are airport-specific.

Redundancy vs serviceability

Redundancy gives resilience. Serviceability criteria define how much degradation is acceptable. A failed Circuit A does not mean everything is acceptable indefinitely: Circuit B still has to meet the applicable limits.

Maintenance configurations to define

StateMeaningDefine
NormalBoth systems availableBaseline alarm status
MaintenanceOne system intentionally unavailableRestrictions, restoration procedure, communication
DegradedOne system failed unexpectedlyAlarm, operational restrictions, recovery
EmergencyMultiple failuresProcedures and escalation
A system already running on one circuit should not be exposed to an additional failure without considering operational restrictions. Redundancy also lets planned maintenance proceed without disabling the whole visual aid, subject to the approved procedure.

Keyword Reference

AGL Power Supply System & Airfield Lighting Circuit Protection

How the lighting is fed and how the circuits are protected.

AGL power supply system

An AGL power supply system takes power from the airport's electrical supply to the CCRs, which feed the lighting series circuits. Redundancy is added through independent feeders, backup power such as UPS or a generator, and alternate circuits, according to the category of operation. The supply design should be checked against the standard the aerodrome follows.

Airfield lighting circuit protection

Airfield lighting circuit protection guards people and equipment against faults. It includes CCR protection such as open-circuit and overcurrent protection, earth fault monitoring, and upstream breakers and fuses. Settings come from the manufacturer's data and the project design, so none are given here. Good protection also helps locate faults quickly.

Keyword Reference

AGL Circuit Fault Troubleshooting: Open Circuit & Short Circuit Faults

Finding and understanding the two most common series-circuit fault types.

AGL circuit fault troubleshooting

AGL circuit fault troubleshooting starts with the symptom and the CCR status: tripped, low current, or high current. Make the circuit safe, then split it into sections and test each with insulation and loop resistance checks until the faulty section is found. Repair, re-test and record the cause. See the testing and failure simulation sections of this guide.

AGL open circuit fault

An AGL open circuit fault is a break in the series loop, such as a damaged cable, a failed joint or a poorly mated connector. Current stops flowing, and the CCR normally trips on its open-circuit protection. Never work on a series circuit that is open but energised. Locate the break by sectionalising the loop and checking continuity.

AGL short circuit fault

An AGL short circuit fault is an unwanted path in the circuit, often a short to earth caused by water or insulation damage. It can cause overcurrent, earth-fault alarms or a CCR trip. Locate the fault by sectionalising and insulation testing, then repair or replace the damaged cable, joint or transformer.

Pitfalls

Common Mistakes in AGL Redundancy Design

Two circuits, one common failure point

Two circuits in the same vulnerable cable route may fail together.

Two CCRs on one common supply

CCR duplication is ineffective if the common supply remains a single point of failure.

UPS without generator support

The UPS gives fast continuity but may not last a prolonged outage.

Generator without fast transfer

Long-duration power that fails the required visual-aid switch-over time.

Redundant power, single control

The lighting is powered but cannot be operated correctly because control failed.

Redundant control, single field circuit

Control survives, but the physical lighting still goes dark.

No failure testing

A system that looks redundant on paper can fail in a real outage.

No common-cause analysis

Duplicate components can still depend on one cable, room, network or control power source.

Final Engineering Perspective

Redundancy is not duplication for its own sake. It is a structured way to make sure one failure does not compromise the airport’s required visual guidance.

Design the system around the failure you need to survive, not around the number of components you want to duplicate.

FAQ

AGL Redundancy FAQ

Q1What is redundancy in AGL?

AGL redundancy is the use of alternate power, circuit, equipment or control paths so that a defined single failure does not unnecessarily remove the required airport lighting function.

Q2Why are two AGL circuits used?

Two circuits can distribute runway lights so that failure of one circuit does not remove the entire lighting system. Interleaving can preserve spatial continuity.

Q3What is an interleaved AGL circuit?

A circuit arrangement in which adjacent lights are distributed between separate circuits rather than placing all consecutive lights on one circuit.

Q4Are two interleaved circuits automatically independent?

No. The circuits may still share common feeders, cable routes, switchgear, chambers or control systems.

Q5What is the difference between independent and dependent circuits?

Independent circuits have sufficiently separate electrical infrastructure to reduce common-cause failure. Dependent circuits can share infrastructure or dependencies.

Q6What does N+1 mean?

N+1 means providing the capacity required for normal operation plus one additional unit, so that one unit can fail without removing the required capacity.

Q7What is 2N redundancy?

2N means two independent complete systems, each capable of supporting the defined critical load.

Q8Does redundancy mean all runway lights stay on after one circuit fails?

Not necessarily. The remaining serviceability is defined by the applicable operational and regulatory criteria, which differ by lighting system, runway category and airport.

Q9Why are runway lights interleaved?

Interleaving spreads lights from separate circuits across the runway so that one circuit failure does not create a large continuous dark section.

Q10Does redundancy apply to CCRs?

Yes. CCR allocation can be an important part of circuit redundancy, provided the alternate path is sufficiently independent.

Q11Does a UPS provide redundancy?

A UPS provides power continuity. It is not automatically redundant. Redundant UPS architecture may use N+1, parallel or dual-independent arrangements.

Q12Why is a diesel generator needed when there is a UPS?

The UPS can give rapid continuity, while a generator can supply the system through a much longer outage.

Q13Is dual utility power better than UPS and DG?

Not universally. Each architecture has different characteristics. The right solution depends on source independence, switch-over requirements, outage duration, reliability and project requirements.

Q14How is AGL redundancy tested?

Engineers simulate defined failures such as circuit trip, CCR failure, utility loss, UPS failure, generator failure and control or network failure, then verify the system response and remaining lighting performance.

Q15Is photometry required after redundancy testing?

Where optical performance forms part of acceptance or verification, photometry can show that the remaining lighting performs as required after a simulated failure.

Q16What is the difference between redundancy and switch-over time?

Redundancy is whether the system can continue after a component failure. Switch-over time is how quickly service is restored after the source fails. They are separate engineering parameters.

Q17How to measure AGL circuit current?

Measure the series circuit current at the CCR output, using the CCR's own display or a calibrated clamp meter or current instrument rated for the circuit. Never open a live series circuit to insert a meter. Take readings at each brightness step, compare them with the rated current for that step, and record them. Work should be done by qualified staff under your site's safety procedure.

Q18How to check an airport lighting circuit?

Start with the CCR status and any alarms on the monitoring system, then measure the circuit current at the brightness steps. With the circuit isolated, test insulation resistance and loop resistance, and inspect connectors, transformers and fixtures along the circuit. Compare results with the commissioning records and acceptance limits, and record the findings.

Q19How to restore an airfield lighting circuit?

Make the circuit safe and find the cause before re-energising, rather than resetting repeatedly into a fault. Isolate the faulty section, repair or replace the damaged cable, joint, connector or transformer, then test insulation and continuity. Re-energise at a low brightness step, check the current, and return the circuit to service in agreement with air traffic control. Where redundancy exists, switch to the alternate circuit first. Record the fault and the repair.

Engineering & Expert Notes

By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Redundancy Has to Be Proven, Not Assumed
Verification

Drawings can show duplicated paths that share a single point of failure in practice. Test failure scenarios to confirm that the airfield stays serviceable.

Look for Hidden Common Points
Single Points

Shared cable routes, ducts, control power or a common network switch can defeat apparent redundancy. Trace each path to its source.

Switch-Over Behaviour Matters as Much as the Backup Source
Power

A standby source that starts correctly may still disturb CCRs or controls during transfer. Test the transfer sequence under realistic load.

Control Redundancy Needs Its Own Check
Control

Dual servers or networks protect the operator view but only help if failover is tested and the local fallback is understood by staff.

Use a Failure Matrix to Make Reviews Concrete
FMEA

List each element, how it can fail and what happens to the lights. The table exposes unexpected consequences before an actual outage does.

Re-Test After Any Modification
Change Control

Extensions and upgrades can quietly remove a redundancy feature. Repeat the relevant failure tests after changes to the architecture.
