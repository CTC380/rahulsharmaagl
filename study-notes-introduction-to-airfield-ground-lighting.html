INTRODUCTION TO AIRFIELD GROUND LIGHTING (AGL)

Airfield Ground Lighting, commonly known as AGL, is the network of lighting and visual guidance systems used at an aerodrome to help pilots identify, approach, land on, taxi along and operate around the airfield. It becomes particularly important during darkness, restricted visibility and instrument operations.

It is not simply a collection of lights. A complete AGL installation combines:
Lighting Equipment + Electrical Circuits + Control Systems + Installation + Testing + Maintenance + Operational Requirements

Terminology: “AGL” is widely used in industry for Airfield Ground Lighting. ICAO documents use the term aeronautical ground light for any light provided specifically as an aid to air navigation, and the FAA groups these systems under airport lighting or visual aids. Expect to meet all three terms in standards, specifications and exams.

New to airfield lighting? Start with What Is Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL)?

Understanding AGL requires more than learning the name of each light. It requires understanding how the visual, electrical and operational elements work together as one system.

Why AGL

WHY IS AGL REQUIRED?

Aircraft operating on an aerodrome need reliable visual references for runway and taxiway identification, alignment, movement and safe navigation. Without appropriate visual guidance, identifying runway and taxiway geometry becomes more difficult, particularly during night operations and reduced-visibility conditions.

Depending on the airport and applicable system, AGL can help pilots identify:

  • Runway edges
  • Runway threshold
  • Runway end
  • Runway centreline
  • Touchdown zone
  • Taxiway routes
  • Holding positions
  • Runway entrances
  • Approach alignment
  • Vertical approach guidance

The exact arrangement depends on the runway, taxiway, approach procedure, operational requirements and applicable airport standards or specifications.

Main Areas

MAIN AREAS OF AIRFIELD GROUND LIGHTING

AGL can be studied by dividing the airfield into major functional areas.

1. Runway lighting

Visual guidance for take-off and landing.

  • Runway edge lights
  • Threshold lights
  • Runway end lights
  • Runway centreline lights
  • Touchdown Zone Lights (TDZL), on precision approach runways where provided
  • Runway End Identifier Lights (REIL), where provided
  • Rapid Exit Taxiway Indicator Lights (RETIL), where provided (installed on the exit taxiway, but they guide aircraft leaving the runway)

Each has a specific location, colour, configuration and operational purpose.

2. Taxiway lighting

Guides aircraft between runways, aprons, terminals, maintenance areas and other locations.

  • Taxiway edge lights
  • Taxiway centreline lights
  • Lead-on and lead-off lights
  • Stop bars
  • Runway guard lights
  • Runway entrance lights in applicable systems

Colours and configurations communicate movement guidance and runway-related information.

3. Approach area

Helps pilots establish visual reference with the runway environment during the transition toward landing.

  • Approach Lighting Systems (ALS)
  • Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI)
  • Abbreviated PAPI (APAPI)
  • Other visual guidance systems

Approach lighting and visual approach slope indicators serve different functions. The ALS extends outward from the runway threshold along the approach path, while PAPI/APAPI units are installed beside the runway, near the threshold, and show the pilot whether the aircraft is above, on or below the approach slope.

4. Airfield signs

Important information for pilots on the aerodrome.

  • Location
  • Direction
  • Mandatory instructions
  • Runway identification
  • Taxiway identification

Signs and lighting should be understood together as one visual guidance environment.

Systems Thinking

AGL AS A COMPLETE SYSTEM

A common mistake among beginners is to study every light or component as an isolated item. AGL should be understood as a complete interconnected system. A simplified electrical chain:

Power SupplyCCRPrimary Series CablePrimary ConnectorIsolating TransformerSecondary ConnectorLight Fixture

Each link in this chain has its own note: 07 CCR, 08 Isolating Transformers, 09 Primary & Secondary Circuits and 10 L-823 Connectors.

The primary cable forms a closed loop: current leaves the CCR, passes through the primary winding of each isolating transformer in turn, and returns to the CCR. The CCR is normally set to the required brightness step by a local or remote control system (for example an ALCMS). The complete system includes both electrical infrastructure and lighting equipment. If one part fails, the visible symptom may appear somewhere else in the circuit.

A light that is not operating does not automatically mean the fixture has failed. The problem could also be in the CCR, primary circuit, connector, isolating transformer, secondary connection or local cable. Note also the difference in symptom: a break or open connector in the primary series loop affects the whole circuit (the CCR will normally shut down on open circuit), whereas a fault on one secondary side usually affects only that fixture.

That is why AGL technicians and engineers need both Component Knowledge + System Knowledge. Understanding the complete circuit is also fundamental to systematic troubleshooting.

In a typical series-circuit arrangement, the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) controls the current supplied to the series circuit. The CCR holds the current constant (commonly up to 6.6 A at maximum brightness) and changes the brightness by changing that current. Each lighting unit is connected through an isolating transformer, which couples the primary series circuit to the individual fixture while electrically isolating the lamp circuit from the primary. It is also designed to tolerate an open-circuit secondary, so a failed lamp does not break the series loop and the remaining lights stay lit. This is one of the most important concepts for students beginning AGL.

Method

THINK LIKE AN AGL ENGINEER

When learning any AGL component, do not memorise only its name. Study four questions:

  1. What is it? Definition and basic construction or arrangement.
  2. Where is it used? Location and application on the airfield.
  3. What colour, intensity or configuration does it use? Visual indication and applicable configuration.
  4. Why is it required? Its operational purpose.

Example: Stop Bar

  • What? A row of unidirectional lights across the taxiway, usually with elevated lights at each side
  • Where? At a runway-holding position (and at some intermediate holding positions)
  • Colour? Red, shown towards aircraft approaching the holding position
  • Why? To instruct the pilot to stop and help prevent runway incursions. It is switched off, on ATC clearance, to allow the aircraft to proceed.

This connects definition + location + characteristics + function instead of relying on memorisation alone.

Key points for exams
  • AGL = Airfield Ground Lighting
  • AGL provides visual guidance for aerodrome operations
  • AGL includes runway, taxiway, approach and other visual guidance applications
  • AGL is both a lighting system and an electrical infrastructure system
  • AGL should be understood as a complete system, not only as individual fixtures
  • A primary-loop fault affects the whole circuit; a secondary-side fault usually affects one fixture
  • Troubleshooting requires understanding the relationship between the CCR, series circuit, cables, connectors, transformers and light fixtures

Exam Questions

EXAM QUESTIONS

What is AGL?

Airfield Ground Lighting is the system of aerodrome lighting and associated visual guidance facilities used to support aircraft operations.

Why is AGL important?

AGL provides visual guidance for aircraft movement and operations, particularly during night and reduced-visibility conditions.

Name the major AGL areas.

Runway, taxiway, approach area and airfield signs, together with associated visual guidance systems.

What is the basic AGL electrical chain?

Power Supply → CCR → Primary Series Circuit (cable and connectors) → Isolating Transformer → Secondary Connection → Light Fixture.

Is AGL only a lighting system?

No. AGL should be understood as a complete system combining lighting equipment, electrical circuits, control systems, installation, testing, maintenance and operational requirements.

Why should AGL be studied as a complete system?

Because a fault in one part of the system can produce a visible symptom elsewhere. Effective troubleshooting therefore requires both component knowledge and system knowledge.

What does an isolating transformer do in an AGL series circuit?

It couples each fixture to the primary series circuit while electrically isolating the lamp circuit from the primary. It also lets primary current keep flowing if a lamp fails open, so the remaining lights stay lit.

What is a stop bar and what colour is it?

A stop bar is a row of red lights across the taxiway at a runway-holding position. When lit it instructs aircraft to stop; it is switched off on ATC clearance to allow the aircraft to proceed, helping to prevent runway incursions.

STUDY TAKEAWAY
