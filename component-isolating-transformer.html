Isolating Transformer

A critical electrical component in AGL series circuits that provides galvanic isolation between the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) and the individual light fixtures. It steps down the high-voltage series current to a safe low-voltage supply for each lamp, while maintaining the constant current characteristic.

Overview

What Is It?

An Isolating Transformer (also known as a series transformer or lighting transformer) is a single-phase transformer used in Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) systems to couple individual light fixtures to the constant current series circuit. Its primary winding is connected in series with the CCR output, carrying the full loop current (typically 6.6A or 20A). The secondary winding supplies power to a single lamp or LED module at a lower voltage (typically 6V, 12V, or 24V), matching the lamp's voltage requirement. This arrangement ensures that each light fixture receives the correct current regardless of the number of fixtures in the loop, while providing electrical isolation for safety and fault tolerance.

The transformer is designed to operate continuously at its rated current and is typically buried in a concrete pit or directly in the ground near the fixture. It is a passive, maintenance-free component with high reliability, but its failure can cause a complete open circuit in the series loop, affecting all downstream lights. Therefore, proper selection, installation, and periodic testing are essential.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The isolating transformer exists to solve a problem specific to constant-current series lighting: every fixture on a loop shares the same current, so there has to be a way to couple hundreds of individual lamps to that shared circuit without letting a single fault at one fixture take down the whole loop, or exposing maintenance staff to the loop's series voltage while working on an individual light. Galvanic isolation is what makes this possible — each fixture sits electrically separate from its neighbours even though they're all fed from the same primary current.

This isolation is also what allows the series circuit itself to function reliably in the first place: because current transfer, not voltage, is what matters in a series loop, a transformer can fail short or a fixture can be removed for maintenance without disrupting the loop's constant current to every other light. That fault-tolerance is the whole reason airfield lighting is wired this way rather than with a conventional parallel circuit, and the isolating transformer is the component that makes it work fixture by fixture.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Isolating transformers are installed at every light fixture position in the AGL series circuit. They are used in:

  • Runway Lighting: Edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, end lights, touchdown zone lights, and approach lighting systems.
  • Taxiway Lighting: Edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, and guidance signs.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Low, medium, and high intensity obstruction lights.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Sequenced flashing lights, barrettes, and crossbar lights.
  • All AGL fixtures: Both elevated and inset fixtures require an isolating transformer for power and isolation.

The transformers are typically located in a handhole or pit adjacent to the fixture, or buried directly in the cable trench. They are rated according to the wattage of the connected lamp (e.g., 45W, 65W, 100W, 200W) and the primary current (6.6A or 20A).

Placement

Installation & Location

Whatever its rating, an isolating transformer is installed at the fixture position it serves, either in a handhole or concrete pit set to grade, or buried directly in the cable trench, with the choice driven by ground conditions, fixture type, and maintenance access requirements. The primary winding is spliced into the constant-current series loop with an L-823 (or larger, for 20A circuits) primary connector, and the secondary leads run a short distance to the fixture's own connector, keeping the low-voltage secondary cable run as short as practical. Enclosure depth and cable routing are tied into the runway or taxiway centreline survey and set out to clear frost, drainage, and mechanical loading, while remaining shallow enough for routine access during fixture maintenance.

Function

How Does It Work?

The isolating transformer operates on the principle of electromagnetic induction. The primary winding (connected in series with the loop) carries the constant current from the CCR. This current creates a magnetic flux in the core, which induces a voltage in the secondary winding proportional to the turns ratio. The secondary voltage is chosen to match the lamp's rated voltage at the rated current. For example, a typical 45W halogen lamp at 6.6A requires about 6.8V. The transformer steps down the loop voltage (which can be hundreds of volts depending on loop length) to the low lamp voltage.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Galvanic Isolation: The primary and secondary windings are electrically separate, providing safety isolation between the high-voltage loop and the lamp.
  • Constant Current Transfer: The transformer maintains the loop current on the primary side, and the secondary current is scaled by the inverse of the turns ratio, ensuring the lamp receives the correct current.
  • Voltage Regulation: The transformer is designed to operate with minimal voltage drop and good regulation across the specified current range (2.8A to 6.6A for 5-step dimming).
  • Short-Circuit Protection: In the event of a lamp or cable short on the secondary side, the transformer's impedance limits the fault current, protecting the CCR and the loop.
  • Open-Circuit Protection: If the secondary is open (e.g., lamp removed), the primary impedance prevents excessive voltage rise, but the loop current still flows through the primary.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The isolating transformer sits in the middle of the same chain that governs every fixture in this library: an Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or pilot-controlled lighting input, and passes that command to the Constant Current Regulator, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A, or the 20A equivalent). The transformer's job is purely local translation, not decision-making — it has no addressable identity on the loop and receives no command of its own; it simply carries whatever current the CCR is driving through the loop at that moment and converts it to the voltage its fixture needs. This is why transformer-level faults surface as loop-level faults: the CCR monitors loop voltage against its commanded current, and a voltage rise signals increased impedance somewhere on the loop — an open-circuit lamp or a failed transformer look identical from the CCR's perspective, and sectionalised field testing is what narrows the fault down to a specific transformer. The physics of how the transformer itself behaves under a fault (rather than how that fault is reported upstream) is covered in the isolation and fault-response logic below.

Construction

Physical Construction

As the baseline design covering the 45W–300W range, the isolating transformer is built as a single encapsulated, potted unit: primary and secondary windings around a laminated core sit inside a moulded shell that is vacuum-impregnated and back-filled with epoxy or polyurethane resin, leaving no internal cavity for moisture to enter. This potting is what delivers the IP68, submersible rating specified for the unit — there is no separate gasket or enclosure lid to maintain. Insulation is specified to Class F or Class H depending on the rating selected, and the unit's leads terminate in sealed L-823 splice connectors suited to direct burial and continuous immersion. In service the transformer sits in a base can alongside the fixture or fixture group it feeds, secured by the surrounding backfill rather than mechanical fasteners, with stainless steel or tinned-copper lead wires resisting corrosion at the connection points over the working life of the loop.

Installation Requirements

Enclosure Excavation & Grade
Civil

Excavate and grade the handhole, pit, or direct-burial position to design depth, keeping the enclosure clear of frost, drainage, and mechanical loading while remaining shallow enough for routine access.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Route the series loop duct or trench to each transformer position in line with the fixture spacing set out along the runway or taxiway, avoiding sharp bends that could stress the connectors.

Primary Connector Termination
Electrical

Splice the primary winding into the series loop using an L-823 (or larger, for 20A circuits) connector sealed against moisture, closing the joint before backfill or enclosure closure.

Rating Match to Fixture & Loop
Electrical

Confirm the transformer's wattage and primary current (6.6A or 20A) match both the fixture demand and the series circuit before connection, since substituting the wrong rating disrupts loop regulation.

Secondary Run Length
Electrical

Keep the secondary lead run from the transformer to the fixture connector as short as practical, positioning the unit close to the fixture it feeds.

Moisture Sealing Check
Mechanical

Confirm all splice connectors are fully seated and rated for direct burial or continuous immersion before the enclosure or trench is closed, since the potted transformer body has no separate gasket to fall back on.

Position Recording
Documentation

Record each transformer's position against the runway or taxiway centreline survey, particularly for direct-buried units with no accessible chamber, so it can be located for testing or replacement later.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test primary and secondary windings against earth and against each other before backfill, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum for the rated current (6.6A or 20A) and power option supplied.

Winding Resistance Check
Pre-Energisation

Measure primary and secondary winding resistance and compare against the manufacturer's factory test figures for the specific rating supplied, flagging any deviation before installation.

Dielectric (Hi-Pot) Withstand Test
Pre-Energisation

Apply the rated dielectric test voltage between primary-secondary and primary-earth to confirm the transformer withstands its rated withstand voltage without breakdown.

Loop Continuity Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity through the transformer's primary winding at the installed primary current rating, confirming no unintended breaks before first energisation.

Secondary Voltage / Turns Ratio Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised at rated primary current, confirm secondary voltage matches the transformer's rated turns ratio for the connected fixture load.

CCR Step Response Check
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through its intensity steps and confirm the transformer's secondary output tracks each step correctly, ruling out a marginal winding or loose connection.

Encapsulation & Connector Sealing Check
Pre-Energisation

Inspect the sealed encapsulation and L-823 connectors for damage, confirming IP68 integrity is intact before the unit is buried or backfilled.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off

Record insulation resistance, winding resistance, dielectric, and secondary voltage readings alongside as-built position and rating supplied in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Moisture Ingress and Corrosion

Isolating transformers are often buried and subject to groundwater ingress. Over time, moisture can enter the encapsulation or terminal box, causing insulation degradation and winding corrosion. Regular insulation resistance testing and use of high-quality moisture-sealed units are essential.

Primary Winding Open Circuit

A broken primary winding or loose connection can open the series circuit, turning off all downstream lights. This is often due to mechanical stress during installation or corrosion of the primary terminals. Using proper torque and anti-corrosion compounds is recommended.

Core Saturation at High Current

At currents above rated (e.g., 6.6A for some transformers), core saturation can occur, causing increased losses and overheating. Ensure transformers are correctly sized for the maximum loop current and that the CCR's step current does not exceed transformer ratings.

Secondary Short Circuit

If the secondary cable or lamp socket is shorted, the transformer will draw excessive primary current, potentially tripping the CCR. Short-circuit protection (e.g., secondary fuses) is not common; proper cable insulation and fixture installation are critical to prevent shorts.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Downstream Lights Dark, Loop Otherwise Continuous
Likely Cause
Open-circuit primary winding or a failed/loose L-823 primary connection at this transformer, breaking the series loop to every fixture fed beyond it.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the transformer primary; megger the primary winding; check for corrosion or looseness at the L-823 connectors on both sides of the unit.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded or loose connectors with anti-corrosion compound and correct torque, or replace the transformer if the primary winding itself has failed.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress into the encapsulation or terminal box has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or partial ground fault on the loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the transformer (target ≥ 50 MΩ at 500V); visually inspect the encapsulation and cable entries for cracking or damage; check for signs of water inside the terminal box if accessible.
Corrective Action
Replace the transformer if insulation resistance is below the acceptable minimum; ensure replacement cable glands and seals are correctly fitted and torqued before backfill.
Secondary Short Circuit / Fixture Draws Excess Current
Likely Cause
Damaged secondary cable insulation or a fault in the connected fixture is creating a short on the secondary side, drawing excessive primary current.
Diagnostic Steps
Disconnect the fixture and test the transformer secondary in isolation; inspect secondary cable jackets for chafing or damage; check the fixture itself for internal shorts.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace damaged secondary cable with proper cable glands to prevent chafing, and repair or replace the faulted fixture before re-energising.
Overheating or Core Saturation at High Loop Current
Likely Cause
The transformer is operating above its rated current (e.g., a CCR step exceeding the transformer's 6.6A rating), causing increased losses, saturation, and heat build-up.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the CCR's step current against the transformer's rated primary current; check for unusual heat, discolouration, or a burning smell at the unit if accessible.
Corrective Action
Correct the CCR step setting or replace the transformer with one correctly rated for the maximum loop current in use.
Intermittent Flicker on Downstream Fixture
Likely Cause
Loose or partially corroded primary or secondary connections are creating a marginal, temperature-sensitive contact.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque all cable connections at the transformer; check for corrosion on crimps or connector pins; monitor secondary voltage for drop under load once the CCR has warmed up.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded connections with new crimps and sealant, or replace the transformer if the fault persists after re-termination.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Primary Winding Open Circuit
High Severity
Root Cause
Wire fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown internal fuse-wire link from a lightning-induced transient.
Effect
Opens the series loop, darkening every fixture downstream of the transformer on that circuit.
Detection Method
CCR loop-continuity/open-circuit indication, and megger or continuity testing at the transformer primary.
Insulation Breakdown from Moisture Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or degraded encapsulation, a failed cable gland or seal, or prolonged submersion in a flooded handhole or pit.
Effect
Reduced insulation resistance leading to a partial or full ground fault that can trip the CCR.
Detection Method
Megger testing against the ≥50 MΩ minimum, and visual inspection of the encapsulation and cable entries.
Secondary Winding Short or Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal insulation failure between secondary turns, often accelerated by sustained overtemperature operation.
Effect
Loss of secondary voltage regulation, fixture dimming or failure, and possible primary overcurrent.
Detection Method
Secondary voltage measurement under load, compared against the transformer's rated output.
Overload / Core Saturation from Rating Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A CCR step or connected fixture load exceeding the transformer's rated primary current or secondary power.
Effect
Increased core losses, overheating, and accelerated insulation ageing.
Detection Method
Verification of the CCR step current against the transformer's rating plate, and thermal inspection where accessible.
L-823 Connector / Termination Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating during installation, gasket failure allowing moisture in, or galvanic corrosion building up at the connector pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent contact, flicker, or a high-resistance joint that generates localised heat.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at scheduled maintenance, and continuity or resistance checks across the joint.
Encapsulation Cracking / Case Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Freeze-thaw cycling, mechanical impact during installation or maintenance, or age-related degradation of the potting compound.
Effect
Exposes the winding to moisture, accelerating insulation breakdown.
Detection Method
Visual inspection during handhole or pit access, and a drop in insulation resistance on megger testing.
Thermal Degradation from Sustained Overcurrent
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated or prolonged operation above rated current, often from an incorrectly set CCR step or a transformer undersized for the installed load.
Effect
Progressive insulation embrittlement that shortens service life and raises the risk of an eventual winding failure.
Detection Method
Rating verification against actual loop current, and insulation resistance trending across successive maintenance cycles.
Output Voltage Drift / Regulation Loss
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual winding degradation or core ageing that shifts the transformer's turns-ratio performance away from its original specification.
Effect
Fixture brightness drifts slightly from the intended intensity step without an outright fault.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity survey and secondary voltage measurement compared against commissioning records.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The isolating transformer sits on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the transformer before opening a handhole, pit, or connector housing.

Confirm Correct Transformer in Circuit
Operational

On multi-transformer series circuits, positively identify the correct unit before isolating work, since opening the wrong handhole can leave live conductors exposed while the intended fixture stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the primary and secondary windings against the minimum insulation resistance before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy transformers on the loop.

Rating Verification Before Replacement
Compliance

Confirm the replacement transformer's primary current rating and secondary power rating match the CCR step and connected fixture before installing it; an incorrectly rated unit will saturate, overheat, or under-drive the fixture.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the transformer case and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

FOD Control at the Handhole
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when a handhole or pit is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the cover, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Hot Surface Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the transformer case to cool before handling after an extended active period, since sustained loop current can leave the encapsulation and connectors noticeably warm to the touch.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a transformer with a degraded insulation reading, a loose connection, or a mis-seated cover after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and secure closure before closing out the work order.

Quick Reference

Airport Isolating Transformer: Selection, Ratings & Testing

An airport isolating transformer is the part that connects each light to the series loop. Searches for isolating transformer airport lighting or airport lighting transformer usually mean this component. The same unit is used as a runway lighting transformer (also called a runway light transformer), as a taxiway light transformer and on approach and signage circuits. What changes between these uses is the rating and the connector type, not the working principle.

Isolating Transformer Primary Voltage & Secondary Voltage

The isolating transformer primary voltage is not a fixed supply voltage. The primary carries the loop current (commonly 6.6 A or 20 A), so the voltage across it is a small drop that depends on the transformer rating and the load. The isolating transformer secondary voltage is the voltage delivered to the lamp or LED module, and it depends on the lamp wattage at the rated secondary current. Always read both from the nameplate and manufacturer data, not from a general rule.

Connectors. An airport lighting transformer connector joins the transformer to the series cable (primary side) and to the fixture (secondary side). Use the connector type that matches the transformer and cable, and see the L-823 connector, primary plug and secondary plug pages.

How Is an Isolating Transformer Selected?

To choose a transformer, match the loop current (6.6 A or 20 A), the rated wattage to the lamp or LED load, the installation type (plug-in, buried or elevated), the connector type, and the standard or project specification that applies. Rating the unit too low risks overheating, and too high wastes loop capacity.

Airport Lighting Transformer Testing

Airport lighting transformer testing normally covers three checks, each compared with the manufacturer data, the previous record or the project specification. The resistance and insulation tests are listed here, and the ratio test follows below.

  • Isolating transformer resistance test: measure the DC resistance of the primary and secondary windings. An open winding reads as an open circuit, and a shorted turn can read low.
  • Isolating transformer insulation test: use an insulation tester between the windings and between each winding and earth, with the transformer disconnected from the circuit.

Never test a transformer that is connected to a live series circuit. Isolate and earth the circuit first, following the airport's permit and lockout procedure. A transformer tester can speed up field checks.

Isolating Transformer Ratio Test

Pass a known current through the primary, measure the secondary current and compare with the rated ratio.

Match Rating to the Fixture, Not the Handhole It Fits
Compliance

A transformer that physically fits an existing handhole doesn't mean it's rated correctly for the fixture now installed there. Check secondary VA against the actual connected load every time, not just physical fit.

Insulation Resistance Trend Beats a Single Pass/Fail
Inspection

Log megger readings over successive visits rather than treating one reading above minimum as “healthy.” A transformer trending toward the threshold is worth pre-emptive replacement before it actually fails.

Core Saturation Looks Like a Lamp Problem at First
Troubleshooting

An overloaded or undersized transformer often first presents as flickering or dim fixtures, which gets misdiagnosed as a lamp or LED fault before anyone checks the transformer's actual loading.

Keep an As-Built Record of Which Transformer Feeds Which Fixture
Maintenance

On a series circuit with many isolating transformers, losing track of which unit feeds which fixture turns every fault into a lengthy elimination exercise. Maintain and update the loop map as transformers are swapped.

Handhole Drainage Outlasts a Good Encapsulation Rating
Durability

Even a transformer rated for wet locations ages faster sitting in standing water than in a dry pit. Confirm the handhole actually drains rather than relying solely on the unit's encapsulation rating.

Retrofit Projects Are Where Rating Mismatches Creep In
Design

When a fixture is upgraded (halogen to LED, for instance) without a project-wide transformer audit, mismatched units tend to persist for years since nothing fails immediately — it just runs less efficiently.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the AGL isolating transformer: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an AGL isolating transformer?

An isolating transformer (also called a series transformer or lighting transformer) is a single-phase transformer that couples an individual light fixture to the constant-current series circuit. Its primary is in series with the CCR output, carrying the full loop current (typically 6.6 A or 20 A), and its secondary supplies a single lamp or LED module at a lower voltage.

Q2Why are isolating transformers used?

They ensure each fixture receives the correct current regardless of how many fixtures are in the loop, while giving galvanic isolation for safety and fault tolerance. An open or failed lamp on the secondary does not break the series loop.

Q3How does an isolating transformer work?

By electromagnetic induction. The loop current creates flux in the core that induces a secondary voltage set by the turns ratio to match the lamp's voltage at rated current. For example, a 45 W halogen lamp at 6.6 A needs about 6.8 V, so the transformer steps the loop voltage down to that low lamp voltage.

Q4Where are isolating transformers installed?

At every light fixture position in the AGL series circuit: runway, taxiway, approach and obstruction lighting. They are typically buried in a concrete pit or directly in the ground near the fixture.

Q5What are the typical ratings?

Primary current 6.6 A (or 20 A), secondary 6 V, 12 V or 24 V depending on lamp type, and rated power 45 W to 300 W. Insulation class is F (155°C) or H (180°C), dielectric strength 3 kV primary-secondary and 2 kV primary-earth, and protection IP68 (submersible) with sealed encapsulation.

Q6What cable and connectors does it use?

L-824 single-conductor cable, direct-burial rated, with an L-823 primary connector and submersible-rated splice. Insulation resistance should be at least 50 MΩ measured cable-to-ground.

Q7Is the transformer a maintenance item?

It is a passive device designed to run continuously at its rated current with little maintenance, but periodic electrical and condition checks are still needed.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are IEC 61823 (and its EN adoption EN 61823), FAA AC 150/5345-47 (L-830, L-831 and L-832 series isolating transformers) and ICAO Annex 14. EN 61822 is the CCR standard and does not cover transformers. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should an isolating transformer be tested?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for secondary voltage and load current verification, and annual for insulation resistance, turns ratio and winding resistance, dielectric withstand test, encapsulation and seal integrity, thermal imaging and cable and connector inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with isolating transformers?

Common problems are moisture ingress and corrosion, primary winding open circuit, core saturation at high current and secondary short circuit. Downstream lights going dark with the loop otherwise continuous, or a CCR tripping on overcurrent or ground fault, are typical symptoms.

Q11How is an airport lighting transformer tested?

An airport lighting transformer is tested with the circuit isolated and earthed. The usual checks are a winding resistance test on the primary and secondary, an insulation resistance test between the windings and to earth, and a ratio test by passing a known primary current and measuring the secondary current. Results are compared with the manufacturer data, previous records or the project specification.

Q12How is an isolating transformer selected?

An isolating transformer is selected by matching the loop current (commonly 6.6 A or 20 A), the rated wattage to the lamp or LED load, the installation type (plug-in, buried or elevated), the connector type, and the standard or project specification that applies. The nameplate and manufacturer data should confirm the primary and secondary ratings.
