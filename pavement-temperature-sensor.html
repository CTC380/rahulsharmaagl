What is a pavement temperature sensor?

The probe or infrared sensor that measures the temperature of the runway and taxiway surface — and why the pavement can be below freezing while the air is not, and how the sensor shares the pavement with the lighting.

The Fact ICAO Annex 14 · Doc 9981 · winter operations

A pavement temperature sensor measures the temperature of the runway or taxiway surface, and often of the layer below it. It is either a probe set flush in the pavement or an infrared sensor on a pole that looks at a spot on the surface. Some units also report whether the surface is dry, wet, icy or treated. The pavement can be below 0 °C while the air is still above it.

On clear, calm nights the pavement loses heat by radiation and can cool faster than the air. When the surface is below 0 °C and at or below the dew point, frost or ice can form; a wet surface below 0 °C can also freeze whatever the dew point. Winter operations teams use the readings, with the air temperature and dew point from the weather station, to decide on anti-icing, de-icing and runway condition action.

The sensor shares the pavement with the in-pavement lights, base cans and ducts, and frost and ice also affect the lights themselves. A probe that is not flush, or a cracked seal, is a hazard to aircraft tyres and lets water in, so installation is coordinated with the lighting and civil works. The air temperature probe is entry 15 of the weather-sensing division.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What does a pavement temperature sensor measure?

It measures the temperature of the runway or taxiway surface, and often of the pavement a few centimetres below it. Some units also report the surface condition, such as dry, wet, icy or treated with chemicals. It is a different instrument from the air temperature probe, because the pavement and the air can be at quite different temperatures.

Q2How do contact and infrared sensors differ?

A contact sensor is a probe set flush in the pavement, sealed in, with its cable in a duct. It measures the pavement it sits in. An infrared sensor is mounted on a pole beside the pavement and looks at a spot on the surface without touching it, so it needs no pavement cutting but needs a clear line of sight and a clean lens. Many aerodromes use embedded probes, sometimes with an infrared unit as a cross-check.

Q3Why not just use the air temperature?

On clear, calm nights the pavement loses heat by radiation and can fall below 0 degrees Celsius while the air a metre above it is still above freezing. Frost or ice can then form on the surface even when the air temperature looks safe. The pavement temperature, together with the dew point from the weather station, tells winter operations teams whether frost or ice is likely.

Q4Where are the sensors placed?

In the runway and taxiway pavement at representative positions, often at the touchdown zone, the mid-point and the stop end, and on key taxiways. The aerodrome and the meteorological authority decide the positions. Probes are placed where they represent the pavement but where wheel loads and snow clearing will not damage them, and they must sit flush with the surface.

Q5How does it connect to the AGL system?

It shares the pavement with the in-pavement lights, base cans and ducts, so the installation is coordinated with the lighting. It is not part of the series circuit, and its cable must be kept separate and recorded like any other airfield cable. Ice and frost on the surface also affect the lights themselves, which is one reason the readings are useful to the lighting maintenance team.

Q6What goes wrong?

Typical faults are a probe that is not flush or has a cracked seal, water in the cable or connector, damage during resurfacing or snow clearing, a dirty or iced infrared lens, calibration drift and a wrong position that does not represent the pavement. A probe left standing above the surface is also a hazard to aircraft tyres and a damage risk, so it needs the same care as an inset light.

Q7Is it safe to work on?

Work in the pavement needs the aerodrome's permit-to-work and aircraft-movement controls, and usually a runway or taxiway closure. Before cutting or drilling, locate and mark the AGL cables and ducts so they are not damaged. Because the readings affect winter operations decisions, tell the airside operations team and the meteorological authority before taking a sensor out of service.
