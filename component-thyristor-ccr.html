Thyristor CCR

A robust and proven constant current regulator technology using Silicon Controlled Rectifiers (SCRs) for phase‑control regulation. Thyristor CCRs are widely used in AGL systems for their high surge capacity, simple design, and reliable performance in demanding airfield environments.

Overview

What Is It?

A Thyristor Constant Current Regulator (SCR CCR) is a type of AGL power supply that uses phase‑control with Silicon Controlled Rectifiers (thyristors) to regulate the output current. The thyristors are triggered at a specific phase angle of the AC waveform to control the power delivered to the series circuit. This technology has been the industry standard for decades due to its robustness, ability to handle high surge currents, and tolerance to harsh electrical environments. Thyristor CCRs typically use a combination of a step‑down transformer, SCR bridge, and a control circuit that compares the output current with a reference and adjusts the firing angle accordingly. They are known for their high reliability and are still widely deployed in airfields worldwide, although IGBT‑based regulators are becoming more common in new installations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The thyristor CCR exists to regulate current using a technology proven over decades to tolerate the harsh electrical conditions an outdoor AGL series circuit presents, particularly surge currents and voltage transients that a more delicate design might not survive without additional protection. Its phase-control method — triggering the SCRs at a specific point in the AC waveform to control delivered power — achieves current regulation through a comparatively simple, well-understood control mechanism that has made it the long-standing industry standard specifically because of its predictability and field-proven reliability. Its purpose today sits between the ferroresonant CCR's pure robustness and the IGBT CCR's superior efficiency and power quality: it remains a dependable, widely deployed choice precisely because so many airfields already rely on its established track record, even as new installations increasingly favour the improved characteristics of IGBT-based regulation.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Thyristor CCRs are used in the same applications as other CCR types, including:

  • Runway Lighting: Edge, centreline, threshold, and touchdown zone lights.
  • Taxiway Lighting: Edge lights, centreline lights, and stop bars.
  • Approach Lighting: All series‑fed approach lighting systems (ALSF, MALS, etc.).
  • Obstruction Lighting: Some high‑intensity obstruction lighting circuits.
  • Retrofit Projects: Often used to replace older ferroresonant units or to expand existing systems.

Thyristor CCRs are particularly favoured in regions with unstable mains supplies or frequent lightning activity, as they are less sensitive to voltage transients than some other designs.

Placement

Installation & Location

Thyristor CCRs are floor- or wall-mounted in the electrical equipment room close to the circuit they feed, in the same style as other CCR types, with clearance kept at the ventilated sides for natural convection or at the fan intake/exhaust where forced-air cooling is fitted. Incoming mains and control/communication wiring (RS-485, Modbus) enter from below, while the high-voltage output to the series circuit is run in its own cable route away from the control wiring. Because the phase-control firing circuit is more sensitive to incoming supply transients than a ferroresonant design, thyristor CCRs are often specified with surge protection on the input feeder, and the cabinet frame is bonded to the room's earthing system in the usual way.

Function

How Does It Work?

The thyristor CCR operates by phase‑controlling the AC voltage applied to the series circuit. The incoming AC is passed through a transformer to step down the voltage, then through a thyristor bridge that rectifies and chops the waveform. By adjusting the firing angle of the thyristors, the RMS voltage across the load is controlled, which in turn regulates the current through the series circuit. A current transformer (CT) senses the output current and feeds it back to a control circuit, which compares it to the desired setpoint (e.g., 6.6A) and adjusts the firing angle to maintain the current constant. The control circuit includes a ramp generator and a comparator to produce the gate pulses for the thyristors. Protection features include overcurrent detection, short‑circuit protection, and overtemperature shutdown. Thyristor CCRs are typically air‑cooled with natural convection or forced air, depending on the rating.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Firing angle is the only control variable — the feedback loop adjusts it continuously to hold current at the 6.6A setpoint.
Construction

Physical Construction

A Thyristor CCR is built around a step-down transformer and an SCR bridge, both mounted within a floor-standing or wall-mounted steel cabinet along with the associated control electronics. The SCR bridge is mounted on a heatsink, typically with forced-air cooling via a thermostatically controlled fan, and is positioned to keep the snubber and gate-drive circuitry close by without excessive heat exposure. The step-down transformer, often the heaviest single component, is mounted on a reinforced base within the cabinet, with the control section — comparator/regulation circuitry, firing-angle control board, and HMI — isolated on a separate sub-panel to shield it from transformer hum and electrical noise from the SCR switching. Cabinet louvres and filtered fan intakes provide the airflow path across the heatsink, and heavy-duty terminal blocks, an input isolator/breaker, and surge protection are provided for the incoming mains and outgoing series circuit, consistent with other CCR technologies.

Rigging & Positioning
Mechanical

Move and set the cabinet using rated lifting equipment sized for the transformer's weight, securing it to the floor to prevent movement under vibration.

Incoming Mains Termination
Electrical

Terminate the incoming supply at the input isolator/breaker, confirming cable size and breaker rating match the regulator's rated input current.

Series Circuit Output Termination
Electrical

SCR Heatsink & Fan Clearance
Mechanical

Earthing & Surge Protection Bonding
Electrical

Bond the cabinet earthing bar to the site earthing system and confirm surge protection is fitted at the incoming supply.

Communication Cabling
Systems

Run and terminate the RS-485 or Modbus cabling to the CCR controller or monitoring system where fitted, keeping it separated from power wiring and SCR gate-drive circuitry.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Testing & Commissioning

Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the CCR's input, output, and series loop windings before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Verify continuity of the complete series loop and record total loop resistance before the CCR is first energised.

Verify snubber networks across the thyristors are intact and confirm the regulator withstands a simulated line surge without misfiring.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Thyristor Module Inspection
Annual

Inspect thyristor modules and thermal paste condition, since voltage spikes, overheating, or aging can cause unbalanced output or loss of regulation.

Gate Pulse Testing
Annual

Check firing pulses with an oscilloscope to catch faulty gate drivers, opto-couplers, or pulse transformers before they cause misfiring.

Capacitor Aging Check
Annual

Test DC link or snubber capacitors and plan replacement every 5-7 years to prevent increased ripple and reduced performance.

Cooling Fan & Filter Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean fans and filters and check for bearing wear to prevent overtemperature shutdown.

Surge Protection & Earthing Check
Annual

Inspect surge protection and earthing to guard thyristors and control electronics against lightning or switching transients.

Annual

Re-torque terminal connections and inspect the enclosure for corrosion or moisture ingress.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Thyristor Failure

Thyristors can fail due to voltage spikes, overheating, or aging. A failed thyristor can cause unbalanced output or complete loss of regulation. Regular inspection of the thyristor modules and thermal paste condition is recommended. Replacement is typically module‑based.

Gate Drive Circuit Issues

The gate drive circuits provide the firing pulses to the thyristors. Faulty gate drivers, opto‑couplers, or pulse transformers can cause misfiring or unstable regulation. Periodic testing of gate pulses with an oscilloscope is advisable.

Capacitor Aging

Filter capacitors in the DC link (if present) or snubber capacitors can degrade, leading to increased ripple and reduced performance. Capacitor replacement every 5–7 years is typical.

Cooling Fan Failure

Forced‑air cooled units rely on fans that can fail due to dust or bearing wear. Regular cleaning and fan replacement schedules are essential to prevent overtemperature shutdown.

Transient Voltage Spikes

Lightning or switching transients can damage thyristors or control electronics. Proper surge protection and earthing are critical for long‑term reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Unbalanced Output or Loss of Regulation
Likely Cause
A thyristor has failed due to a voltage spike, overheating, or normal aging.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for unbalanced phase output and inspect thyristor modules and thermal paste condition; review temperature logs around the fault.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed thyristor module per the manufacturer's procedure, and re-verify balanced, regulated output under load.
Misfiring or Unstable Regulation
Likely Cause
A fault in the gate drive circuit — a faulty gate driver, opto-coupler, or pulse transformer — is delivering incorrect firing pulses.
Diagnostic Steps
Test gate drive pulses with an oscilloscope for correct timing across all thyristors; check opto-couplers and pulse transformers for degradation.
Corrective Action
Replace the faulty gate drive component and confirm correct, stable firing pulses before returning to service.
Increased Ripple or Reduced Performance
Likely Cause
Filter or snubber capacitors in the DC link (if present) have degraded, typical after 5-7 years of service.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure ripple and capacitor condition against manufacturer tolerance; compare against commissioning baseline if available.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded capacitors on the recommended service interval, and re-verify output performance with a load test.
Overtemperature Shutdown
Likely Cause
A cooling fan on the forced-air cooled unit has failed due to dust or bearing wear.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fan operation and airflow; inspect for dust buildup blocking ventilation; review the temperature log for the shutdown event.
Corrective Action
Clean the cooling path and replace the failed fan; confirm temperatures stay within limits under load before returning to service.
Thyristors or Control Electronics Damaged After a Storm
Likely Cause
A lightning strike or switching transient has bypassed inadequate surge protection and damaged thyristors or control electronics.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect surge protection devices for tripped or burnt-out indicators; test thyristors and control electronics for correct operation; verify site earthing.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged surge protection devices and any affected components, and improve earthing or SPD coordination if the fault recurs.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Thyristor (SCR) Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling fatigue, an overcurrent or overvoltage event beyond the device's rating, or a latent manufacturing defect.
Effect
Unbalanced output or loss of regulation on the affected phase or leg, and in severe cases a protective shutdown.
Detection Method
Fault log review for the tripped protection, and static thyristor testing to confirm the failed device.
Firing Circuit Misfire
High Severity
Root Cause
A degraded firing/trigger circuit component, incorrect firing angle calibration, or noise coupling onto the gate trigger signal.
Effect
Misfiring or unstable regulation, with output current deviating from the commanded setpoint.
Detection Method
Firing angle waveform capture under load, and trigger circuit calibration check against the manufacturer's specification.
Output Filter / Choke Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Insulation ageing or core heating in the output choke, or filter capacitor degradation under sustained ripple current over years of service.
Effect
Increased ripple and reduced performance, which can shorten the life of downstream lamps or LED fixtures.
Detection Method
Output ripple measurement against tolerance, and periodic choke/capacitor testing during major maintenance.
Overtemperature Shutdown
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Blocked or fouled heatsink fins, a failed cooling fan, or operation in an enclosure with inadequate ventilation for the ambient conditions.
Effect
The CCR trips on overtemperature protection, interrupting the runway lighting circuit until it cools and is reset.
Detection Method
Internal temperature monitoring against the trip threshold, and inspection of cooling fans/fins during scheduled maintenance.
Storm Damage to Thyristors or Control Electronics
High Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced transient or switching surge on the input, output, or control wiring that exceeds the unit's surge protection rating.
Effect
Damaged thyristors or control board, erratic regulation, or complete failure to energise the series circuit.
Detection Method
Post-storm functional and self-test check, and inspection of onboard surge protection devices and thyristors for visible damage.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

High Open-Circuit Series Voltage
Electrical Safety

As with any CCR, the output loop can present several kV open-circuit; de-energise and lock out the unit at its supply, and verify zero energy at the output terminals with a suitably rated meter before touching them.

Snubber Capacitor Residual Charge
Electrical Safety

Snubber capacitors across the SCR bridge can retain a charge after shutdown; observe the manufacturer's specified discharge time or confirm discharge with a rated meter before opening the cabinet.

Hot Heatsinks and Transformer
Personnel Safety

Allow the SCR heatsink and step-down transformer to cool before handling after active operation.

PPE and Arc-Flash Precautions
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working near the SCR bridge or incoming mains connection, and follow the site's arc-flash procedure.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Taking a Thyristor CCR out of service darkens an entire series circuit; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before isolation.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the series loop before restoring power after any downstream work, since a residual fault can damage the SCR bridge or trip protection on re-energisation.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm output current is stable at the commanded step, with correct firing-angle behaviour and no abnormal harmonic content, before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Firing Angle Calibration Drift Is the Signature Fault of This Technology
Calibration

Because a thyristor CCR regulates current by triggering the SCRs at a specific phase angle, a slowly drifting firing angle produces a gradual, easy-to-miss shift in output current rather than a sudden fault, and it's the failure mode most specific to this design compared with ferroresonant or IGBT alternatives. Prioritise firing angle calibration checks on the maintenance schedule for thyristor CCRs specifically, given how characteristic this drift is to the technology.

Gate Pulse Testing Catches a Failing SCR Before It Takes Down the Loop
Diagnostics

An SCR with a degrading gate trigger circuit can continue operating for some time with reduced margin before it fails outright and drops the whole series loop, and a routine gate pulse test is one of the few checks that reveals this degradation ahead of the hard failure. Include gate pulse testing as a distinct item rather than folding it into a general output-current check.

Snubber Circuit Condition Is What Actually Gives This CCR Its Surge Tolerance
Reliability

The surge-current robustness thyristor CCRs are known for depends heavily on the snubber circuit around each SCR staying in good condition, and a degraded snubber quietly erodes that surge tolerance well before the CCR shows any symptom under normal, non-surge operating conditions. Verify snubber circuit and surge withstand performance directly rather than assuming the technology's reputation for ruggedness is a permanent, maintenance-free property.

Capacitor Aging in the Control Circuit Causes Subtle Regulation Errors
Maintenance

Aging capacitors in the control circuit that compares actual to desired output can introduce small regulation errors or increased output ripple long before they fail outright, an effect that's easy to miss on a unit that's "still working" by any obvious external measure. Include capacitor aging checks in scheduled maintenance rather than waiting for an output ripple complaint to prompt investigation.

Output Ripple Is a More Sensitive Early-Warning Indicator Than Average Current
Diagnostics

A thyristor CCR's average output current can stay within tolerance while the ripple content on that output rises measurably as components age, and ripple is often the more sensitive indicator of developing trouble in the control loop or SCR bridge. Trend output ripple and harmonic readings across services rather than relying solely on the average current reading to judge unit health.

Cooling Clearance for SCR Heatsinks Is Less Forgiving Than It Looks
Installation

SCR modules concentrate heat at a few junction points on the heatsink, and even a modest reduction in airflow clearance from a poorly sited enclosure or blocked filter has a disproportionate effect on device temperature compared with the more distributed heat generation of an IGBT design. Give SCR heatsink and fan clearance the same attention as the incoming supply termination during installation, not an afterthought.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about thyristor CCRs: how phase control works, efficiency, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a thyristor CCR?

A thyristor (SCR) constant current regulator is an AGL power supply that uses phase control with Silicon Controlled Rectifiers to regulate output current. It has been the industry standard for decades because of its robustness, ability to handle high surge currents and tolerance of harsh electrical environments.

Q2How does a thyristor CCR work?

Mains passes through a step-down transformer and a thyristor bridge, and the thyristors are fired at a set phase angle of the AC waveform. Adjusting the firing angle changes the RMS voltage on the series circuit, and a current transformer feeds the measured output current back to a control circuit that compares it to the setpoint, such as 6.6 A.

Q3How accurate and efficient is a thyristor CCR?

Regulation accuracy is typically ±1% of set current and efficiency is 85–90% at full load. Output is 6.6 A RMS in five steps (2.8, 3.4, 4.1, 5.2 and 6.6 A) at 5–30 kVA.

Q4Where are thyristor CCRs used?

Runway edge, centreline, threshold and touchdown zone lighting, taxiway lighting and stop bars, series-fed approach lighting (ALSF, MALS), some high-intensity obstruction circuits and retrofit projects replacing older ferroresonant units. They are favoured where mains supplies are unstable or lightning is frequent.

Q5What is the difference between thyristor and IGBT CCRs?

A thyristor CCR uses phase control, which inherently adds some harmonic distortion and ripple. IGBT CCRs use high-frequency PWM for higher efficiency (above 90%), lower harmonics and about ±0.5% accuracy, while thyristor units are prized for their proven ruggedness and surge tolerance.

Q6How is a thyristor CCR installed?

Floor- or wall-mounted in the electrical equipment room close to the circuit it feeds, with clearance at the ventilated sides for natural convection or at the fan intake and exhaust where forced-air cooling is fitted. Incoming mains and RS-485 or Modbus control wiring enter from below, and the high-voltage output runs in its own cable route.

Q7Which standards apply to thyristor CCRs?

The main references are IEC 61822 and EN 61822, FAA AC 150/5345-10 (constant current regulators and regulator monitors) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q8How often should a thyristor CCR be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cooling fan and filter cleaning, and annual for thyristor module and thermal paste inspection, gate pulse testing, capacitor ageing checks (replace roughly every 5–7 years), surge protection and earthing checks and terminal and enclosure checks. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9Why has my thyristor CCR lost regulation or shown unbalanced output?

Usually a failed thyristor from a voltage spike, overheating or ageing. Check phase balance, inspect the thyristor modules and thermal paste, review temperature logs and replace the failed module per the manufacturer's procedure.

Q10What causes misfiring or unstable regulation?

A fault in the gate drive circuit, such as a faulty gate driver, opto-coupler or pulse transformer, sending incorrect firing pulses. Test the firing pulses with an oscilloscope for correct timing across all thyristors and replace the degraded component.
