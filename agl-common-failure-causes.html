Overview

Why AGL Failures Need a Structured Approach

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) failures can originate in the light fixture, secondary connection, isolating transformer, primary series cable, Constant Current Regulator (CCR), control interface or Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS).

A failed runway light does not always mean that the fixture itself is defective. The actual cause may be an electrical connection problem, damaged cable, transformer fault, circuit protection operation or control-system issue.

Effective fault diagnosis starts with recording the symptoms, identifying the affected asset and circuit, reviewing available monitoring information and following the approved fault-investigation procedure. Replacing a fixture without identifying the underlying cause may lead to repeated failures, unnecessary expenditure and incomplete corrective action.

This guide explains common AGL failure symptoms, their possible causes, evidence to review and general corrective-action considerations.

Important safety note: AGL systems support aircraft movement and airport operations. Troubleshooting, testing, isolation, repair and return-to-service decisions must be performed by authorized personnel under the airport’s approved safety, access, isolation, maintenance and operational procedures. The possible causes listed below are diagnostic starting points, not confirmation of a fault or instructions for intrusive electrical work.

1 · Lights Not Working

Why Are Runway Lights Not Working?

When runway lights stop operating, the first task is to establish the extent of the failure. Determine whether the symptom affects one fitting, several fittings on a circuit or a wider lighting system.

The number and location of affected lights can help narrow the investigation, but the failure pattern alone does not establish the root cause.

Common causes of runway light failure

Possible causeTypical area to investigateEvidence to review
Fixture failureIndividual light fittingInspection observations, asset history and relevant equipment records
Secondary connection faultFixture connection and associated wiringConnector condition and approved inspection records
Isolating transformer faultTransformer and its connectionsTransformer identification, ratings and available test records
Primary cable faultSeries-circuit cable and jointsCircuit records, prior fault reports and approved cable-test results
CCR protection operationCCR and circuit power equipmentAlarm history, operating indications and maintenance records
Supply or internal CCR problemCCR power and internal equipmentAvailable event records and authorized inspection findings
Control command problemLocal or remote control interfaceCommand status, feedback, event logs and operating records
Multiple equipment defectsAffected lighting circuit or installation areaFailure distribution, asset history and inspection evidence

Individual light failure versus multiple light failure

When one light is not working

A local fixture fault, secondary connection issue, isolating transformer fault or another associated equipment problem may be involved. The cause must be confirmed using the approved investigation method.

When several lights on the same circuit are affected

The investigation may need to include the CCR, circuit condition, primary cable, connections, protection indications and relevant control functions.

When several unrelated systems are affected

Review any common equipment, control interface, supply arrangement or event that could explain the symptoms, while checking each affected system according to its approved procedure.

Recommended investigation record

Record the following before concluding the fault investigation:

Affected runway, taxiway, approach system or other installationExact fixture or equipment identification, where knownCircuit number and associated CCR, where applicableTime the failure was observed and reportedWhether the fault is continuous, intermittent or newly detectedAvailable CCR alarms and control-system indicationsRecent maintenance, replacement or modification historyInspection and test records relevant to the suspected fault
Do not replace every non-operating fitting automatically. Establish the likely failure area and record the evidence that supports the diagnosis.
2 · Flickering

What Causes Runway Light Flickering?

Runway light flickering or intermittent operation may indicate a problem with the fixture, electrical connections, associated equipment, circuit or control system.

Because similar symptoms can result from different faults, an observation of flickering should be treated as a symptom rather than a definitive diagnosis.

Possible causes of flickering

Possible causeWhat to investigate
Intermittent connectionAvailable connector and connection inspection records
Fixture component problemFixture condition, product history and relevant manufacturer guidance
Isolating transformer issueTransformer identification, condition and approved test results
Cable or joint deteriorationCircuit history, accessible installation condition and prescribed test records
CCR operating issueRecorded output indications, alarms and applicable event history
Control or monitoring issueRelationship between observed lighting behaviour and recorded commands or feedback
Equipment incompatibilityCompatibility of the installed components with the approved circuit design
Environmental damageEvidence of moisture, contamination, impact or deterioration

How to document flickering

Record whether the flickering is observed at one fitting, multiple fittings on the same circuit or multiple systems. Include the time, operating conditions, available equipment indications and any recurring pattern.

Review the information using the airport’s approved troubleshooting process. Do not assume that an apparent change in light output proves a CCR fault or that a control-system alarm proves a field-equipment failure.

Corrective action should follow the confirmed cause and the relevant equipment instructions.

3 · Water Ingress

Airport Light Water Ingress

Water ingress can contribute to corrosion, connection problems, insulation deterioration and damage to electrical or optical components.

The significance of water ingress depends on the fixture design, the affected component, the degree of exposure and the manufacturer’s requirements.

Common contributors to water ingress

Damaged or deteriorated sealing componentsCompromised cable entries or connection arrangementsIncorrect assembly or installationPhysical damage to the fitting or enclosureEnvironmental exposure beyond the equipment’s intended conditionsMaintenance or replacement work that does not restore the specified sealing arrangementDrainage or installation conditions inconsistent with the approved design

Inspection and corrective-action considerations

Record the equipment identification and observed condition. Where required, document visible moisture, corrosion, damaged seals, cable-entry concerns and relevant maintenance history.

The corrective-action process should establish the extent of the damage and whether associated electrical components require inspection, testing, replacement or other manufacturer-approved attention.

Cleaning or drying a fitting alone may not resolve the underlying cause. Any repair, reassembly, sealing or verification must follow the equipment instructions and the airport’s approved maintenance procedure.
4 · Inset Lights

Inset Light Damage and Failure

Inset airfield lights are installed within pavement and may experience mechanical loading, impact, contamination and environmental exposure. Their installation condition and compatibility with the approved mounting arrangement are important to reliable operation.

Common causes of inset light damage

Possible causeInspection considerations
Pavement-related loading or impactLook for evidence of damage to the fixture or surrounding pavement
Incorrect installationReview installation records and the approved mounting arrangement
Damaged lens or optical surfaceRecord cracks, contamination or other visible abnormalities
Loose or damaged mounting arrangementReview the prescribed inspection findings and installation details
Water ingressCheck relevant indicators and available sealing or maintenance records
ContaminationRecord deposits or material that may affect the optical surface
Connection or component defectReview relevant connection and equipment records
Inappropriate handlingCheck maintenance and replacement history where available

Why the surrounding pavement matters

A fitting and its surrounding pavement may need to be assessed together. Visible pavement damage, deterioration around the mounting area or evidence of physical impact may be relevant to the investigation.

Replacement decisions should take account of the specified fitting, approved installation details, required optical performance and any necessary pavement work. After corrective work, perform the inspections and acceptance checks required by the approved procedure.

Do not assume that installing a replacement fixture alone resolves the underlying installation or pavement-related cause.

5 · Isolating Transformers

What Causes AGL Isolating Transformer Failure?

An airfield lighting isolating transformer forms part of the lighting circuit and must be suitable for its specified electrical and installation conditions.

Transformer-related failures may be associated with insulation deterioration, moisture ingress, physical damage, connection defects or operating conditions outside the equipment’s intended ratings.

6 · CCR & Series Circuits

CCR Problems and Series Circuit Failures

The Constant Current Regulator (CCR) supplies the required current to the associated AGL series circuit. Its indications, alarms and event records can be useful evidence when investigating a lighting failure.

However, a CCR alarm or shutdown indication does not, by itself, identify the defective component.

Common CCR failure areas

Possible issueEvidence to review
Protection operationAlarm history, recorded event information and applicable protection records
Internal component faultAuthorized maintenance findings and manufacturer guidance
Cooling or ventilation problemEquipment indications and prescribed inspection records
Power supply issueAvailable equipment indications and authorized inspection results
Incorrect configuration or settingsApproved configuration records and relevant event history
Control interface problemCommand, feedback and communication records
Circuit-related faultAffected circuit information and approved investigation results
Recurring equipment problemMaintenance history, fault frequency and prior corrective actions

Series circuit failure investigation

Series-circuit failures may involve the primary cable, cable joints, connectors, isolating transformers, light fittings or associated power and control equipment.

Useful evidence may include

Circuit identification and approved circuit drawingsPattern and location of affected lightsCCR alarm and event recordsPrevious circuit faults and maintenance activitiesAvailable cable, connection and transformer test recordsRecent equipment changes or repairsResults of approved inspection and testing

The investigation should distinguish between a confirmed component fault and a symptom that could have several causes.

Safety note: Do not open energized equipment, disconnect series-circuit components, change CCR settings or perform intrusive tests without the approved isolation and work procedures. Authorized personnel must follow the specified method for the equipment and circuit being investigated.

7 · ALCMS & Control

ALCMS and Control-System Failures

The Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) may provide operator control, status indications, alarms and other approved functions. A failure within the control system can affect the command or indication path, while a field-equipment failure can generate an alarm or status abnormality within the system.

The investigation should distinguish between the reported indication and the actual condition of the lighting equipment.

8 · Cables & Connectors

Airfield Lighting Cable and Connector Failure Causes

AGL cable and connector faults may arise from physical damage, deteriorated insulation, poor joints, moisture exposure, installation defects or external excavation.

Primary series cables and secondary connections have different functions and should be investigated using the applicable circuit documentation and test methods.

A cable investigation record should include:

9 · LED Fittings

Why LED Airfield Lights Fail

LED airfield lights can experience failures involving the light source, electronic components, power or control interfaces, optical components, connections or environmental protection.

The likely cause depends on the particular product design, installation and operating conditions.

Possible LED light failure causes

Possible causeWhat to review
Electronic component failureManufacturer’s diagnostic guidance and equipment history
Thermal stressInstallation conditions and relevant product requirements
Environmental exposureEvidence of moisture, contamination or physical damage
Connection faultAccessible inspection records and approved test findings
Power-system compatibilityCompatibility information for the installed circuit and equipment
Control-interface issueOperating commands, indications and related event logs
Optical damageLens and other optical inspection findings
Product deteriorationService history and available manufacturer assessment

LED compatibility considerations

An LED fitting should not be assumed compatible with an existing AGL circuit simply because its physical dimensions are suitable.

Before a replacement or retrofit is accepted, verify the required electrical characteristics, approved system compatibility, control interfaces and any specific acceptance tests required by the project.

Potential energy or maintenance savings do not replace the need to demonstrate technical suitability and compliance with the applicable requirements.

11 · Investigation Process

A Systematic AGL Fault Investigation Process

A documented fault investigation helps teams identify the cause, avoid unnecessary component replacement and reduce recurring defects.

01

Record the reported symptom. Capture the observation, time, location, affected asset and any available operator or maintenance report. Avoid recording an assumed cause as if it were already confirmed.

02

Establish the failure scope. Identify whether the problem affects one fixture, multiple fittings, one circuit or several systems. Confirm equipment and circuit identities against approved records.

03

Review available indications. Review applicable CCR alarms, control-system status, circuit records and event history. Determine whether the available indications are consistent with the reported symptom.

04

Review previous maintenance. Check work orders, previous failures, equipment replacement history and any recent installation or configuration changes. Repeated faults may indicate an unresolved underlying cause, but the pattern alone is not sufficient to prove one.

05

Define the authorized investigation. Select the inspection or test method using the approved maintenance procedure, equipment instructions, applicable specifications and authorization requirements. Identify any access restrictions, isolation requirements, permits or operational coordination needed before work begins.

06

Record findings. Document observed conditions, tests performed, actual results, relevant instrument details, acceptance criteria and the procedure used.

07

Establish the cause. Separate the confirmed cause from other possible explanations. If the available evidence is inconclusive, record the investigation status and identify the next authorized action.

08

Carry out corrective action. Repair, adjust or replace equipment only under the approved procedure and within the responsible personnel’s authorization.

09

Verify the result. Perform the required post-maintenance tests, inspections or functional checks and record the results against the applicable acceptance criteria.

10

Close the record. Document the root cause where established, corrective work, verification evidence, outstanding items and authorized closure.

A fault should not be considered fully resolved merely because the original symptom is no longer visible. The required verification and closure process must be completed.

13 · Prevention

Preventing Recurring AGL Failures

Failure prevention depends on correct equipment selection, quality installation, suitable inspection and maintenance practices, reliable records and effective defect closure.

Useful reliability practices

Maintain accurate asset registers and circuit drawingsRecord equipment model, rating and replacement historyPerform scheduled inspections according to approved maintenance requirementsRetain test reports and relevant instrument recordsReview recurring faults rather than treating each incident in isolationCheck compatibility before replacing fixtures, transformers or control equipmentDocument installation changes and update controlled records as requiredInvestigate repeated water ingress, physical damage or connection problemsTrack outstanding defects and verify their closureReview relevant alarms and maintenance trendsUse root-cause analysis where appropriate to the severity and recurrence of the faultUse only approved components, procedures and acceptance criteria

Why root-cause analysis matters

Replacing a failed component may restore function, but it may not address the condition that caused the failure.

For example, if a replacement component fails repeatedly, the investigation may need to consider installation conditions, external damage, component compatibility, electrical conditions or maintenance history. These are hypotheses to investigate—not conclusions to make without evidence.

Engineering objective: Identify the actual cause, implement the appropriate corrective action and retain enough evidence to verify the result.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What should be checked first when runway lights fail?

Record the affected location and circuit, establish whether one or multiple lights are affected, and review available CCR and control-system indications. Follow the airport’s approved fault-investigation procedure to identify the cause.

Q2Why do runway lights keep failing?

Repeated failures may be associated with unresolved connection issues, equipment deterioration, environmental exposure, cable or installation problems, compatibility concerns or other system conditions. Review the failure history and evidence before determining the root cause.

Q3Why do airfield lights flicker?

Flickering may result from intermittent connections, equipment faults, circuit-related problems or control and power equipment issues. Establish the extent and timing of the symptom and review available records before selecting an authorized test method.

Q4What causes water ingress in airfield lights?

Possible contributors include damaged seals, compromised cable entries, incorrect assembly, physical damage or environmental conditions outside the equipment’s intended requirements. The corrective action should address the cause and assess any related electrical or optical damage.

Q5What causes isolating transformer failure?

Possible causes include insulation deterioration, moisture ingress, physical damage, connection defects, installation issues or operation outside the intended rating. Verify the actual cause using the applicable approved inspection and test procedures.

Q6Why does the CCR show a fault when some lights are not working?

A CCR indication can arise from protection operation, regulator-related issues or a circuit problem, among other possibilities. Review the recorded alarm and the affected lighting circuit. The indication alone does not prove which component has failed.

Q7Can an AGL fault be repaired while the circuit is energized?

All work must follow the airport-approved electrical safety, isolation, permit and maintenance procedures. Do not undertake intrusive electrical work simply because a lighting circuit appears to be operating normally.

Q8How can AGL failures be reduced?

Maintain accurate asset and circuit records, perform authorized preventive maintenance, use compatible specified equipment, investigate recurring symptoms and document corrective-action verification. Follow the approved programme for the particular airport and equipment.

Q9Does replacing a failed light solve the problem?

It may resolve a confirmed fixture defect, but it may not address an underlying connection, transformer, cable, installation or system problem. The failure cause and required post-maintenance verification should be documented.

Q10Why runway lights are not working: what are the common causes?

When runway lights are not working, common causes include a tripped or faulted CCR, an open circuit, a failed transformer or connector, water ingress, power loss and control problems. Work through the fault methodically from the supply and CCR to the circuit and the individual light.

Q11What causes AGL transformer failure?

AGL transformer failure can be caused by water ingress, insulation breakdown, overheating, mechanical damage, poor connections and surge damage. An isolating transformer fault can lead to an open circuit or an earth fault.

Q12How to document an AGL fault?

To document an AGL fault, record the date, location, circuit, symptoms, readings, suspected cause, action taken, parts used and who verified the repair. Add photographs and refer to the fault report template on this page.

Q13What are airfield lighting failure causes, airfield ground lighting failure causes and airport lighting failure causes?

The main airfield lighting failure causes are electrical (open circuits, insulation faults), environmental (water, heat, corrosion), mechanical (impact, vibration, jet blast), control-related and maintenance-related. Airfield ground lighting failure causes and airport lighting failure causes are the same in principle.

Q14What are runway lighting failure causes and runway edge lights failure causes?

Runway lighting failure causes include circuit faults, damaged fixtures, water ingress and CCR faults. Runway edge lights failure causes typically include lamp or LED failure, broken lenses, connector problems and transformer faults.

Q15What are runway centreline lights failure causes and threshold lights failure causes?

Runway centreline lights failure causes are often mechanical damage from aircraft wheels and water ingress in inset fixtures. Threshold lights failure causes include similar damage, plus aiming and cabling issues.

Q16What are taxiway lighting failure causes and approach lighting failure causes?

Taxiway lighting failure causes include damage from vehicles, water ingress, and cable and connector faults. Approach lighting failure causes include lightning, structural damage, cable faults and failures in remote elevated fixtures.

Q17What are AGL series circuit failure causes?

AGL series circuit failure causes include open circuits from a failed transformer or connector, insulation or earth faults, cable damage and incorrect loading. Series circuits are sensitive to any break in the loop.

Q18What are constant current regulator failure causes?

Constant current regulator failure causes include component failure, overheating, overload, supply problems, surges and control faults. Alarms and CCR trip indications help narrow down the cause.

Q19What are airfield lighting isolation transformer failure causes?

Airfield lighting isolation transformer failure causes include moisture, insulation damage, overheating, connector failure and mechanical stress.

Q20What are airfield lighting cable failure causes?

Airfield lighting cable failure causes include mechanical damage during excavation, insulation breakdown, water ingress at joints, rodent or ground movement damage and age.

Q21What are airfield lighting control system failure causes and ALCMS failure causes?

Airfield lighting control system failure causes include communication faults, power supply failures, software or configuration errors and hardware faults. ALCMS failure causes are similar and may also include network and interface problems.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Find the Pattern Before Replacing Parts
Diagnosis

Check whether failures cluster by circuit, location, age or weather. A pattern points to a shared cause such as a cable fault, a drainage problem or a batch issue, not a run of unrelated bad luck.

Water Is the Most Repeated Root Cause
Water Ingress

Poor seals, blocked drainage and damaged connectors let moisture into fixtures, transformers and bases. Fix the entry path as well as the failed component, or the fault will return.

Intermittent Faults Usually Mean a Connection Problem
Connections

Flickering or a light that works on some days is more often a marginal joint, corroded contact or movement in the base than a failed lamp or LED.

A Replaced Transformer Can Hide a Cable Fault
Series Circuit

If a secondary fault is repeated after replacement, test the cable and connector before accepting the transformer as the cause. Measure rather than swap by habit.

Keep a Fault Log That Others Can Learn From
Records

Record symptom, location, cause found and fix applied. After a few months the log shows which parts and areas deserve design changes or more frequent inspection.

Confirm Safe Isolation Before Touching the Circuit
Safety

Series circuits can carry hazardous voltage even when lights look off. Follow the lock-out and test procedure for every fault visit, however minor the job appears.
