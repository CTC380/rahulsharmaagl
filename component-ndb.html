Non‑Directional Beacon (NDB)

A ground‑based radio transmitter that provides bearing information to aircraft equipped with Automatic Direction Finder (ADF) receivers. NDBs are simple, reliable, and cost‑effective navigation aids used for en‑route navigation, instrument approaches, and position fixing, particularly in areas where VOR or GPS coverage is limited.

Overview

What Is It?

The Non‑Directional Beacon (NDB) is a ground‑based radio transmitter that radiates a vertically polarised signal in all directions (omnidirectional). The signal is in the low‑frequency or medium‑frequency bands (190 kHz to 1750 kHz) and is amplitude modulated with a Morse‑code identifier. Aircraft equipped with an Automatic Direction Finder (ADF) receiver can determine the bearing to the NDB by comparing the received signal with a directional antenna pattern. The bearing is displayed as a relative bearing or as a magnetic bearing to the station. NDBs are widely used as stand‑alone navigation aids, as part of non‑precision approaches (NDB approaches), and as en‑route fixes in areas with limited VOR coverage.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The NDB's enduring purpose is to provide the simplest possible form of radio bearing guidance: a signal that radiates equally in all directions, requiring only a basic ADF receiver on the aircraft to resolve a bearing to the station. That simplicity is precisely what has kept it in service in areas with limited VOR coverage or as a low-cost non-precision approach aid, since the ground infrastructure and aircraft equipment needed to use an NDB are far less demanding than for more accurate systems. Its low- and medium-frequency signal also travels differently than VHF-based aids, following the curvature of the earth and penetrating some terrain and weather conditions that would block a VOR or ILS signal, which gives it a role distinct from precision navigation aids rather than a diminished version of the same function.

Application

Where Is It Used?

NDBs are versatile and are used across various operational contexts:

  • En‑route Navigation: NDBs are used to define airways and waypoints, particularly in mountainous or remote regions where VOR coverage is sparse. They are also used as holding fixes.
  • Instrument Approaches: NDBs are the basis for non‑precision approaches (NDB approaches) that provide lateral guidance to runways without ILS. They are often combined with DME for distance information.
  • Terminal Area: NDBs are used for departure and arrival procedures, providing bearing information for aircraft transitioning to/from the en‑route structure.
  • Military Operations: NDBs are used for tactical navigation and training.
  • Offshore and Remote Locations: NDBs are particularly useful in areas where GPS or VOR signals are unreliable or unavailable, such as over oceans or in polar regions.
Placement

Installation & Location

An NDB is sited either on the airfield (often collocated with the outer marker as a "locator" beacon) or well off-site at a location chosen for its role in the airway or approach structure, since — unlike a localizer — its bearing accuracy does not depend on the runway alignment. The vertical antenna and its ground plane or counterpoise require a level, unobstructed site with good ground conductivity, clear of nearby metal structures, fencing, or power lines that would distort the radiated pattern. The transmitter, its standby power supply, and monitoring equipment are housed in a small hut at the base of the antenna, with a landline or radio link back to the ATC facility for status and remote control, and the site is fenced to keep the counterpoise and guy wires clear of vehicles and livestock.

Function

How Does It Work?

The NDB system operates on a simple but effective principle:

  • Signal Transmission: The NDB transmitter generates a carrier signal at a specific frequency (between 190 kHz and 1750 kHz) and amplitude modulates it with a Morse‑code identifier (a two‑ or three‑letter code). The signal is radiated omnidirectionally using a vertical antenna.
  • Aircraft Reception: The aircraft's ADF receiver uses a directional antenna (loop antenna) and a sense antenna to determine the direction of the incoming signal. By rotating the antenna or using electronic phase comparison, the ADF finds the direction of minimum signal (the null) to determine the bearing to the station.
  • Bearing Display: The ADF system provides two types of bearings: relative bearing (the angle between the aircraft's heading and the station) and magnetic bearing (the magnetic bearing to or from the station). The pilot uses this information to navigate to or from the NDB.
  • Identification: The station transmits its Morse‑code identifier at regular intervals, allowing the pilot to confirm the correct station selection.
  • Coverage: NDB coverage varies with transmitter power, frequency, and environmental conditions. Typical coverage ranges from 25 nautical miles (low power) to over 200 nautical miles (high power).
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

NDB facilities run a self-protection loop similar to other airfield radio aids: an integral monitor continuously samples the radiated carrier and identification tone, checking transmitted power, modulation depth and frequency against tolerance. Dual-transmitter installations hold a standby unit ready, and the monitor logic executes an automatic changeover if the online transmitter drifts out of tolerance or fails outright; if the standby also fails to bring the signal within tolerance, the facility shuts down rather than radiate a degraded or unidentified signal. Status, changeover events and alarms are reported to the remote control and monitoring system for ATC and maintenance awareness — and because NDB carries no integrity broadcast of its own, this internal monitor is the only automated check on signal validity.

Construction

Physical Construction

The transmitter is housed in an equipment shelter or rack cabinet at the station site, built around dual-redundant, hot-standby transmitter modules so the beacon stays on air through a hot changeover if the active unit faults. The antenna is a vertical monopole — a mast or tower radiator, often paired with a buried counterpoise or ground-radial system beneath it to improve ground-wave propagation — sited on cleared, low-lying ground away from tall structures or terrain that could distort the radiated pattern. Feed cabling runs from the shelter to the antenna base through a matching/tuning unit that couples the transmitter efficiently into the antenna's impedance, and the whole installation is bonded to a dedicated earthing and lightning-protection system given the mast's height and exposure. Power comes from the mains supply backed by an uninterruptible supply and standby generator, with continuous self-monitoring of frequency, modulation, and identifier keying that raises an alarm on any parameter drifting out of tolerance.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Site Interference and Multipath

NDB signals are susceptible to interference from terrain, buildings, and other radio transmitters. Reflections can cause bearing errors (scalloping). Proper site selection and antenna tuning are critical.

Atmospheric Noise and Lightning

LF/MF bands are affected by atmospheric noise (static) and lightning, which can degrade signal quality and reduce effective range. Receiver filtering and notch filtering can mitigate this.

Antenna System Degradation

Corrosion, grounding issues, or physical damage to the antenna can reduce efficiency and range. Regular inspection of the antenna, counterpoise, and grounding system is essential.

Modulation and Identifier Drift

The amplitude modulation depth and the accuracy of the Morse‑code identifier can drift with component ageing. Periodic calibration and flight inspection are required to maintain compliance.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Bearing Errors or Scalloping Reported
Likely Cause
Signal reflections from terrain, buildings, or other transmitters near the site are causing multipath interference.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the flight inspection report for bearing error patterns; survey the site for new structures or terrain changes since installation.
Corrective Action
Retune the antenna system, address the reflecting obstruction where practical, and confirm correction with a follow-up flight inspection.
Reduced Effective Range
Likely Cause
Atmospheric noise or lightning activity is degrading signal quality on the LF/MF band, or the antenna system has degraded.
Diagnostic Steps
Check receiver filtering/notch settings against current noise conditions; inspect the antenna, counterpoise, and grounding for corrosion or damage.
Corrective Action
Adjust receiver filtering, and repair or replace degraded antenna or grounding components before re-verifying range.
Weak or Absent Identifier
Likely Cause
Modulation depth or the Morse-code identifier accuracy has drifted from component ageing.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure modulation depth and identifier timing against specification; review calibration history for drift.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the transmitter's modulation and identifier keying, then confirm compliance with a flight inspection.
Antenna System Efficiency Has Dropped
Likely Cause
Corrosion, a grounding fault, or physical damage to the antenna or counterpoise.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the antenna, counterpoise wires, and grounding system for visible damage or corrosion; measure ground system resistance.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged antenna or counterpoise elements, restore grounding integrity, and re-verify output power and range.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Multipath / Bearing Error Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Signal reflections from terrain, new structures, or other transmitters near the site introduce multipath interference into the radiated signal.
Effect
Aircraft receive bearing errors or scalloping, which can go undetected between scheduled flight inspections.
Detection Method
Flight inspection report review for bearing error patterns, and periodic site surveys for new structures or terrain changes.
Antenna / Grounding System Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion, a grounding fault, or physical damage to the antenna, counterpoise, or ground system accumulating over time outdoors.
Effect
Antenna system efficiency drops, reducing effective range and, in severe cases, threatening full signal loss.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the antenna and counterpoise for damage or corrosion, and periodic ground system resistance measurement.
Transmitter Modulation / Identifier Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Component ageing in the transmitter causing modulation depth or Morse-code identifier keying accuracy to drift from specification.
Effect
Weak or absent identifier reaching aircraft, reducing the NDB's usability for identification even if bearing data is otherwise usable.
Detection Method
Modulation depth and identifier timing measurement against specification, and review of calibration history for a drift trend.
Atmospheric Noise Susceptibility
Low Severity
Root Cause
Increased atmospheric noise or lightning activity on the LF/MF band degrading signal quality independent of equipment condition.
Effect
Effective range reduces during high-noise periods, which can be mistaken for equipment degradation.
Detection Method
Receiver filtering/notch setting review against current noise conditions, distinguishing atmospheric effects from equipment faults.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

RF Exposure Near the Antenna
RF Safety

Avoid close proximity to the antenna element while the transmitter is energised, and follow the manufacturer's minimum safe distance for RF exposure during inspection or antenna work.

Transmitter Power Isolation Before Antenna Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the transmitter before working on the antenna, feedline, or grounding system, and verify zero energy before touching any antenna connection.

NOTAM Before Taking the NDB Off-Air
Operational

Issue a NOTAM and coordinate with ATC before taking the NDB out of service for maintenance, since aircraft may be relying on it for a non-precision approach or en-route navigation.

Lightning/Static Grounding Verification
Electrical Safety

Check the station's lightning protection and grounding system for continuity before and after any storm activity, since the antenna and mast present an elevated strike risk.

Working at Height on the Mast
Personnel Safety

Use fall protection and a permit-to-work procedure when climbing the antenna mast or tower, and confirm the transmitter is isolated before any work above ground level.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Ground System Condition Drives Range More Than Power
Design

A degraded counterpoise or ground radial system quietly erodes effective radiated field strength long before the transmitter itself shows any fault indication. Where coverage complaints appear despite the transmitter reading nominal power, check ground system continuity before assuming an antenna or transmitter problem.

Seasonal Ground Conductivity Shifts Are Real
Reliability

Frozen or very dry ground raises effective ground resistance and can shift coverage or modulation readings between seasons even with no equipment change. Track field strength or monitor readings against season, not just against a single commissioning baseline.

Identification Tone Faults Often Precede RF Faults
Troubleshooting

A degrading modulator or keying circuit tends to show up first as intermittent or distorted identification tone, well before the carrier itself is affected. Treat ident quality complaints as an early diagnostic signal rather than a cosmetic nuisance.

Lightning Damage Can Be Cumulative, Not Just Catastrophic
Durability

Near-miss lightning events that don't take the station off-air can still progressively degrade surge protection components and antenna coupling networks. Include component-level inspection of lightning protection after any nearby strike, not only after an outage.

Dual-Equipment Changeover Logic Needs Real Testing
Compliance

A standby transmitter that has never actually been forced into service through a simulated fault can fail to change over correctly when it's needed for real. Periodically exercise the automatic changeover function rather than relying on it having "always worked" since commissioning.

Mast Guy-Wire Tension Affects More Than Structural Safety
Mechanical

Loose or unevenly tensioned guy wires on a mast antenna can subtly change the antenna's electrical characteristics as well as its mechanical stability. Include guy tension in the same inspection cycle as electrical performance checks rather than treating them as unrelated maintenance items.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about non-directional beacons (NDB): how they work, frequency, range, siting, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an NDB?

A non-directional beacon is a ground-based radio transmitter that radiates a vertically polarised signal equally in all directions in the LF/MF bands, from 190 kHz to 1750 kHz. The signal is amplitude modulated with a Morse-code identifier.

Q2How does an aircraft use an NDB?

An aircraft with an Automatic Direction Finder (ADF) receiver uses a loop and sense antenna to find the direction of the incoming signal, which is shown as a relative or magnetic bearing to the station. Only a basic ADF receiver is needed.

Q3What is an NDB used for?

En-route navigation and airways in remote or mountainous areas where VOR coverage is sparse, NDB non-precision approaches (often combined with DME), holding fixes, and terminal departure and arrival procedures. Collocated with an outer marker it is called a locator beacon.

Q4How far can an NDB be received?

Typically 25 NM to more than 200 NM depending on transmitter power (10 W to 2,000 W) and ground-wave propagation. Bearing accuracy is about ±2° to ±5° depending on the receiver and conditions.

Q5How does an NDB identify itself?

With a two- or three-letter Morse code transmitted at regular intervals, which pilots must check before relying on the beacon. Modulation depth and identifier are calibrated periodically.

Q6Where should an NDB be sited?

Either on the airfield or well off-site, chosen for its role in the airway or approach, since its bearing accuracy does not depend on runway alignment. The vertical antenna and ground plane or counterpoise need a level, unobstructed site with good ground conductivity, clear of metal structures, fencing and power lines.

Q7What power and redundancy does an NDB have?

Mains power with UPS and generator backup. A dual redundant (hot standby) transmitter is typical for critical installations, with continuous self-monitoring that alarms on power or transmitter failure. The antenna and shelter are bonded to a dedicated earth and lightning protection system.

Q8Which standards apply to NDBs?

The main references are ICAO Annex 10 Vol. I (NDB frequency, modulation and performance, §3.4), EUROCAE ED-51 and RTCA DO-179 for airborne ADF equipment. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q9How often should an NDB be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for transmitter power output checks, semi-annual for modulation and identifier calibration, and annual for antenna and counterpoise inspection, grounding system checks, flight inspection and a site survey for new obstructions. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why are there bearing errors or scalloping?

Signal reflections from terrain, buildings or other transmitters near the site cause multipath interference. Review the flight inspection report, survey the site for new structures, retune the antenna system, address the reflecting obstruction where practical and confirm with a follow-up flight inspection.

Q11Why has the effective range of my NDB dropped?

Atmospheric noise or lightning activity on the LF/MF band, or a degraded antenna system. Check receiver filtering against current noise conditions, and inspect the antenna, counterpoise and grounding for corrosion or damage.
