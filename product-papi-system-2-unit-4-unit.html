PAPI System (2-Unit / 4-Unit)

What PAPI Does

Precision AGL Solutions provides Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) systems for runway approaches requiring visual vertical guidance. PAPI presents a combination of red and white indications that changes with the aircraft's position relative to the approach slope, showing whether the pilot is above, on, or below the intended path.

FAA guidance describes PAPI as a row of two or four focused light units, normally installed on the left side of the runway. ICAO Annex 14 likewise places the wing bar on the left side unless that is physically impracticable.

More Than the Light Units

Performance depends on runway reference data, siting, elevation, approach angle, unit spacing, azimuth alignment, aircraft eye-to-wheel characteristics, threshold crossing height, obstruction clearance and verification procedures.

We therefore support PAPI projects from configuration review through installation, alignment and final performance verification.

Important Configuration Terminology

Specify by Number of Light Units, Not “Bars”

FAA designationConfigurationDescription
L-8804 light unitsStandard four-unit PAPI arrangement
L-8812 light unitsTwo-unit PAPI arrangement

The term 3-bar is more commonly used for three-bar VASI systems. FAA aeronautical information distinguishes two- and three-bar VASI from PAPI, which uses two or four light units. Where a brief says “2-bar / 3-bar PAPI”, confirm the intended arrangement against the national aerodrome standard, project specification and authority-approved configuration before equipment selection.

Overview of Indications

Red / White Optical Sectors

A PAPI is a runway-side visual aid giving vertical slope information through sharply defined red/white sectors. The pilot interprets the relative number of red and white indications to judge position against the approach path.

Aircraft positionTypical indication (four-unit)
Well above approach slopeAll units white
Slightly above approach slopeThree white, one red
On or close to approach slopeTwo white, two red
Slightly below approach slopeOne white, three red
Well below approach slopeAll units red

Four-Unit PAPI — Pilot's View

A two-unit PAPI (APAPI, FAA L-881) gives three sectors instead of five. On or close to the slope the unit nearer the runway shows red and the farther unit white; above the slope both show white; below it both show red. It must still be located and aimed to provide the required guidance for the approved runway application.

PAPI Installation Engineering

Small Errors Change the Apparent Slope

Small errors in elevation, angular setting or physical position can alter the apparent approach slope and reduce the consistency of the visual indication.

Siting

The reference position comes from threshold geometry, desired slope, threshold crossing height and aircraft characteristics.

Where ILS, GLS or LPV exists, FAA AC 150/5340-30J places the PAPI runway reference point relative to the glide-path touchdown point, so the visual and electronic paths are coordinated. Without one, FAA describes a method using threshold crossing height and the angle of the lowest on-course signal to find the distance from the threshold.

The FAA equipment standard (AC 150/5345-28H) is written for light units spaced 20–30 ft (6–10 m) apart, and ICAO Annex 14 requires equal spacing. Confirm the exact spacing and the distance from the runway edge in the applicable standard. FAA Engineering Brief #95 covers additional siting and survey considerations.

Obstacle Protection Surface

ICAO Annex 14 requires an obstacle protection surface for a PAPI or APAPI. No object may penetrate it unless it is shielded by an existing object or the authority judges it will not adversely affect safety. Survey the approach for obstacles before the PAPI position and angle are fixed, and keep the check current during the life of the installation.

Approach Angle

The standard PAPI glideslope referenced by FAA guidance is 3°. For certain non-jet runways, FAA permits considering a higher angle for obstacle clearance, subject to design requirements.

Do not treat the angle as a generic product setting; establish it from the approved runway design and aircraft population.

ICAO Annex 14 also ties the setting to a minimum eye height over the threshold for the aircraft eye-to-wheel height group using the runway, so confirm the group and the resulting threshold crossing height before fixing the angle.

Transverse Alignment

  • Common elevation reference and relative unit height
  • Transverse slope and unit spacing
  • Azimuth alignment
  • Perpendicular relationship to runway centreline
  • Stability of each mounting point
  • Correct unit identification and sequence
  • Freedom from obstruction by pavement, vegetation, equipment or structures

ICAO-aligned guidance stresses a consistent horizontal plane and an installation as low as practicable, with tolerances set by the applicable standard.

Setting Angles

For a four-unit PAPI the setting angles are normally the approach angle θ −30′, θ −10′, θ +10′ and θ +30′. A 3° path therefore gives 2°30′, 2°50′, 3°10′ and 3°30′, which is why a 10-minute graduation is the minimum useful resolution. Confirm the settings, including those for a two-unit system, against the applicable Annex 14 or FAA edition.

Tools & Coordination

A clinometer, alignment telescope and suitable PAPI alignment/check equipment can be used, depending on project methodology. Under FAA AC 150/5345-28H the PAPI system is furnished with its own aiming tool, which must read the beam-centre angle from 2° to 8° in 10-minute graduations with ±3-minute repeatability. A telescope or check stick complements that tool rather than replacing it.

Where the PAPI is associated with ILS, GLS or LPV, check the visual and non-visual references together, not independently. FAA AC 150/5340-30J specifically addresses PAPI siting against these glide paths.

Why the Transition Matters

PAPI is fundamentally angular guidance. A fixture can produce substantial output and still perform poorly if the transition sector, aiming angle or unit alignment is wrong.

FAA pilot guidance states PAPI normally provides useful vertical guidance to about ±10° of the extended runway centreline, though local conditions may reduce the effective area.

FAA guidance notes basic PAPI systems may operate independently through a photoelectric device for continuous operation, while others use remote control. FAA equipment is also classed by operating temperature (Class I: −35 °C to +55 °C; Class II: −55 °C to +55 °C). Take exact supply characteristics from the selected product's approved technical data.

Excessive-Tilt Protection

The FAA standard requires the system to de-energise if a light unit is displaced by a fraction of a degree from its preset aiming angle, with a 10–30 second delay to avoid nuisance trips, and the sensing must fail safe. A tripped PAPI is therefore a prompt to re-verify alignment, not just to reset the supply.

FAQ

PAPI System — Frequently Asked Questions

What does a PAPI system do?

A Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) gives pilots an immediate visual indication of whether they are above, on, or below the approach path, using sharply defined red and white sectors. FAA guidance describes it as a row of two or four focused light units, normally installed on the left side of the runway; ICAO Annex 14 likewise places the wing bar on the left unless that is physically impracticable.

What is the difference between FAA L-880 and L-881 PAPI?

L-880 is the standard four-unit PAPI arrangement and L-881 is the two-unit arrangement (APAPI). PAPI should be specified by the number of light units, not by bars: the term 3-bar is associated with three-bar VASI. If a brief says 2-bar or 3-bar PAPI, confirm the intended arrangement against the national aerodrome standard and project specification before equipment selection.

What do the PAPI red and white indications mean?

On a four-unit PAPI, all units white means well above the slope, three white and one red slightly above, two white and two red on or close to the slope, one white and three red slightly below, and all units red well below. A two-unit PAPI gives three sectors instead of five: both units white above the slope, both red below it, and on or close to the slope the unit nearer the runway shows red and the farther unit white.

Where is a PAPI installed and how are the units spaced?

A PAPI is normally on the left side of the runway. The FAA equipment standard (AC 150/5345-28H) is written for light units spaced 20 to 30 ft (6 to 10 m) apart, and ICAO Annex 14 requires equal spacing. Confirm the exact spacing and the distance from the runway edge in the applicable standard; FAA Engineering Brief #95 covers additional siting and survey considerations.

What approach angle and unit setting angles does a PAPI use?

The standard PAPI glideslope referenced by FAA guidance is 3 degrees, and for certain non-jet runways FAA permits considering a higher angle for obstacle clearance, subject to design requirements. For a four-unit PAPI the setting angles are normally the approach angle minus 30 minutes, minus 10 minutes, plus 10 minutes and plus 30 minutes, so a 3 degree path gives 2°30′, 2°50′, 3°10′ and 3°30′. ICAO Annex 14 also ties the setting to a minimum eye height over the threshold for the aircraft eye-to-wheel height group, so establish the angle from the approved runway design and not as a generic product setting.

Why is an obstacle survey needed before fixing the PAPI position?

ICAO Annex 14 requires an obstacle protection surface for a PAPI or APAPI. No object may penetrate it unless it is shielded by an existing object or the authority judges it will not adversely affect safety. Survey the approach for obstacles before the position and angle are fixed, and keep the check current during the life of the installation.

How is a PAPI powered and controlled?

FAA Style A equipment is voltage-powered with a power and control unit and runs from a utility voltage supply; Style B is current-powered and runs on a series lighting circuit (2.8 to 6.6 A) with isolation transformers. Style A has a day mode and two night settings at about 5% and 20% of day intensity. FAA equipment is also classed by operating temperature: Class I from -35 °C to +55 °C and Class II from -55 °C to +55 °C. Take exact supply characteristics from the selected product's approved technical data.

What is excessive-tilt protection on a PAPI?

The FAA standard requires the system to de-energise if a light unit is displaced by a fraction of a degree from its preset aiming angle, with a 10 to 30 second delay to avoid nuisance trips, and the sensing must fail safe. A tripped PAPI is therefore a prompt to re-verify alignment, not just to reset the supply.

What equipment is used to aim and check a PAPI?

A clinometer, alignment telescope and PAPI alignment or check equipment can be used, depending on project methodology. Under FAA AC 150/5345-28H the PAPI is furnished with its own aiming tool, which must read the beam-centre angle from 2° to 8° in 10-minute graduations with ±3-minute repeatability; a telescope or check stick complements that tool rather than replacing it. Where the PAPI is associated with ILS, GLS or LPV, check the visual and non-visual references together.

When must a PAPI be re-verified?

Commission it with recorded as-installed settings, then consider re-verification after replacing a light unit or optical assembly, mounting repairs or foundation movement, major pavement rehabilitation, cable or electrical modifications, changes to the runway threshold or ILS, GLS or LPV parameters, significant vegetation changes, or reported inconsistent pilot indications. After realignment or unit replacement, the authority may require a flight inspection or a notified unserviceability before the PAPI returns to service.

What do common PAPI fault indications point to?

All units white when a mixed indication is expected points to aircraft position, unit aiming, vertical setting error or optical transition problems; all units red points to an aircraft well below the path or an incorrect vertical reference. Inconsistent indications between units suggest misalignment, differing elevations or mechanical movement, and one unit differing in colour or intensity suggests optical contamination, light-source degradation or an electrical problem. A PAPI that differs from ILS glide-path expectations is an engineering coordination issue, not simply a fixture fault.

What information is needed to quote or review a PAPI project?

Useful information includes the runway layout drawing, threshold coordinates and elevations, the runway longitudinal profile, approach category, aircraft fleet or critical aircraft information, existing ILS, GLS or LPV information, an obstacle survey, existing PAPI coordinates and settings, the electrical single-line diagram, available supply parameters, the control philosophy, applicable national and airport standards, and the commissioning and acceptance procedure.

Engineering Note

PAPI is a geometrically controlled visual guidance system, not simply a row of high-intensity lights. Correct performance depends on the relationship between the light units, runway datum, threshold, approach angle, aircraft geometry and obstacle environment.

For procurement accuracy, specify the actual approved system classification, such as FAA L-880 or L-881 where applicable, rather than relying only on the terms “2-bar” or “3-bar”. FAA standards distinguish PAPI configurations by number of light units, while three-bar terminology is associated with VASI.

Final selection, siting and commissioning should be verified against the applicable aerodrome authority requirements and current approved technical documentation.
