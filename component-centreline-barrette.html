Centreline Barrette

A short row of closely‑spaced lights on the approach centreline, providing precise lateral guidance and distance‑to‑runway cues. Centreline barrettes are a fundamental element of precision approach lighting systems (ALSF‑1/2), helping pilots maintain accurate alignment during low‑visibility approaches.

Overview

What Is It?

A Centreline Barrette is a short row of lights installed on the approach centreline, typically consisting of 3 to 5 lights spaced 1–2 metres apart, forming a “barrette” (short bar). These barrettes are placed at fixed distances from the runway threshold (e.g., 500 ft, 1,000 ft, 1,500 ft, etc.) as part of the approach lighting system. They provide a series of bright, white light points that help pilots maintain precise lateral alignment and judge distance to the runway during final approach.

Centreline barrettes are a standard component of ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 configurations, as well as other precision approach lighting systems. They work in conjunction with crossbar lights, side row bars, and the decision bar to create a comprehensive visual reference for the pilot, particularly in low visibility (CAT I/II/III) operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The centreline barrette exists to give a pilot a series of discrete, evenly spaced fix points along the approach path, rather than a single continuous line, which matters because discrete points at known distances let the pilot judge both lateral drift and remaining distance to the threshold at a glance. If a barrette appears shifted relative to the pilot's expected sightline, that's an immediate, intuitive cue that the aircraft has drifted off centreline, correction that would be much harder to detect from a continuous light line alone. Its purpose sharpens under CAT I/II/III conditions specifically, where visibility may only allow the pilot to see one or two barrettes ahead at a time, making the fixed, known spacing between barrettes a critical distance reference in addition to a lateral one.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Centreline barrettes are deployed on precision approach runways and are used in:

  • CAT I precision approach runways (ALSF‑1) – providing essential centreline guidance for approaches with 200 ft decision height.
  • CAT II and III precision approach runways (ALSF‑2) – critical for low visibility operations with decision heights as low as 100 ft or less.
  • Runways with low visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) – ensuring pilots have continuous centreline cues.
  • Major international airports – where high traffic volumes and adverse weather demand reliable approach guidance.
  • Military airfields – requiring all‑weather precision approach capability.
Placement

Installation & Location

Barrettes are set out on the extended runway centreline, on the approach lighting pier or gantry structure that carries the full ALSF configuration, spaced at 30 m (100 ft) intervals extending from near the threshold out to 731 m (2,400 ft) — or 914 m (3,000 ft) where the approach glide slope is below 2.75°. Where the approach terrain is level, each barrette mounts on its own frangible pole set into an individual pier foundation; where the approach crosses low-lying ground, water, or a road, the piers are raised on a stilted platform or bridge structure to keep the barrette at the correct beam elevation above grade. Cabling runs along the pier line in duct or cable tray back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with each barrette's isolating transformer housed at the base of its pole. Pole height and pier spacing are set out from the ILS/PAPI survey so the barrette row stays true to the extended runway centreline across the full approach length.

Function

How Does It Work?

Centreline barrettes function as a series of visual reference points along the approach path:

  • Configuration: Each barrette is a short row of 3‑5 lights, typically spaced 1–2 m apart, installed perpendicular to the centreline. Multiple barrettes are placed at specific distances from the threshold (e.g., 500 ft, 1,000 ft, 1,500 ft, 2,000 ft, etc.) to provide a regular visual pattern.
  • Lighting: The lights are high‑intensity white, steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by the CCR (5‑step). In some systems, barrettes may flash in sequence to create a “running” effect that guides the pilot toward the runway.
  • Guidance: As the aircraft descends, the pilot sees the barrettes as short horizontal lines. If the aircraft is off‑centreline, the barrettes will appear asymmetrical, indicating the need for lateral correction. The spacing between barrettes also provides distance‑to‑go information, helping the pilot judge the approach progress.
  • Integration: Centreline barrettes are integrated with crossbar lights (perpendicular bars), side row bars, and the decision bar to form a complete visual approach system. They are typically the most numerous lights on the approach lighting structure.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Centreline barrettes are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the approach lighting series circuit, following the same constant-current principle as runway and taxiway lighting. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR or ATC instruction. This command is passed to the approach lighting CCR, which regulates the entire barrette loop to the corresponding fixed current (5-step). Every barrette on that loop, via its own isolating transformer at the base of its pole, draws from the same current and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no way to dim or brighten one barrette independently of the row. Where a system uses sequenced flashing to create a "running" effect toward the threshold, that timing is layered on by a separate approach lighting sequencer/flasher controller and is distinct from the steady-burning current-step control described here. Fault detection remains loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault. Because barrettes are the most numerous fixture on the approach lighting pier line, a single lamp failure has minimal visual effect on the overall pattern, but the fault is still flagged for the affected loop as a whole pending sectionalised testing to locate the specific pole.

Engineering View

Barrette Pattern & Alignment Cue Overview

Every barrette is electrically identical — 3–5 lights, 1–2m apart, on the same series circuit — and the pattern's only job is repeating at a fixed interval so distance-to-go reads consistently. The actual guidance cue comes from the pilot's eye, not the wiring: a barrette centred on the extended centreline reads as symmetric and aligned, while one that's shifted off it signals drift and calls for lateral correction until it re-centres.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each barrette is built as a short crossarm carrying 3 to 5 sealed-beam or LED lamp heads, mounted at a fixed height atop a frangible pole set into its own pier foundation. Individual lamp heads use a die-cast aluminium housing with a toughened-glass or polycarbonate lens, gasket-sealed for an IP65/IP66 rating against rain and windblown debris at the exposed approach-lighting site. The pole itself incorporates a frangible coupling near its base, engineered to shear on vehicle or aircraft impact without bringing down the adjacent piers in the approach lighting structure. Where piers sit on raised platforms over water or low ground, the crossarm and lamp heads are the same construction, only the supporting structure changes to a stilted or bridge-mounted frame. Cabling from each lamp head runs down the pole to an isolating transformer housed at the pole base, then out along the pier line in duct or cable tray, with stainless-steel fasteners and weatherproof junction boxes used throughout to withstand prolonged outdoor exposure.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Pier Foundation Setting
Civil

Set each barrette's pier foundation to design elevation and line along the approach lighting structure, holding tolerance so the crossarm reads level once the pole is erected.

Frangible Pole Erection
Mechanical

Erect the frangible pole and crossarm at the specified height, torquing the frangible coupling near the base to the specified value so it shears on impact without affecting adjacent piers.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay the series circuit duct/cable route along the pier line to design depth and separation from other services, avoiding sharp bends that could stress connectors at final termination.

Isolating Transformer Installation
Electrical

Install and confirm the isolating transformer at each pole base matches the lamp head wattage and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Lamp Head Wiring & Sealing
Electrical

Wire each lamp head on the crossarm and seal the junction box to the fixture's IP65/IP66 rating, keeping all splices weatherproof before energisation.

Barrette Spacing Setout
Mechanical

Set out inter-light spacing within each barrette and barrette-to-barrette intervals against the approach lighting design before final fixing of poles.

Raised Platform / Bridge Structure Check
Civil

Where piers sit over water or low ground, confirm the stilted or bridge-mounted frame is structurally complete and matches design elevation before crossarm and lamp head installation.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record pier positions, cable routing, transformer ratings, and barrette spacing as-built before the approach lighting system is energised.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test each barrette's isolating transformers and the series loop before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity end to end across all barrettes and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each light's L-823 connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm every light in each barrette responds correctly, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Barrette Spacing & Alignment Verification
Final Acceptance

Survey inter-light spacing within each barrette and barrette-to-barrette intervals against the approach lighting design, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Flashing Sequence & Synchronisation Test
Final Acceptance

Where a "running" flash effect is used, verify sequencing and timing across barrettes so the visual cue reads correctly from the cockpit.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity, colour, and unidirectional beam spread with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances prior to operational handover.

Mounting & Frangibility Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm frangible coupling integrity on elevated units, or lens flushness and drainage on flush-mounted units, meet as-built tolerances before final backfill.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, spacing, and photometric readings alongside as-built barrette positions in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Precise Spacing and Alignment

Centreline barrettes must be installed with exact spacing both within the barrette and between successive barrettes. Any misalignment or deviation in spacing can create confusing visual cues for pilots. Regular surveys using theodolites or laser alignment tools are essential.

Lens Contamination and Light Output

Barrette lights are exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits, and de‑icing fluids. Contamination can reduce light output and affect the clarity of the visual pattern. Routine cleaning and photometric checks are critical, especially before low‑visibility seasons.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated barrette lights are subject to wind loading and thermal cycling, which can fatigue the frangible couplings. Regular inspection and replacement per manufacturer schedules ensure the lights remain safe and properly positioned.

Flashing Synchronisation Failure

In systems where barrettes flash in sequence, timing errors can occur due to control circuit issues. This can disrupt the “running” effect and confuse pilots. Periodic testing of the flashing sequence and synchronization is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

One or Two Lamps in a Barrette Dark, Rest Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module/lamp within the barrette, or an open isolating transformer feeding that fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Barrette or Row Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop (severed cable, corroded L-823 splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point) affecting a full barrette or a section of the row.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked lenses, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Reduced or Uneven Light Output Across the Barrette
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from jet blast, rubber deposits, or de-icing fluid on one or more lamps, or an aging LED array in part of the barrette.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check across all lamps in the barrette against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean lenses; compare individual lamp output.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace affected lenses, replace a degraded LED module, and re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Flashing Sequence Out of Sync (Where Fitted)
Likely Cause
Timing drift or fault in the sequencer/flasher controller, or a signal-cable fault between the controller and one barrette, disrupting the "running" effect.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the flash pattern against the specified timing; check controller diagnostics and calibration; test signal continuity to the affected barrette.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the sequencer/flasher controller, and repair or replace the faulted signal cable.
Barrette Misaligned or Leaning (Elevated Mounting)
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling on an elevated barrette pole has fatigued from wind loading and thermal cycling, or the pavement/subgrade around a flush-mounted barrette has settled.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically check the frangible coupling for cracking or play; survey the barrette's spacing and aim against the design pattern using a theodolite or laser tool.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling on schedule if fatigued, re-level and re-aim the barrette, and address any subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Module / Lamp Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised LED board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
One or two lamps within a barrette go dark or drop below minimum intensity while the rest of the pier line continues to operate normally; because a barrette has several lamps, the overall pattern usually stays visible.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken a single lamp or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down an entire barrette or section of the approach lighting row.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at the pier base during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run along the pier line.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire barrette loop, disrupting the visual pattern along the approach.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading on the elevated pole, thermal cycling of the coupling material, or corrosion at the coupling threads over the service interval.
Effect
Barrette leans, loosens, or shifts out of its designed spacing pattern, distorting the alignment cue the pattern is meant to provide.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic barrette spacing/alignment survey against the design pattern.
Lens Contamination / Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Jet blast erosion, rubber and de-icing fluid deposits, and prolonged UV exposure building up on the outer lens surface.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output across the barrette that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds, especially before low-visibility seasons.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of lenses during routine cleaning.
Sequencer / Flasher Controller Fault (Where Fitted)
Low Severity
Root Cause
Timing drift in the controller electronics, a firmware fault, or a damaged signal cable between the controller and an individual barrette.
Effect
Loss or distortion of the "running" flash effect toward the threshold, though the barrettes typically remain lit at steady intensity.
Detection Method
Visual observation of the flash pattern against specified timing, and controller diagnostic/calibration checks.
Pier / Pole Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure on stilted approach structures over low-lying ground or water.
Effect
Barrette tilts out of aim or spacing tolerance, and cable connections at the pole base come under mechanical stress.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of the pier foundation and pole alignment during annual maintenance, and a survey of barrette spacing against the design pattern.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Barrette fixtures sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the pole base before climbing or handling connectors.

Elevated Pier Work
Personnel Safety

Barrettes mount on poles atop the approach lighting pier structure; use fall protection appropriate to the pole height, and where the pier is a raised platform or bridge over water or uneven ground, follow the structure's own access and rescue procedure.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible pole mounting with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; poles in the approach must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft on a low approach or go-around.

Approach Surface Coordination
Operational

The pier line sits within the approach protected surface; any work, including crane or lift use, requires ATC coordination, a NOTAM for the affected barrettes, and confirmation that temporary equipment stays within the height limit for that surface.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken multiple barrettes along the pier line.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a barrette dark, misaligned, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; verify pole plumb, beam orientation, and photometric conformance before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Match Lamps Within Each Barrette
Retrofit

Replacing a single failed lamp in a 3-to-5-lamp barrette with a newer LED often leaves a visible colour or intensity mismatch against its neighbours; treat the barrette as a matched set and requalify every lamp in it together rather than swapping one at a time.

Tire Scrub Shortens Lens Life at Centreline
Mechanical

Centreline barrettes sit directly in the aircraft wheel path and see far more abrasion and rubber deposit than edge fixtures a few metres away; put centreline lenses on a shorter inspection and cleaning cycle than the general approach lighting rotation.

Hydrocarbon Exposure at Touchdown Zone
Durability

Fixtures in the touchdown zone are more likely to contact spilled fuel and hydraulic fluid than lights further along the approach; specify lens and gasket materials rated for hydrocarbon contact rather than weather exposure alone.

One Fault Can Dark an Entire Barrette
Electrical

Because the lamps in a barrette typically share one isolation transformer tap on the series loop, a single shorted transformer takes out the whole group at once rather than dimming one lamp; check the transformer first when an entire barrette goes dark.

Resurvey Spacing After Pavement Rehabilitation
Installation

Mill-and-overlay resurfacing can subtly shift barrette position, spacing, or protrusion height relative to the original design pattern; resurvey against the published approach lighting layout after any pavement rehabilitation, not only after fixture replacement.

Trend Loop Insulation, Don't Just Spot-Check
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at a barrette's L-823 connector usually appears first as a slow drift in loop insulation resistance rather than a hard fault; logging megger readings across maintenance cycles catches a failing splice before it takes the loop down.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about centreline barrettes: role in approach lighting, configuration, photometrics, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a centreline barrette?

A centreline barrette is a short row of lights on the approach centreline, typically 3-5 lights spaced 1-2 m apart, forming a short bar. Barrettes are placed at fixed distances from the runway threshold.

Q2Why use barrettes instead of a continuous line of lights?

Discrete, evenly spaced fix points at known distances let the pilot judge both lateral drift and rate of closure, which a single continuous line does not convey as clearly.

Q3Where are centreline barrettes used?

On precision approach runways: CAT I (ALSF-1) for approaches with a 200 ft decision height, and CAT II and III runways (ALSF-2).

Q4How do centreline barrettes work?

Each barrette is a short row of 3-5 lights installed perpendicular to the centreline, and several barrettes are placed at regular longitudinal intervals along the approach, giving a series of visual reference points.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

An LED array or 45/65 W halogen source, white light (steady or flashing) of 10,000-20,000 cd, unidirectional towards the approaching aircraft, barrette width of about 4-8 m, and barrettes at about 30 m longitudinal intervals. Fixtures are elevated on frangible poles or flush-mounted, rated IP67/IP68.

Q6How is a centreline barrette powered and controlled?

From a CCR-fed constant current series circuit at 6.6 A with a 5-step schedule (2.8, 3.4, 4.1, 5.2 and 6.6 A), with an individual 45/65 W isolating transformer per light.

Q7What photometric performance is expected?

A main beam of 10,000-20,000 cd white, a horizontal spread of ±4.5° to ±8°, an intensity tolerance of ±25% at each CCR step in field measurement, and chromaticity within the ICAO Annex 14 white boundary. A maintenance factor of at least 50% of new-fixture intensity applies before cleaning or replacement is due.

Q8Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (fixture specification), FAA AC 150/5340-30 (ALSF-1/ALSF-2 layout and configuration) and IEC TS 61827.

Q9How often should barrettes be maintained?

Quarterly lens cleaning; semi-annual flashing sequence tests where a running effect is used; and annual spacing and alignment surveys, frangible coupling checks, photometric intensity checks and insulation resistance tests.

Q10What if one or two lamps in a barrette are dark?

A failed LED module or lamp within that barrette, or an open isolating transformer feeding that fixture. Confirm series loop continuity through the transformer primary.
