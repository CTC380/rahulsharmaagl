Terminal Block

A modular, insulated electrical connector used to securely terminate and connect wires in AGL control panels, distribution boards, junction boxes, and equipment. Terminal blocks provide a safe, organized, and serviceable interface for multiple circuits, enabling efficient wiring, testing, and maintenance.

Overview

What Is It?

A Terminal Block (also known as a terminal strip or connector block) is a modular electrical component used to connect and organize multiple wires in a single housing. In AGL systems, terminal blocks are essential for creating reliable, serviceable connections between cables, control signals, power supplies, and equipment such as CCRs, PLCs, and monitoring devices. They consist of a conductive metal strip (usually brass or copper alloy) enclosed in an insulating plastic housing, with screw, spring, or push-in clamping mechanisms to secure wire ends. Terminal blocks enable easy circuit identification, disconnection, and testing, which is critical for maintaining airfield lighting systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The terminal block's purpose is to make the countless wire-to-wire connections inside an AGL control panel, junction box, or transformer enclosure both electrically reliable and individually serviceable, rather than relying on twisted or soldered joints that are difficult to inspect, test, or safely disconnect. By giving every wire its own clamped connection point on a common, labelled strip, it lets a technician isolate a single circuit, take a test measurement, or swap a faulty component without disturbing every other connection in the enclosure — a capability that matters directly to how quickly an airfield lighting fault can be found and fixed. This combination of a secure long-term connection and quick, non-destructive access is what makes the terminal block a standard fixture everywhere AGL wiring needs to be organised.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Terminal blocks are ubiquitous in AGL electrical infrastructure. They are found in:

  • CCR Control Panels: Connecting control wires from the CCR to the ALCMS and monitoring systems
  • Power Distribution Panels: Interconnecting bus bars, circuit breakers, and outgoing feeders
  • Junction Boxes: Field termination points for primary and secondary AGL cables
  • Isolating Transformer Enclosures: Connecting primary and secondary cables to transformer terminals
  • ALCMS Cabinets: Terminating sensor, communication, and control cables
  • Remote I/O Panels: Connecting field sensors and actuators to the control system
Placement

Installation & Location

Terminal blocks are DIN-rail mounted or panel-fixed inside whichever enclosure they serve — CCR control cabinets, power distribution panels, junction boxes, isolating transformer enclosures, ALCMS cabinets, and remote I/O panels — rather than at a location of their own. Blocks are grouped in rows or rails matched to the circuit they belong to, with incoming and outgoing wires clamped at adjacent terminals and jumper bars used to bridge commoned points. Clear labelling and circuit identification at each block is standard practice given how many terminations a typical panel contains, and blocks are sized and rated to match the wire gauge and current of the circuit passing through them.

Function

How Does It Work?

A terminal block provides a mechanical and electrical connection point for wires. The stripped wire end is inserted into a clamping mechanism (screw, spring, or push-in) that secures it firmly against a conductive metal bar. The metal bar acts as a common node, allowing multiple wires to be connected together (jumpers can bridge adjacent terminals). The insulating housing prevents short circuits and provides strain relief. Terminal blocks are designed for easy insertion and removal, enabling quick circuit changes, testing, and troubleshooting. They are typically mounted on DIN rails or directly onto panels, and are available in various pitch sizes, current ratings, and connection types to suit different wire gauges and applications.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A terminal block is a purely passive connection point with no control logic of its own — it has no monitoring, no signal processing, and no automated function beyond providing a mechanically and electrically sound clamped joint between conductors. Its place in the wider system architecture is structural: it is the interface point where field cabling from a fixture, sensor, or transformer lands on a controlled circuit, and where jumpering, fusing, or disconnect links can be inserted to isolate a branch for testing or fault-finding without disturbing the rest of the circuit. Because it carries no self-diagnostic capability, any degradation — a loosened clamp, corrosion, or overheating from a poor connection — is only detected through the behaviour of the circuit it is part of, such as a loop fault reported by the CCR, not through any signal the terminal block itself generates.

Construction

Physical Construction

The current-carrying element is a machined brass or copper-alloy strip, tin- or nickel-plated to resist corrosion and maintain low contact resistance over the block's service life, molded into an insulating polyamide, polycarbonate, or ceramic housing sized to the block's voltage and current rating. Screw-clamp, spring-clamp, or push-in connection points accept the specified wire gauge range, with the clamping mechanism designed to maintain consistent contact pressure through vibration and thermal cycling without loosening. Individual blocks snap onto a standard 35 mm DIN rail and interlock side-by-side to build up a terminal strip of the required length, with end stops and partition plates used to organise circuits and prevent adjacent terminals from being bridged accidentally. Where higher protection is required, the assembled strip is enclosed in a sealed IP67 housing; otherwise the open DIN-rail block relies on the surrounding cabinet's IP rating, and the housing material is selected for a UL94 V-0 or V-2 flammability rating appropriate to its installation in an electrical enclosure.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

DIN Rail Mounting
Mechanical

Snap terminal blocks onto the 35 mm DIN rail in the correct sequence, fitting end stops and partition plates to organise circuits and prevent adjacent terminals bridging accidentally.

Circuit Identification & Labelling
Electrical

Label each terminal or terminal group per the circuit schedule before wiring, since a mislabelled strip is difficult to trace once the enclosure is closed up.

Wire Gauge Selection
Electrical

Confirm each conductor's gauge falls within the terminal's rated range before stripping and inserting, since an undersized or oversized fit compromises clamp pressure.

Conductor Termination
Electrical

Strip and insert each conductor fully into the screw-clamp, spring-clamp, or push-in terminal, tightening screw-clamp types to the specified torque.

Jumper & Fuse Link Fitting
Electrical

Fit jumpers and isolating/fuse links only where the design calls for them, since an unintended bridge between adjacent terminals can cross-connect circuits.

Enclosure IP Rating Coordination
Mechanical

Where a sealed IP67 terminal enclosure is used, confirm the housing is fully closed and glanded; otherwise confirm the surrounding cabinet meets its rated IP protection.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record terminal strip layout, circuit identification, and torque values as-built before the circuit is energised.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Connection Torque Verification
Pre-Energisation

Torque every screw-clamp terminal to spec before the circuit is energised, since a loose clamp is a leading cause of early-life heating faults.

Continuity & Polarity Check
Pre-Energisation

Verify each clamped joint carries continuity end to end and that jumpered terminals bridge the correct circuits.

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger between adjacent terminals and from terminal to earth to rule out accidental bridging or damaged insulation before backfill.

Wire Gauge & Terminal Match Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm each conductor's gauge falls within the terminal's rated range, since an undersized or oversized fit compromises clamp pressure.

Jumper & Fuse Link Verification
Final Acceptance

Check that jumpers and any isolating/fuse links are fitted only where the design calls for them, preventing unintended circuit bridging.

Thermal Imaging Scan
Energisation

Scan the terminal strip under load with a thermal camera to catch any hot spot indicating a marginal connection before it fails.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record terminal strip layout, circuit identification, and torque/insulation readings in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Connection Torque Check
Annual

Re-torque terminal screws to spec, since loose connections are a leading cause of localized heating and failure.

Corrosion Inspection
Annual

Check terminals for oxidation or corrosion, particularly in humid, coastal, or de-icing-fluid-exposed environments.

Insulation Condition Check
Annual

Inspect the block body for cracking caused by thermal cycling or prolonged UV exposure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Loose Screw Connections

Vibration and thermal cycling can cause screw terminals to loosen, resulting in increased contact resistance, localised heating, and potential arcing. Regular torque checks (using a calibrated torque screwdriver) and the application of anti-vibration compounds are recommended to maintain connection integrity.

Corrosion and Oxidation

Moisture ingress and environmental contaminants can cause oxidation of the conductor and clamp surfaces, leading to poor connectivity and overheating. Using tin-plated or gold-plated terminals and applying protective coatings can mitigate this. In humid environments, sealed terminal blocks with IP67 rating are preferred.

Insulation Cracking

Over time, the insulating housing can become brittle due to UV exposure, heat, or chemical attack. Cracked insulation can lead to short circuits and tracking. Regular visual inspection and replacement of damaged terminal blocks is essential for airfield safety.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Localised Heating or Arcing at a Terminal
Likely Cause
A loose screw connection from vibration or thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Check contact resistance at the terminal, use a torque screwdriver to verify clamping force, and look for discolouration indicating heat.
Corrective Action
Re-torque the connection to specification, apply anti-vibration compound, and replace the terminal if contact surfaces are damaged.
Poor Connectivity or Overheating in Humid Locations
Likely Cause
Corrosion/oxidation of the conductor or clamp surfaces from moisture ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect terminal and conductor surfaces for oxidation and check the enclosure sealing/IP rating.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace corroded terminals with tin- or gold-plated types, and upgrade to sealed IP67 terminal blocks in humid environments.
Cracked or Brittle Terminal Housing
Likely Cause
Insulation cracking from UV exposure, heat, or chemical attack.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect housings for cracking and check for tracking marks or discolouration.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged terminal blocks, and re-route away from UV/chemical exposure where practical.
Intermittent Circuit Continuity
Likely Cause
A combination of a loose connection and corrosion causing marginal contact under vibration.
Diagnostic Steps
Wiggle-test the connection while monitoring continuity, and inspect for a combination of looseness and surface oxidation.
Corrective Action
Clean contact surfaces, re-terminate with the correct torque, and monitor for recurrence.
Terminal Block Overheating Under Normal Load
Likely Cause
An undersized terminal for the conductor/current rating, or an excessive number of conductors per terminal beyond the rated limit.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the terminal current rating against the actual circuit load and verify the conductor count per terminal against manufacturer limits.
Corrective Action
Replace with a correctly rated terminal block and redistribute conductors within rated limits.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Loose Screw / Clamp Connection
High Severity
Root Cause
Insufficient torque at installation, or thermal cycling gradually relaxing the clamping force on the conductor.
Effect
Intermittent circuit continuity or localised heating and arcing at the terminal as contact resistance rises.
Detection Method
Connection torque verification against the manufacturer's specified value, and a thermal imaging scan under load.
Corrosion and Oxidation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Humid or corrosive environments, dissimilar-metal contact, or a compromised enclosure seal allowing moisture ingress at the terminal.
Effect
Poor connectivity or overheating develops gradually in humid locations as the oxide layer increases contact resistance.
Detection Method
Corrosion inspection at the terminal, and an insulation resistance test to catch moisture-related degradation.
Insulation Cracking / Housing Embrittlement
Low Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure, thermal cycling, or age-related embrittlement of the terminal block's insulating housing.
Effect
Cracked or brittle terminal housing that can compromise creepage/clearance distances or allow moisture ingress.
Detection Method
Insulation condition check and an insulation resistance test as part of scheduled inspection.
Localised Heating / Arcing at a Terminal
High Severity
Root Cause
A loose connection, corrosion, or a wire-gauge mismatch concentrating current through a reduced contact area.
Effect
Progressive thermal damage to the terminal and surrounding wiring, with a risk of arcing under sustained load.
Detection Method
Thermal imaging scan under normal operating load to identify hot-spot terminals before failure.
Wire Gauge / Terminal Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A conductor gauge installed that falls outside the terminal's rated clamping range, either too thin or too thick.
Effect
An unreliable connection that may pass an initial continuity check but degrade under vibration or thermal cycling.
Detection Method
Wire gauge and terminal match verification against the terminal's rated range during installation and audit.
Vibration-Induced Loosening
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Sustained vibration from nearby equipment or vehicle traffic gradually working a properly torqued connection loose over time.
Effect
Terminal block overheating or intermittent continuity under otherwise normal load, without an obvious installation defect.
Detection Method
Periodic connection torque checks at locations known to experience vibration, rather than a one-time installation check alone.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out and verify zero energy on every circuit landed at the terminal block before disconnecting or re-terminating wires; a shared block can carry multiple circuits, so confirm which terminals are actually de-energised rather than assuming the whole block is safe.

Circuit Identification
Operational

Verify wire labelling and circuit identification before disconnecting anything, and restore labels exactly as found; a mislabelled or unlabelled terminal is a common cause of the wrong circuit being isolated during later work.

Correct Torque on Termination
Mechanical

Torque screw-clamp terminals to the manufacturer's specified value; an under-torqued connection can loosen and arc under load, while over-torquing can damage the terminal or conductor.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Check for correct polarity, secure connections, and no stray strands before restoring power, since a loose conductor at a terminal block is a common source of intermittent faults that are hard to trace later.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools rated for the voltage present, and treat adjacent live circuits in the same enclosure as energised even while working on an isolated one.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm all terminals are secure, correctly labelled, and the enclosure is closed before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Loose Terminal Fails Intermittently Before It Fails Completely
Failure Mode

A loosening screw-clamp connection typically produces intermittent, load- or vibration-dependent faults for weeks or months before it fails outright; a circuit reported as "fixed itself" after an inspection where nothing was found is a common sign of a marginal connection worth re-torquing preventively rather than dismissing as a one-off.

Discolouration on a Terminal Is a Leading Indicator
Diagnostics

Browning or heat discolouration on a terminal or the surrounding insulation indicates it has been running hot from resistive heating at a marginal connection, typically well before the connection fails completely; treat any discolouration found during routine inspection as a reason to re-terminate that connection, not just note it for a future visit.

Torque Values Can Need Re-Checking After Thermal Cycling
Mechanical

Repeated thermal expansion and contraction from load cycling can allow a correctly-torqued terminal to loosen slightly over time, particularly with certain conductor and terminal material combinations; where a circuit sees frequent on/off cycling, re-torquing at scheduled intervals catches this drift before it becomes an intermittent fault.

Label Integrity Degrades Faster Than the Hardware
Documentation

Wire labels and terminal identification fade or fall off well before the terminal block itself shows any wear, and a mislabelled terminal in a shared enclosure is a genuine safety risk during future isolation work; treat label legibility as its own maintenance item, checked and refreshed independently of the electrical condition of the block.

Dissimilar Metal Combinations Corrode at the Terminal First
Durability

Where copper conductors land on an aluminium terminal block, or vice versa, without the correct anti-oxidant compound and terminal rating for that combination, galvanic corrosion develops at the junction faster than either material alone would suggest; verify terminal block material compatibility with the conductor type before re-terminating rather than assuming any terminal accepts any wire.

Condensation in a Poorly Vented Enclosure Corrodes From the Inside
Environmental

A terminal block enclosure that seals well against direct water ingress can still accumulate internal condensation from daily temperature cycling if it isn't properly vented or desiccated; corrosion found on terminals in an outwardly intact enclosure often traces to this rather than a failed external seal.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about terminal blocks: function, ratings, connection types, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a terminal block in AGL systems?

A terminal block, also known as a terminal strip or connector block, is a modular insulated connector that terminates and connects multiple wires in one housing. It consists of a conductive brass or copper alloy strip inside an insulating plastic housing, with screw, spring or push-in clamps holding the wire ends.

Q2Why use terminal blocks instead of twisted or soldered joints?

Terminal blocks give every wire its own clamped, labelled connection point, so a technician can isolate a single circuit, take a test measurement or swap a component without disturbing other connections. Twisted or soldered joints are harder to inspect, test or safely disconnect.

Q3Where are terminal blocks used in an AGL installation?

They are used in CCR control panels, power distribution panels, junction boxes (field termination of primary and secondary AGL cables), isolating transformer enclosures, ALCMS cabinets and remote I/O panels.

Q4How does a terminal block work?

The stripped wire end is inserted into a clamping mechanism (screw, spring or push-in) that secures it against a conductive metal bar, which acts as a common node. Adjacent terminals can be bridged with a jumper to distribute one feed further. The insulating housing prevents short circuits and provides strain relief.

Q5What are typical terminal block ratings?

Current rating ranges from 10 A to 1000 A and voltage rating from 300 V to 1000 V AC/DC. Wire range is AWG 26 to AWG 2 (0.14 mm² to 35 mm²), with 2 to 48 terminals per block. Mounting is typically TS35 DIN rail or panel mount, with protection class from IP20 to IP67 and flammability rating UL94 V-0 or V-2.

Q6What connection types are available?

Screw clamp, spring clamp and push-in (quick connect). Pitch options include 5 mm, 7.5 mm, 10 mm and 12 mm, or custom.

Q7Which standards apply to terminal blocks?

The main references are IEC 60947-7-1 (terminal blocks for low-voltage switchgear), UL 1059, CSA C22.2 No. 158 and IEC 60085 (thermal classification of insulating materials). Always verify the current edition and your project's approved requirements.

Q8How often should terminal blocks be inspected?

Typical practice is an annual connection torque check, corrosion inspection and insulation condition check. Loose connections are a leading cause of localised heating and failure. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common terminal block problems?

The common issues are loose screw connections from vibration and thermal cycling (leading to contact resistance, heating and arcing), corrosion and oxidation from moisture ingress or contaminants, and insulation cracking from UV, heat or chemical attack.

Q10What precautions apply when working on terminal blocks?

Lock out and verify zero energy on every circuit landed at the block, verify wire labelling before disconnecting and restore labels exactly as found, and torque terminals to the manufacturer's specified value. Before re-energising, check polarity, secure connections and that there are no stray strands.
