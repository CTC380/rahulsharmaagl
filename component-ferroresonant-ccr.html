Ferroresonant CCR

A robust, transformer‑based constant current regulator that uses ferroresonant (saturated‑core) technology for current regulation. Known for its exceptional reliability, simple construction, and tolerance to harsh electrical environments, ferroresonant CCRs are a proven workhorse in airfield ground lighting systems worldwide.

Overview

What Is It?

A Ferroresonant Constant Current Regulator (Ferroresonant CCR) is a type of AGL power supply that uses the principle of ferroresonance — a nonlinear magnetic circuit phenomenon — to maintain a constant current output. The core of the regulator is a specially designed transformer with a saturable magnetic shunt and a capacitor that forms a resonant circuit. This arrangement inherently regulates the output current by clamping the voltage and maintaining a near‑constant current regardless of variations in the input voltage or the load impedance. Ferroresonant CCRs are known for their extreme robustness, simplicity, and ability to withstand severe overloads and short circuits without damage. They have been the traditional choice for AGL systems for decades and are still widely used in many airfields, particularly where high reliability and minimal electronics are desired.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The ferroresonant CCR exists to achieve constant-current regulation through a purely magnetic and electrical phenomenon rather than active electronic control, which matters because a design with fewer active components has fewer things that can fail, particularly under the kind of severe overload or short-circuit event that can occur on an outdoor series circuit exposed to weather, lightning, and physical damage. Its purpose is best understood as prioritizing robustness and simplicity above all else: by clamping voltage and current through the inherent physics of a saturable magnetic circuit, the regulator continues to function correctly through fault conditions that could damage a more electronically sophisticated design, without relying on a control loop that itself needs to detect and react to the fault. This is precisely why it remained the standard choice for decades and is still selected today at airfields where minimal electronics and maximum resilience to harsh conditions outweigh the efficiency and communication advantages of newer technologies.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Ferroresonant CCRs are used in a wide range of AGL applications, particularly in installations that prioritise reliability and simplicity:

  • Runway Lighting: Edge lights, centreline lights, and threshold lighting for both precision and non‑precision runways.
  • Taxiway Lighting: Edge lights and centreline lighting systems.
  • Approach Lighting: Many older approach lighting systems (SALS, SSALS, MALS) use ferroresonant regulators.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Some obstruction lighting circuits, especially in remote locations.
  • Legacy Systems: Widely used in existing airfields where replacement with modern CCRs is not yet undertaken.
  • Remote and Harsh Environments: Where minimal maintenance and high tolerance to electrical noise are required.
Placement

Installation & Location

Ferroresonant CCRs are floor-mounted on a concrete plinth in the electrical equipment room or shelter, close to the runway or taxiway circuit they feed, and their heavier core-and-coil construction — occasionally oil-filled at higher ratings — calls for a plinth rated for the additional weight compared with electronic CCR types. Incoming mains and the tap-changer control wiring enter from below, while the high-voltage series-circuit output is routed separately to the runway loop. Because the design has no forced cooling on smaller units, clearance is kept around the enclosure for natural convection, and oil-filled units need a bunded plinth and ventilation appropriate to the coolant. The frame is bonded to the room's earthing system, and the manual or motorised tap-changer is left accessible from the front for step adjustment.

Function

How Does It Work?

The ferroresonant CCR uses a transformer with a primary winding, a secondary winding, and a magnetic shunt (a magnetic bypass path) that can be saturated. A capacitor is connected in parallel with the secondary winding to form a resonant tank circuit. When the input voltage is applied, the transformer operates in a region where the magnetic shunt saturates at a certain current level. The capacitor and the transformer's leakage inductance form a resonant circuit that effectively "clamps" the output voltage and current. The result is that the output current remains essentially constant over a wide range of input voltage variations (typically ±15% or more) and load variations (from short circuit to open circuit). The output current can be adjusted by changing taps on the transformer or by using a variac (variable autotransformer) on the input. Brightness steps are achieved by selecting different tap settings, either manually or via motorised tap changers. The ferroresonant design is inherently rugged and free from semiconductor devices, making it highly resistant to transients and lightning.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Regulation happens through the magnetic/capacitive tank itself — there's no control loop or switching electronics to fail.
Construction

Physical Construction

Because the regulating action is entirely magnetic, a Ferroresonant CCR is built around a large, heavy ferroresonant transformer with a saturable shunt and a dedicated resonant capacitor bank, mounted on a substantial steel chassis within a floor-standing, powder-coated cabinet. The transformer and capacitors occupy the bulk of the enclosure's internal volume and account for most of the unit's weight, with the capacitor bank typically arranged in a separate compartment or rack for ease of replacement. A comparatively simple control section — an output current sensor, a tap-changer or step relay assembly for brightness selection, and basic protection relays — is mounted on a sub-panel, with far less electronics than a thyristor or IGBT design. The cabinet relies on natural convection or simple forced-air cooling through louvred panels, given the resonant circuit's inherent tolerance to heat and lack of sensitive switching components. Incoming and outgoing cabling terminates at heavy-duty terminal blocks sized for the transformer's current rating, with earthing bars and surge protection at the input.

Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the ferroresonant transformer windings and series loop before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Verify continuity of the complete series loop and record total loop resistance before the ferroresonant tank is first energised.

Output Current Stability Across Mains Variation

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Capacitor Failure

The resonant capacitor is a critical component that experiences high voltage and current stress. Capacitor failure (often due to dielectric breakdown or aging) causes the regulator to lose regulation and may result in erratic output or complete failure. Regular capacitance and ESR checks are recommended.

Tap Changer Malfunction

Motorised tap changers (if fitted) can fail due to contact wear, motor failure, or control circuit issues. This results in inability to change brightness steps. Regular inspection and contact cleaning are necessary.

Transformer Overheating

Ferroresonant transformers operate with a saturated core, which generates significant heat. Insufficient cooling, blocked ventilation, or overloading can cause overheating and insulation failure. Regular thermal monitoring and cleaning of ventilation paths are essential.

Humming and Vibration

The saturated core and resonant operation can produce audible humming and mechanical vibration. While normal, excessive vibration can loosen connections or cause mechanical fatigue. Regular tightening of fasteners is recommended.

Input Voltage Sensitivity

While ferroresonant regulators tolerate input variations, extreme voltage fluctuations can affect regulation accuracy. In areas with unstable mains, additional input voltage stabilisation may be required.

Poor Power Factor

The ferroresonant design has a low power factor (0.6‑0.8), which can cause upstream power quality issues. Power factor correction may be required, especially for larger installations.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Loss of Regulation / Erratic Output
Likely Cause
The resonant capacitor has degraded from dielectric breakdown or aging under high voltage and current stress.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure capacitance and ESR of the resonant capacitor against manufacturer specification; check output waveform for irregularity.
Corrective Action
Replace the resonant capacitor with a matched replacement, and re-verify regulation accuracy across the full brightness step range.
Unable to Change Brightness Step
Likely Cause
The motorised tap changer (where fitted) has failed due to contact wear, motor failure, or a control circuit fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Command a step change and observe whether the tap changer motor responds; inspect tap contacts for wear or pitting.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace worn tap changer contacts, or replace the motor/control circuit as needed; confirm all steps are reachable afterward.
Overtemperature Shutdown or Insulation Degradation
Likely Cause
Blocked ventilation or overloading has caused the saturated transformer core to overheat beyond its design margin.
Diagnostic Steps
Check ventilation paths for obstruction; verify load current against the unit's rating; monitor core/winding temperature during operation.
Corrective Action
Clear blocked ventilation, correct any overload condition, and allow the unit to cool before re-energising; consider added ventilation if overheating recurs.
Excessive Humming or Vibration Loosening Connections
Likely Cause
Normal saturated-core resonant operation produces audible hum and vibration, which over time can loosen fasteners and terminal connections.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and torque-check all electrical connections and mounting fasteners for looseness; listen for any change from the unit's normal hum.
Corrective Action
Re-torque all connections and fasteners to specification on a regular schedule to prevent vibration-related loosening.
Regulation Accuracy Degraded Under Voltage Swings
Likely Cause
Extreme mains voltage fluctuation has exceeded the ferroresonant design's normal input tolerance window.
Diagnostic Steps
Log input mains voltage over a representative period and compare swings against the CCR's rated input tolerance.
Corrective Action
Install input voltage stabilisation ahead of the CCR if mains instability is confirmed to exceed its tolerance.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Tuning Capacitor Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Dielectric ageing under sustained resonant voltage and current stress, or internal partial-discharge damage accumulating over years of service.
Effect
Loss of regulation or erratic output, since the ferroresonant circuit depends on the tuning capacitance to hold its operating point.
Detection Method
Output waveform and regulation-accuracy check, and periodic capacitance measurement of the tuning bank during major maintenance.
Ferroresonant Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Insulation breakdown from age or moisture ingress, or a winding short driven by sustained saturation-related heating.
Effect
Overtemperature shutdown or progressive insulation degradation, and in severe cases complete loss of output.
Detection Method
Winding resistance and insulation (megger) testing, and thermal imaging of the transformer core and windings during load.
Step Selector Contactor Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical and electrical wear on contactor contacts from repeated brightness-step switching over years of operation.
Effect
Unit becomes unable to change brightness step reliably, sticking on the last commanded level.
Detection Method
Functional step-change test across all levels and physical inspection of contactor contacts during scheduled maintenance.
Loose Connections from Vibration
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The core's characteristic operating hum gradually loosening terminal screws and bus connections if not periodically re-torqued.
Effect
Excessive humming or vibration that progressively worsens, eventually leading to a poor connection and intermittent output.
Detection Method
Audible/vibration inspection during routine visits, and torque-check of terminal connections at scheduled intervals.
Regulation Accuracy Degraded Under Voltage Swings
Low Severity
Root Cause
Incoming supply voltage operating near the edge of the ferroresonant circuit's designed compensation range.
Effect
Output current drifts outside tolerance during utility voltage swings, even though the unit shows no fault indication.
Detection Method
Regulation-accuracy test across the expected input voltage range, and utility supply voltage logging for correlation.
Insulation Degradation from Sustained Overtemperature
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged operation at elevated core temperature from marginal ventilation, accelerating insulation ageing beyond its rated life.
Effect
Reduced insulation margin that increases the risk of a winding fault, and can trigger repeated overtemperature shutdowns.
Detection Method
Periodic insulation resistance testing and internal temperature trend review against the rated operating range.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

High Open-Circuit Series Voltage
Electrical Safety

As with any CCR, the output loop can present several kV open-circuit; de-energise and lock out the unit at its supply, and verify zero energy at the output terminals with a suitably rated meter before touching them.

Resonant Tank Capacitor Charge
Electrical Safety

The ferroresonant tank capacitor can retain significant stored charge after shutdown, independent of the load-side output; allow the manufacturer's specified discharge time or confirm discharge with a rated meter before opening the cabinet.

Transformer Weight and Handling
Personnel Safety

The saturable-shunt transformer core is substantially heavier than an equivalent electronic CCR; use correct lifting equipment and technique, and never attempt to reposition the unit without adequate support.

Hot Surfaces and Continuous Hum/Vibration
Personnel Safety

Allow the transformer and any load resistors to cool before handling; be aware the unit runs with an audible hum and mechanical vibration in normal operation, which is not itself a fault but can mask other abnormal noises during inspection.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Taking a Ferroresonant CCR out of service darkens an entire series circuit; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before isolation.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the series loop before restoring power after any downstream work, since a residual fault can damage the ferroresonant core or trip protection on re-energisation.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm output current is stable at the commanded step and the resonant circuit is not producing abnormal harmonics or heating before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Resonant Tank Tuning Drifts With Component Age, Not Just Damage
Reliability

The saturable shunt and capacitor that give a ferroresonant CCR its inherent stability can drift out of their original tuning as the resonant capacitor ages, even with no visible fault, gradually degrading output current stability well before any alarm triggers. Recheck resonant tank tuning against baseline commissioning values periodically rather than assuming the design's inherent stability makes ongoing tuning checks unnecessary.

Foundation Load Rating Isn't Optional on This Unit
Installation

A ferroresonant transformer's iron core makes this CCR substantially heavier than a solid-state equivalent of the same rating, and a foundation sized by analogy to a lighter thyristor or IGBT unit can be genuinely under-specified. Confirm cabinet siting and foundation load rating against this unit's actual mass, not a generic CCR foundation spec.

Tap-Changer Setting Errors Are an Easy Miss on Retrofit
Commissioning

Reinstalling or replacing a ferroresonant CCR without re-verifying the tap-changer setting against the actual loop's fixture count and cable run leaves the output current correct in theory but wrong for the loop it's actually feeding. Re-confirm tap setting against the as-built loop configuration at every reinstallation, not just at first commissioning.

Its Surge Tolerance Is a Feature, Not an Excuse to Skip Protection
Electrical

Ferroresonant CCRs are known for inherent robustness against input voltage variation, but that tolerance is not a substitute for proper earthing and surge protection bonding, since a large enough transient can still damage windings or insulation regardless of the unit's inherent stability. Maintain full surge protection and bonding practice on this fixture type rather than relaxing it on the strength of the technology's reputation for ruggedness.

Power Factor Degrades Before Output Current Stability Does
Diagnostics

A worsening power factor reading on a ferroresonant unit is often the first measurable sign of resonant tank aging, appearing before the output current itself shows any drift a technician would notice at the loop. Track power factor as a leading indicator alongside output current rather than treating it as a secondary metric.

Thermal Rise Testing Matters More at High Ambient Sites
Testing

The iron losses inherent to a ferroresonant design generate more standing heat than a solid-state CCR of the same rating, which is manageable at design ambient but can push the unit closer to its thermal limit at hot-climate airfields. Weight thermal rise testing and ventilation clearance checks more heavily at sites running consistently above the unit's design ambient temperature.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about ferroresonant CCRs: how they regulate current, brightness control, efficiency, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a ferroresonant CCR?

A ferroresonant constant current regulator is an AGL power supply that uses ferroresonance, a nonlinear magnetic circuit effect, to hold a constant output current. A transformer with a saturable magnetic shunt and a resonant capacitor clamps the voltage and keeps current near-constant despite changes in input voltage or load impedance.

Q2How does a ferroresonant CCR work?

The transformer's magnetic shunt saturates at a certain current level, and a capacitor across the secondary forms a resonant tank with the transformer's leakage inductance. This clamps the output so current stays essentially constant over a wide range of input and load conditions, with no active electronic control.

Q3Why choose a ferroresonant CCR?

It is known for robustness and simplicity, and it can withstand severe overloads and short circuits without damage because it has inherent short-circuit protection and fewer active components that can fail. It is widely used in existing airfields and in remote locations.

Q4How is brightness controlled on a ferroresonant CCR?

By tap changing, either manual or motorised, to select the brightness steps around the 6.6 A nominal output. Most units have no digital communication, though remote tap changer control and dry-contact or current-loop feedback to a regulator monitor are optional.

Q5How accurate and efficient is a ferroresonant CCR?

Regulation accuracy is typically ±2–3% of set current, efficiency 75–85% at full load, and power factor 0.6–0.8 lagging. These figures are lower than for electronic CCR types such as IGBT designs.

Q6Where are ferroresonant CCRs used?

Runway edge, centreline and threshold lighting, taxiway lighting, many older approach lighting systems (SALS, SSALS, MALS), some obstruction lighting circuits in remote locations and legacy installations where replacement is not yet planned.

Q7What installation needs do ferroresonant CCRs have?

They are floor-mounted on a concrete plinth in the equipment room or shelter. The core-and-coil construction is heavier than electronic types and is sometimes oil-filled at higher ratings, so the plinth must be rated for the extra weight.

Q8Which standards apply to ferroresonant CCRs?

The main references are IEC 61822 and EN 61822, FAA AC 150/5345-10 (constant current regulators and regulator monitors) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a ferroresonant CCR be maintained?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for thermal and ventilation checks, and annual for resonant capacitor checks, tap changer inspection, fastener tightening, input voltage monitoring and power factor checks. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why has my ferroresonant CCR lost regulation or cannot change brightness step?

Loss of regulation or erratic output usually means the resonant capacitor has degraded from dielectric breakdown or ageing, so measure its capacitance and ESR. An inability to change brightness step usually points to a failed motorised tap changer through contact wear, motor failure or a control circuit fault.
