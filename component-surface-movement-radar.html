Surface Movement Radar

A high‑performance surveillance system that provides real‑time detection and tracking of aircraft and vehicles on the airfield surface. Surface Movement Radar (SMR) enhances situational awareness for Air Traffic Control, enabling safe and efficient ground movement management even in low visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Surface Movement Radar (SMR) is a specialized radar system designed to detect and track the position, speed, and heading of aircraft and vehicles operating on the airport surface—including runways, taxiways, and aprons. Operating in the X‑band (8‑12 GHz) or Ku‑band (12‑18 GHz), SMR provides high‑resolution detection with range resolution as fine as 5‑15 metres, enabling controllers to distinguish between multiple targets in close proximity. The radar antenna is typically mounted on a tower adjacent to the control tower or on a dedicated mast, providing a 360° scan of the airfield surface. The system processes radar returns to generate a real‑time display overlay on airport maps, often integrated with ADS‑B, multilateration, and other surveillance sources to provide a comprehensive surface picture. SMR is a critical component of Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control Systems (A‑SMGCS), enhancing safety and throughput, particularly during low visibility operations (RVR < 400m) where visual observation is limited.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Surface movement radar exists specifically for the situation where a controller's own eyes are the least reliable source of surface traffic information: low visibility conditions where fog, rain, or darkness make it impossible to visually confirm an aircraft or vehicle's position from the tower. Because SMR's X-band or Ku-band returns are essentially unaffected by the same weather that degrades human vision, the radar's purpose is to maintain the same level of surface awareness in RVR below 400 metres that a controller would otherwise only have in clear daylight. Fusing that radar picture with ADS-B and multilateration data extends this further, giving controllers identity information alongside position so a return on the radar display can be matched to a specific aircraft or vehicle rather than remaining an anonymous target.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Surface Movement Radar is deployed at airports of all sizes where enhanced ground surveillance is required:

  • Air Traffic Control Towers: Providing controllers with a high‑fidelity, real‑time display of all surface traffic for safe and efficient ground movement management.
  • ATC Approach Control Rooms: Integrating SMR data into the broader surveillance picture alongside approach radar and ADS‑B.
  • Airport Operations Centers: Monitoring surface activity for overall airport situational awareness and resource management.
  • A‑SMGCS Systems: Serving as the primary surveillance sensor for Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control Systems, enabling automated conflict detection and alerting.
  • Runway Incursion Prevention Systems: Providing target detection to Runway Status Light (RWSL) systems and other safety alerting systems.
  • Low Visibility Operations: Enabling safe ground movements when RVR is below 400m (CAT II/III conditions) where visual observation is insufficient.
  • Apron Management: Monitoring aircraft and vehicle movements on the apron for improved ground handling coordination.
  • Large Hub Airports: Managing complex surface traffic with multiple simultaneous movements, reducing delays and enhancing safety.
Placement

Installation & Location

The SMR antenna is mounted at height on the control tower roof or a dedicated tower structure, sited for line-of-sight coverage across the full manoeuvring area with minimal shadowing from buildings, terrain, or other masts. Foundation and mast design account for the antenna's rotating mass and wind loading, with the mast earthed and lightning-protected given its elevated, exposed position. Signal and data cabling runs from the antenna head down the mast to the processing unit in the tower equipment room, typically over fibre to keep the run immune to the electromagnetic environment around the radar itself; siting is also checked against other radars and radio installations on the airfield to avoid mutual interference.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Surface Movement Radar operates on pulse‑based radar principles, transmitting short, high‑frequency pulses and receiving the echoes from reflecting targets. Key functional aspects include:

  • Antenna Scan: The rotating parabolic or slotted‑array antenna provides a continuous 360° azimuth scan, typically at 20‑60 revolutions per minute, covering the entire airfield surface.
  • Pulse Transmission: The radar transmits short pulses (typically 0.5‑2 µs) at high peak power (5‑100 kW) in the X‑band or Ku‑band, with a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 1‑10 kHz.
  • Echo Reception: The radar receives the reflected echoes (from aircraft, vehicles, buildings, and other surfaces) and processes the signals to extract range, azimuth, and radial velocity information.
  • Moving Target Indication (MTI): SMR uses Doppler processing (MTI or Pulse‑Doppler) to discriminate moving targets from stationary clutter (buildings, terrain, static aircraft), isolating only the moving targets of interest.
  • Target Extraction & Tracking: The radar signal processor extracts target centroids (position and velocity) and passes them to the tracker, which uses Kalman filtering or similar algorithms to maintain continuous target tracks, smoothing position updates and predicting future positions.
  • Clutter Suppression: Advanced filtering techniques (CFAR, map‑based clutter mapping) suppress false returns from terrain, buildings, and parked aircraft, ensuring only relevant targets are displayed.
  • Display Integration: The processed track data is transmitted to the ATC display system, where targets are overlaid on an airport map with labels, track history, and velocity vectors for clear operator interpretation.

Modern SMR systems are often integrated with multilateration and ADS‑B to provide a fused surveillance picture, improving accuracy and coverage in areas where radar returns may be shadowed.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Surface Movement Radar feeds raw target detections into the A-SMGCS or ALCMS processing chain rather than making any lighting decision at the sensor head itself. The antenna and receiver produce return echoes, which a radar processor converts into tracked targets (position, heading, identity where fused with other sensors); that track data is what actually reaches the A-SMGCS or ALCMS server, typically over the airfield's fibre/Ethernet backbone rather than a dedicated link. Where the airfield uses Runway Status Lights, the A-SMGCS logic combines SMR tracks with other surveillance sources to decide when an aircraft or vehicle occupies or is approaching a runway, and that decision — not the raw radar return — is what triggers the corresponding CCR and stop bar or runway status light circuit. A radar fault therefore shows up as a gap in surveillance coverage at the A-SMGCS level, not as a lighting fault.

Construction

Physical Construction

The radar antenna — a parabolic dish or planar slotted-array panel — is mounted on a motor-driven rotating pedestal atop a dedicated tower or mast adjacent to the control tower, engineered to maintain the specified rotation speed and pointing accuracy through wind loading up to the site's design limits. A radome typically encloses the antenna to protect it from precipitation, ice, and debris while remaining transparent to the X-band or Ku-band signal, and the pedestal drive motor and slip rings are housed in a weatherproof base unit rated to at least IP65/NEMA 4X. Transmitter, receiver, and signal-processing electronics are housed either in the tower base or in a nearby equipment room, connected to the antenna via low-loss waveguide or coaxial feed running up the tower structure. Outdoor units, cabling entries, and the antenna drive assembly are all built for continuous unattended exposure to airfield weather, with lightning protection and surge suppression fitted at the base of the tower to protect the sensitive receiver electronics from strikes to the elevated structure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Clutter from Stationary Objects

Buildings, hangars, parked aircraft, and terrain can produce strong clutter returns that mask moving targets. Advanced clutter mapping and MTI processing are required, but regular calibration and tuning are necessary to optimise performance as the airfield environment changes (new buildings, parked aircraft positions).

Rain Clutter & Attenuation

Heavy rain can attenuate radar signals and produce clutter returns, particularly in X‑band systems. Real‑time rain clutter suppression algorithms and, in some systems, dual‑polarisation or frequency agility are used to mitigate this.

Shadowing & Multipath

Tall structures (control towers, hangars) can create radar shadows, while multipath reflections from large buildings can cause ghost targets. Careful antenna siting and site‑specific calibration are essential.

Transmitter/Receiver Performance Degradation

Magnetron or solid‑state amplifiers degrade over time, reducing transmit power and receiver sensitivity. Regular maintenance and performance testing (using a test target or radar cross‑section calibration) are required to ensure coverage remains within specifications.

Data Latency

Processing delays in the radar signal processor or track extraction can introduce latency in the displayed target position, which is critical for real‑time control. Ensure processors meet performance specifications and monitor system timing.

Interference from Other Radio Systems

Other radar systems, communication transmitters, or satellite uplinks can interfere with SMR operation. Frequency coordination and the use of interference‑resistant techniques (e.g., frequency diversity) are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Moving Targets Masked Near Buildings or Parked Aircraft
Likely Cause
Strong clutter returns from stationary structures not adequately suppressed.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the clutter map against the current airfield layout, check MTI processing settings, and correlate missed detections with known clutter zones.
Corrective Action
Update the clutter map for the current environment and retune the MTI/clutter suppression parameters.
Reduced Coverage or False Returns in Heavy Rain
Likely Cause
Rain clutter and signal attenuation, particularly in X-band systems.
Diagnostic Steps
Correlate performance degradation with weather reports and check the rain clutter suppression algorithm status.
Corrective Action
Verify or enable rain clutter suppression, and apply dual-polarisation or frequency agility features if equipped.
Ghost Targets or Blind Spots Near Tall Structures
Likely Cause
Radar shadowing from tall structures or multipath reflections from large buildings.
Diagnostic Steps
Map reported anomalies against known structures and review antenna siting and site-specific calibration.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate for site-specific conditions, and adjust siting or add a gap-filler sensor where shadowing is unavoidable.
Reduced Detection Range Over Time
Likely Cause
Transmitter/receiver performance degradation (magnetron or solid-state amplifier ageing).
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a test-target or radar cross-section calibration check and compare transmit power and receiver sensitivity against baseline.
Corrective Action
Service or replace the degraded transmitter/receiver component and re-verify coverage meets specification.
Delayed or Jumping Target Position on Display
Likely Cause
Processing delay in the radar signal processor or track extraction system.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor system timing and processing load, and check processor performance against specification.
Corrective Action
Address the processing bottleneck (hardware upgrade, software optimisation) and verify timing meets the real-time requirement.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Antenna / Radome Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Weathering, UV exposure, or accumulated contamination on the radome reducing signal transmission through it.
Effect
Reduced detection range over time as more of the transmitted and received energy is absorbed or scattered by the degraded radome.
Detection Method
Antenna and radome inspection, and a transmitter/receiver performance check against the commissioning baseline.
Transmitter / Receiver Performance Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Component ageing in the transmitter power stage or receiver front end, or a developing fault in the RF chain.
Effect
Reduced coverage, weaker returns from smaller targets, and an overall drop in detection reliability across the covered area.
Detection Method
Transmitter power and pulse verification, and a receiver performance check against rated specification.
Clutter Map Drift from Stationary Objects
Medium Severity
Root Cause
New parked aircraft, ground equipment, or construction near the covered area that the stored clutter map hasn't been updated to reflect.
Effect
Persistent detections on a clear runway or taxiway, or conversely, real moving targets masked near the new stationary object.
Detection Method
Clutter map review against current site conditions, triggered by any nearby physical change.
Rain Clutter and Signal Attenuation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Heavy precipitation scattering and attenuating the radar signal across the covered area.
Effect
Reduced coverage or false returns in heavy rain, temporarily degrading the picture presented to A-SMGCS.
Detection Method
Correlating detection-quality drops with weather radar data, and reviewing MTI/clutter suppression performance under rain.
RF Interference from Other Radio Systems
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Nearby radar, radio, or wireless equipment operating in or near the SMR's frequency band.
Effect
Data latency or spurious detections that don't correspond to real traffic, reducing confidence in the surface picture.
Detection Method
RF interference survey across the operating band, and correlation of anomalies with known nearby transmitters.
Data Latency / Processing Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Processing load, a software fault in the radar processor, or a network bottleneck between the processor and the A-SMGCS/ALCMS server.
Effect
Delayed or jumping target position on the display, undermining controllers' confidence in the real-time picture.
Detection Method
Data latency check against the required update interval, and a sensor fusion cross-check against other surface sensors.
Shadowing and Multipath Near Tall Structures
Medium Severity
Root Cause
New buildings, parked wide-body aircraft, or other tall structures creating radar shadow zones or multipath reflections.
Effect
Ghost targets or blind spots appear near the structure, which can mask a genuine target or create a false one.
Detection Method
Target tracking accuracy test focused on the affected zone, and a clutter map review after any new structure is erected.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

RF / Microwave Exposure
Personnel Safety

The transmitter can produce high peak power (5-100 kW) in the X-band or Ku-band; do not work in front of the antenna aperture while the radar is transmitting, and follow the manufacturer's RF safety exclusion distance and lockout procedure for maintenance access.

Rotating Antenna Hazard
Mechanical

The parabolic or slotted-array antenna rotates continuously at 20-60 RPM during operation; lock out antenna rotation before working on or near the mast head, and never assume a momentarily stopped antenna will stay stopped.

Working at Height
Personnel Safety

Antenna and mast-head access is typically on the control tower roof or a dedicated tower structure; use fall-arrest equipment and a stable access platform, and avoid work during high wind given the mast's elevated, exposed position.

Lightning Exposure
Electrical Safety

As one of the tallest structures in its immediate area, the mast is a lightning attraction point; avoid working at or near the antenna during an electrical storm, and confirm mast earthing and lightning protection are intact after any strike.

High-Power Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the radar's mains supply (and, where fitted, generator/UPS backup) before internal work on the transmitter or power supply, and verify zero energy given the multi-kW power levels involved.

Airside/ATC Coordination
Operational

SMR is a primary surface-surveillance tool for controllers; coordinate any outage with ATC and raise a NOTAM, since taking it down removes a safety-critical situational-awareness capability for ground movement.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the radar returns full 360° coverage with correct range resolution before closing out the work and returning it to operational service.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
New Construction Can Create a Coverage Shadow
Siting

A new hangar, terminal extension, or even a temporary crane erected within line of sight of the antenna can create a permanent blind spot in ground coverage without any fault on the radar itself; treat any new tall structure near the airfield as a trigger to re-verify coverage, not just changes made to the radar installation.

Weather Clutter Degrades Gradually With Radome Condition
Maintenance

A degraded or contaminated radome (dirt film, ice buildup, coating wear) increases signal attenuation and can be mistaken for a transmitter power issue during heavy rain or snow; check radome condition and cleanliness before troubleshooting a weather-correlated performance complaint as an electronics fault.

Track Magnetron or Transmitter Output Over Time
Reliability

Transmitter output power on older magnetron-based systems declines gradually over years of service rather than failing suddenly; logging output power at each scheduled maintenance visit reveals a slow decline trend long before it becomes an operationally noticeable range-reduction complaint.

Rotary Joint Wear Shows Up as Intermittent Signal Dropout
Mechanical

The rotary joint connecting the stationary waveguide to the continuously rotating antenna is a common source of intermittent signal degradation that correlates with antenna position rather than weather or transmitter condition; a fault that seems to come and go with the sweep is worth checking here before assuming it's a transmitter issue.

Lightning Protection Needs Post-Strike Verification, Not Just Visual Check
Electrical

A lightning strike near the mast can degrade the earthing system's actual resistance without leaving any visible sign at the structure; after a known nearby strike, verify earth resistance with a proper test rather than relying on a visual inspection of the bonding connections alone.

Antenna Rotation Speed Drift Is Easy to Miss
Verification

A motor or drive system that's slowly slowing down from its rated RPM can still produce a usable-looking picture at reduced update rate, which delays operator complaints; verify actual rotation speed against spec at scheduled maintenance rather than relying on the display appearing normal.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about surface movement radar: surface surveillance, specifications, power, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a surface movement radar?

A Surface Movement Radar (SMR) is a specialised radar that detects and tracks the position, speed and heading of aircraft and vehicles on runways, taxiways and aprons, operating in the X-band (8-12 GHz) or Ku-band.

Q2Why is surface movement radar needed?

It covers situations where a controller's own eyes are least reliable, such as fog, rain or darkness, by giving a real-time picture of surface traffic.

Q3Where is surface movement radar deployed?

At airports of all sizes needing enhanced ground surveillance, with the display used by controllers in air traffic control towers.

Q4How does it work?

It transmits short high-frequency pulses from a rotating antenna and receives echoes from targets, using MTI or Doppler processing with clutter suppression to separate moving targets from fixed objects.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

Range coverage of 5-20 km, range resolution 5-15 m, azimuth resolution 0.5-2 degrees, antenna rotation 20-60 RPM, tracking of 500 or more targets, and outputs such as ASTERIX, Ethernet, RS-422 and CCTV overlay.

Q6What electrical supply does it need?

120/230 VAC single-phase or 400 VAC three-phase, typically 2-15 kW, backed by UPS and a standby generator, with a slip ring or rotary joint across the rotating antenna and SPDs at power and data entries.

Q7Which standards apply?

ICAO Doc 9830 (A-SMGCS Manual), EUROCAE ED-116 (MOPS for SMR sensor systems), EUROCAE ED-87 (MASPS for A-SMGCS Levels 1 and 2) and FAA Order JO 6330.36 (maintenance of ASDE-X facilities).

Q8How often should surface movement radar be maintained?

Semi-annually for antenna and radome inspection and data latency checks, and annually for clutter map review, transmitter and receiver performance checks and an RF interference survey.

Q9Why are moving targets masked near buildings or parked aircraft?

Strong clutter returns from stationary structures may not be suppressed enough. Review the clutter map against the current airfield layout and check the MTI processing settings.
