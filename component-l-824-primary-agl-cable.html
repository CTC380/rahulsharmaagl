L‑824 Primary AGL Cable

The FAA‑specified 5kV shielded cable that forms the backbone of airfield ground lighting series circuits. Engineered with cross‑linked polyethylene insulation and a copper tape or braid screen, it provides reliable, high‑voltage power transmission in buried, duct, and elevated installations while ensuring electromagnetic compatibility and safety in the demanding airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

The L‑824 Primary AGL Cable is a specialised, high‑voltage (5kV) shielded power cable manufactured to the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) specification L‑824. It is the standard cable used for primary series circuits in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems, carrying the constant current (typically 6.6A or 20A) from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to every isolating transformer and light fixture in the loop. The cable is designed for direct burial, underground duct, or elevated installation and is engineered to withstand the mechanical, thermal, and environmental stresses of airfield operations.

Key characteristics of L‑824 primary cable include:

  • 5kV Voltage Rating: A single‑conductor cable rated 5,000 V for the series circuit it carries, providing ample margin for the high transient voltages that occur in inductive series circuits.
  • Shielded Construction: Features a metallic screen (copper tape or braid) that provides electromagnetic interference (EMI) protection, reduces radio frequency interference (RFI), and ensures safety by confining the electric field within the cable.
  • XLPE Insulation: Cross‑linked polyethylene insulation offers excellent dielectric strength, high temperature rating (90°C continuous), and resistance to moisture, chemicals, and thermal ageing.
  • PVC Outer Jacket: A tough, durable polyvinyl chloride jacket provides mechanical protection against abrasion, impact, and environmental exposure, with options for additional armouring in high‑risk areas.
  • Conductor Options: Available with stranded (7‑wire Class B or 19‑wire Class C) annealed copper conductors, sized from 8 AWG to 4 AWG per FAA AC 150/5345‑7F Table 1, to suit circuit length and current rating requirements.

The L‑824 cable is the standard primary cable specified in FAA Advisory Circular AC 150/5345‑7 and is used in airports worldwide, providing reliable power transmission for runway, taxiway, approach, and obstruction lighting systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

L-824 cable exists to carry the 5kV constant current safely across an entire airfield's primary lighting loop, connecting a CCR to potentially hundreds of isolating transformers and fixtures spread across kilometres of runway and taxiway. Because this cable typically runs buried underground, its construction has to anticipate decades of exposure to soil moisture, mechanical stress from surface loads, and thermal cycling, all while maintaining the insulation integrity that keeps 5kV safely contained rather than posing a shock or fire hazard.

The shielded construction serves a second, equally important purpose beyond insulation: containing the cable's electric field and reducing electromagnetic interference protects both the circuit's own signal integrity and any nearby sensitive equipment, which matters given how close primary cable runs often pass to navigation aids, sensors, and communication infrastructure across the airfield.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The L‑824 primary cable is used throughout the airfield for all primary series circuit applications, including:

  • Runway Lighting Circuits: Powering runway edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, end lights, touchdown zone lights, and other runway fixtures on precision and non‑precision runways.
  • Taxiway Lighting Circuits: Powering taxiway edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, clearance bars, and guidance signs.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Powering ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSR, and other approach lighting configurations, where the cable is often routed in underground ducts from the CCR to the approach lighting structures.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Powering low‑, medium‑, and high‑intensity obstruction lights on structures, where the cable may be installed in conduits or directly buried.
  • CCR to Field Interface: Connecting the CCR output to the field distribution panel or directly to the series loop, often using L‑824 cable with L‑823 connectors at termination points.
  • Runway and Taxiway Intersections: Cabling through underground duct banks and handholes, with the cable's robust construction providing protection against mechanical damage.

The cable is suitable for use in temperatures ranging from -40°C to +90°C and is resistant to oils, fuels, de‑icing chemicals, and UV exposure. It is typically installed in trenches at a depth of at least 0.5m, in concrete‑encased duct banks, or on elevated cable trays.

Placement

Installation & Location

L-824 primary cable is installed in trenches at a depth of at least 0.5m, in concrete-encased duct banks beneath runway and taxiway intersections, or on elevated cable trays inside equipment shelters, following the route set out from the CCR to the field distribution panel and on to each transformer along the series loop. Direct-buried runs are laid on a bed of sand or fine fill to protect the jacket from sharp stones, with warning tape laid above the cable at a shallower depth so future excavation work is alerted to its presence before digging reaches it. Where the cable crosses paved surfaces or enters a structure, it's sleeved through duct or conduit, and slack loops are left at each transformer or junction box connection so a future splice or transformer swap doesn't require pulling in fresh cable from the nearest access point.

Function

How Does It Work?

The L‑824 primary cable functions as the main power distribution conductor for the AGL series circuit, carrying constant current from the CCR to all isolating transformers. Its design ensures efficient, safe, and reliable power transmission over long distances:

  • Conductor: High‑conductivity stranded annealed copper minimises I²R losses, ensuring that the voltage drop along the loop remains within acceptable limits. The conductor is sized to carry the full primary current (6.6A or 20A) with minimal temperature rise.
  • Conductor Screen (Semiconductive Layer): A semiconductive layer over the conductor provides a smooth, stress‑relieving interface between the conductor and the insulation, preventing partial discharge and electrical treeing that can lead to premature failure.
  • XLPE Insulation: The cross‑linked polyethylene insulation provides high dielectric strength (typically > 25 kV/mm) and excellent thermal stability. Its low dielectric constant and low loss tangent minimise capacitive currents and power losses in long cable runs.
  • Insulation Screen (Semiconductive Layer): A semiconductive layer over the insulation ensures a uniform electric field distribution, preventing stress concentration and partial discharge at the insulation surface.
  • Metallic Screen (Shield): A copper tape (overlapping) or copper braid screen provides a low‑impedance path for fault currents and shields the cable from external electromagnetic interference. The screen is designed to carry fault currents and is connected to earth at termination points.
  • Outer Jacket: The PVC jacket provides mechanical protection against abrasion, moisture ingress, and chemical attack, ensuring the cable's long‑term reliability in buried and exposed installations.

The cable's shielded construction is particularly important in AGL applications, as it prevents the cable from radiating electromagnetic interference that could affect sensitive communication and navigation systems, and protects the series circuit from external EMI sources such as radar and radio transmitters.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The L-824 primary cable carries no control logic of its own — it is the physical medium that makes loop-level control possible in the first place. An ALCMS command sets the required brightness step, the CCR converts that into a fixed regulated current, and it is this cable that then delivers that exact current, unchanged in magnitude, around the entire series loop to every isolating transformer in turn: the cable's role is to keep the current identical at every point on the loop, not to route or vary it. Because the circuit is series-connected, the cable itself becomes part of the CCR's fault-monitoring loop — the same instrument that measures loop voltage against commanded current to detect an open-circuit lamp or failed transformer will just as readily flag a cable fault (an insulation breakdown, a screen discontinuity, or a full open circuit) as a voltage anomaly on that loop. It does not, however, distinguish a cable fault from a fixture fault: both present as the same class of loop-level electrical symptom to the ALCMS, and locating the actual break requires insulation-resistance testing and sectionalised loop testing in the field, rather than any diagnostic capability built into the cable itself.

Construction

Physical Construction

L-824 primary cable is built as a single-conductor construction with a stranded, bare or tinned copper conductor sized per the current rating, surrounded by a cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) or ethylene propylene rubber (EPR) insulation layer rated for the 5kV class. A semi-conducting insulation shield and a copper tape or braided shield are applied over the insulation to control the electric field and provide fault-current capacity, followed by an outer jacket of polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene, or a similar UV- and chemical-resistant compound. The finished cable is round and flexible enough for duct pulling or direct burial, and is marked at regular intervals with its type, voltage class, and manufacturer for identification during installation and maintenance. FAA L‑824 defines two insulation types at the 5,000 V rating: Type C (cross‑linked polyethylene, XLPE) and Type B (ethylene propylene rubber, EPR); Type C's XLPE insulation is the more common choice for direct burial and duct installation.

Installation Requirements

Trench Depth & Bedding
Civil

Excavate the trench to design burial depth and lay a bedding layer of clean sand or fine fill free of sharp stone before placing the cable, protecting the jacket from puncture during backfill.

Warning Tape & Route Marking
Civil

Lay warning tape above the cable at the specified cover depth and mark the as-laid route so future excavation does not strike the circuit.

Bend Radius During Pulling
Electrical

Keep every bend at or above the minimum bend radius (12× diameter while pulling, 8× once fixed) to avoid stressing the insulation or shield at duct entries and corners.

Duct/Conduit Fill & Separation
Electrical

Keep duct fill within the pulling tension and jam-ratio limits, and maintain separation from other services per the design drawing, especially where crossing other utilities.

Type & Current Rating Match
Electrical

Confirm the cable insulation type (Type C – XLPE, or Type B – EPR) and current rating (6.6A or 20A) match the circuit it is being installed on before pulling.

Screen/Shield Bonding at Splices
Mechanical

Bond the copper tape or braid screen continuously at every splice or termination, since a broken screen bond compromises fault-current capacity along the run.

Splice Kit Preparation
Mechanical

Follow the manufacturer's strip and shield-transition dimensions exactly when preparing L-823 splice connections, since a misprepared cable end is the most common source of a field insulation failure.

Pre-Backfill Sign-off
Documentation

Record as-laid route, burial depth, splice locations, and cable type/batch markings before the trench is closed, since the run is not inspectable once buried.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Insulation Damage During Installation

Sharp edges in conduits, ducts, or trench backfill can nick or cut the XLPE insulation, leading to premature failure. Use cable rollers, protect corners with radius bends, and inspect cables before pulling. Always perform an insulation resistance test (5000V megger) after installation.

Moisture Ingress at Cable Ends

If cable ends are not properly sealed during storage or installation, moisture can enter the conductor strands and travel along the cable, causing corrosion and insulation breakdown. Seal cable ends with heat‑shrink caps or self‑amalgamating tape immediately after cutting.

Excessive Voltage Drop on Long Runs

Long primary cable runs can result in excessive voltage drop, reducing the voltage available at the farthest isolating transformers. This can cause dim lights or failure. Use the largest standard L‑824 conductor size (4 AWG) or reduce the circuit length by splitting into multiple loops.

Overheating Due to Overloading

If the primary cable is undersized for the current (e.g., using 6 AWG cable on a 20A circuit), it will overheat, potentially melting the XLPE insulation and causing a short circuit. Always select the cable size based on the circuit current rating and the installation method.

Screen Damage and Loss of Continuity

The metallic screen can be damaged during installation or can corrode over time, leading to loss of screen continuity. This compromises EMI protection and can cause interference with sensitive equipment. Test screen continuity after installation and at regular intervals.

Rodent Damage

In buried installations, rodents can chew through the PVC jacket and XLPE insulation, causing faults. Use armoured cable (SWA or STA) or install the cable in rodent‑proof conduits in areas with high rodent activity.

Improper Termination

Using the wrong connector or poor termination technique can result in high resistance, overheating, and eventual failure. Always use approved L‑823 connectors or similar, and follow the cable manufacturer's termination instructions.

Incorrect Cable Gland Selection

Using a cable gland that does not match the cable diameter will fail to seal properly, allowing water ingress into the connector or equipment housing. Always select the correct gland size for the cable's overall diameter.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Insulation Resistance Test Fails (Low Megger Reading)
Likely Cause
Insulation damage from a nicked jacket during installation, or moisture that has tracked into the conductor strands from an unsealed cable end.
Diagnostic Steps
Sectionalise the run and megger-test each segment at 5000V to isolate the faulted length; inspect accessible cable ends and any known nick points from the pull.
Corrective Action
Cut back to sound cable and install a proper splice kit, or replace the faulted section, then re-test insulation resistance before backfilling.
Open Circuit / No Continuity
Likely Cause
A severed conductor from rodent damage, mechanical impact during backfill, or a failed termination.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a continuity test from both ends; use time-domain reflectometry or a cable fault locator if available to estimate the break location.
Corrective Action
Excavate at the estimated fault location, repair or replace the damaged section with a proper splice, and consider armoured cable if rodent damage is confirmed.
Excessive Voltage Drop at Far End of Run
Likely Cause
Conductor size undersized for the circuit length, resulting in more voltage drop than the farthest isolating transformers can tolerate.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure voltage at the CCR output and at the farthest transformer under normal load; compare the calculated voltage drop against the conductor size and run length.
Corrective Action
Upsize the conductor on a future cable replacement, or split the circuit into shorter loops to bring voltage drop within tolerance.
Cable Runs Hot / Insulation Softening
Likely Cause
The cable is undersized for the actual circuit current, causing it to overheat under normal load.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the installed cable size against the circuit's rated current and installation method; check for visible signs of insulation softening or discoloration at accessible points.
Corrective Action
Replace the undersized section with correctly rated cable for the circuit current and installation method.
Loss of Screen Continuity
Likely Cause
The metallic screen was damaged during installation or has corroded over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Test screen continuity end to end; inspect accessible splice and termination points for a missing or broken screen connection.
Corrective Action
Repair the screen bond at the affected splice or termination, and re-test continuity to confirm EMI shielding is restored.
Water Ingress at a Termination or Gland
Likely Cause
An incorrectly sized cable gland or improperly terminated connector failed to seal against moisture.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the gland size against the cable's overall diameter; check for evidence of moisture tracking at the termination.
Corrective Action
Replace the gland with the correctly sized unit, re-terminate using approved connectors, and re-verify the seal before re-energising.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

L-824 cable is rated for 5kV and carries constant-current series loop power at that level. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the cable with a rated voltage detector before cutting, splicing, or terminating it.

Confirm the Correct Cable Run
Operational

On multi-circuit airfields, positively identify the correct cable and circuit before isolating work, since cutting into the wrong run can leave live conductors exposed at up to 5kV while the intended circuit stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice, repair, or termination, megger test the affected cable run at the appropriate DC test voltage before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the loop.

Screen/Shield Continuity
Electrical Safety

Maintain and reconnect the cable's metallic screen at every splice, termination, and transformer connection; a broken screen removes the confinement of the electric field within the cable and degrades EMI/RFI protection.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE rated for 5kV work when splicing or terminating L-824 cable, and confirm the screen is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Splice Sealing
Electrical Safety

Seal every splice and termination to the specified standard before burial or reinstatement; moisture ingress at a cable joint is one of the most common causes of an eventual ground fault on a 5kV series loop.

FOD Control at the Excavation
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and offcuts accounted for at all times when a cable run is exposed for repair, and reinstate the surrounding ground or duct before leaving the area, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a cable run with an unsealed splice, a broken screen connection, or a residual insulation fault after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and correct burial depth before closing out the work.

Quick Reference

L-824 Airport Cable

L-824 airport cable is cable made to the FAA L-824 specification for underground use in airport lighting circuits. The 5 kV type is commonly used for primary series circuits, and a lower-voltage type is used for secondary and other circuits. The cable is chosen for direct burial or duct use and for the voltage class of the circuit it serves.

Check the voltage rating, conductor size, shielding and insulation type against the project specification and the CCR loop design before ordering. For testing, see the AGL primary cable page, which covers cable insulation, continuity and fault checks.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Insulation Resistance Trends Beat Single Readings
Troubleshooting

A cable run failing insulation resistance at a routine test rarely fails suddenly; it usually shows a slow downward trend across successive maintenance cycles first. Log megger readings by cable run over time rather than only recording pass/fail against the acceptance threshold.

Water Migrates Along the Conductor, Not Just Through the Jacket
Failure Mode

Moisture that enters at a nicked jacket or a poorly sealed splice can travel a considerable distance along the conductor strands before showing up as a fault at a completely different point in the run. Trace a suspected moisture path to its actual entry point rather than replacing only the section where the fault reads worst.

Screen Continuity Is Easy to Overlook During Routine Splicing
Installation

It's possible to make a mechanically sound and well-insulated splice while still leaving the cable's screen or shield discontinuous, since the two are tested separately. Always verify screen continuity as its own step, not as an assumed byproduct of a good conductor splice.

Bend Radius Violations During Pulling Cause Delayed Failures
Durability

Exceeding the minimum bend radius while pulling cable into a duct can crack internal insulation without any visible external damage, and the resulting fault may not appear until the joint sees thermal or mechanical stress months later. Enforce bend radius limits during installation, not just at the final inspection.

Cable Run Length Mismatches Show Up as Uneven Brightness
Design

On a series circuit, meaningfully different cable run lengths between loops can create visibly uneven fixture brightness even though every fixture and cable section individually tests within spec. Account for loop impedance balance at the design stage rather than diagnosing it after commissioning complaints.

Record As-Built Routing Accurately, Every Time
Operational

An inaccurate as-built record for buried cable routing is one of the most common causes of accidental strikes during later excavation, and the cost of a strike far exceeds the time it takes to record the route correctly the first time. Treat as-built documentation as part of the job, not an optional afterthought.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about L-824 primary AGL cable: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is L-824 primary AGL cable?

L-824 is a specialised 5 kV shielded power cable made to the FAA L-824 specification (AC 150/5345-7). It is the standard cable for primary series circuits in airfield ground lighting, carrying constant current, typically 6.6 A or 20 A, from the CCR to every isolating transformer and fixture in the loop.

Q2How is L-824 cable constructed?

From the centre out: a stranded annealed copper conductor, a semiconductive conductor screen, XLPE insulation, a semiconductive insulation screen, a metallic screen of copper tape or tinned copper braid, and a UV-stabilised PVC outer jacket.

Q3What conductor sizes are used?

Typically 8 AWG, 6 AWG or 4 AWG (about 8.4 mm², 13.3 mm² and 21.2 mm²), stranded as 7-wire Class B or 19-wire Class C per FAA AC 150/5345-7F.

Q4What is the voltage rating?

5,000 V for a single-conductor series-circuit cable, giving margin for the high transient voltages in inductive series circuits.

Q5What insulation and screen dimensions are typical?

XLPE insulation thickness of about 2.16 mm to 3.05 mm (85 to 120 mils) for shielded 5 kV cable per ICEA S-93-639, screen coverage of at least 85% for braid or 100% for tape, and a jacket at least 1.52 mm (0.060 in) thick.

Q6Where can L-824 cable be installed?

Direct burial, underground duct or elevated installation, for all primary series circuits: runway, taxiway and approach lighting.

Q7How is the screen bonded?

The metallic screen is bonded and grounded at each transformer or connector according to the circuit's earthing design. Splices and connectors are L-823 primary connectors or factory splice kits, fully submersible and rated to the cable's voltage class.

Q8What tests apply after installation?

Installed insulation resistance of at least 50 MΩ (megger, cable-to-earth) verified before energising, plus a high-potential test at the manufacturer-specified voltage without breakdown.

Q9Which standards apply?

The main references are FAA L-824, FAA AC 150/5345-7, ICEA S-93-639 and UL 1072. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q10What are the common problems with L-824 cable?

Common problems are insulation damage during installation, moisture ingress at cable ends, excessive voltage drop on long runs, overheating from overloading, screen damage and loss of continuity, rodent damage, improper termination and incorrect cable gland selection. Typical symptoms are a low megger reading, an open circuit or water ingress at a termination.

Q11What is L-824 airport cable?

L-824 airport cable is underground electrical cable made to the FAA L-824 specification for airport lighting circuits. The 5 kV type is commonly used for primary series circuits carrying the constant current from the CCR to the isolating transformers, and a lower-voltage type serves secondary circuits. It is suitable for direct burial or duct installation.
