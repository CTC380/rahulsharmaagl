Cable Marker / Identification Sleeve

A durable sleeve or label applied to cables, wires, and connectors to provide permanent identification of circuit function, cable designation, voltage rating, and termination points. Essential for maintenance, troubleshooting, and system documentation, these markers ensure that AGL cables can be quickly and accurately identified throughout their service life in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Cable Marker (also known as an identification sleeve, cable label, or wire marker) is a durable sleeve, label, or tag applied to cables, wires, and connectors to provide clear, permanent identification. In airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems, cable markers are essential for identifying circuit function, cable designation (e.g., primary series cable, secondary lead), voltage rating, termination points, and circuit reference numbers.

Key characteristics of cable markers include:

  • Durable Construction: Made from materials that resist moisture, UV radiation, temperature extremes, oils, fuels, and de‑icing chemicals—typically heat‑shrinkable polyolefin, PVC, polyester, or stainless steel.
  • Permanent Marking: Legends are printed, engraved, or heat‑stamped to ensure long‑term legibility without fading or smudging.
  • Multiple Formats: Available as pre‑printed sleeves, heat‑shrink sleeves (with or without adhesive), wrap‑around labels, zip‑ties, and flag tags.
  • Colour Coding: Available in various colours to denote circuit type, voltage level, or phase, aiding rapid visual identification.
  • Compliance: Meets the marking and identification requirements of international standards, including those for safety and maintenance.

Proper cable identification is critical for safe and efficient maintenance, troubleshooting, and modification of AGL systems. Cable markers reduce the risk of errors during installation and repair, and they support accurate system documentation and as‑built records.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Cable markers exist because an AGL system can contain hundreds of visually identical cables buried, ducted, or routed through junction boxes across the airfield, and a technician working on a fault or planning a modification has no way to know which cable serves which circuit without a reliable identification system. Working on the wrong cable — disconnecting or testing the wrong circuit — wastes time at best and can introduce an unintended outage or safety hazard at worst.

Because these markers have to remain legible for the working life of the cable, often decades in a buried or outdoor environment, their durability isn't a convenience feature but a safety-relevant requirement: a marker that fades or falls off within a few years leaves the airfield back where it started, with unidentified cables that make every future maintenance task slower and riskier than it needs to be.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Cable markers are used throughout the airfield on all cables and wiring in the AGL system, including:

  • Primary Series Cables (L‑824): Identifying circuit numbers, phase, and voltage rating on both buried and elevated primary cables.
  • Secondary Cables and Lead Assemblies: Marking transformer‑to‑fixture leads with fixture designations, polarity, and voltage ratings.
  • Shielded Cables and Instrumentation Cables: Identifying signal, control, and monitoring cable functions and termination points.
  • Earthing and Bonding Cables: Marking earth conductors with identification for connection points and resistance values.
  • Junction Boxes and Connectors: Labeling cable entries, connector pairs, and terminal blocks within enclosures.
  • CCR and Control Panel Wiring: Identifying internal wiring, control signals, and power connections within CCRs, distribution panels, and ALCMS equipment.
  • As‑Built Documentation: Providing physical markers that correspond to circuit diagrams and cable schedules.

Markers are applied at regular intervals along cable runs (typically every 10–30m), at both ends of each cable, at each termination point, and at splice or joint locations. This ensures that cables can be identified at any point without having to trace them back to the source.

Placement

Installation & Location

Markers are applied directly onto the cable or conductor at the point of termination, at regular intervals along exposed runs (typically every 10 to 30 m), and at every splice or joint, so a cable's identity is recoverable at any accessible point without needing to trace it back to source. Heat-shrink and wrap-around sleeves are applied before the cable is buried or the enclosure is closed, since access to add or replace a marker becomes far more difficult once the run disappears into a duct, trench, or sealed pit. At termination points inside junction boxes, transformers, and CCR cabinets, markers are placed close to the connection itself rather than further back along the cable, so the label remains visible without needing to pull the cable to read it. Where markers are used on buried sections, tags rated for direct burial or embedded in the splice enclosure itself are used in place of surface-applied labels that wouldn't survive continuous soil contact.

Function

How Does It Work?

Cable markers work by providing a permanently attached label that conveys essential information about the cable or wire. The method of application and the information encoded vary by marker type:

  • Heat‑Shrink Sleeves: Pre‑printed or blank sleeves are slid over the cable and heated to shrink tightly around the cable jacket. Adhesive‑lined versions provide a permanent, waterproof bond that resists movement and moisture ingress. The legend is printed on the sleeve before or after shrinking.
  • Wrap‑Around Labels: Self‑adhesive labels with a transparent protective layer are wrapped around the cable. They are quick to apply and suitable for retrofitting, but may have shorter outdoor life than heat‑shrink types.
  • Push‑On Markers (Tubing): Pre‑printed flexible PVC or nylon sleeves that are pushed onto the cable before termination. They are held in place by friction and are commonly used in junction boxes and panels.
  • Flag Tags: Flexible tags that extend from the cable, providing a larger surface area for detailed information. They are often used at termination points where more text is required.
  • Cable Ties with Marking: Zip‑ties with an integrated legend plate, allowing cable identification without sliding markers over the cable.
  • Stainless Steel Tags: Engraved metal tags attached to cables with tie‑wraps or crimps, providing the highest level of durability for buried or harsh environments.

The information typically encoded includes:

  • Cable designation (e.g., "P‑1", "S‑3", "L‑824")
  • Circuit or loop number
  • Voltage rating (e.g., "5kV", "600V")
  • Phase or polarity marking
  • Termination point references (e.g., "CCR‑2", "XFMR‑17")
  • Installation date or batch number
  • Special instructions or warnings
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Cable markers have no electrical control function — they play no part in the CCR's or ALCMS's command or fault-detection logic. Their place in the system's architecture is entirely on the human side of it: when the CCR reports a fault, it does so at the loop level, not by naming a specific cable, so identifying the exact conductor to test, isolate, or replace depends on a technician being able to read that cable's designation, circuit number, and termination references directly off it at the point of work.

Without reliable, durable markers, that identification step falls back on tracing cables through as-built drawings or physically following a run through ducts and pits — slow, error-prone work that turns every loop-level fault the electrical system already isolates into a much longer manual search before the right cable is even found, which is why marker durability is treated as a safety-relevant requirement rather than a labelling convenience.

Construction

Physical Construction

Heat-shrink markers are extruded from UV-stabilised polyolefin tubing, printed with the cable's legend using thermal-transfer or laser-marking equipment before shrinking, so the printed surface becomes part of the tube itself rather than a separate label that could peel away. The tube is slid over the cable end before termination and shrunk down with a heat gun to roughly half its original diameter, gripping the cable tightly enough to resist being pulled or rotated out of position, with adhesive-lined variants adding a hot-melt inner layer that flows around the cable jacket to achieve the IP68 rating needed for buried or submerged sections. Wrap-around and self-laminating labels use a similar printed legend on a durable polyester or vinyl substrate, with a clear laminate overlay bonded on top to protect the print from abrasion, UV exposure, and chemical contact while remaining flexible enough to wrap around cables too large or inaccessible for a pre-cut heat-shrink length. Metal tags, used where extreme durability or high temperature resistance is required, are stamped or engraved directly into stainless steel or aluminium and secured to the cable or termination point with a corrosion-resistant cable tie or wire loop rather than adhesive, since the legend needs to survive independently of any bonding layer degrading over time.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Fading or Smudging of Legends

Exposure to UV radiation, heat, and chemicals can cause legends to fade or become illegible over time. Use high‑quality, UV‑stabilised materials and consider engraving or heat‑stamping for critical identification.

Incorrect Sleeve Size Selection

Choosing a sleeve that is too large will allow it to slide along the cable, while one that is too small may not fit. Always measure the cable diameter and select the appropriate sleeve size. For heat‑shrink sleeves, ensure the recovered diameter is smaller than the cable.

Markers Not Applied Before Termination

Heat‑shrink and push‑on sleeves must be slid onto the cable before connectors or terminations are installed. Forgetting to do so can result in markers being omitted or requiring costly re‑work. Include marker application in the installation work sequence.

Inconsistent Identification Scheme

If the marking scheme is not standardised across the airfield, maintenance personnel may misinterpret the markings. Use a consistent identification scheme that aligns with the cable schedule and circuit diagrams.

Adhesive Failure on Wrap‑Around Labels

In harsh environments, self‑adhesive labels may peel off or degrade. Use heat‑shrink sleeves or mechanical tags for critical applications where adhesive labels are not reliable.

Insufficient Marker Density

If markers are spaced too far apart, cables may not be identifiable at intermediate points. Mark cables at both ends, at each termination, at splice points, and at regular intervals along the cable run (typically every 10–30m).

Markers Obscured by Dirt or Corrosion

In buried or outdoor installations, markers can become covered in dirt or corroded. Use stainless steel tags or encapsulated labels for buried applications, and install markers in accessible locations where they can be cleaned and inspected.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Marker Legend Illegible in the Field
Likely Cause
UV, heat, or chemical exposure has faded the print, or a self-adhesive label has smudged or partially peeled.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the marker against installation records to confirm what it should read; check whether the marker type (printed, engraved, heat-stamped) matches what was specified for that environment.
Corrective Action
Replace with a UV-stabilised, engraved, or heat-stamped marker suited to the exposure conditions, and update the cable schedule if the original identity had to be inferred rather than read directly.
Marker Sliding Along the Cable or Fallen Off
Likely Cause
The sleeve was oversized for the cable diameter, or a heat-shrink sleeve wasn't fully recovered/shrunk during installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the cable diameter against the sleeve's rated size range; inspect a heat-shrink sleeve for incomplete shrink or a loose fit.
Corrective Action
Replace with a correctly sized sleeve (or reheat-shrink if the original was undersunk), and record the correct size in the installation procedure for that cable type.
Cable Found Unmarked at a Termination
Likely Cause
The heat-shrink or push-on sleeve was not slid onto the cable before the connector or termination was installed, and cannot be retrofitted without cutting back the termination.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm with the installation crew or work records whether marker application was included in the work sequence for that run; check for a marker at the opposite end or at splice points that could confirm identity in the meantime.
Corrective Action
Add marker application as an explicit checklist step before termination for future work, and for the unmarked cable, use a wrap-around or mechanical tag as an interim identifier until it can be properly re-terminated with a sleeve.
Technician Misidentifies a Cable During Maintenance
Likely Cause
The marking scheme in use doesn't match the cable schedule or circuit diagrams, or different contractors have applied inconsistent labelling conventions over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the marker's identifier against the current cable schedule and circuit diagram; check whether other cables in the same run or duct bank use a different naming convention.
Corrective Action
Re-mark to the standardised scheme, and update or reconcile the cable schedule so the documented identifier matches what's physically on the cable.
Marker Unreadable Due to Dirt, Corrosion, or Burial
Likely Cause
The marker type wasn't suited to a buried or exposed outdoor location, or it hasn't been cleaned as part of routine inspection.
Diagnostic Steps
Excavate or access the marker location; assess whether the marker material (adhesive label vs. stainless tag vs. encapsulated label) is appropriate for the installation environment.
Corrective Action
Clean the marker if legible underneath, or replace with a stainless steel tag or encapsulated label rated for buried/outdoor use, and relocate to a more accessible point if practical.

Visual inspection for sleeve position and shrink quality during installation sign-off and subsequent maintenance visits.

High Severity
Root Cause
Heat-shrink or push-on sleeve not slid onto the cable before the connector or termination was installed, and a marker step omitted from the work sequence.

Installation checklist sign-off requiring marker confirmation before termination, and audit of terminations against the cable schedule during commissioning.

Periodic reconciliation of physical markers against the cable schedule and circuit diagrams, flagging any naming convention mismatches.

Effect
Marker unreadable due to dirt, corrosion, or burial, requiring excavation or access work to confirm cable identity.
Detection Method
Condition assessment during scheduled maintenance, with a check of marker material suitability for the specific installation environment.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Verify Isolation Before Fitting Near Live Cable
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the circuit and verify zero energy at the cable before fitting or replacing a marker sleeve on an in-service series circuit, connector, or termination.

Accurate Labelling is a Safety Control
Compliance

Confirm the marker text matches the as-built circuit designation, voltage rating, and reference number exactly; an incorrect or ambiguous label can lead a future technician to work on the wrong circuit or skip an isolation step.

Avoid Insulation Damage When Fitting
Mechanical

Fit sleeves without cutting, abrading, or over-compressing the cable jacket underneath, since a nicked insulation layer can develop into a fault under the marker where it isn't visible during inspection.

Environmental Durability
Compliance

Use markers rated for UV exposure, moisture, and the temperature range of the installation location, so identification remains legible and reliable over the cable's service life.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for offcuts, backing material, and tools used to fit sleeves, particularly when working inside a base can or handhole on the movement area.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools when fitting a marker near any live circuit connection, and appropriate PPE for confined-space or below-grade work in a base can or trench.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
UV Fade Can Make a Marker Unreadable Before It Physically Fails
Durability

Printed or embossed markers exposed to direct sun for years can fade to illegibility well before the sleeve material itself degrades structurally; treat legibility, not just physical presence, as the pass/fail criterion during inspection.

Inconsistent Marking Conventions Cost More Time Than No Markers at All
Standardisation

A site where different contractors have used different marking schemes over the years creates real risk during troubleshooting, since a technician may trust a marker that reflects an outdated circuit configuration. Standardise and periodically audit marking convention consistency across the whole site, not just presence of a marker.

De-Icing Fluid Degrades Some Sleeve Materials Faster Than Others
Chemical Exposure

Not all marker sleeve materials hold up equally to glycol-based de-icing fluid; a material selected for general weather resistance may still degrade faster than expected near heavy de-icing traffic areas. Specify glycol-rated materials for markers in those specific zones.

Re-Mark Immediately After Any Circuit Rework, Not on a Deferred Schedule
Process

A cable re-terminated or re-routed during fault repair that isn't re-marked on the spot is easy to forget once the immediate fault is resolved, leaving a mismatched or missing marker that misleads the next technician. Build re-marking into the fault-repair checklist itself.

Cross-Check Markers Against As-Built Records Periodically
Verification

Markers can be installed incorrectly at the outset or drift out of sync with as-built documentation over years of incremental changes; a periodic audit comparing physical markers against current records catches this before it causes a troubleshooting error.

Never Rely on a Marker Alone Before De-Energising a Circuit
Safety

A marker is a labeling aid, not a verified isolation proof; always confirm zero energy directly at the point of work regardless of how confident the marking appears, since markers can be wrong, faded, or from a prior configuration.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the cable marker / identification sleeve: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a cable marker or identification sleeve?

A cable marker (also called an identification sleeve, cable label or wire marker) is a durable sleeve, label or tag applied to cables, wires and connectors to give clear, permanent identification.

Q2What information do AGL cable markers carry?

They identify circuit function, cable designation such as primary series cable or secondary lead, voltage rating, termination points and circuit reference numbers.

Q3What types of cable marker are there?

Common formats are pre-printed or blank heat-shrink sleeves, self-adhesive wrap-around labels with a transparent protective layer, and metal tags. Wrap-around labels apply quickly and suit retrofits but may have a shorter outdoor life than heat-shrink.

Q4What materials are cable markers made of?

Heat-shrink polyolefin, PVC, polyester, nylon or stainless steel, resistant to moisture, UV, oils, fuels and de-icing chemicals. Polyolefin is typically rated -55°C to +135°C, metal tags up to +200°C, and heat-shrink types come in 2:1, 3:1 or 4:1 shrink ratios.

Q5How are legends applied?

By thermal transfer printing, laser engraving, heat-stamping or pre-printing, so the legend stays legible over the long term without fading or smudging.

Q6Where are cable markers used?

On all cables and wiring in the AGL system, including L-824 primary series cables, secondary cables and transformer-to-fixture lead assemblies, and shielded, signal, control and instrumentation cables.

Q7Are cable markers waterproof?

Properly applied markers, especially adhesive-lined heat-shrink, can achieve IP68 water resistance and a permanent, waterproof bond.

Q8Which standards apply to cable identification?

The main references are IEC 60445 (marking and identification of terminals and conductors), BS 7671, IEEE C2 and FAA AC 150/5345-7 (L-824 cable, including cable identification requirements). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should cable markers be checked?

Typical annual checks cover legibility, attachment security, chemical and weathering condition, content accuracy, seal integrity of heat-shrink types, a sweep for missing markers, colour coding consistency and metal tag corrosion. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with cable markers?

Common problems are fading or smudging of legends, wrong sleeve size, markers not applied before termination, an inconsistent identification scheme, adhesive failure on wrap-around labels, insufficient marker density and markers obscured by dirt or corrosion. A cable found unmarked at a termination risks misidentification during maintenance.
