Surge Module

A dedicated protective device designed to clamp transient overvoltages and divert surge currents away from sensitive electronics in AGL systems. Protects LED drivers, constant current modules, control circuits, and communication interfaces from damage caused by lightning strikes, switching transients, and electrostatic discharge.

Overview

What Is It?

A Surge Module (also known as a surge protection device – SPD, or transient voltage suppressor – TVS module) is a protective electronic component designed to limit transient overvoltages and divert surge currents away from sensitive equipment. In AGL systems, surge modules are critical for protecting LED drivers, constant current modules, control electronics, and communication interfaces from damage caused by lightning strikes, power switching transients, and electrostatic discharge (ESD).

Key functions of a surge module include:

  • Voltage Clamping: Limiting the voltage at the protected equipment's input to a safe level (e.g., ≤ 100 V for a 24 V system).
  • Current Diversion: Shunting high surge currents (up to 10–20 kA, 8/20 µs waveform) safely to ground.
  • Fast Response: Responding within nanoseconds to picoseconds to suppress fast‑rise transients.
  • Multiple Protection Stages: Combining different suppression technologies (MOV, TVS, GDT) for comprehensive protection.
  • Status Indication: Some modules include visual indicators (LED) or remote signalling to show when the protection has been compromised.

Surge modules are typically installed at the point of entry of power and signal lines into equipment shelters, or integrated directly into AGL fixtures and LED drivers. They are designed to fail safely (short‑circuit mode) to ensure continued protection.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The surge module's purpose is the same protective function performed by a surge protection device at the panel level, but scaled down and positioned to guard specific sensitive electronics — an LED driver, a constant current module, a communication interface — at the point where a transient would otherwise do the most damage. Because lightning-induced and switching transients can appear on power or signal lines anywhere in the system, not just at the main incoming supply, the surge module's role is to provide a final, close-to-the-load stage of clamping that catches whatever energy earlier, upstream protection didn't fully absorb, using a cascade of GDT, MOV, and TVS technologies matched to the speed and energy of the threat at that specific point in the circuit.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Surge modules are deployed throughout AGL systems wherever sensitive electronics need protection:

  • LED Drivers and Constant Current Modules: Protecting the input and output stages of LED power supplies from lightning‑induced surges.
  • Communication Lines: Protecting RS‑485, Ethernet, and fibre optic transceivers used for ALCMS communication.
  • CCR Control Inputs: Protecting the control inputs of Constant Current Regulators from transients on the control bus.
  • Power Distribution Panels: Installed at the main power entry to protect the entire AGL system.
  • Sensor and Monitoring Lines: Protecting analog and digital inputs from lightning and ESD.
  • ALCMS Servers and Workstations: Protecting network and power interfaces of control room equipment.
  • Apron Guidance Systems: Protecting VDGS sensors and controllers from transients.

Surge modules are also used in:

  • Retrofit Upgrades: Adding surge protection to existing AGL systems where it was not originally installed.
  • Outdoor Fixtures: Protecting LED fixtures in lightning‑prone areas.
  • Temporary Installations: Providing protection for mobile or temporary AGL systems during construction.
Placement

Installation & Location

The module is fitted in-line on the circuit it protects, as close as practical to the equipment it's defending (the fixture terminals, the CCR's control input, or the panel's incoming supply), since protection effectiveness drops off with the length of unprotected cable between the surge module and the device it's guarding. It's bonded to a low-impedance earth so let-through energy has a short, direct path to ground rather than diverting through the equipment itself, and where it's fitted inside a fixture or cabinet, it's mounted clear of the optical or thermal path so it doesn't obstruct servicing. Correct polarity and voltage rating are confirmed against the circuit at installation, and the module's end-of-life indicator (where fitted) is checked as part of routine inspection, since a surge module that has already absorbed a major event may need replacing even though the circuit continues to function normally.

Function

How Does It Work?

A surge module employs one or more surge‑suppression technologies, often in a cascaded arrangement:

  • Gas Discharge Tube (GDT): A spark gap device that acts as a high‑impedance open circuit during normal operation but becomes a low‑impedance path when a transient voltage exceeds its breakdown threshold (typically 90–600 V). GDTs handle high surge currents (up to 20 kA) but have a relatively slow response time (microseconds). They are often used as the primary (first stage) protection.
  • Metal Oxide Varistor (MOV): A voltage‑dependent resistor that clamps the voltage to a safe level by shunting current when the voltage exceeds its rated value. MOVs have a faster response (nanoseconds) than GDTs and can handle moderate surge currents (up to 10 kA). They are commonly used as the secondary (second stage) protection.
  • Transient Voltage Suppressor (TVS) Diode: A silicon‑based device that provides fast (picosecond) clamping of voltage spikes. TVS diodes have a very low clamping voltage and are ideal for protecting sensitive semiconductor circuits. They are used as the tertiary (third stage) protection, often in combination with MOVs and GDTs.
  • Decoupling Inductors: Series inductors that slow the rise time of transients, allowing the protection devices more time to respond.
  • Circuit Configuration: A typical surge module uses a cascaded design:
    • Stage 1 (GDT): Diverts the bulk of the surge current to ground.
    • Stage 2 (MOV): Clamps the residual voltage to a lower level.
    • Stage 3 (TVS): Provides final clamping to a level safe for the protected equipment.
  • Status Monitoring: Advanced surge modules include a visual indicator (LED) or remote contact that signals when the protection element has degraded or failed, prompting replacement.

The module is designed to fail in a short‑circuit mode (fail‑safe) to ensure that the protected equipment remains safe, and to protect against fire hazards.

Integration

System Architecture & Control Logic

Surge modules are fitted at defined points in the wider system — CCR output, isolating transformer primary, or PLC/sensor supply — rather than integrated into the lighting control loop itself, so they play no role in intensity or switching commands. Their only interface with the control system is status reporting: a degraded or failed protection stage trips a visual indicator and, on advanced modules, a dry contact or communication signal to the ALCMS, flagging that specific protection point rather than the downstream circuit it protects. Because the module fails in a controlled short-circuit (fail-safe) mode rather than open-circuit, its failure does not register as a loop fault at the CCR — it has to be picked up via its own status contact or a scheduled inspection.

Construction

Physical Construction

A surge module is built as a compact, sealed unit housing one or more protection stages — a gas discharge tube, metal oxide varistor, and transient voltage suppressor diode, depending on the design — mounted on a small internal circuit board within a flame-retardant polymer enclosure. The module terminates in either DIN-rail mounting clips for panel installation inside a CCR, controller, or junction box, or in sealed, potted connections for direct in-line installation within a base can or fixture housing exposed to outdoor conditions. Internal conductor traces and component leads are sized to carry the module's rated surge current without excessive heating or arcing, and where multiple protection stages are combined, they are arranged with coordinated response times and decoupling components so each stage clamps and diverts the surge current in the correct sequence. A visual status indicator, typically an LED or a mechanical fault flag, is built into the enclosure so a failed or end-of-life module can be identified during a routine visual inspection without needing to open a panel. Encapsulation or conformal coating over the internal components protects the module from moisture and vibration in the harsh electrical environment of an AGL power circuit.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Degradation Without Visible Signs

MOVs and GDTs degrade over time after multiple surge events, but the degradation may not be visible. Even if the module's status indicator is green, the protection level may be compromised. Regular replacement (every 3–5 years) based on manufacturer recommendations or after a known lightning event is advised.

Inadequate Grounding

Surge modules rely on a low‑impedance ground path to effectively divert surge currents. Poor grounding (high resistance ground) can cause the surge voltage to rise, reducing protection effectiveness. Ensure that the ground connection is made to a dedicated earth pit with resistance < 5 Ω.

Incorrect Voltage Rating

Selecting a surge module with the wrong nominal voltage (Un) can lead to premature activation or failure to protect. For example, a 24 V system should use a module with a nominal voltage of 24 V (or 28 V DC). Installing a module with a higher voltage rating will reduce its ability to clamp transients, potentially damaging the protected equipment.

Wiring Errors

Connecting the surge module with incorrect polarity (for DC systems) or connecting the ground terminal to a neutral line instead of true earth can render the protection ineffective. Always follow the manufacturer's wiring diagram and verify connections with a multimeter.

Mechanical Damage

Surge modules can be damaged by physical impact, moisture ingress, or excessive heat. Inspect modules regularly for signs of cracking, corrosion, or discolouration, and replace any that show physical damage.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Status Indicator Shows Fault Despite Normal Operation
Likely Cause
The internal MOV or GDT element has silently degraded after repeated surge events, tripping the module's end-of-life indicator even though the circuit still passes current.
Diagnostic Steps
Read the module's fault/status window and cross-check against the surge event log or lightning strike record for the site; do not assume the module is still providing protection.
Corrective Action
Replace the entire module as a sealed unit per manufacturer guidance — individual GDT/MOV/TVS elements inside are not field-serviceable — rather than resetting or ignoring the indicator; log the replacement date for future service interval tracking.
CCR Continues to Trip During Storms Despite Module Fitted
Likely Cause
Inadequate grounding is preventing the surge module from diverting transient current to earth, so the surge energy is still reaching the CCR or downstream circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure earth pit resistance at the surge module's ground connection; verify the bonding conductor is continuous and correctly sized.
Corrective Action
Improve the grounding system (additional earth rods, chemical earthing, or a dedicated earth pit) to bring resistance below the required threshold, then re-verify protection during the next surge event.
No Clamping Action Evident During a Known Surge Event
Likely Cause
An incorrect voltage rating (Un) was selected for the module relative to the circuit's nominal voltage, so the module does not activate at the expected transient threshold.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the circuit's nominal operating voltage and compare it to the module's rated Un; review the original wiring diagram and module datasheet for a mismatch.
Corrective Action
Replace with a module rated for the correct nominal voltage of the protected circuit; retain commissioning documentation confirming the correct part was installed.
Module Shows Discolouration, Cracking, or Overheating Signs
Likely Cause
Mechanical impact, moisture ingress, or sustained overvoltage has physically damaged the module housing or internal components.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the module body for cracking, staining, or heat discolouration; check the enclosure for evidence of water ingress.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged module immediately regardless of indicator status, and address the enclosure sealing or physical protection issue that caused the damage.
Nuisance Protection Activation in Dry, Storm-Free Conditions
Likely Cause
A wiring error — reversed polarity on a DC circuit or the ground terminal bonded to neutral instead of true earth — is causing the module to see abnormal voltage during normal switching transients.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify polarity and terminal connections against the manufacturer's wiring diagram using a multimeter; check that the ground terminal lands on true earth, not a neutral or return conductor.
Corrective Action
Correct the wiring error, re-terminate as per the diagram, and confirm normal operation before returning the circuit to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit Isolation Before Replacement
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the circuit the surge module protects, then verify zero energy at its terminals before disconnecting or replacing it; a module can retain a hazardous residual charge briefly after a recent surge event.

Treat a Tripped Indicator as a Live Fault Signal
Electrical Safety

A module showing its end-of-life or fault indicator has likely absorbed a significant surge; isolate the circuit before removing it rather than assuming the module itself is now passive.

Correct Polarity & Type on Replacement
Compliance

Fit a replacement module of the correct voltage rating, protection technology, and orientation for the position; an incorrectly rated or miswired module can fail to clamp a surge or create a new fault path.

Insulation & Continuity Check Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After replacing a module, verify insulation resistance and correct earth-path continuity before restoring power, since the module's ground connection is central to how it diverts surge current.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on protected circuits, and confirm the module's earth terminal is bonded to the local earthing system per the electrical design before reconnecting.

Avoid Live Work During Electrical Storms
Operational

Do not carry out surge module replacement or testing on circuits exposed to an approaching electrical storm; suspend the work and follow the aerodrome's lightning safety procedure until the storm has passed.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools and fasteners accounted for at all times when the module's enclosure is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a circuit without functioning surge protection after maintenance; confirm the replacement module's status indicator shows healthy before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Degradation Is Cumulative, Not Binary
Reliability

A module rarely fails on a single dramatic strike; repeated near-threshold transients erode the clamping element step by step until the visible end-of-life indicator finally trips. Treat a module that has seen several storm seasons as due for replacement on schedule, not just on indicator failure.

Lead Length Sets the Real Let-Through Voltage
Installation

A module's datasheet clamping voltage assumes short, direct connections; every extra centimetre of lead between the module and the conductor it protects adds inductive voltage drop under a fast transient. Keep leads as short and straight as the enclosure allows rather than coiling slack out of habit.

Coordinate Cascaded Protection Stages
Design

Where a coarse upstream module and a finer downstream module protect the same circuit, mismatched let-through voltages between the two stages can leave the downstream device absorbing more energy than intended. Check the manufacturer's coordination tables rather than assuming any two SPDs in series will share the load correctly.

An Intact Indicator Doesn't Mean Full Capacity Remains
Troubleshooting

Most end-of-life indicators are threshold trips, not a running gauge of remaining protection margin. On circuits with a known history of lightning activity, factor cumulative event count into replacement planning instead of relying solely on the indicator staying green.

The Earth Path Is Half the Protection
Electrical

A surge module is only as effective as the earth connection it diverts current into; a high-resistance or corroded earth path can leave a module testing fine in isolation while still failing to protect the circuit under an actual strike. Verify earth continuity at the same interval as the module itself, not as an afterthought.

Log Replacements Against Storm Activity
Maintenance

Correlating module replacement dates with local lightning strike data over a few seasons often reveals which circuits or feeders are disproportionately exposed. That pattern is far more useful for planning proactive replacement than treating every module on the airfield as an equal risk.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about surge modules: how they work, placement, ratings, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a surge module in an AGL system?

A surge module, also known as a surge protection device (SPD) or TVS module, limits transient overvoltages and diverts surge currents away from sensitive equipment. In AGL systems it protects LED drivers, constant current modules, control electronics and communication interfaces from lightning, switching transients and electrostatic discharge.

Q2How does a surge module work?

It typically uses a cascaded design. A gas discharge tube (GDT) diverts the bulk of the surge current to ground, a metal oxide varistor (MOV) clamps the residual voltage, and a TVS diode provides the final fast clamping. Decoupling inductors slow the rise time of the transient so the stages have time to respond.

Q3Where are surge modules installed?

They are installed close to the equipment they protect: at LED driver and constant current module inputs, on RS-485, Ethernet and fibre transceiver lines used for ALCMS communication, on CCR control inputs, at power distribution panel entries, on sensor and monitoring lines, at ALCMS servers, and on apron guidance systems. They are also used in retrofits, outdoor fixtures in lightning-prone areas and temporary installations.

Q4Why must a surge module be close to the equipment it protects?

Protection effectiveness drops as the length of unprotected cable between the module and the device increases. Short, low-inductance leads and a low-impedance bond to the local earth bar keep the let-through voltage low.

Q5What are typical surge module specifications?

Typical maximum surge current is 10 to 20 kA (8/20 µs) with nominal voltages of 12, 24, 48 or 230 V. Clamping voltage is at or below 100 V for a 24 V system. Response time is under 1 ns for TVS, under 25 ns for MOV and under 1 µs for GDT. Operating temperature is -40°C to +85°C.

Q6What happens when a surge module fails?

It is designed to fail in short-circuit (fail-safe) mode so the protected equipment stays safe and fire hazards are limited. Some modules include an LED indicator or remote contact that signals when the protection element has degraded, and a tripped indicator should be treated as a live fault signal.

Q7Which standards apply to surge modules?

The main references are IEC 61643-11 (low-voltage surge protective devices), IEC 62305 (lightning protection), UL 1449, FAA-STD-019 and AC 150/5345-10 section 3.4.12. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a surge module be inspected or replaced?

Typical intervals are quarterly for status indicator and wiring verification, semi-annual for visual damage checks, annual for ground resistance testing (earth pit below 5 Ω), and every 3 to 5 years for scheduled replacement of MOV/GDT-based modules. After a known nearby lightning event, inspect and consider replacing modules even if the indicator still reads green.

Q9Can a surge module be degraded even if its indicator is green?

Yes. MOVs and GDTs degrade after multiple surge events, and the degradation may not be visible on the status indicator. That is why scheduled replacement and post-lightning inspection are recommended.

Q10What are the common surge module installation problems?

The common issues are inadequate grounding (a high-resistance ground path lets surge voltage rise), the wrong nominal voltage rating for the system, wiring errors such as reversed DC polarity or connecting the ground terminal to a neutral line instead of true earth, and mechanical damage from impact, moisture or heat.
