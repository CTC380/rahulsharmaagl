Alignment Jig

A precision mechanical or optical tool used to verify and adjust the orientation of AGL fixtures, ensuring that the light beam is aimed at the correct azimuth and elevation angles as specified by ICAO and FAA standards. Essential for commissioning, maintenance, and troubleshooting of runway, taxiway, and approach lighting systems.

Overview

What Is It?

An Alignment Jig is a specialised tool designed to check and adjust the angular orientation of airfield ground lighting (AGL) fixtures. Proper alignment is critical because each fixture has a defined beam pattern that must be aimed precisely to provide the intended visual guidance to pilots. Even a small angular error (e.g., 0.5°) can shift the beam centre, reducing light intensity at the critical viewing angle and potentially compromising safety.

Alignment jigs come in various forms, depending on the fixture type and the required accuracy:

  • Mechanical Level Jigs: Simple tools with spirit levels and reference surfaces that attach to the fixture to check its physical level and tilt. Used for runway edge lights, threshold lights, and other fixtures where the beam is referenced to the horizontal plane.
  • Optical Sighting Jigs: Feature a telescope or laser pointer that projects a reference beam. The jig is mounted on the fixture, and the operator checks that the reference beam aligns with a target at a known distance and height. This is common for approach lights and PAPI systems where precise elevation angles (e.g., 3°) must be verified.
  • Digital Inclinometer Jigs: Incorporate an electronic inclinometer that displays the fixture's tilt angle digitally. These offer high accuracy (typically ±0.1°) and are used for fixtures that require precise angular settings, such as runway centreline lights or PAPI units.
  • Fixture-Specific Jigs: Custom‑made tools designed for a particular fixture model. They engage with the fixture's optical housing or mounting points to provide a repeatable reference for alignment checks.
Purpose

Purpose & Function

The alignment jig's purpose is to guarantee that a fixture's certified beam pattern is actually delivered where the airfield design intends it — because a fixture that is optically perfect but mounted a fraction of a degree out of true will put its peak intensity at the wrong angle, potentially leaving pilots with a dim, off-axis, or invisible light exactly where they need reliable guidance. By giving a technician a precise, repeatable reference for azimuth and elevation, the jig turns alignment from a matter of judgement into a verifiable, documented setting, whether the fixture is being installed for the first time, checked after ground settlement, or reassembled following a lens or LED module replacement. Its function is therefore less about the fixture itself and more about closing the gap between a design specification on paper and the actual light a pilot sees in the air.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Alignment jigs are deployed at various stages of the AGL lifecycle to ensure correct beam orientation:

  • Installation and Commissioning: During new AGL system installation or major upgrades, alignment jigs are used to set the correct azimuth and elevation for every fixture according to the approved lighting layout plan. This ensures that the system provides the required visual guidance from the first day of operation.
  • Routine Maintenance Inspections: As part of periodic maintenance (e.g., annual checks), alignment jigs are used to verify that fixtures have not shifted due to pavement settlement, ground movement, or mechanical damage. Any misalignment can be corrected before it affects pilot visibility.
  • Post-Repair Verification: After a fixture has been repaired or replaced (e.g., lens replacement, LED module change), the alignment jig is used to confirm that the new components have not altered the beam direction. This is particularly important for fixtures with replaceable optical assemblies.
  • Troubleshooting: When pilots report that a specific light appears dim or off‑centre, an alignment jig can quickly determine whether the fixture is misaligned or whether the issue is due to other factors (e.g., low intensity, contamination).
  • Calibration of PAPI Systems: PAPI (Precision Approach Path Indicator) units require very precise elevation angle settings (typically 3° ± 0.1°). Specialised alignment jigs with spirit levels and sighting telescopes are used to set and verify the PAPI's glide slope angle during commissioning and periodic checks.
Placement

Installation & Location

As a portable field tool, the alignment jig has no permanent airfield location — it is kept in the maintenance vehicle or tool store and carried out to each fixture as required. In use, it is temporarily clamped or seated onto the fixture's mounting flange or lens housing so its reference surfaces, spirit level, or sighting optics line up with the fixture's own optical axis, and it is removed once the check or adjustment is complete. For PAPI and other precision-angle work, the jig is set up alongside a fixed target or sighting point at a known distance and height so the elevation angle can be verified against that external reference. Between uses, the jig itself is periodically checked against a known-good reference to confirm it has not lost calibration from handling or vehicle transport.

Function

How Does It Work?

The use of an alignment jig typically involves the following steps:

  • Preparation: The jig is cleaned and checked for calibration (if it has built‑in instruments). The fixture is powered and allowed to stabilise, though alignment checks are often performed with the fixture off or at a low intensity to avoid glare.
  • Mounting: The jig is attached to the fixture using the fixture's existing mounting points or a dedicated reference surface. For elevated fixtures, the jig may be clamped to the pole or the fixture housing. For inset fixtures, the jig is placed over the fixture or inserted into a recess.
  • Alignment Reference: The jig provides a reference line or plane that corresponds to the fixture's optical axis. For mechanical level jigs, the operator adjusts the fixture until the spirit level shows the required tilt. For optical jigs, the operator sights through the telescope or aligns the laser spot with a target set at the specified distance and height.
  • Adjustment: The fixture is adjusted (using its mounting screws or shims) until the jig indicates the correct orientation. For fixtures with two‑axis adjustment (azimuth and elevation), both axes are checked and set.
  • Verification: The adjustment is checked again with the jig to ensure that the fixture has not moved during tightening. A final check is often performed with the fixture powered at the required intensity to confirm that the beam pattern appears correct.

The specific procedure depends on the fixture type and the jig design. For example, runway edge lights are typically aligned to be level (0° tilt) and aimed perpendicular to the runway centreline. Runway centreline lights are aligned to have their beam peak at a specific elevation angle (e.g., 4° above the horizontal) and no azimuth offset. PAPI units are set to a specific glide slope angle (e.g., 3°) using an inclinometer and sighting telescope.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The alignment jig itself carries no active electronics or control loop — it is a mechanical reference instrument, not a monitored subsystem. Its "architecture" is really the calibration chain that keeps its output trustworthy: a certified survey control point (or GPS-derived coordinate) establishes the geodetic reference, a theodolite, total station or laser level is set up and checked against that control point, and the jig is then mounted to the fixture base and read against the same reference. Because the jig has no self-monitoring, its accuracy depends entirely on periodic recalibration against a certified reference standard and on correct field procedure — there is no automated fault detection, so operator technique and calibration currency are the only safeguards against a systematically misaligned fixture.

Setting Both Axes: Azimuth & Elevation

A fixture's beam is only correct once both axes agree: azimuth (aim relative to the runway centreline) and elevation (tilt above the horizontal). The jig checks and corrects each axis independently — via sighting telescope, laser, spirit level or digital inclinometer depending on the model — and only converges to a confirmed alignment once neither axis is left uncorrected, which is why single-axis levels alone cannot replace a full alignment jig for two-axis fixtures like PAPI units.

Construction

Physical Construction

The jig is machined from lightweight aluminium alloy or corrosion-resistant stainless steel, with precisely toleranced locating pins, slots, or a fixture-specific cradle that mates directly to the AGL fixture's mounting flange or base can to guarantee repeatable positioning. Depending on type, the jig integrates a bubble-level vial, an optical sighting scope, or a digital inclinometer module rigidly bonded to the reference frame so that any tilt reading reflects the true orientation of the fixture rather than jig flex. Adjustment screws or vernier scales allow fine-tuning of tilt and azimuth within the jig's rated angular range, with locking collars or clamps to hold the set position once achieved. The complete jig is supplied in a foam-lined carrying case sized to protect its calibrated accuracy and prevent damage to sighting optics or digital sensors during transport between fixture locations on the airfield.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Jig Calibration Drift

Digital inclinometers and optical sighting devices can drift over time due to temperature changes, mechanical shocks, or ageing of components. Regular calibration (annually) against a known reference is essential. For spirit level jigs, the accuracy of the vials should be checked periodically.

Improper Mounting Interface

Using a jig that does not precisely match the fixture's mounting points can introduce alignment errors. Ensure that the correct jig or adapter is used for each fixture model. If adapters are worn or damaged, they should be replaced.

Ambient Lighting and Glare

When using optical sighting jigs, bright sunlight or glare from the fixture itself can make it difficult to see the reference crosshairs or laser spot. Using a sunshade, working at twilight, or using a filter can help. For laser jigs, ensure the laser is Class 2 or lower for eye safety.

Pavement Settlement and Dynamic Effects

Even if a fixture is correctly aligned during installation, pavement settlement, thermal expansion, or ground movement can slowly alter the orientation over time. Regular checks (e.g., annually) are necessary to catch these gradual changes before they affect performance.

Human Error in Reading

Reading a spirit level or sighting through a telescope requires careful technique. Parallax error, incorrect interpretation of the level bubble position, or misalignment of the eye can lead to inaccuracies. Training and using jigs with digital readouts can reduce these errors.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Alignment Reading Inconsistent Between Repeat Checks
Likely Cause
Calibration drift in the digital inclinometer or optical sighting device, or the jig is not seating identically on the fixture each time.
Diagnostic Steps
Repeat the measurement several times without moving the jig to isolate reading noise from seating error; check the jig against a certified reference; inspect the mounting interface for wear.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the drifted sensor, and replace worn adapters or mounting points so the jig registers consistently on the fixture.
Laser or Sighting Reference Not Visible
Likely Cause
Bright ambient light or glare washing out the laser spot or crosshair, or a misaligned/dirty optical path in the sighting device.
Diagnostic Steps
Check ambient light levels at the time of testing; inspect the laser aperture and sighting optics for dirt or damage; confirm the laser is powered and within its rated output.
Corrective Action
Work at twilight or shade the sighting area, clean the optical path, and replace a weak or failed laser module; keep laser class at 2 or lower for eye safety.
Jig Will Not Seat Correctly on the Fixture
Likely Cause
Wrong jig or adapter for the fixture model, or a worn/damaged mounting interface on the jig or fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the jig/adapter part number matches the fixture model; inspect mounting pins, collars, or magnets for wear and correct fit.
Corrective Action
Swap in the correct jig or adapter for the fixture model and replace worn mounting hardware before repeating the alignment check.
Digital Readout Erratic or Drifting
Likely Cause
Low battery voltage, temperature-induced sensor drift, or a mechanical shock has disturbed the internal reference.
Diagnostic Steps
Check battery/power status; allow the unit to stabilise to ambient temperature before reading; recalibrate against a known reference angle to check for offset.
Corrective Action
Replace batteries, allow adequate warm-up/stabilisation time, and recalibrate or send the unit for service if drift persists after warm-up.
Fixture Fails Alignment Again Shortly After Correction
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement, thermal expansion, or ground movement continuing to shift the fixture after it was re-aimed.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare current alignment against the last recorded reading and installation date; inspect surrounding pavement and subgrade for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Re-aim the fixture, and if settlement is confirmed, escalate for pavement/subgrade repair to prevent the fixture from drifting out of tolerance again.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Mounting Interface Wear
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated mounting and dismounting on fixtures wearing the jig's locating pins or reference surfaces.
Effect
Alignment reading inconsistent between repeat checks, even when the underlying fixture alignment has not changed.
Detection Method
Repeatability test by taking multiple readings at a known reference point, and inspection of the mounting interface for wear.
Laser/Sighting Reference Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
A degraded laser diode or misaligned internal optics, or battery depletion reducing beam visibility in bright conditions.
Effect
Laser or sighting reference not visible, preventing the jig from being used to set alignment in the field.
Detection Method
Visual check of the laser/sighting output before each use, and battery voltage verification.
Jig Seating / Locating Pin Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wear or bending of locating pins from repeated use, or accumulated debris in the fixture's mating interface.
Effect
Jig will not seat correctly on the fixture, preventing an accurate alignment reading from being taken.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of locating pins and the fixture interface, with cleaning as part of routine maintenance.
Digital Readout Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing of the internal sensor or reference component, or condensation forming inside the readout housing over repeated temperature cycling.
Effect
Digital readout erratic or drifting, undermining confidence in the reading taken during an alignment check.
Detection Method
Periodic calibration check against a certified reference standard, at the interval specified by the manufacturer.
Underlying Fixture Instability
Low Severity
Root Cause
The fixture being aligned has a loosened mount or degraded locking mechanism that lets it drift back out of position shortly after correction.
Effect
Fixture fails alignment again shortly after correction, even though the jig itself reads correctly at the time of adjustment.
Detection Method
Re-check the fixture's mount and locking mechanism when a repeat failure occurs shortly after a documented correction.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise the Fixture Before Clamping
Electrical Safety

Confirm the fixture is isolated from its series circuit and, for incandescent units, allowed to cool before clamping the jig onto the lens housing or mounting flange; the jig itself carries no power, but the fixture it attaches to may still be live or hot.

Laser Sighting Eye Safety
Personnel Safety

Where an optical sighting jig uses a laser reference beam, follow the laser's classification handling rules, avoid direct or reflected eye exposure, and brief nearby personnel before energising the beam.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Alignment checks are carried out fixture by fixture on live movement areas; coordinate with ATC/airside operations for each location worked, particularly for centreline, TDZ, or PAPI units that sit within the runway strip or approach path.

FOD Control During Use
Operational

Secure the jig firmly to the fixture before releasing it, and keep clamps, fasteners, and calibration targets accounted for at all times; a dropped component on a runway or taxiway is a foreign object hazard.

Jig Calibration Verification
Compliance

Check the jig itself against a known-good reference before a round of alignment checks, since a jig that has lost calibration from handling or vehicle transport will report a fixture as correctly aimed when it is not — a false-good reading is harder to catch than an obvious fault.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture with an in-progress or unconfirmed adjustment; verify the final angle against the design aiming diagram, record the result, and remove the jig completely before returning the fixture to service.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Check the Ground Reference, Not Just the Jig
Technique

An alignment jig reads relative to the surface it's seated on, so a settled or uneven base beneath the fixture mount will produce a confidently wrong angle even though the jig itself is functioning perfectly. Verify the base surface is true before trusting the reading.

A Bumped Jig Can Read Confidently Wrong
Diagnostics

A jig that has been dropped or knocked in transit can shift its internal reference without any obvious external damage, giving a plausible but incorrect reading on the next job. Zero-check the jig against a known reference at the start of each session, not just at initial calibration.

Temperature Swings Affect Bubble and Digital Levels Differently
Environment

A bubble-level jig used across a wide temperature range through the day can show a small reading shift from fluid viscosity changes, while a digital inclinometer is comparatively stable but can drift with battery voltage. Know which type is in use and its specific sensitivity when a reading looks borderline.

Repeatability Matters More Than a Single Precise Reading
Verification

Take the aiming reading two or three times, removing and reseating the jig between attempts, rather than trusting a single reading no matter how precise it looks. A jig that isn't seating consistently will show up as scatter across repeated checks, which a single reading can't reveal.

Record the Jig's Own Calibration Date on the Work Record
Compliance

An aiming record that shows the final fixture angle but not which jig, and its calibration status, was used to set it leaves a gap if that jig's calibration is later found to have lapsed. Tie the aiming record to the jig's calibration reference, not just the fixture result.

Two-Person Verification Catches Jig-Seating Errors
Process

Where practical, have a second technician independently reseat the jig and confirm the reading before final sign-off on a critical fixture's aim, since a subtle jig-seating error is one of the easiest mistakes for a single technician to miss on their own work.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the alignment jig: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an alignment jig?

An alignment jig is a specialised tool for checking and adjusting the angular orientation of AGL fixtures. Each fixture has a defined beam pattern that must be aimed precisely to give pilots the intended visual guidance.

Q2Why does fixture alignment matter?

Even a small angular error, for example 0.5°, can shift the beam centre and reduce light intensity at the critical viewing angle, which can compromise safety.

Q3What types of alignment jig are there?

Mechanical level jigs use spirit levels and reference surfaces to check tilt against the horizontal plane. Optical sighting jigs use a telescope or laser pointer to project a reference beam. Digital inclinometer types give an electronic angle reading.

Q4When is an alignment jig used?

During installation and commissioning to set azimuth and elevation for every fixture according to the approved lighting layout, and during routine maintenance inspections to verify beam orientation remains correct.

Q5How is an alignment jig used?

The jig is cleaned and checked, then attached to the fixture using its mounting points or a reference surface (clamped to the pole for elevated fixtures, or placed over or into the recess for inset fixtures). It provides a reference line or plane against which the fixture's aim is read and adjusted.

Q6How accurate is an alignment jig?

Typical accuracy is ±0.1° to ±0.5° depending on type, with an adjustable tilt range of about -10° to +10°.

Q7What are typical materials, weight and conditions?

Aluminium alloy or stainless steel for light weight and corrosion resistance, weighing 0.5 kg to 3 kg. Operating temperature is typically -20°C to +50°C, and the jig is stored in a foam-lined carrying case.

Q8Which standards apply to alignment jigs?

Relevant references are ICAO Annex 14 (beam alignment), FAA AC 150/5340-30 (visual aid installation, including aiming and alignment procedures), IEC 61827 and the fixture manufacturer's alignment procedures and jig requirements. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should an alignment jig be calibrated?

Digital and optical instruments are typically calibrated annually. The mounting interface is checked before each use, and the reading procedure and storage and handling are checked on an ongoing basis.

Q10What are the common problems with alignment jigs?

Common problems are calibration drift, an improper mounting interface, ambient lighting and glare, pavement settlement and dynamic effects, and human error in reading. Inconsistent readings between repeat checks, or a fixture that fails alignment again shortly after correction, points to jig or fixture instability.
