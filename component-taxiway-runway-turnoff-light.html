Taxiway Runway Turnoff Light

A specialised taxiway guidance fixture that marks the point where an aircraft exits the runway onto a taxiway (turnoff) or enters the runway from a taxiway. These lights provide a clear visual cue for pilots during the critical transition between runway and taxiway operations, typically using a distinctive green/yellow combination to indicate the turnoff point and direction.

Overview

What Is It?

A Taxiway Runway Turnoff Light is a specialised taxiway guidance fixture that marks the point where an aircraft exits the runway onto a taxiway (turnoff) or enters the runway from a taxiway. These lights provide a clear visual cue for pilots during the critical transition between runway and taxiway operations, typically using a distinctive green/yellow combination to indicate the turnoff point and direction. Turnoff lights are essential for ensuring safe and efficient runway occupancy, preventing runway incursions, and guiding pilots accurately through the complex transition between high-speed runway operations and slower taxiway movements.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The turnoff light's purpose is to mark, unambiguously, the exact point where an aircraft's path shifts from the high-speed environment of the runway to the slower, more constrained environment of the taxiway — a transition that happens quickly after landing and where a missed or late turn can extend runway occupancy time and create a conflict with traffic behind the exiting aircraft. Giving that specific point a distinctive light pattern removes any doubt about exactly where the transition occurs.

Because the same location can also serve traffic entering the runway from the taxiway, the light's visual cue works in both directions of use, helping prevent runway incursions in either scenario: an aircraft mistakenly continuing past its intended exit, or one entering the runway without a clear sense of exactly where the taxiway/runway boundary sits.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Taxiway runway turnoff lights are installed at runway exit points and entrance points where taxiways connect to the runway. They are used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where efficient runway exit is critical
  • Rapid exit taxiways where high-speed exits are required
  • Busy airports with high traffic volumes requiring rapid runway clearance
  • Complex taxiway layouts where accurate turnoff guidance is essential
  • Low visibility operations where visual cues are critical
  • Military airfields requiring precise ground movement guidance
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the specific point where the taxiway centreline meets the runway, positioned to mark the transition clearly for aircraft moving in either direction through that point. Mounting is generally inset, matching the surrounding runway and taxiway lighting at this transition, with the fixture's green/yellow pattern set out precisely at the boundary line surveyed between the two pavements rather than offset into either one. Because this location carries both high-speed runway lighting circuits and taxiway lighting circuits in close proximity, cabling is routed and labelled carefully to keep the two circuits correctly assigned, avoiding a wiring error that could leave the turnoff light following the wrong circuit's brightness steps or switching logic. Position is verified against the specific runway exit design — rapid exit taxiways in particular are angled and radiused for their intended exit speed, so the turnoff light's location is set out to match that specific geometry rather than a standard perpendicular intersection.

Function

How Does It Work?

Taxiway runway turnoff lights operate as part of the integrated runway and taxiway guidance system:

  • Installation: Turnoff lights are installed at the point where a taxiway meets the runway, typically in a row across the taxiway entrance or as a series of lights leading from the runway centreline to the taxiway.
  • Lighting: The lights typically emit green light to indicate the turnoff point, sometimes with yellow or amber lights to provide additional cautionary cues. The green light aligns with the taxiway centreline guidance, while yellow provides a cautionary indication for the turnoff.
  • Guidance: The lights provide a clear visual path from the runway centreline onto the taxiway, guiding pilots to safely exit the runway and enter the taxiway.
  • Integration: Turnoff lights are integrated with runway edge lights, taxiway centreline lights, lead-off lights, stop bars, and other AGL systems to provide seamless transition guidance.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Turnoff lights mark the point where control responsibility hands over from the runway lighting circuits to the taxiway lighting circuits, even though the physical row of lights sits at a single, continuous location. The green and any cautionary yellow/amber elements are typically tied to the taxiway centreline and edge CCR loops rather than the runway edge circuit, so their intensity is stepped by the taxiway lighting control logic, and their loop-level fault reporting is separate from a runway edge fault at the same general area of pavement. Because the fixture's job is to be read correctly at high speed and in a single glance while an aircraft is still moving at landing roll-out speeds, the ALCMS generally keeps turnoff lighting intensity synchronised with the runway lighting step in use for that landing, even though the two are electrically independent circuits — a coordination handled at the ALCMS level rather than by any direct electrical link between the runway and taxiway loops.

Diagram

Tracing the Turnoff Path

Where the exit indicator fixture consolidates two signals into one housing, this light's job is tracing the geometry of the exit itself: a row of lights curves away from the runway centreline at the angle of the physical turnoff, with a yellow marker flagging the point where the turn begins so the pilot can start braking and steering before reaching it, then green lights carry that same visual thread onto the taxiway centreline.

Construction

Physical Construction

Green turnoff fixtures and, where fitted, yellow/amber caution fixtures are each built as separate die-cast aluminium units, anodised or powder-coated against fuel and de-icing fluid exposure, with the LED array or filtered halogen lamp sealed behind an optical prism to reach an IP67/IP68 rating. Flush-mounted installations seat each fixture in an L-868 base can rated for a 100,000 kg (220,000 lb) aircraft wheel load, set flush with the pavement across the runway entrance or along the turnoff path; elevated installations instead mount fixtures on a frangible coupling in an L-867 base can. The green and yellow fixtures are wired through separate 45W–65W and 15W–45W isolating transformers respectively, each housed in its own nearby base can, so the two colour circuits can be tested and faulted independently even though they're laid out together to form a single visual turnoff indication. Sealed L-823 splice connectors carry each circuit back to the primary series cable.

Installation Requirements

Base Can Excavation & Grade
Civil

Excavate and set the inset base can to match the surrounding runway and taxiway lighting at this transition, holding it flush with the pavement.

Boundary Line Set-out
Civil

Set out the fixture's green/yellow pattern precisely at the surveyed boundary line between the two pavements, matched to the specific runway exit design (rapid exit taxiways are angled and radiused for their exit speed) rather than a standard perpendicular intersection.

Runway/Taxiway Circuit Labelling
Documentation

Label runway and taxiway lighting circuits carefully at this location, since both high-speed runway circuits and taxiway circuits run in close proximity and a wiring error could leave the fixture following the wrong circuit's brightness steps or switching logic.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay the duct/trench to design depth, keeping the two circuit types physically separated and clearly identified along the route.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal the primary connector per manufacturer procedure, keeping the splice submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer wattage matches the fixture and the correct circuit's rated current step before connection.

Cross-Circuit Verification
Electrical

Trace and confirm the fixture is connected to its intended circuit (runway or taxiway) before energisation.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record base can position, circuit assignment, and connector seal as-built before backfill, verified against the specific runway exit design.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the series loop feeding the turnoff light row before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity through the fixtures marking the turnoff and record total loop resistance before first energisation.

Colour Segment Verification
Energisation

Confirm the green (taxiway) and yellow (runway boundary) segments illuminate correctly and are wired to the intended fixtures, not swapped.

Turn Geometry Alignment Check
Final Acceptance

Confirm the light row traces the as-designed turnoff geometry from runway centreline onto the taxiway, correcting any positional deviation.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity and colour for both green and yellow segments with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Final Acceptance

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm all fixtures along the turnoff respond together.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record loop resistance, colour-segment verification, and photometric readings alongside as-built turn geometry in the commissioning dossier.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric & Filter Check
Annual

Test green and yellow filter output for colour shift or reduction that could confuse pilots during the critical turnoff phase.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean lenses of rubber deposits, de-icing fluid, and dirt to maintain visibility during turnoff manoeuvres.

Alignment Check
Semi-annual

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Green/Yellow Filter Degradation

Both green and yellow filters in turnoff lights are susceptible to UV degradation and thermal stress. Over time, colour shift or reduced output can occur, potentially confusing pilots during the critical turnoff phase. Regular photometric testing and filter replacement is essential.

Lens Contamination

Turnoff lights are exposed to rubber deposits from aircraft tyres, de-icing fluids, and dirt. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and colour visibility during the critical turnoff phase.

Misalignment from Pavement Settlement

Pavement settlement or impact damage can cause turnoff lights to tilt, altering the beam pattern and reducing visibility for pilots. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Colour Shift or Reduced Output in Green or Yellow Segment
Likely Cause
UV degradation and thermal stress have affected the green or yellow filter, shifting colour or reducing light output during the critical turnoff phase.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour and intensity test on both filters against the applicable standard; visually inspect filters for discolouration or crazing.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded filter (or LED module where colour is produced electronically) and re-verify photometric compliance.
Reduced Visibility of the Turnoff Indication
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits, de-icing fluid, or dirt have contaminated the lens, scattering light and reducing colour clarity during the critical turnoff phase.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for film or contamination; take a photometric reading and compare against the expected output.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush per the manufacturer's procedure, and re-verify output after cleaning.
Beam Pattern Appears Tilted or Off-Axis
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement or impact damage has caused the fixture to tilt from its design aim.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture alignment with a digital level or manufacturer-specified alignment jig, and inspect the base for settlement.
Corrective Action
Re-aim and re-level the fixture to the design tolerance, and address any base or subgrade settlement causing the tilt.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Array / Isolating Transformer Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or a manufacturing defect in the LED array, or moisture-driven insulation breakdown in the isolating transformer winding.
Effect
The fixture goes dark, removing the advance flag of the turnoff point during the landing roll-out at exactly the phase it's needed.
Detection Method
Test activation of the fixture, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Yellow Lens Chromaticity Fade Reducing Advance-Warning Conspicuity
Medium Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure and thermal stress fading or shifting the yellow lens or filter over years of service.
Effect
The 'turnoff point flagged in advance' colour cue weakens, reducing how clearly it stands out against the runway centreline and edge lighting a pilot is also tracking during roll-out.
Detection Method
Chromaticity check against the applicable yellow boundary limits during scheduled photometric testing.
Aim / Alignment Drift Affecting Advance-Warning Legibility
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Settlement or an impact event shifting the fixture's aim out of its commissioned position.
Effect
Reduced conspicuity from the landing roll-out direction the fixture is specifically aimed to warn, at high speed with little time to react.
Detection Method
Fixture aim check against the runway centreline and turnoff geometry during scheduled inspection.
L-823 Connector / Series Circuit Insulation Breakdown
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress at the base can, gaskets, or connectors feeding the fixture.
Effect
Intermittent operation or a partial ground fault on the circuit serving the fixture.
Detection Method
Megger testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors and gaskets during maintenance.
Frangible / Housing Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind and wheel-load fatigue on the frangible coupling, or a strike from debris or ground equipment.
Effect
Fixture leans, misaligns, or separates from its mount, degrading or removing the advance-warning cue.
Detection Method
Coupling condition check during scheduled inspection, and post-strike inspection following any reported incident.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate All Circuits Feeding the Fixture
Electrical Safety

The green/yellow combination is typically fed by separate circuits; de-energise and lock out every CCR involved and verify zero energy before opening the base can.

Colour/Circuit Verification Before Restoration
Compliance

Confirm the green and yellow elements are reconnected to their correct circuits before re-energising; a swap at this transition point can misdirect a pilot during the high-speed runway-to-taxiway changeover.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

This fixture sits at an active runway-exit point; coordinate with ATC, raise a NOTAM if it will be dark or degraded, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the runway and turnoff path.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for while the base can is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture on a surface subject to high-speed rollout traffic.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test each affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or leave the turnoff point poorly defined.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE on series-circuit connections, and confirm the base can is bonded/grounded before reconnecting.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify correct colour, intensity, and aim before closing out the work; do not leave the runway/taxiway transition point ambiguous or non-compliant.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
High-Speed Rollout Leaves No Margin for a Colour Swap
Compliance

A green/yellow cross-connection at this transition point is far more consequential than at a low-speed junction, since the pilot reads the cue at high speed during rollout; treat colour/circuit verification at this location as a distinct, higher-scrutiny step rather than routine.

Flush Fit Matters as Much as Photometrics
Mechanical

An inset fixture sitting even slightly proud of grade becomes a snag or FOD risk under high-speed rollout traffic well before it fails photometrically; verify flush seating against the surrounding pavement grade at every reinstall, not just light output.

Trend Loop Readings, Don't Just Spot-Check
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at an L-823 connector usually shows up first as a slow drift in loop insulation resistance, not a hard fault. Logging megger readings over successive maintenance cycles catches a failing splice months before it takes the loop down.

Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED fixture is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual LED load rather than assuming the existing transformer is "close enough."

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven brightness even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

De-Icing Fluid Ages Seals Faster Than UV Alone
Durability

Repeated exposure to glycol-based de-icing fluid degrades gaskets and lens seals faster than sun and weather alone at a high-traffic runway exit point. Specify seal materials rated for glycol contact and inspect gasket condition at overhaul, not only at initial install.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about taxiway runway turnoff lights: colour, location, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a taxiway runway turnoff light?

It is a taxiway guidance fixture that marks the point where an aircraft leaves the runway onto a taxiway, or enters the runway from a taxiway. It gives a clear visual cue at that transition.

Q2What colour are runway turnoff lights?

Primarily green, indicating taxiway entry, with optional yellow or amber lights for additional cautionary cues.

Q3Where are runway turnoff lights installed?

At the point where the taxiway centreline meets the runway, typically inset in a row across the taxiway entrance or as a series leading from the runway centreline to the taxiway. They are used at rapid exit taxiways and busy or complex layouts.

Q4Do turnoff lights work in both directions?

Yes. The same location can serve traffic exiting and entering the runway, so the cue helps prevent runway incursions in either case.

Q5How bright are taxiway runway turnoff lights?

Green output is about 200 to 2,000 cd depending on the setting, with the main beam typically 100 to 1,000 cd. The optional yellow is about 100 to 500 cd.

Q6Which standards apply to taxiway runway turnoff lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46, IEC TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief 67 for LED light sources. Always verify the current edition.

Q7How often should turnoff lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly lens cleaning, semi-annual alignment checks and annual photometric and filter checks and housing and seal inspection.

Q8What are the common faults on turnoff lights?

Green and yellow filter degradation from UV and heat, lens contamination from tyre rubber and de-icing fluid, and beam misalignment from pavement settlement or impact damage.
