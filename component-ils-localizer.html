ILS Localizer

The ground-based antenna array that provides precise lateral guidance to aircraft during the final approach phase of an Instrument Landing System (ILS) approach. The localizer transmits a directional signal that indicates the runway centreline, enabling pilots to align the aircraft with the runway for a safe landing.

Overview

What Is It?

The ILS Localizer is a ground-based radio navigation system that forms the lateral guidance component of the Instrument Landing System (ILS). It consists of a phased array of antennas located beyond the far end of the runway (typically 300 to 600 metres from the runway end). The localizer transmits a modulated radio signal at a frequency between 108.10 MHz and 111.95 MHz. The signal creates two distinct lobes: one modulated at 90 Hz (on the left side of the runway centreline) and the other at 150 Hz (on the right side). The aircraft's receiver measures the difference in modulation depth between the two signals, providing the pilot with a deviation indication that guides them to the runway centreline.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The localizer's purpose is to keep the aircraft's track aligned with the extended runway centreline throughout the approach, a role that becomes critical precisely when visual cues that would normally confirm alignment, runway lighting, markings, the runway edge itself, are obscured by low visibility until very late in the approach. The two-lobe signal structure means any lateral drift away from centreline immediately produces an imbalance between the 90 Hz and 150 Hz modulation depths, giving the pilot a continuously updated correction cue rather than a single go/no-go indication. Because the localizer signal is usable from much further out than the point where visual contact with the runway is typically established, it is what allows the aircraft to be stabilised on the correct lateral track well before touchdown rather than making a late visual correction close to the ground.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The ILS Localizer is installed on precision approach runways that support ILS operations. It is a critical component for:

  • CAT I Precision Approaches: Providing lateral guidance down to a decision height of 200 feet (60 metres) with RVR of 550 metres or greater.
  • CAT II Precision Approaches: Providing lateral guidance down to a decision height of 100 feet (30 metres) with RVR of 350 metres or greater.
  • CAT III Precision Approaches: Providing lateral guidance down to a decision height as low as 15 feet (5 metres) with RVR as low as 200 metres (CAT IIIA), 50 metres (CAT IIIB), or even zero (CAT IIIC).
  • Military Airfields: Supporting military aircraft ILS operations.
  • Civil Airports: Supporting commercial and general aviation ILS approaches.
Placement

Installation & Location

The localizer antenna array is sited beyond the stop end of the runway, on the extended runway centreline, at a distance (typically 300-600 m past the far threshold) set so the course line it radiates is centred precisely on the runway. The array sits within a graded, cleared area free of fencing, vehicles, or vegetation that could distort the radiated pattern, and the site is chosen to keep the array clear of the runway end safety area while remaining within the obstacle-limitation surface for the approach it serves. The transmitter and monitoring equipment are housed in a shelter at the base of the array, fed from dual power supplies with automatic changeover, and linked by cable to a remote control and status panel in the ATC tower or navaid maintenance facility.

Function

How Does It Work?

The ILS Localizer operates on the principle of amplitude modulation and spatial signal distribution:

  • Signal Generation: The localizer transmitter generates an RF carrier signal at the assigned frequency. Two modulating signals (90 Hz and 150 Hz) are applied to the carrier.
  • Antenna Array: A multi-element antenna array (typically 8 to 24 radiating elements) creates a directional pattern with two overlapping lobes. The 90 Hz signal is predominant on the left side of the runway centreline, while the 150 Hz signal is predominant on the right side.
  • Course Line: Along the runway centreline (the "course line"), the modulation depths of the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals are equal. This results in a zero deviation indication on the aircraft's Instrument Landing System indicator (CDI).
  • Aircraft Reception: The aircraft's ILS receiver measures the difference between the two modulation depths. The difference is displayed as a deviation indication on the Course Deviation Indicator (CDI), showing the pilot whether the aircraft is left or right of the runway centreline.
  • Coverage: Per ICAO Annex 10, the localizer signal is usable to 25 nautical miles (46 km) within ±10° of the front course line, 17 NM (31 km) between 10° and 35°, and 10 NM (18.5 km) beyond that out to ±35°–45°. Where terrain or frequency congestion requires it, a reduced-coverage localizer (down to 18 NM / 33 km within ±10°) may be sited instead — this is a siting option, not a distinction between ILS categories.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The localizer facility is not remotely commanded in real time for its RF output — its control logic is a self-monitoring and protection loop. Integral near-field monitors sample the radiated signal directly at the antenna array and check course alignment, displacement sensitivity and RF power against tolerance, with some installations adding a far-field monitor for independent confirmation. Dual-equipment sites run online and standby transmitters, and the monitor logic executes an automatic changeover to standby on an out-of-tolerance reading; if neither channel can meet tolerance, the facility shuts itself down and radiates no signal rather than risk erroneous lateral guidance. Status, alarms and changeover events are reported to the airport's remote control and monitoring system for ATC and maintenance visibility.

Construction

Physical Construction

The localizer antenna array, a phased arrangement of 8 to 24 radiating elements, is mounted on a frangible support structure beyond the far end of the runway, with each element individually fed and phased from a weatherproof equipment shelter through carefully matched-length coaxial cable runs to maintain course accuracy. The support structure is designed to break away cleanly if struck by an aircraft during an overrun, similar to elevated runway light fixtures, while still holding the array rigidly enough to preserve the precise element spacing and height the system depends on. The equipment shelter houses the dual redundant VHF transmitters, 90 Hz/150 Hz modulation electronics, and continuous course-monitoring equipment in a temperature-controlled, weatherproof cabinet consistent with other ILS ground equipment. Feeder cables between the shelter and antenna array are sealed and often buried or ducted for protection, and automatic monitoring watches course alignment and signal strength, switching to the standby transmitter and raising a remote alarm if performance drifts outside tolerance.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Antenna Array Misalignment

Physical displacement or misalignment of the antenna array can cause the localizer course line to shift, resulting in a false centreline indication. Regular alignment checks using specialized survey equipment and calibration aircraft are essential.

Multipath Interference

Reflections from buildings, terrain, aircraft, and vehicles near the localizer site can cause multipath interference, distorting the signal and creating false deviation indications. Proper site selection and obstruction clearance are critical.

Modulation Balance Drift

Over time, the modulation balance between the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals can drift, causing the course line to shift. Regular monitoring and calibration using flight inspection is required to maintain accuracy.

Power Amplifier Failure

The transmitter power amplifiers are subject to wear and thermal stress. Regular maintenance and redundancy (hot standby) are essential to prevent loss of the ILS service.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Flight Inspection Reports Course Line Shift
Likely Cause
Physical displacement or misalignment of the antenna array, often from ground settlement, storm damage, or unauthorised work near the site.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the antenna array's physical alignment against the last certified survey; inspect the mounting structure and mast for movement, corrosion, or damage; review recent flight inspection trend data for a gradual versus sudden shift.
Corrective Action
Re-align the antenna array to the certified position using survey equipment, and confirm correction with a follow-up calibration flight before returning the facility to unrestricted service.
Course Roughness or Bends Reported by Aircraft
Likely Cause
Multipath interference from buildings, terrain, aircraft, or vehicles near the localizer site distorting the reflected signal.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the obstruction clearance survey for recent changes near the site; check for parked vehicles, new structures, or vegetation growth within the critical/sensitive area; correlate reported roughness with aircraft type or approach direction if possible.
Corrective Action
Remove or relocate the offending obstruction, enforce critical-area vehicle/equipment restrictions, and confirm resolution with a flight check.
Course Line Drifting Off Centreline
Likely Cause
Modulation balance between the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals has drifted due to component ageing in the transmitter.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the current 90 Hz/150 Hz modulation depth and balance against the monitor readout; review monitor alarm history for a gradual trend versus a sudden step change.
Corrective Action
Adjust modulation balance per the maintenance manual and verify with flight inspection; if drift is rapid or recurring, investigate the modulator/transmitter stage for a developing fault rather than just re-trimming.
Transmitter Trips to Standby or Off-Air
Likely Cause
Power amplifier failure under thermal stress or age-related component wear in the primary transmitter.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the monitor alarm log to identify which parameter (power, modulation, course) triggered the changeover; check power amplifier output level and temperature; confirm standby unit is radiating correctly within tolerance.
Corrective Action
Service or replace the faulted power amplifier stage before returning the primary unit to service, and re-verify with a calibration/monitor check; do not leave the facility running on standby longer than necessary.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

RF Exposure Around the Antenna Array
Personnel Safety

Follow the facility's RF safety procedure and any posted exclusion distance when working near the radiating antenna array, and confirm the transmitter is placed in maintenance/standby mode before working at or near the array.

Transmitter Shelter Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the transmitter before working inside the equipment shelter, and verify zero energy before disturbing internal connections; RF power amplifier stages can carry hazardous voltages that remain present briefly after shutdown.

Keep the Critical Area Clear
Operational

Keep vehicles, personnel, and equipment out of the localizer critical area during low-visibility operations or whenever the facility is radiating for an aircraft on approach, since reflections from an obstruction there can distort the course line in flight.

Frangibility of the Array Structure
Mechanical

Where the array or its supporting fence is of frangible construction, do not defeat that design during repair or reinforcement; the structure must still fail predictably if struck to limit damage to an aircraft.

NOTAM for Service Interruption
Operational

Issue a NOTAM before taking the localizer out of service or below tolerance for maintenance, since aircraft on an ILS approach rely on it for lateral guidance.

Verify Monitor Function Before Return to Service
Compliance

Confirm the near-field (and far-field, where fitted) monitor correctly detects an out-of-tolerance alignment, sensitivity, or power reading and executes changeover or shutdown before returning the facility to service after maintenance.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate PPE and confirm the array, shelter, and feeder are bonded to the facility earth system and lightning protection before working on internal connections.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the facility radiating an out-of-tolerance course, modulation, or power reading after maintenance; verify monitored parameters are within tolerance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Large Aircraft Parked Near the Antenna Distort the Pattern
Siting

A localizer course alarm that appears only when a particular gate or holding position is occupied by a large aircraft is usually a critical-area incursion effect, not an equipment fault. Correlate alarm timing with ground movement before troubleshooting the transmitter.

Sideband Antenna Imbalance Shows Up as Modulation Drift First
Maintenance

A slow imbalance between the clearance and course sideband elements typically appears as a gradual modulation percentage trend well before it becomes an alarm condition. Trending modulation readings across checks catches this early rather than waiting for a hard fault.

Terrain and Building Changes Beyond the Antenna Matter
Environment

New construction, vegetation growth, or even seasonal crop height in the localizer's critical and sensitive areas can introduce multipath that a ground monitor doesn't fully capture. Periodic site surveys of the surrounding terrain belong alongside electronic checks, not instead of them.

Ground Monitor Pass Doesn't Guarantee In-Space Quality
Verification

Near-field ground monitoring can miss far-field multipath and bend effects that show up only on an aircraft receiver during approach. Treat a flight inspection anomaly as new information rather than assuming the ground check result was more reliable.

Check Full Parameters After Any Surge Event
Troubleshooting

A facility that returns to service normally after a lightning event or power surge can still carry subtle course or clearance degradation in a single transmitter module. Run a complete parameter verification after any surge event rather than relying on the automatic monitor status alone.

Log Seasonal Patterns Separately From Genuine Faults
Process

Some sites show a repeatable, benign seasonal shift in monitored course parameters tied to ground moisture or foliage cycles. Keeping a season-aware log helps distinguish that pattern from a genuine equipment drift that actually needs corrective maintenance.
