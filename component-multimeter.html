Multimeter

The most fundamental and versatile diagnostic instrument in any AGL technician's toolkit. A multimeter measures voltage, current, resistance, continuity, capacitance, and frequency — enabling rapid troubleshooting of electrical circuits, verifying power supplies, and diagnosing faults in runway, taxiway, and approach lighting systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Multimeter (also known as a volt-ohm-milliammeter or VOM) is an electronic measuring instrument that combines several measurement functions in one unit. It is the primary diagnostic tool used by AGL technicians for electrical testing and troubleshooting. A typical multimeter can measure:

  • AC and DC Voltage – verifying supply voltages at CCRs, distribution panels, and fixtures
  • AC and DC Current – measuring circuit currents (typically up to 10A direct, higher with clamp attachments)
  • Resistance – checking cable continuity, transformer winding integrity, and contact resistance
  • Continuity – audible beep for quick cable and connection testing
  • Capacitance – testing capacitors in LED drivers and power supplies
  • Frequency – measuring AC frequency for power quality assessment
  • Diode Test – checking LED and semiconductor junctions

Modern multimeters are available in both digital (DMM) and analog formats, with digital versions offering higher accuracy, auto-ranging, and data logging capabilities. They are the first line of defense for electrical fault diagnosis in AGL systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The multimeter exists to give an AGL technician the fastest possible first read on an electrical problem — is there voltage where there should be, is a circuit continuous, is a component within spec — without needing a different specialised instrument for every individual measurement. In a fault-finding situation, especially one affecting live airfield lighting, the speed of that first diagnostic step directly determines how quickly the actual root cause can be identified and the circuit restored.

Its versatility across voltage, current, resistance, continuity, and component-level tests is what makes it the default starting point for troubleshooting: a technician can narrow down whether a fault lies in the supply, the wiring, or the fixture itself using one tool, before reaching for the more specialised instruments needed to pin down the exact cause.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Multimeters are used across all AGL systems for measurement and troubleshooting:

  • CCR Output Verification: Measuring AC voltage and current at CCR terminals to confirm proper output.
  • Power Supply Testing: Verifying 230V/415V supply at distribution panels, UPS outputs, and generator feeds.
  • Circuit Continuity Testing: Checking cable integrity from CCR to field junction boxes and fixtures.
  • Transformer Testing: Measuring primary and secondary voltages, winding resistances, and continuity.
  • Fixture Troubleshooting: Checking voltage at fixture terminals, testing LED driver output, and verifying lamp connections.
  • Control Circuit Testing: Measuring control voltages in ALCMS, PLC, and remote I/O systems.
  • Safety Verification: Confirming circuits are de-energized before maintenance using the voltage measurement function.
  • Battery and Power Supply Checks: Testing backup batteries and UPS systems for proper voltage and charge.

From the CCR room to the runway edge, the multimeter is the technician's most frequently used test instrument.

Care

Storage & Handling

The meter is stored with its rotary selector set to the off position (or to voltage, per the manufacturer's recommendation) to protect the internal circuitry, and test leads are coiled loosely and kept in the case's lead compartment rather than wrapped tightly around the meter body. Fuses are checked periodically, since a blown current-measurement fuse is one of the more common reasons a meter appears to fail when in fact only the fuse needs replacing, and a spare fuse of the correct rating is kept with the instrument. Batteries are checked before significant field use and removed if the meter will sit unused for an extended period, avoiding corrosion from a leaking cell. Being the most frequently used tool in the kit, the meter is inspected for lead and probe damage before each use, and any meter that has been dropped or exposed to moisture is checked against a known reference before being relied on for further diagnosis.

Function

How Does It Work?

A multimeter operates through a combination of analog-to-digital conversion (in digital models) and precision measurement circuits. The user selects the desired measurement function and range using a rotary switch or push buttons. Key operational principles include:

  • Voltage Measurement: The meter applies a high-impedance input (typically 10 MΩ) across the test points and measures the voltage drop. AC voltage is measured using True RMS detection for accurate readings on non-sinusoidal waveforms.
  • Current Measurement: The meter is placed in series with the circuit (requiring circuit interruption) and measures the voltage drop across a precision shunt resistor.
  • Resistance Measurement: The meter applies a small known current through the resistor under test and measures the resulting voltage drop, calculating resistance using Ohm's Law.
  • Continuity Test: A low-resistance measurement with an audible beep for quick verification of connections (typically < 30 Ω).
  • Auto-Ranging: The meter automatically selects the most appropriate range for the measured value, simplifying operation and improving accuracy.
  • Data Hold: Freezes the current reading on the display for easy recording.

For AGL applications, a multimeter with True RMS capability is recommended to accurately measure the non-sinusoidal currents often found in LED lighting systems and electronic drivers.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Behind the rotary switch, the meter is a signal-routing and decision chain rather than four separate instruments. Test leads first pass through input protection — fuses in series with the current jacks and a PTC/varistor network on the voltage input — before reaching an analog multiplexer that the function/range switch positions mechanically or electronically. Depending on the function selected, the multiplexer routes the signal through an attenuator (voltage), a precision shunt (current), or an internal reference current source (resistance) so that every function ultimately presents a proportional voltage to the analog-to-digital converter. The microcontroller (MCU) reads the ADC, applies stored calibration constants, and — on auto-ranging models — compares the digitised value against range thresholds each cycle: too high, it steps the range switch up; too low, it steps down, then re-reads before committing a value to the display. Continuity and diode-test functions reuse this same ADC path but add a threshold comparison that drives the audible buzzer. Data Hold does not freeze the measurement itself, only the MCU's display refresh, so what is shown is whatever was last converted before the button was pressed.

Every function shares the same ADC-to-MCU path; only the conditioning stage before it changes. The MCU's auto-range decision is a closed loop back to the function/range switch, not a one-time selection.
Operating Principle

One Selector, Four Measurement Circuits

The same rotary or button selector routes the test leads into one of four distinct internal circuits. For AGL work, True RMS voltage and current measurement matters most, since LED drivers and electronic ballasts draw non-sinusoidal currents that an averaging meter would misread.
Construction

Physical Construction

The multimeter is built into a compact, hand-held polymer case with an over-moulded rubber holster to absorb drops and provide a secure grip during field measurements. Input jacks for voltage, current, resistance, and capacitance measurements are recessed and colour-coded, with physical spacing and shrouding designed to meet CAT III/CAT IV safety clearances when working on live AGL circuits. The rotary function selector and range switches use a sealed, detented mechanism to give positive, tactile confirmation of the selected mode, while internal high-energy fuses protect the current-measurement inputs against accidental overload. A backlit LCD sits behind an impact-resistant window, and the battery compartment is accessed via a separate rear cover, isolated from the sealed measurement circuitry, so routine battery changes don't compromise the meter's protection rating.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Function or Range Selection

Selecting the wrong measurement function (e.g., measuring voltage on the resistance setting) can damage the meter or cause injury. Always double-check the selector switch before making measurements.

Test Lead Integrity

Damaged or worn test leads can introduce errors or create safety hazards. Regular inspection of leads for cuts, cracks, or exposed wires is essential. Leads should be replaced if any damage is found.

Battery Depletion

Low battery voltage can cause inaccurate readings, especially on resistance and capacitance measurements. Always check the battery status before critical measurements and replace as needed.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

No Display / Meter Won't Power On
Likely Cause
Depleted battery, a blown internal fuse, or a failed display module.
Diagnostic Steps
Check battery voltage and replace if low; open the case and inspect fuses against the rated value; confirm the display shows a startup self-test sequence.
Corrective Action
Replace the battery or fuse with the correct rated part, and return the meter for repair if the display remains dead after a fresh battery and fuse.
Readings Appear Inaccurate or Unstable
Likely Cause
Low battery affecting resistance/capacitance accuracy, damaged test leads, or the wrong function/range selected for the measurement.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify battery status; inspect test leads for cuts, cracks, or exposed wire; confirm the selector switch matches the intended measurement; cross-check against a known reference value.
Corrective Action
Replace the battery or leads as needed, correct the function/range selection, and re-verify readings against a known reference before relying on the meter.
"OL" or Overrange Indication on a Known-Good Circuit
Likely Cause
Wrong range or function selected for the circuit being measured, or an internal fuse has blown from a prior overcurrent event.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the selector switch and input jacks match the expected measurement type and range; check the relevant internal fuse.
Corrective Action
Select the correct function/range, or replace the blown fuse with the correctly rated type before resuming measurements.
No Continuity Beep or Erratic Continuity Function
Likely Cause
Damaged or intermittent test leads, or dirty/worn input jacks preventing a solid connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Flex the leads while testing a known short circuit to check for intermittent breaks; inspect and clean input jacks.
Corrective Action
Replace worn or damaged test leads, and clean or repair input jacks showing poor contact.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Match Meter and Lead Category Rating to the Circuit
Electrical Safety

Confirm the multimeter and test leads carry a CAT rating and voltage rating adequate for the circuit being tested before connecting; using an under-rated meter on a higher-energy circuit is a serious arc-flash hazard.

Select the Correct Function and Range First
Electrical Safety

Set the correct function (voltage, current, resistance) and range before connecting test leads to the circuit, and never measure resistance or continuity on an energised circuit.

Never Measure Current in a High-Voltage Series Loop
Electrical Safety

Do not attempt to measure current directly in an AGL series circuit with a standard multimeter; use an appropriately rated clamp meter or a current transformer method instead, since breaking a live series loop is hazardous.

Check Fuses Before Trusting a Current Reading
Operational

Verify the meter's internal fuse is intact before relying on a current-mode reading of zero, since a blown fuse can silently mask a live circuit.

PPE for Live Measurements
Personnel Safety

Wear appropriate PPE and use one-handed measurement technique where practical when taking voltage readings on an energised circuit.

Inspect Leads and Jacks Before Use
Operational

Check test leads and input jacks for damage, cracked insulation, or worn tips before each use, and replace damaged leads rather than continuing to use them.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Category Rating Is About the Circuit, Not the Meter's Price Tag
Personnel Safety

A more expensive meter isn't automatically safer for a given task; what matters is whether its CAT rating and leads match the specific circuit being measured. Check the CAT rating against the actual point in the electrical system being tested every time, not just once when the meter was issued.

A Blown Fuse Reads as a Real-Looking Zero
Troubleshooting

A meter with a blown internal fuse on the current range will often display a clean zero rather than an obvious error, which can be misread as "no current flowing" when the actual circuit is live and functioning. Verify the meter's fuse against a known live source before trusting a zero current reading in a troubleshooting scenario.

Wrong-Range Selection Is the Most Common Setup Mistake
Operational

Selecting the wrong function or range before connecting to a circuit is one of the most frequent causes of a damaged meter or a blown fuse in the field, and it's an easy mistake to make when moving quickly between tasks. Get in the habit of confirming function and range as a distinct step before every connection, not just when working on an unfamiliar circuit.

Current Mode on a Series Circuit Is an Easy Trap
Electrical Safety

Leaving a meter in current mode from a previous task and then connecting it across a high-voltage series circuit as if measuring voltage effectively creates a near dead short through the meter's low-resistance current path. Make returning the meter to voltage mode a habitual last step after every current measurement, not an occasional afterthought.

Lead Damage Often Starts Internally, Invisible at the Jacket
Maintenance

A test lead can develop an internal conductor break or intermittent connection well before any damage is visible on the outer insulation, especially after repeated flexing near the plug end. Periodically test lead continuity and resistance directly rather than relying solely on a visual inspection to judge lead condition.

Verify the Meter Before and After a Critical Measurement
Compliance

Checking the meter against a known live source both before and after a critical reading, not just before, catches the case where the meter fails or its fuse blows partway through the task and produces a false result on the very measurement being relied on for a safety decision.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about multimeters in AGL maintenance: what they measure, safety ratings, accuracy, standards, calibration and common faults.

Q1What is a multimeter?

A multimeter, also called a volt-ohm-milliammeter (VOM), is an electronic measuring instrument that combines several functions in one unit. It is the primary diagnostic tool AGL technicians use for electrical testing and troubleshooting.

Q2What can a multimeter measure in an AGL system?

AC and DC voltage, AC and DC current (up to 10 A direct, higher with clamp attachments), resistance, continuity, capacitance and frequency. That covers supply voltages at CCRs and panels, cable continuity, transformer winding integrity and capacitors in LED drivers and power supplies.

Q3Why is a multimeter the first tool used in fault-finding?

It gives the fastest first read on a problem: whether voltage is present, a circuit is continuous or a component is within spec, without needing a specialised instrument for each measurement. On live airfield lighting, the speed of that first step affects how quickly the root cause is found.

Q4Where is a multimeter used in AGL?

CCR output verification, power supply testing at distribution panels, UPS outputs and generator feeds, circuit continuity from the CCR to junction boxes and fixtures, transformer primary and secondary checks and fixture troubleshooting, including LED driver output.

Q5How accurate is a multimeter?

DC voltage accuracy is typically ±0.05% to ±0.5% of reading, AC voltage (True RMS) ±1% to ±2%, and current ±1% to ±2%. Voltage input impedance is typically 10 MΩ.

Q6What ranges does a typical multimeter cover?

Voltage from 0.1 mV to 1,000 V, current from 0.1 µA to 10 A direct, resistance from 0.1 Ω to 60 MΩ, capacitance from 1 pF to 100 mF and frequency from 10 Hz to 100 kHz, usually with auto-ranging.

Q7What safety rating should an AGL multimeter have?

CAT III 600 V / CAT IV 300 V, with fused current inputs and overvoltage protection to the rated category. Test leads must also be rated to the instrument's CAT rating.

Q8Which standards apply to multimeters?

The main references are IEC 61010-1 (safety for electrical test and measurement equipment), EN 61326 (EMC) and IEC 61557 (test instruments for low voltage distribution systems). Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a multimeter be checked and calibrated?

Typical intervals are before each use for function and range selector checks, quarterly for test lead inspection, fuse and battery checks, and annually for calibration against traceable references. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is my multimeter blank or won't power on?

A depleted battery, a blown internal fuse or a failed display. Replace the battery, check fuses against the rated value, and return the meter for repair if the display stays dead after a fresh battery and fuse.

Q11Why are my readings inaccurate or unstable?

A low battery affecting resistance and capacitance accuracy, damaged test leads, or the wrong function or range selected. Check the battery, inspect the leads for cuts or exposed wire, confirm the selector setting and cross-check against a known reference value.
