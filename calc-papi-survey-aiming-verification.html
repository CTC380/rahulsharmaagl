Calculator #62 · Safety-Critical

PAPI Survey / Aiming Verification

Measured aiming angles and unit elevations checked per unit against the design settings and the aiming tolerance — with the effective glide path, on-slope sector and indication bands that result.

About this calculator

Set the nominal glide-path angle and the design setting of each unit, then enter what was measured: the angle read from an inclinometer or theodolite, or the height at which the red/white transition was observed on a staff at a known distance. The tool reduces every unit to an aiming angle, compares it with its design angle, checks unit elevations, optional azimuth and unit order, and shows the indication bands a pilot would actually see. Unit 1 (P1) is always the unit nearest the runway.

Method & Reference

How This Calculator Works

Every unit is reduced to one number, its aiming angle above horizontal. Design angle, deviation, effective glide path and indication bands all follow from that.

Aiming angle

From an inclinometer or theodolite:

α = reading − index error + housing offset

From a staff observed at horizontal distance L, with the transition seen at elevation Et and the unit’s optical centre at Eo:

α = atan( (Et − Eo) / L )

Corrections for index error and housing offset apply to direct readings only. Earth curvature and refraction are ignored, which is acceptable at the short distances used for staff observations.

Deviation and effective glide path

Deviation = measured − design angle, in minutes of arc, positive when the unit is aimed high. The effective glide path is the mean of the middle two measured angles (the middle one for an odd count) and is compared with the nominal θ.

Δh = d × ( tanαeff − tanθ )

Δh is the height error of the on-slope indication at distance d.

Indication bands

With the measured angles sorted lowest to highest, the band with w white lights lies between the w-th and (w+1)-th angle. Comparing each band edge with its design edge shows how the on-slope sector has moved or narrowed.

Terminology kept separate

Aiming angle (one unit’s optical setting), glide-path angle (the resulting on-slope path), design setting (offset from θ) and deviation (measured minus design) are different quantities. P1 is the unit nearest the runway and has the highest setting; ICAO labels the units A to D from the farthest, so A = P4. Unit position and spacing are checked in the PAPI Unit Separation & Lateral Geometry tool.

What this calculator does not do

It does not replace a flight inspection, check beam cut-off sharpness, intensity, colour or the obstacle clearance surface, and it cannot tell whether an instrument is calibrated. It does not correct the aiming angles for runway longitudinal gradient or cross-slope; apply the correction your standard or the manufacturer requires and enter the corrected nominal angle θ. A PASS means the measured values are within the tolerances entered, not that the PAPI is commissioned.

FAQ

PAPI Survey and Aiming Verification Calculator: Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers on how measured PAPI aiming angles are checked against the design settings.

What does the PAPI Survey and Aiming Verification Calculator do?

It reduces each PAPI unit to one number, its aiming angle above horizontal, and compares it with the design setting. It reports the deviation of each unit, the effective glide path, the indication bands as measured against the design bands, elevation and azimuth checks, and gives a chart, CSV export and PDF report.

How is the aiming angle measured and calculated?

From an inclinometer or theodolite, α = reading − index error + housing offset. From a staff observed at horizontal distance L, with the transition seen at elevation Et and the unit's optical centre at Eo, α = atan((Et − Eo) ÷ L). Index error and housing offset corrections apply to direct readings only, and earth curvature and refraction are ignored at the short distances used for staff observations.

What design settings does it start with?

The common 4-unit pattern, inner unit first, is θ + 30′, +10′, −10′ and −30′. A 2-unit APAPI uses ±15′ and a 3-unit pattern uses +20′, 0 and −20′ as a placeholder. These are editable starting points, and you should confirm every setting and tolerance against the current edition of the governing standard, the manufacturer's instructions and the approved design.

How is deviation and the effective glide path worked out?

Deviation = measured − design angle, in minutes of arc, positive when the unit is aimed high. The effective glide path is the mean of the middle two measured angles (the middle one for an odd count) and is compared with the nominal θ. The height error of the on-slope indication at distance d is Δh = d × (tan αeff − tan θ).

How are the indication bands built?

With the measured angles sorted from lowest to highest, the band with w white lights lies between the w-th and (w+1)-th angle. Comparing each band edge with its design edge shows how the on-slope sector has moved or narrowed. Heights are given above the optical-centre plane at the reference distance you enter.

Are the tolerances standard values?

No. The pre-filled values are placeholders, not standard figures. They are ±5′ for the aiming angle, 0.05 m for the elevation tolerance and height spread, and a blank azimuth tolerance, which means it is not checked. A blank tolerance switches that check off, so replace the values with those from your governing standard or project specification. The verdict cannot show PASS while the aiming-angle tolerance is blank.

Do I need to correct for runway gradient or cross-slope?

Yes, outside the tool. It does not correct the aiming angles for runway longitudinal gradient or cross-slope. Apply the correction your standard or the manufacturer requires and enter the corrected nominal angle θ.

Does a PASS replace a flight inspection?

No. A PASS means the measured values are within the tolerances entered. The calculator does not replace a flight inspection, check beam cut-off sharpness, intensity, colour or the obstacle clearance surface, and it cannot tell whether an instrument is calibrated. Unit position and spacing are checked in the PAPI Unit Separation and Lateral Geometry tool.
