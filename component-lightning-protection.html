Lightning Protection

A comprehensive system of conductors, air terminals, down conductors, and earthing components designed to protect Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) infrastructure from the damaging effects of direct lightning strikes and induced surges. Lightning protection is essential for ensuring the safety of personnel, safeguarding sensitive electronic equipment, and maintaining the operational integrity of airfield lighting systems.

Overview

What Is It?

Lightning Protection for AGL systems is a holistic approach that encompasses both external (structural) and internal (electrical) protection measures. The system is designed to:

  • Intercept direct lightning strikes via air terminals (lightning rods) installed on structures such as CCR buildings, control towers, and equipment shelters.
  • Conduct the lightning current safely to ground using down conductors and earth termination systems.
  • Prevent side‑flashing and sparking by ensuring proper bonding and equipotentialisation of all metallic parts.
  • Protect sensitive AGL electronics (CCRs, ALCMS, LED drivers, communication systems) from induced surges and transient overvoltages caused by nearby lightning strikes.
  • Ensure personal safety for maintenance personnel and airfield staff during lightning events.

A typical AGL lightning protection system includes air terminals, down conductors, earth electrodes, surge protection devices, and a comprehensive bonding network. The design follows international standards such as IEC 62305, NFPA 780, and FAA Advisory Circulars.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Lightning protection's purpose is to manage a threat that cannot be prevented — a direct or nearby lightning strike — by controlling where its energy goes and how much of it reaches sensitive AGL equipment, rather than leaving that outcome to chance. Air terminals and down conductors give a strike a preferred, engineered path to ground instead of an uncontrolled path through a building or piece of equipment, while the internal surge protection cascade absorbs and clamps the transient energy that still couples onto power and data lines even when the direct strike itself is safely diverted. Because a single lightning event can otherwise destroy CCRs, ALCMS electronics, and LED drivers across an entire airfield simultaneously, this layered external-and-internal approach exists to keep an isolated weather event from becoming a widespread, expensive equipment failure — or a hazard to personnel working nearby.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Lightning protection is applied throughout the AGL infrastructure:

  • CCR Rooms and Substations: Protecting the building structure, internal power distribution, and control equipment from direct strikes and surges.
  • ALCMS and Communication Shelters: Protecting sensitive electronic racks, servers, and communication links.
  • Lighting Control Panels (LCPs): Outdoor panels housing MCBs, contactors, and control relays require external and internal protection.
  • Airfield Lighting Circuits: Series circuits (primary and secondary) are protected from induced surges through SPDs and proper earthing.
  • Obstruction Lighting Structures: Towers, masts, and chimneys equipped with AGL obstruction lights must have dedicated lightning protection.
  • Apron Floodlight Masts: Tall structures supporting floodlights and camera systems require air terminals and down conductors.
  • Wind Cones and Sensor Masts: Meteorological equipment on exposed masts must be protected.
Placement

Installation & Location

Air terminals are mounted at the highest points of the structures they protect — rooftops of CCR rooms and shelters, the tops of obstruction masts, floodlight poles, and sensor masts — positioned and spaced according to the rolling-sphere or protection-angle method so the whole structure and any equipment on it falls within the protected zone. Down conductors are run by the shortest, most direct route from each air terminal to its earth termination, avoiding sharp bends, and are bonded to structural steelwork and metallic services along the way to prevent side-flashing. Surge protection devices are installed at the point cables enter a building or cabinet — main distribution boards, LCPs, and ALCMS racks — as close as practical to the incoming terminals, with the earth pit for the whole system positioned so the down-conductor and SPD earth paths bond back into the same low-resistance earthing network.

Function

How Does It Work?

The lightning protection system operates through a combination of external and internal protection measures:

  • External Lightning Protection:
    • Air Terminals (Lightning Rods): Installed at the highest points of structures, they intercept the lightning strike and provide a preferential strike point.
    • Down Conductors: Heavy‑gauge copper or aluminum conductors that carry the lightning current from the air terminal to the earth termination system.
    • Earth Termination (Earthing): A network of earth electrodes (rods, plates, or grids) that dissipates the lightning current safely into the ground, preventing dangerous step and touch voltages.
    • Bonding: All metallic parts (structural steel, cable trays, equipment enclosures) are bonded together to prevent side‑flashing and ensure equipotentialisation.
  • Internal Lightning Protection (Surge Protection):
    • SPD Cascade: A coordinated cascade of Surge Protection Devices (Type 1, Type 2, Type 3) is installed at the main distribution, sub‑distribution, and equipment levels to progressively reduce surge energy.
    • Equipotential Bonding: All incoming services (power, data, telecom) are bonded to the main earthing terminal to prevent potential differences.
    • Shielded Cables: Use of shielded cables for control and communication lines reduces the coupling of induced surges.
  • Risk Assessment: The design is based on a risk assessment (following IEC 62305‑2) that evaluates the lightning strike frequency, exposure, and consequences to determine the required protection level (I–IV).
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The lightning protection system is architecturally layered rather than centrally controlled: air terminals and down-conductors form a passive external network with no logic at all, while the internal surge protection is a coordinated cascade of SPDs (Type 1 at the main distribution board, Type 2 at sub-distribution, Type 3 at sensitive equipment) sized so that each stage progressively clamps and absorbs surge energy the previous stage let through, protecting the next device down the chain. There is no active decision-making in this cascade — each SPD simply conducts once its clamping voltage is exceeded — but many modern SPDs include a mechanical or electronic end-of-life indicator and a volt-free alarm contact, which can be wired back to a local panel, a building management system, or the ALCMS to report that a particular SPD stage has been exhausted by a surge event and needs replacement. Because the earth termination network underlies both the external and internal protection, its resistance is verified on the same testing schedule as the rest of the earthing system rather than monitored continuously.

Construction

Physical Construction

The system is built as a chain of physical elements rather than a single fixture: air terminals (copper, aluminium, or stainless-steel rods or meshed conductors) positioned at the highest points of a structure or mast to intercept a strike, down conductors of adequately sized copper or aluminium cable routed by the shortest practical path to ground, and earth electrodes — rods, plates, or ring earths — bonded at the base to dissipate the strike current into the soil. Down conductors are clipped or strapped to the structure with corrosion-resistant fixings, keeping a minimum isolation distance from other metalwork and cabling to avoid dangerous side-flashing, and bonding conductors tie every nearby metallic system (rails, ducting, cable armour) back to a common earthing busbar so no part of the installation sits at a different potential during a strike. Surge protection devices are fitted at points where power and data cabling enter equipment, housed in their own enclosures or DIN-rail modules within existing cabinets, and test links are built into the down-conductor run at ground level so the electrode resistance can be measured without disturbing the rest of the system.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Earth Electrode Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Measure the resistance of each earth electrode in the system using the fall-of-potential method, confirming the design target (typically ≤ 10Ω) is met before conductors are connected.

Down Conductor Continuity Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity and resistance of each down conductor from air terminal to earth electrode, ruling out breaks or high-resistance joints before commissioning.

Bonding Conductor Continuity Test
Energisation

Test continuity of all bonding conductors to the main earthing busbar, confirming equipotential bonding is achieved across the protected structure.

Surge Protection Device (SPD) Function Test
Energisation

Verify each Type 1/2/3 SPD is correctly installed, rated for its location, and shows healthy status indication (where fitted) before the protected circuits are energised.

Isolation Distance Verification
Final Acceptance

Confirm the physical separation between lightning protection conductors and other services meets the calculated isolation distance for the system's protection level.

Air Terminal Placement & Zone of Protection Verification
Final Acceptance

Survey air terminal positions against the design rolling-sphere or mesh method calculation, confirming the protected structure and equipment fall within the intended zone of protection.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record electrode resistance, conductor continuity, SPD status, and isolation-distance verification alongside as-built terminal and conductor positions in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Inadequate Earthing (High Resistance)

High earth resistance (> 10Ω) prevents effective dissipation of lightning current, leading to dangerous step voltages and equipment damage. Regular earthing testing and improvement (e.g., adding more rods, using chemical earthing) are essential.

Corrosion of Conductors and Connections

Copper and aluminium conductors are susceptible to corrosion, particularly in coastal or humid environments. Use of corrosion‑resistant materials, proper terminations, and regular inspection are critical.

Inadequate Bonding of Metallic Parts

Failure to bond all metallic parts (cable trays, equipment enclosures, structural steel) can result in side‑flashing and sparking, creating fire hazards and damaging sensitive electronics. Ensure comprehensive equipotential bonding.

SPD Degradation and End‑of‑Life

Surge Protection Devices have a limited lifespan and degrade with each surge event. Regular inspection of status indicators and timely replacement are essential to maintain protection.

Lightning Strike Damage to AGL Fixtures

Direct or nearby strikes can damage AGL fixtures, particularly those on elevated structures (runway edge lights, approach lights). Use of spark gaps, surge‑protected LED drivers, and proper earthing of fixture mounting hardware is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Earth Resistance Test Exceeds 10Ω
Likely Cause
Soil conditions, insufficient electrode count, or corrosion have raised earth resistance beyond the safe dissipation threshold.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-test using the fall-of-potential method; inspect earthing rods and connections for corrosion; review the number and spacing of rods against design.
Corrective Action
Add earthing rods, apply chemical earthing treatment, or replace corroded electrodes, then re-test to confirm resistance is back under 10Ω.
Visible Corrosion on Down-Conductors or Terminations
Likely Cause
Coastal or humid conditions attacking copper or aluminium conductors and their terminations.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect conductors and terminations along their full run; measure resistance across suspect terminations.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded conductors or terminations with corrosion-resistant materials and properly rated terminations.
Evidence of Sparking Between Metallic Parts
Likely Cause
A metallic part (cable tray, enclosure, structural steel) was not properly bonded into the equipotential system.
Diagnostic Steps
Survey all metallic structures near the protected zone and verify each has a bonding connection; check bond continuity with a low-resistance ohmmeter.
Corrective Action
Install or repair the missing bonding connection, and re-verify continuity across the full equipotential system.
SPD Status Indicator Shows Fault or End-of-Life
Likely Cause
The Surge Protection Device has reached the end of its service life after absorbing repeated surge events.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the SPD's visual status indicator or remote signalling contact; review surge event logs if available.
Corrective Action
Replace the SPD module promptly to restore protection, and log the replacement date for future service-life tracking.
AGL Fixture Damaged Following a Strike
Likely Cause
A direct or nearby strike overwhelmed the fixture's spark gap or driver-level surge protection, particularly on elevated fixtures.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the fixture, spark gap, and mounting hardware grounding for damage; check the LED driver for surge-related failure.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged fixture or driver, verify the spark gap and mounting hardware earthing are intact, and upgrade to surge-protected drivers if not already fitted.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

No Work on the System During a Storm
Personnel Safety

Suspend all work on air terminals, down conductors, and earth termination points when an electrical storm is in the vicinity, and follow the aerodrome's lightning safety procedure until it has passed.

Assume Down Conductors May Carry Current
Electrical Safety

Treat down conductors and earth termination connections as potentially carrying lightning current at any time; do not disconnect a bonding link without confirming the disconnection is planned and the structure is not under active threat.

Work at Height on Air Terminals
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate fall protection when accessing rooftop or mast-mounted air terminals, and inspect mounting brackets and fixings for corrosion or looseness before relying on them to bear body weight or tools.

Test Before Relying on a Connection
Electrical Safety

Measure earth resistance and continuity along the down-conductor path after any work, and do not treat a reinstated connection as effective protection until it has been tested.

Secure Bonding at Every Joint
Compliance

Torque bonding connections along the conductor run to the manufacturer's specification and protect each joint from corrosion, since a single loose or corroded joint can compromise the whole down-conductor path.

PPE & Continuity Testing
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate PPE and calibrated continuity/resistance test equipment when verifying the system, rather than a visual check alone, since a conductor can look intact while its electrical path is compromised.

FOD Control at Roof and Mast Work
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and fixings accounted for at all times when working at height on structures near the movement area, and secure or remove any loose material before leaving the area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a system with a disconnected conductor, an out-of-tolerance resistance reading, or a corroded joint after maintenance; verify the measured values meet the design target before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Down Conductor Routing Matters as Much as Cross-Section
Design

A down conductor sized correctly on paper can still under-perform if it's routed with tight bends or run close to other services; lightning current follows the path of least inductance, and sharp bends or bundled cabling increase side-flash risk even when the conductor's rated capacity is adequate.

Equipotential Bonding Gaps Are the Usual Root Cause of Side-Flash Damage
Bonding

Most lightning-related equipment damage traces back to a metallic part that wasn't bonded into the equipotential system, not a failure of the air terminal or down conductor themselves; treat bonding continuity checks as core to the protection system, not a secondary item.

Earth Resistance Drifts Seasonally — Trend It, Don't Spot-Check
Testing

Soil moisture and temperature can swing measured earth resistance significantly between wet and dry seasons; a single annual reading taken at the same time each year can mask a genuinely degrading earth system. Logging readings across seasons gives a far more reliable picture than one spot-check.

Surge Protection Devices Need Coordination, Not Just Installation
Electrical

Installing an SPD at the incomer without coordinating let-through voltage and response time against downstream SPDs can leave sensitive electronics under-protected even though a device is technically present at every stage; verify the cascade is properly coordinated, not just populated.

Rolling Sphere Method Beats Guesswork for Air Terminal Placement
Design

Air terminal spacing set by eye or by copying a neighbouring structure's layout can leave unprotected zones on taller or irregular structures; applying the rolling sphere (or equivalent) method for the applicable protection level gives a defensible, repeatable placement rationale.

Corroded Earth Pit Connections Fail Silently Between Strikes
Durability

An earth pit connection can corrode to the point of high resistance without any visible external sign, and the fault only becomes apparent during an actual strike or the next scheduled continuity test; inspect and clean earth pit connections physically at overhaul rather than relying on resistance readings alone.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL lightning protection: air terminals, earthing, surge protection, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is lightning protection for AGL systems?

A holistic approach with external protection (air terminals intercepting strikes) and internal protection (earthing, bonding and surge protection) to protect structures and AGL equipment.

Q2Why is lightning protection needed?

A direct or nearby strike cannot be prevented, so the goal is to control where the energy goes and how much reaches sensitive AGL equipment instead of leaving it to chance.

Q3Where is lightning protection applied?

Throughout AGL infrastructure, including CCR rooms and substations and ALCMS and communication shelters.

Q4How does a lightning protection system work?

Air terminals at the highest points intercept the strike, down conductors carry the current to earth electrodes, and bonding and surge protective devices limit potential differences and surges inside the installation.

Q5What protection level is typical?

IEC 62305 defines Level I (highest) to Level IV (lowest), and Level I is typical for critical AGL infrastructure.

Q6What conductor and earthing specifications are typical?

Copper down conductors of at least 50 mm² (or 70 mm² aluminium tape), an earth electrode resistance of 10 ohms or less, and bonding conductors of at least 16 mm² copper cross-bonded to the main earthing busbar.

Q7What types of surge protection are used?

Type 1 (10/350 µs), Type 2 (8/20 µs) and Type 3 (equipment level) surge protective devices, coordinated to protect power and data entries.

Q8Which standards apply?

IEC 62305 and its European equivalent BS EN 62305, NFPA 780 and FAA AC 150/5340-30, which covers counterpoise and light-base grounding.

Q9How often should lightning protection be inspected?

Annually for earth resistance, conductor and connection condition and bonding continuity; quarterly for SPD end-of-life indicators; after any recorded strike; with a full test every 2-5 years.

Q10What if earth resistance exceeds 10 ohms?

Soil conditions, too few electrodes or corrosion may be responsible. Re-test using the fall-of-potential method and inspect the earthing system.
