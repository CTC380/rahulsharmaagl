Power Distribution Panel

A centralised electrical enclosure that receives main power supply and distributes it to multiple Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) or directly to AGL series circuits. Provides circuit protection, isolation, monitoring, and control for the airfield ground lighting power infrastructure.

Overview

What Is It?

A Power Distribution Panel (also known as a CCR distribution panel or main distribution board) is the central hub that routes electrical power from the airport's main supply (typically 415V AC, 3-phase) to individual Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) and other AGL power equipment. It houses main incoming breakers, busbars, outgoing circuit breakers or MCCBs, monitoring instruments, and often surge protection devices.

The panel provides safe and reliable power distribution, allowing maintenance staff to isolate individual CCRs or circuits for servicing without affecting the entire AGL system. It may also include automatic transfer switches (ATS) for backup generator integration and power quality monitoring to ensure stable operation of the AGL lighting systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The distribution panel exists to let individual CCRs and circuits be isolated, serviced, or replaced without shutting down the entire AGL power supply, a capability that matters enormously in an environment where the airfield can rarely afford to take all lighting circuits offline for routine maintenance. By centralizing incoming supply, protection devices, and outgoing circuits into one coordinated hub rather than wiring each CCR directly to the mains independently, the panel makes fault isolation, load management, and future circuit additions dramatically simpler than they would be with an unstructured, ad hoc distribution scheme. Its role as the integration point for automatic transfer switches and power quality monitoring reflects the same underlying purpose: giving a single, well-organized location where the health and configuration of the entire AGL electrical supply can be managed and observed.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Power Distribution Panels are installed in central electrical rooms, equipment shelters, or dedicated AGL power buildings located on the airfield. They are used in:

  • Main AGL Power Centers: Distributing power to all CCRs that feed runway, taxiway, and approach lighting circuits.
  • Remote Equipment Huts: Smaller distribution panels for decentralised CCR installations located near the airfield perimeter.
  • Backup Power Integration: Panels with automatic transfer switches to seamlessly switch between utility and generator power during outages.
  • Load Shedding and Prioritization: Panels equipped with contactors to shed non-critical loads during peak demand or generator operation.
  • Integration with ALCMS: Panels with communication modules that report status, current draw, and alarms to the central ALCMS.
Placement

Installation & Location

The panel is floor-standing in the main AGL power centre, positioned close to the incoming utility or generator switchgear so the main busbar run stays short, with outgoing ways fanning out to the CCRs, UPS, and other loads it serves. Smaller decentralised panels for remote equipment huts are wall-mounted in a weatherproof outdoor enclosure where the site has no dedicated power building. Incoming and outgoing cables enter through a gland plate at the top or bottom depending on the cable route (overhead tray or underground duct), and the panel frame and busbar earth link are bonded to the site earthing system. Clearance is maintained at the front for breaker operation and metering access, and at the rear where rear-access wiring is used, per the enclosure's IP rating.

Function

How Does It Work?

The panel receives main power from the utility transformer or generator switchgear via a main incoming circuit breaker or MCCB. This power feeds a common busbar system (typically copper) that distributes to multiple outgoing branch circuits. Each branch circuit is protected by a dedicated MCCB or circuit breaker and supplies a single CCR or group of CCRs.

Key functional elements include:

  • Main Incoming Section: Includes the main isolation switch, main circuit breaker, and metering for total system current, voltage, and power.
  • Busbar System: High-conductivity copper bars that distribute power to all outgoing circuits, rated for the total system load.
  • Outgoing Branch Circuits: Individual MCCBs or breakers for each CCR, with auxiliary contacts for status monitoring and remote control.
  • Surge Protection: Surge arresters and transient voltage suppressors to protect sensitive CCR electronics from lightning-induced surges.
  • Monitoring and Control: Digital multimeters, current transducers, and communication modules (Modbus, Ethernet) that provide real-time data to the ALCMS.
  • Automatic Transfer Switch (optional): For installations with backup generators, an ATS automatically switches between utility and generator power upon mains failure.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Control in the PDP is layered rather than centralised: most of it is simple electromechanical protection, with a thin layer of remote monitoring and (optionally) automatic switching on top. The main incoming breaker and each branch MCCB trip purely on overcurrent/fault condition — that logic is local to the breaker and needs no communication link to act. Auxiliary contacts fitted to the main breaker and each branch report open/closed status and, on some installations, allow remote trip/close commands; those signals, together with current transducers and digital metering on the busbar, feed a communications module (typically Modbus or Ethernet) that reports real-time voltage, current, and breaker status to the ALCMS — the ALCMS can see the state of every branch and, where fitted, command it, but it isn't in the path of the protection itself. Where a standby generator is installed, the Automatic Transfer Switch runs its own independent logic: it monitors utility supply quality continuously and switches the busbar to generator power on mains failure without waiting for or depending on the ALCMS link, then switches back once utility supply is confirmed stable. Surge protection devices sit passively across the busbar and act instantaneously on transient overvoltage, again with no communication dependency. The result is a panel that keeps functioning and protecting its downstream CCRs even if the monitoring link to the ALCMS is down — remote visibility and control are an add-on layer, not a dependency for the panel's core switching and protection.

Construction

Physical Construction

The Power Distribution Panel is built as a floor-standing or wall-mounted steel cabinet or switchboard, powder-coated and fitted with lockable, hinged doors for safe access to live busbars and breakers. Internally, tinned-copper busbars run the length of the panel, fed from a main incoming circuit breaker or isolator, with outgoing MCCBs or MCBs for each CCR or distribution circuit mounted in rows and clearly labelled. Instrumentation — ammeters, voltmeters, or a digital power meter — is typically mounted on the door or a dedicated instrument panel, along with indicator lamps for each circuit's status. Surge protection devices are fitted at the incoming supply, and where an ATS is integrated, the transfer switchgear occupies a separate compartment within the same cabinet or an adjacent enclosure. Cable entry is via a gland plate or cable ladder at the top or base, and an earthing bar runs the width of the panel for bonding all outgoing circuits.

Installation Requirements

Cabinet Siting & Foundation
Civil

Position the switchboard on a level plinth or floor fixing per the design layout, keeping clearance for hinged door access to live busbars and breakers.

Busbar & Incoming Breaker Termination
Electrical

Terminate the incoming supply at the main circuit breaker or isolator, torquing busbar connections to the specified value for the panel's full load current.

Outgoing Circuit Wiring & Labelling
Electrical

Terminate each outgoing MCCB or MCB circuit and label it clearly to match the design circuit schedule, since mislabelled ways are a common source of commissioning delay.

Instrumentation Wiring
Electrical

Wire the ammeters, voltmeters, or digital power meter and their associated CTs/PTs, confirming polarity and burden ratings match the meter's specification.

Earthing Bar Bonding
Electrical

Bond the earthing bar running the width of the panel to the site earthing system and connect each outgoing circuit's earth conductor before energisation.

Surge Protection Device Fitting
Electrical

Fit and connect the surge protection device at the incoming supply, confirming its status indicator shows healthy before the panel is closed up.

Communication Cabling
Systems

Run and terminate the Modbus, Ethernet, or IP cabling from the digital metering to the ALCMS or monitoring system where fitted.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record busbar torque values, circuit labelling, and earthing continuity as-built before the panel is first energised.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test busbars and outgoing feeders before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of first power-up.

Busbar Torque & Connection Check
Pre-Energisation

Verify every busbar joint and terminal is torqued to the manufacturer's specification, ruling out loose connections that could arc under load.

Phase Sequence & Voltage Verification
Energisation

Confirm incoming phase sequence and voltage at the main breaker match design before closing any outgoing breakers.

Breaker Trip & Protection Coordination Test
Energisation

Primary-inject or secondary-inject test each breaker's protection settings, confirming trip times coordinate correctly with upstream and downstream devices.

Outgoing Circuit Load Verification
Energisation

Energise each outgoing circuit to its connected CCR or load in turn and confirm correct voltage, phase, and load current at the panel.

Surge Protection Device Check
Final Acceptance

Verify surge protection devices are correctly rated and show healthy status indicators before final sign-off.

Thermal Imaging Survey
Final Acceptance

Run the panel at normal load and thermal-scan all connections, confirming no hot spots at busbars, breakers, or terminals.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, torque, protection coordination, and thermal survey results in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Busbar Thermal Imaging
Annual

Thermally image bolted busbar connections and verify torque, since thermal cycling from load variations can loosen connections and cause overheating or arcing.

Breaker & Harmonic Check
Annual

Check breaker trip history for harmonic-related nuisance tripping caused by IGBT-based CCRs, and evaluate harmonic filters if needed.

Surge Protection Check
Annual

Inspect Type 1 and Type 2 surge protection devices and shielded communication cabling to guard digital meters and modules against lightning-induced surges.

Ventilation & Cooling Check
Semi-annual

Verify clearance and ventilation are adequate for the panel's heat load, especially in high-density configurations under high load.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test feeder circuits to confirm insulation resistance stays above minimum, catching moisture ingress or degraded insulation early.

Enclosure & Seal Inspection
Annual

Check enclosure gaskets and cable glands for degradation to keep the panel sealed against moisture and dust ingress.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Loose Busbar Connections

Thermal cycling from load variations can cause bolted busbar connections to loosen over time, leading to overheating, arcing, and potential failure. Regular thermal imaging inspections and torque verification of all connections is recommended.

Breaker Tripping Due to Harmonic Distortion

Modern CCRs with IGBT technology can inject harmonics into the supply, causing nuisance tripping of MCCBs. Installing harmonic filters or using harmonic-tolerant breakers can mitigate this issue.

Surge Damage to Monitoring Electronics

Lightning strikes near the airfield can induce surges that damage digital meters and communication modules within the panel. Proper surge protection (Type 1 + Type 2) and shielded communication cables are essential.

Inadequate Cooling in Enclosed Spaces

Distribution panels generate significant heat, especially at high loads. Insufficient ventilation or cooling can reduce component life and cause premature breaker failure. Ensure adequate clearance and consider forced ventilation for high-density panels.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Overheating or Arcing at Busbar Connections
Likely Cause
Thermal cycling from load variations has loosened a bolted busbar connection over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a thermal imaging inspection of all busbar connections under load; verify torque on any connection showing an elevated temperature signature.
Corrective Action
Re-torque or repair the affected connection to specification, and schedule a routine thermal imaging inspection cycle to catch recurrence early.
Nuisance Breaker Tripping
Likely Cause
Harmonic distortion injected by IGBT-based CCRs downstream is causing MCCBs to trip without an actual overload.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure harmonic content at the affected breaker with a power quality analyzer; compare against the breaker's harmonic tolerance rating.
Corrective Action
Install harmonic filters upstream of the affected circuits, or replace the breaker with a harmonic-tolerant type rated for the actual load profile.
Damaged Monitoring Electronics After a Storm
Likely Cause
A lightning-induced surge has bypassed or exceeded the panel's surge protection and damaged digital meters or communication modules.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect surge protection devices for tripped or burnt indicators; test digital meters and communication modules for correct operation.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged SPDs (Type 1 + Type 2) and affected electronics; verify shielded communication cabling is intact and properly grounded.
Premature Breaker Failure in Enclosed Spaces
Likely Cause
Insufficient ventilation or cooling in a high-density panel is allowing heat to build up beyond component ratings.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure internal panel temperature under full load and compare against breaker and component ratings; check clearance and ventilation paths.
Corrective Action
Improve ventilation or add forced cooling as needed, and ensure adequate clearance is restored around heat-generating components.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Busbar Connection Overheating / Arcing
High Severity
Root Cause
Loose or under-torqued busbar connections that develop high resistance under load current, or corrosion building up at the joint over time.
Effect
Localised overheating that can progress to arcing, discolouration, or in severe cases a connection failure within the panel.
Detection Method
Thermal imaging survey of busbar connections under load, and periodic torque-check during scheduled maintenance.
Circuit Breaker Nuisance Tripping
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A worn trip mechanism, a breaker rated too close to normal operating current, or an intermittent downstream fault causing repeated momentary overloads.
Effect
Unplanned circuit outages that interrupt AGL circuits without a genuine sustained fault present.
Detection Method
Trip event log review to look for a pattern, and functional testing of the breaker's trip curve against its rated characteristics.
Monitoring Electronics Surge Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced transient or switching surge on the metering or communication wiring that exceeds the panel's protection rating.
Effect
Loss of monitoring data or false readings from the panel's metering electronics, even while the distribution function itself remains intact.
Detection Method
Post-storm functional check of monitoring outputs, and inspection of onboard surge protection devices for visible damage.
Premature Breaker Failure in Enclosed Spaces
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Elevated ambient temperature inside a poorly ventilated enclosure accelerating the ageing of breaker internals beyond their rated life.
Effect
Breakers fail or drift out of calibration earlier than expected, requiring more frequent replacement than the rated service life.
Detection Method
Enclosure temperature monitoring against the panel's rated ambient, and breaker trip-curve verification during scheduled maintenance.
Terminal Connection Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress at cable entry points or dissimilar-metal contact at a terminal, driving galvanic corrosion over time.
Effect
Increased connection resistance leading to localised heating, and eventually an intermittent or open connection.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of terminals for corrosion during maintenance, and thermal imaging to catch a developing hot spot early.
Enclosure Seal Failure / Moisture Ingress
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set, a cracked cable gland, or a door seal that no longer closes flush after repeated access.
Effect
Internal corrosion and a gradual increase in the risk of insulation breakdown or a ground fault.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of gaskets and seals during scheduled maintenance, and internal humidity check where indicators are fitted.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Upstream and Downstream Isolation
Electrical Safety

Before internal work, isolate and lock out the panel's incoming mains supply and confirm every outgoing CCR/circuit breaker is also open, since a downstream CCR can back-feed limited energy into the busbar through its own transformer if left connected.

Arc-Flash Risk at Busbars and Main Breakers
Electrical Safety

Treat the main incoming breaker and busbar compartment as an arc-flash hazard area; follow the site's arc-flash procedure, use rated PPE, and avoid working on the panel live wherever the task allows isolation instead.

Multiple Simultaneous Sources
Electrical Safety

Where the panel is fed from both utility mains and a standby generator or UPS, verify and lock out all sources independently; isolating only the normally active source can leave the panel live from a backup path.

Verify Phase Sequence Before Reconnection
Electrical Safety

After any work on incoming supply connections, confirm correct phase sequence and rotation before reconnecting downstream CCRs, since an incorrect sequence can affect three-phase CCR operation or trip protective devices.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

The panel feeds multiple series circuits simultaneously; coordinate any outage with ATC and raise a NOTAM covering every circuit affected, not just the one being directly serviced.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm all outgoing breakers are correctly labelled, torqued, and delivering rated voltage to each CCR before closing out the work and re-energising the panel.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Thermal Imaging Finds Loose Connections Before They Arc
Diagnostics

A busbar joint or breaker connection that's loosened slightly under thermal cycling shows up as a localised hot spot on a thermal imaging survey long before it actually arcs or trips a breaker. Make busbar thermal imaging a standing item on the maintenance schedule rather than a reactive step taken only after a fault occurs.

Protection Coordination Needs Re-Checking After Any Downstream Change
Design

Adding or replacing a downstream CCR or load changes the fault-current profile the panel's breakers were originally coordinated against, and a coordination study that was correct at commissioning can leave breakers mis-sequenced years later after several incremental changes. Re-verify protection coordination whenever downstream equipment changes materially, not only at initial design.

Surge Protection Devices Have a Finite, Silent Service Life
Electrical

A surge protection device that's absorbed several transient events over its life can be significantly degraded while still passing a basic visual or continuity check, with no indication until the next major surge passes through unprotected equipment. Check the SPD's actual condition indicator or replace on a manufacturer-specified interval rather than assuming presence equals protection.

Outgoing Circuit Labelling Drifts as Fast as the Circuits Themselves Change
Maintenance

A panel that's had circuits added, moved, or relabelled informally over years of incremental work can have labelling that no longer matches the as-built wiring, which turns a routine fault-finding job into a much longer exercise than it should be. Re-verify labelling against actual circuit routing at every major service, not just accept the existing labels at face value.

Harmonic Content From Downstream CCRs Accumulates at the Panel
Power Quality

Multiple thyristor or switching-based CCRs on the same distribution panel can contribute cumulative harmonic distortion back onto the incoming supply that no single CCR's own harmonic check would reveal in isolation. Check harmonic levels at the panel itself periodically, not only at each individual CCR's terminals.

Phase Balance Deserves a Recheck After Circuit Changes
Electrical

Adding new outgoing circuits without tracking which phase each was connected to can leave the panel unbalanced across the three phases over time, which increases neutral loading and reduces overall system efficiency without tripping any single protective device. Re-verify phase balance whenever new circuits are added rather than assigning connections without reference to the existing load distribution.
