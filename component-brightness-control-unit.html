Brightness Control Unit

A dedicated control device that adjusts the luminous intensity of AGL lighting circuits by varying the current output of Constant Current Regulators (CCRs). Enables step‑wise brightness selection (typically 5 steps) based on prevailing visibility conditions, ensuring optimal visual guidance while minimising energy consumption.

Overview

What Is It?

A Brightness Control Unit (BCU) is an electronic device that provides remote or local selection of the brightness level (intensity step) for AGL lighting circuits. It interfaces with the CCR(s) to command the desired current level—typically one of five steps (2.8A, 3.4A, 4.1A, 5.2A, 6.6A)—corresponding to different light intensities for varying visibility conditions (e.g., clear, mist, fog). The BCU can be a standalone panel with push‑buttons and indicators, or it can be integrated into a larger control system such as the ALCMS. In modern systems, the BCU function is often embedded within the CCR controller or the ALCMS workstation, but standalone units remain common in smaller airfields or as backup controls. The BCU ensures that pilots receive the correct visual cues based on current weather and operational requirements.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The BCU exists to give an operator a direct, simple way to command the correct brightness step for current conditions, translating a judgement call about visibility (clear, mist, fog) into the specific current setpoint the CCR needs to deliver it. Its purpose becomes especially important as a standalone or backup control at smaller airfields, or wherever a full ALCMS workstation isn't practical or available, ensuring that brightness selection doesn't depend entirely on a single, more complex control system that could itself be a point of failure. Whether embedded in a modern CCR controller or ALCMS, or standing alone with simple push-buttons, its core purpose remains the same: making sure pilots receive lighting intensity that actually matches the visibility conditions they're flying in, rather than a fixed brightness that's wrong for the weather.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Brightness Control Units are deployed in various airfield control and maintenance locations:

  • Air Traffic Control Towers: For quick manual adjustment of lighting intensity based on real‑time visibility.
  • Electrical Equipment Rooms: Near CCR banks for local control during maintenance or testing.
  • Central Control Rooms: As part of the ALCMS, often as a hardware backup to software‑based control.
  • Remote Substations: Where CCRs are located far from the tower, a BCU allows on‑site intensity selection.
  • Legacy Systems: Retrofitted to older CCRs to add electronic brightness selection where only manual tap‑changers existed.
Placement

Installation & Location

The BCU is panel- or rack-mounted, either built into a control desk in the tower cab, fitted into a 19″ enclosure in the central control room alongside the ALCMS workstation, or wall-mounted in the equipment room next to the CCR bank it serves. Control wiring — dry contacts, 4-20mA, or an RS-485 Modbus link — is run in a dedicated screened cable back to each CCR's control terminals, kept separate from power cabling to avoid interference. Where a BCU is provided as a local override at a remote substation, it is mounted at eye level near the CCR it controls so an operator can select a step without opening the regulator cabinet. Power for the unit is taken from the local 24V DC or 230V AC control supply rather than the CCR's high-current output.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Brightness Control Unit communicates with the CCR(s) via a control interface—typically discrete signals (dry contacts) or serial communication (Modbus, RS‑485). When an operator selects a brightness step (e.g., Step 3 for moderate fog), the BCU sends a command to the CCR to adjust its output current to the corresponding value. The CCR then regulates its output to maintain that current, changing the intensity of all lights in the series circuit. The BCU may also receive feedback from the CCR confirming the current step and indicating any faults. Some BCUs include ambient light sensors to automatically adjust brightness based on daylight levels, or they can be programmed to follow a time‑based schedule. The BCU typically provides visual indication of the selected step and may include alarm outputs for system faults. In many systems, the BCU is integrated with the ALCMS, allowing remote control from a central workstation.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Whether embedded in the CCR controller or standalone, the BCU's role stays the same: turn a step selection into a setpoint the CCR can act on, and confirm it happened.
Construction

Physical Construction

The BCU is typically housed in a compact 19-inch rack-mount enclosure, a DIN-rail mounted module, or a small wall/desk-mounted panel with a powder-coated steel or ABS front fascia. The front panel carries a row of illuminated push-buttons or a rotary selector switch for the five intensity steps, backed by LED step indicators, and on advanced units a small LCD or OLED display showing the selected step and CCR status. Internally, a microcontroller PCB decodes the selector input and drives isolated relay, opto-coupled, or serial/Modbus outputs that carry the step-select command to the CCR controller. Terminal blocks on the rear or base of the enclosure accept the control cabling, and the unit is typically powered from a low-voltage DC supply (24V) or fed from the control cabinet's auxiliary supply. Standalone panels intended for tower or apron use are usually rated for indoor environments only, while ruggedised versions add a sealed enclosure for outdoor equipment shelters.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Control Signal Interference

Analog control signals (4‑20mA or 0‑10V) are susceptible to electromagnetic interference, especially when cables run parallel to high‑power AGL cables. Use of shielded cables and proper routing is essential to maintain reliable brightness selection.

Relay Contact Wear

If discrete relay outputs are used for step selection, the contacts can wear out over time, leading to intermittent or stuck steps. Regular inspection and replacement of relays is recommended.

Communication Protocol Mismatch

When using serial communication, mismatched baud rates, parity, or addressing can prevent the BCU from communicating with the CCR. Proper configuration and documentation are crucial.

Power Supply Issues

BCUs often share power with other control equipment. Voltage dips or spikes can cause erratic behaviour or lock‑up. A dedicated, regulated power supply with battery backup is recommended.

Human Error

Operators may inadvertently select an incorrect brightness step, especially during rapid weather changes. Clear labelling, interlocking, and confirmation prompts can reduce the risk.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Erratic or Unreliable Step Selection
Likely Cause
Electromagnetic interference on the analog control signal (4-20mA or 0-10V), typically from unshielded cable routed parallel to high-power AGL cabling.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect cable routing and shielding along the run; check for proper shield grounding at one end; measure signal integrity at the CCR end during a step change.
Corrective Action
Re-route the control cable away from power cabling or replace it with properly shielded cable, and re-ground the shield correctly at one end only.
Step Sticks or Fails to Change
Likely Cause
Worn or pitted relay contacts on a discrete step-selection output, common after extended service.
Diagnostic Steps
Cycle through each step while monitoring the relay contacts for chatter, pitting, or failure to close; check for a stuck or welded contact.
Corrective Action
Replace the worn relay(s) and verify clean, repeatable switching through a full step-change test cycle.
BCU Cannot Communicate with CCR
Likely Cause
Mismatched serial communication parameters (baud rate, parity, or device addressing) between the BCU and CCR, often after a configuration change or replacement unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the configured communication parameters on both the BCU and CCR against the commissioning documentation; verify with a protocol analyzer or diagnostic port if available.
Corrective Action
Correct the mismatched parameter on whichever device is out of configuration, and document the working settings for future reference.
Erratic Behaviour or Lock-Up
Likely Cause
Voltage dips or spikes on a shared, unregulated power supply feeding the BCU along with other control equipment.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor the BCU's supply voltage during normal operation to catch transients; check whether a dedicated regulated supply with battery backup is present.
Corrective Action
Install or repair a dedicated, regulated power supply with battery backup for the BCU, and confirm stable operation afterward.
Wrong Brightness Step Selected by Operator
Likely Cause
Ambiguous or unclear step labelling, combined with the need for rapid selection during fast-changing weather, leading to operator error.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the BCU's labelling and confirmation workflow against ICAO/FAA intensity-step requirements; interview the operator on what step was intended versus selected.
Corrective Action
Improve labelling and add confirmation prompts or interlocks where supported, and retrain operators on the correct step-selection procedure.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Step Selector Contact Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical wear and pitting on the rotary switch or push-button contacts from repeated daily step changes over years of service.
Effect
Erratic or unreliable step selection, requiring several attempts to register the intended brightness level.
Detection Method
Operational log review for repeated step-change attempts, and physical inspection/cleaning of the selector contacts during maintenance.
Communication Link Failure to CCR
High Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded RS-485/network cabling, a failed communication port on the BCU or CCR end, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
BCU can no longer command or confirm brightness steps at one or more CCRs, forcing local manual control until restored.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the BCU, continuity testing of the comms cable, and port-loopback testing at both ends.
Backup Battery Depletion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ordinary battery ageing beyond its service life, or a failed charging circuit that leaves the battery undercharged.
Effect
Stored brightness settings and schedules are lost on a power dip or outage, reverting the BCU to default values.
Detection Method
Scheduled battery voltage/capacity testing during preventive maintenance, and monitoring for unexplained setting resets.
Firmware Lock-Up or Corruption
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A firmware update interrupted mid-write, electrical transient during operation, or a latent software fault triggered by a specific input sequence.
Effect
Unit becomes unresponsive or behaves erratically, requiring a power cycle or reflash to restore normal operation.
Detection Method
Watchdog/event log review after a lock-up, and firmware version verification against the latest validated release.
Surge Damage to Control Electronics
High Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced transient or switching surge on the power or communication lines that exceeds the unit's surge protection rating.
Effect
Partial or total loss of BCU function, ranging from erratic readings to complete failure to power on.
Detection Method
Post-storm functional check, inspection of onboard surge protection devices for visible damage, and power-up self-test.
Enclosure Seal Failure / Moisture Ingress
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set, cracked cable glands, or a door seal that no longer closes flush after repeated access.
Effect
Corrosion on internal contacts and circuit boards, leading to intermittent operation over time.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of gaskets and seals during scheduled maintenance, and internal humidity check where indicators are fitted.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate the Associated CCR, Not Just the BCU
Electrical Safety

The BCU's own control wiring is low-voltage, but it commands a series circuit carrying high open-circuit voltage; before internal work on the BCU or its wiring, isolate and lock out the CCR(s) it controls and verify zero energy at the loop, not just at the BCU panel.

ESD-Sensitive Electronics
Operational

Use anti-static precautions when handling the BCU's internal circuit boards, since brightness-step logic and communication interfaces are electrostatic-discharge sensitive.

Coordinate Step Changes with ATC
Operational

A manual override at the BCU changes runway/approach light intensity in real time; coordinate significant step changes with ATC, especially during active approach or takeoff operations, rather than treating it as a routine local adjustment.

Verify Correct Step Before Closing Out
Compliance

After servicing or a firmware/configuration change, confirm the BCU commands and the CCR delivers the intended brightness step before returning the unit to unattended operation, since a stuck or mis-mapped step is not always obvious from the BCU panel alone.

Physical Lockout Over Software Disable
Electrical Safety

Where the BCU supports remote or networked control, use a physical lockout on the CCR during hands-on work rather than relying on a software-disabled or local-only mode, which a remote command could still override.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm all brightness steps are selectable and correctly mapped, and that alarm/status indications are accurate, before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Physical Lockout Over Software Disable, Every Time
Electrical Safety

A software or remote disable of step-change commands isn't a substitute for physical isolation before working inside the unit. Treat any BCU work with the same lockout discipline as CCR work itself.

Step Changes During Maintenance Need Real-Time ATC Coordination
Operational

A mid-test step change can visibly alter runway or taxiway brightness for active traffic. Coordinate the timing of any test step change with ATC rather than assuming a “quick test” won't be noticed operationally.

ESD Precautions Aren't Optional Because It's “Just a Control Box”
Electrical Safety

The BCU's electronics are as ESD-sensitive as any other airfield control electronics. Follow grounding and ESD strap procedures before opening the enclosure, not just for the CCR itself.

Verify the Actual Step, Not Just That a Command Was Sent
Compliance

Confirm the commanded intensity step is physically reflected on the circuit — via a photometric spot-check or loop current reading — before signing off, since a command can be accepted by the BCU without the CCR actually responding correctly.

Isolate the CCR Directly for Internal Work, Not Just the BCU
Electrical Safety

The BCU is a control layer on top of the CCR. Isolating the BCU alone can leave the CCR itself live if the two aren't wired the way you expect, so verify isolation at the CCR directly before assuming the circuit is safe.

Keep an External Log of Step-Change History
Maintenance

Maintain a record of step changes and the reasons for them — weather, RVR, ATC instruction — independent of the BCU's own memory, in case that internal log is lost during a fault or replacement.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the brightness control unit: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a brightness control unit (BCU)?

A brightness control unit is an electronic device that provides remote or local selection of the brightness step for AGL lighting circuits. It interfaces with the constant current regulator (CCR) to command the required current level for different visibility conditions.

Q2What brightness steps does a BCU provide?

Typically five steps corresponding to CCR currents of 2.8 A, 3.4 A, 4.1 A, 5.2 A and 6.6 A, although 3-step variants also exist.

Q3How does a BCU communicate with the CCR?

Through a control interface such as dry relay contacts, 4-20 mA, Modbus RTU over RS-485 or Ethernet. When an operator selects a step, the BCU commands the CCR, which regulates its output current to that value. The BCU may receive feedback confirming the current step and any faults.

Q4Does a BCU control individual lights?

No. The CCR changes the current in the whole series circuit, so all lights on that circuit change intensity together.

Q5Is a BCU a standalone unit or part of the ALCMS?

Either. It can be a standalone panel with push-buttons and indicators, or integrated into a larger system such as the ALCMS. In modern systems the function is often embedded in the CCR controller or ALCMS workstation, while standalone units remain common at smaller airfields or as backup controls.

Q6Where are BCUs installed?

Typical locations are air traffic control towers, electrical equipment rooms near CCR banks, central control rooms as a hardware backup to software control, and remote substations where CCRs are far from the tower.

Q7What are the typical specifications?

A BCU typically controls 1 to 8 circuits, runs from 24 V DC or 230 V AC with low power draw (typically under 15 W excluding display backlight), has relay alarm outputs, an optional ambient light sensor input and a panel-mount or free-standing enclosure with an IP54 front.

Q8Which standards apply to BCUs?

The main references are IEC 61822 and EN 61822 (AGL constant current regulators and associated control equipment), FAA AC 150/5345-10 (CCRs and regulator monitors) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should a BCU be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover control signal verification, relay contacts, power supply and backup, interlock and labelling, and enclosure and connections, with a semi-annual communication configuration check. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with BCUs?

Common problems are control signal interference, relay contact wear, communication protocol mismatch, power supply issues and human error such as selecting the wrong brightness step. Symptoms include erratic or stuck step selection, or a BCU that cannot communicate with the CCR.
