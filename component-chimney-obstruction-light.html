Chimney Obstruction Light

A dedicated aviation warning light for chimneys, smokestacks, and industrial stacks, typically featuring medium-intensity red steady‑burning or flashing lights. Designed to withstand harsh industrial environments, high temperatures, and corrosive emissions while providing reliable 24/7 obstruction marking.

Overview

What Is It?

A Chimney Obstruction Light is a medium‑intensity aviation warning light installed on industrial chimneys, smokestacks, and similar tall structures to alert aircraft of their presence. These lights are typically red (steady‑burning or flashing) and are mounted at the top and sometimes at intermediate levels on chimneys exceeding 45 metres (150 feet). They are engineered to operate reliably in extreme conditions—high temperatures, corrosive flue gases, heavy soot, and severe weather—while maintaining the required photometric output and colour. Chimney obstruction lights are critical for aviation safety near industrial areas, power plants, and incinerators.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Chimney obstruction lighting exists to mark a category of tall structure that combines the usual height hazard with an unusually hostile operating environment for the fixture itself: sustained heat, corrosive flue gases, and heavy soot accumulation that would degrade a standard obstruction light's housing, lens, and photometric output over time if it weren't specifically engineered for this exposure. Because industrial chimneys are frequently sited at power plants and incinerators, often clustered with other tall industrial structures, marking them clearly and reliably matters for the same reason as any tall obstacle — but the light's ability to keep functioning through the specific conditions a chimney generates is itself part of the purpose, since a light that degrades quickly in this environment would fail exactly the structures it's most needed on.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on chimneys, smokestacks, and industrial stacks that exceed 45 metres (150 feet) AGL, or any chimney that falls within an aviation corridor or near an aerodrome. Typically used in power plants (thermal, nuclear, biomass), incinerators, cement plants, steel mills, and chemical factories. Multiple lights are often installed at different levels (top, mid‑height) on very tall chimneys to indicate the overall height and shape. The lights must be visible from all directions, requiring omnidirectional or 360° coverage.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the chimney top and, on taller stacks, at intermediate levels at the specified vertical spacing, mounted on brackets designed to hold the light clear of the flue gas stream and any soot or condensate runoff that would otherwise coat the lens. Mounting hardware and cabling are specified in corrosion-resistant and high-temperature-rated materials given the combination of flue gas chemistry, heat radiating from the stack, and prolonged outdoor exposure. Access for lamp changes and cleaning is typically provided via a permanent ladder, davit arm, or lifting platform built into the chimney design, since the top of an operating stack is not otherwise reachable. Power and control cabling is run down the outside of the stack in a protected conduit to the base, where it connects back to the controller and, on industrial sites, into the plant's own monitoring and alarm systems.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each chimney obstruction light is powered from a dedicated power supply—either a direct 230V AC line or a low‑voltage system with a transformer. The light typically uses an LED array or a high‑intensity xenon or halogen lamp, with a red filter or dichroic coating to produce aviation red. The intensity is either fixed (medium intensity, ~2,000 cd) or adjustable using a photocell to switch between day and night modes (though many chimney lights are steady red 24/7). A flashing option (20–40 fpm) may be used for enhanced visibility. The housing is constructed from corrosion‑resistant materials (stainless steel, marine‑grade aluminium) with IP66/IP67 protection and high‑temperature seals to withstand stack heat and chemical attack.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Chimney obstruction lights are usually the simplest control chain in the AGL family, and many installations deliberately simplify it further: rather than a photocell deciding day/night mode, the top light is wired permanently on, steady red, 24/7, so there is one less component that can fail silently on an unmanned stack. Where a photocell or timer is fitted, it switches a dedicated power supply — either direct 230V AC or a transformer-derived low-voltage feed, kept electrically separate from the stack's own process and combustion-control wiring — which drives the mandatory top light and any intermediate-level lights required on taller stacks. There is no loop-level dimming or intensity stepping here; each fixture is either fully on or off, with a flashing option (20–40 fpm) available where required by the governing standard. Because chimneys are frequently unstaffed, lamp-failure status is typically wired back to the plant control room or a dedicated remote monitoring unit rather than relying on a site visit, so an outage on an unmanned stack still reaches someone who can act on it.

Photocell / Permanent-On → Dedicated Power Supply → Top & Intermediate Lights (with remote fault reporting)

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is built into a rugged, heat- and corrosion-resistant housing, typically die-cast aluminium or stainless steel, engineered to withstand the elevated temperatures, exhaust gases, and thermal cycling encountered at chimney-top and intermediate mounting positions. A toughened red lens, heat-shielded and gasket-sealed with a high-temperature silicone seal, protects the LED array or halogen/xenon source while achieving an IP66/IP67, corrosion-resistant rating. Mounting brackets are designed to clamp securely to the chimney's curved or tapered structure, often with a standoff to keep the fixture body clear of the hottest exhaust plume while maintaining unobstructed omnidirectional beam coverage. Internal wiring runs through heat-resistant, sealed conduit to a junction box positioned away from direct exhaust exposure, connecting to the 230V AC or 24V DC supply via a weatherproof converter where required. Corrosion-resistant fasteners and coatings are used throughout to withstand the combination of heat, moisture, and chemical exposure typical of chimney environments.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion from Flue Gases

Chimney lights are exposed to acidic and corrosive emissions. Stainless steel housings and sealed optical assemblies are essential, but even these can degrade over time. Regular inspection of housing integrity and replacement of corroded fasteners is required.

Soot and Particulate Buildup

Soot from combustion can accumulate on the lens and prism, drastically reducing light output. A regular cleaning schedule with approved non‑abrasive cleaners is critical—especially for chimneys burning heavy fuels or waste.

Thermal Stress and Seal Failure

High stack temperatures can cause thermal expansion and contraction, leading to gasket and seal failure. Moisture ingress then causes electrical faults. Using high‑temperature silicone seals and ensuring proper venting (pressure equalisation) can mitigate this.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Rapid Loss of Light Output
Likely Cause
Soot and particulate buildup from flue emissions has coated the lens and prism, blocking light transmission.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens and prism for soot film; take a photometric reading and compare to the rated candela.
Corrective Action
Clean with an approved non-abrasive cleaner on a schedule matched to the fuel type; increase cleaning frequency for heavy-fuel or waste-burning stacks.
Housing Corrosion or Structural Pitting
Likely Cause
Acidic flue gas exposure is attacking the housing material or fasteners faster than the coating can protect them.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect housing seams, welds, and fasteners for pitting or wall-thinning; check protective coating integrity.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded fasteners with the specified corrosion-resistant grade and touch up or recoat affected housing surfaces; escalate to full housing replacement if structural pitting is found.
Seal Failure and Moisture/Condensation Ingress
Likely Cause
Repeated thermal expansion and contraction from stack heat has fatigued the gasket, letting moisture or flue condensate into the electrical compartment.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect gaskets for hardening, cracking, or compression set; check for condensation or corrosion inside the housing after a thermal cycle.
Corrective Action
Replace with high-temperature-rated silicone seals, confirm pressure-equalisation venting is clear, and dry and megger the circuit before re-energising.
Fixture Dark or Intermittent
Likely Cause
Thermally stressed wiring insulation has cracked near the stack penetration, causing an intermittent open or short.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the supply cable near the penetration; visually inspect insulation for heat-cracking or discoloration.
Corrective Action
Replace the affected cable run with high-temperature-rated cable and re-route away from direct flue heat where possible.
Reduced Intensity Despite Clean Lens
Likely Cause
LED module or lamp degradation accelerated by sustained elevated ambient temperature at the stack mounting point.
Diagnostic Steps
Take a photometric reading against rated output; check fixture body temperature against the manufacturer's maximum rating.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp and verify the mounting standoff still provides adequate thermal clearance from the stack surface.

Medium Severity
Root Cause
Flue emissions depositing soot and particulate film on the lens and prism.
Effect
Rapid loss of light output that can progress faster than on other obstruction lights due to constant flue exposure.
Detection Method

Acidic Flue Gas Corrosion
High Severity
Root Cause
Acidic flue gas exposure attacking the housing material or fasteners faster than the protective coating can withstand.
Effect
Housing corrosion or structural pitting that can progress to a compromised enclosure or fastener failure.
Detection Method
Inspection of housing seams, welds, and fasteners for pitting or wall-thinning, and protective-coating integrity checks.
Thermal-Cycling Gasket Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Repeated thermal expansion and contraction from stack heat fatiguing the gasket over time.
Effect
Moisture or flue condensate ingress into the electrical compartment, risking corrosion or short circuits.
Detection Method
Gasket inspection for hardening, cracking, or compression set, and internal checks for condensation or corrosion after a thermal cycle.
Heat-Cracked Wiring Insulation
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermally stressed wiring insulation cracking near the stack penetration point.
Effect
Fixture goes dark or operates intermittently from an open or short in the affected cable run.
Detection Method
Megger testing of the supply cable near the penetration, and visual inspection for heat-cracking or discoloration.
Heat-Accelerated LED Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
LED module or lamp degradation accelerated by sustained elevated ambient temperature at the stack mounting point.
Effect
Reduced intensity despite a clean lens, since the light source itself has aged faster than normal.
Detection Method
Photometric readings against rated output, and fixture body temperature checks against the manufacturer's maximum rating.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Extreme-Height Access
Fall Protection

Follow the plant's permit-to-work and confined-height access procedure for chimney work; use certified climbing/rigging equipment and never rely on the fixture bracket as an anchor point.

Flue Gas / Thermal Hazard
Process Safety

Coordinate with plant operations before working near an active flue; hot gases, soot, and elevated ambient temperature at the stack top present burn and respiratory hazards even when the light itself is isolated.

Corrosive / Particulate Atmosphere
Personnel Safety

Wear respiratory protection and chemical-resistant PPE appropriate to the flue gas composition; soot and corrosive residue can also degrade climbing equipment over time.

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the fixture circuit at the local disconnect and verify zero energy before opening the housing; confirm the isolation point is accessible from the working platform before ascending.

Plant Coordination
Operational

Notify the aviation authority and raise a NOTAM if applicable before the obstruction light goes dark, and align the outage window with the plant's own shutdown or turnaround schedule where possible.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use heat-resistant gloves, insulated tools, and full fall-protection PPE rated for the chimney's height and thermal environment.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify photometric output and colour at each mounted level before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Standoff Distance Degrades the Fixture Faster Than the Lens Fails
Design

A fixture mounted with adequate standoff from the exhaust plume at commissioning can end up too close if the chimney's operating profile changes to higher flue temperatures or a different fuel over its service life. Re-verify standoff and heat-shield adequacy against actual operating conditions periodically, not only against the original design plume.

Soot Accumulation Reads as a Lamp Failure If You Only Check Intensity
Troubleshooting

A photometric intensity reading that comes back low can be soot fouling on the lens rather than a degrading lamp or LED source, especially on chimneys handling heavier flue gas loads. Clean the lens and re-test before condemning the light source on an intensity failure at this fixture type.

Corrosive Flue Gas Attacks Seals From the Inside
Reliability

Corrosion protection and seal checks on a chimney fixture need to account for gasket attack from flue-gas condensate working inward, not just weather ingress from outside as on a typical rooftop light. Specify gasket and housing materials rated for the specific flue chemistry rather than assuming a standard weatherproof rating is sufficient.

Converter Placement Matters as Much as Its Rating
Electrical

A power supply/converter correctly rated for the load can still fail prematurely from heat if it's mounted too close to the standoff bracket rather than in a cooler junction location down the structure. Site switching-mode converters away from the direct thermal zone even when the enclosure itself is rated for the ambient temperature.

Intermediate-Level Lights See Different Conditions Than the Top Light
Inspection

Lights at intermediate levels on a tall chimney sit in a different soot and thermal environment than the top marker, since plume dispersion and direct exposure vary with height. Inspect each level on its own merits rather than assuming the condition of the top light represents the whole installation.

Access Planning Should Assume the Stack Is Never Fully Cool
Safety

Industrial chimneys rarely go fully cold even during planned outages, so access and PPE planning for lamp or lens work should assume residual heat and off-gassing rather than treating the structure like an unheated mast. Coordinate maintenance windows with the plant's own shutdown procedures rather than scheduling on the lighting maintenance calendar alone.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about chimney obstruction lights: when they are required, colour, heat and corrosion resistance, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a chimney obstruction light?

A chimney obstruction light is a medium-intensity aviation warning light installed on industrial chimneys, smokestacks and similar tall structures. It is red, steady-burning or flashing, and built to survive high temperatures, corrosive flue gases, heavy soot and severe weather.

Q2Which chimneys need obstruction lights?

Chimneys and stacks that exceed about 45 m (150 ft) above ground level, or that lie in an aviation corridor or near an aerodrome. Typical sites are power plants, incinerators, cement plants, steel mills and chemical factories.

Q3What colour and intensity are chimney obstruction lights?

They show aviation red at about 2,000 cd for medium intensity, and the photometric range is 2,000–20,000 cd depending on day or night mode. Many chimney lights run steady red 24/7, and a flashing option of 20–40 flashes per minute may be used for extra visibility.

Q4Where are the lights mounted on a chimney?

At the top of the chimney and, on taller stacks, at intermediate levels at the specified vertical spacing. Brackets hold the light clear of the flue gas stream and any soot or condensate runoff, and the light gives 360° coverage so it is visible from every direction.

Q5How do chimney obstruction lights handle heat and corrosion?

Housings use corrosion-resistant materials such as stainless steel, with sealed optics, heat shielding and heat-resistant cabling. The typical operating range is −40 °C to +85 °C with IP66/IP67 protection, and optical output is designed to hold up against heat haze and soiling near the stack outlet.

Q6How are chimney obstruction lights powered?

From a dedicated supply, either 230 V AC directly or 24 V DC with a converter, independent of the airfield series circuit. Thermal and overcurrent protection sits at the local isolator, and the fixture is bonded to the chimney's lightning protection system because the stack is usually the tallest point on site.

Q7Which standards apply to chimney obstruction lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 70/7460-1, FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment) and IEC 62305 for lightning protection. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q8How often should chimney obstruction lights be cleaned and inspected?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for flash controller tests, and annual for photometric intensity checks, heat shield and thermal protection inspection and corrosion and housing inspection. Heavy-fuel or waste-burning stacks may need more frequent cleaning.

Q9Why is my chimney obstruction light losing output quickly?

The usual cause is soot and particulate film on the lens and prism blocking light transmission. Compare a photometric reading to the rated candela, then clean with an approved non-abrasive cleaner and increase the cleaning frequency to suit the fuel type.

Q10What causes corrosion on chimney light housings?

Acidic flue gas attacks the housing, seams and fasteners faster than the coating can protect them. Inspect for pitting or wall thinning, replace corroded fasteners with the specified corrosion-resistant grade and recoat affected surfaces.
