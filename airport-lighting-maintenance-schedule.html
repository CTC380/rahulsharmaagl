Overview

Why Airport Lighting Needs a Planned Maintenance Schedule

Airport lighting is safety-critical infrastructure. A runway, taxiway, approach system or PAPI can keep operating while it quietly degrades, so maintenance has to be a planned preventive programme, not a response to failures. ICAO Annex 14 requires aerodromes to establish maintenance programmes, including preventive maintenance where appropriate, so facilities stay in a condition that does not impair the safety, regularity or efficiency of air navigation.

The goal is the whole system

Preserve the correct pattern, intensity, colour, orientation, electrical performance, availability and reliability of the complete lighting system, not just keep lamps lit.

A practical schedule combines

Routine operational inspection, preventive maintenance, electrical testing, component inspection, photometric verification, corrective maintenance, post-maintenance testing, serviceability monitoring and documented trend analysis.

This is a typical framework for planning and contracts. It does not replace the airport's approved maintenance programme, applicable DGCA, AAI or other State requirements, manufacturer instructions, runway operating category or project specifications. FAA AC 150/5340-26C, which FAA lists as Active (issued 20 June 2014), describes a scheduled preventive-maintenance inspection programme for visual aids and their cable systems. For ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, Amendment 18 is applicable from 27 November 2025 unless otherwise indicated.

      Visual Aids

      Runway, Taxiway and Approach Lighting Maintenance

      Runway lighting

      Think in patterns, not fittings. Edge: output, colour, lens, alignment, mounting, frangibility, transformer. Centreline (inset): lens, cover, pavement interface, water ingress, continuity. Threshold, end and touchdown zone: colour, intensity, alignment, circuit continuity. The remaining serviceable lights must still preserve the intended pattern. ICAO guidance sets stricter serviceability objectives for CAT II/III systems and significant lighting elements.

      Taxiway lighting

      Keep guidance continuous and unambiguous: edge and centreline lights, inset and elevated fixtures, stop bars, guard lights, signs, connectors, transformers, foundations. Adjacent centreline failures can matter more than isolated ones, and ICAO-derived criteria include serviceability objectives for taxiway centreline lighting in low-RVR conditions. So classify defects by location and pattern, not just count.

      Approach lighting

      Maintain it as a geometric visual system: availability, intensity, colour, alignment, elevation, barrettes, crossbars, supports, frangible parts, cable and transformer connections, contamination and damage. Pay particular attention to the critical inner portions for high-category operations. ICAO material includes visual inspection plus in-field measurement of intensity, beam spread and orientation for precision approach lighting.

      Taxiway Lighting Maintenance Checklist

      A taxiway lighting maintenance checklist should keep guidance continuous and unambiguous, and defects should be classified by location and pattern, not just count. Adapt it to your approved programme and the frequencies in the AGL Maintenance Checklist further down this page.

      • Taxiway edge and centreline lights operational, with no abnormal pattern or adjacent centreline failures
      • Inset and elevated fixtures: lens condition, alignment, mounting, light base and water ingress
      • Stop bars, guard lights and signs checked
      • Connectors, isolating transformers, junction boxes and foundations inspected
      • Defects classified, closed or escalated, and recorded for trend analysis

      PAPI and Electrical

      PAPI, CCR, Transformers, Cables and Connectors

      PAPI

      Beyond checking lamps are lit: optical (lenses, contamination, water ingress, deterioration), physical (housing, foundation, mounting, corrosion, fasteners), alignment (vertical angle, horizontal alignment, unit-to-unit relationship, elevation, relation to threshold and approved geometry), electrical (transformer, cables, connectors, supply, brightness operation, controls) and performance (white/red transition, beam, intensity, consistency, photometry). Consider calibration after significant maintenance, physical disturbance or changes that affect geometry. The AAI example includes PAPI angle checks with clinometers.

      Constant current regulator

      Inspect enclosure, ventilation, cooling, terminals, supply, output circuit, current indication, control interface, brightness stages, alarms, protection, switching devices, power electronics, communication modules, local and remote operation and status feedback. Where applicable verify output current and stability, each step, alarm response, remote command, circuit indication, changeover and fault reporting, against the CCR design, manufacturer documentation and airport specification, not one generic tolerance.

      Isolating transformers

      Check condition, primary and secondary connectors, leads, housing, moisture, corrosion, overheating, insulation condition, damage and connection security, plus the surrounding enclosure and drainage in housings and bases.

      Airport Lighting Cable Connector & Cable Maintenance

      Surface lamp failures can hide insulation breakdown, water ingress, damaged cable, connector failure, poor joints, transformer failure, earth leakage or discontinuity. Review cable identification and route records, joints, connectors, entry points, water ingress, insulation resistance, continuity, earth faults and fault-location results. Repeated failures at one location are a system-level defect, not repeated lamp replacement.

      Performance and Control

      Photometry, Control Systems, Surge Protection and LED

      Photometric verification

      A light can be electrically operational and no longer give the required optical performance. Photometry evaluates intensity, beam spread, colour, orientation, horizontal and vertical distribution, coverage, PAPI beam characteristics and brightness-stage performance. Trigger it at scheduled intervals, after major maintenance, rehabilitation, replacement or alignment work, after repeated failures, at commissioning and acceptance, when trends show degradation or when the authority requires it. A recent AAI tender covers runway, threshold, approach and PAPI/inset fittings with reports and corrective measures; that is project-specific, not a universal annual rule.

      Control and monitoring (ALCMS/ILCMS)

      Test both paths. Command: operator selects the circuit, the system sends the command, the CCR changes output. Feedback: the CCR changes state, the field status is detected, and the control system shows the correct status or alarm. Check local and remote control, circuit and CCR status, brightness command, alarm generation and acknowledgement, communication, event logs, loss-of-communication alarms and redundant paths. A wrong status indication can matter even when the lights work.

      Lightning and surge protection

      Inspect surge-protection devices, earthing and grounding, bonding, cabinets, CCR and control-system protection, cable-entry protection and visible damage. After a significant lightning event an unscheduled inspection may be appropriate even if the system seems fine.

      LED AGL

      LED cuts some lamp work but not maintenance. ICAO APAC working material highlights vibration, moisture and temperature issues. Inspect optical output, lens, housing, thermal condition, connectors, power supply, electronics, control interface, surge protection, moisture and alignment. An LED fitting that is "on" may still have degraded output.

      Triggers

      Event-Driven Maintenance

      A preventive programme should not run on calendar intervals alone. Trigger unscheduled inspection after lightning, heavy rainfall, flooding, severe storm, high winds, aircraft or vehicle excursion, construction, runway rehabilitation, pavement work, accidental damage, electrical incidents or unexplained alarms. A minor impact to an elevated light can change its orientation enough to affect photometric performance while it keeps working.

      Defects

      Defect Classification and Response

      ClassTypical meaningTypical response
      CriticalMay immediately compromise required guidance or system availabilityImmediate notification, operational assessment, urgent rectification
      MajorSignificant degradation of part of an important lighting systemPriority corrective maintenance
      MinorLocalized defect with limited immediate operational effectPlanned corrective maintenance
      ObservationNeeds monitoring but not immediate repairRecord and trend
      This is a maintenance-management classification, not a replacement for airport-specific operational criteria. Always weigh runway category, operating condition, defect location and applicable serviceability criteria.

      Critical examples

      Major runway circuit failure, loss of critical centreline guidance, PAPI not giving its guidance, serious CCR failure on an operational runway, widespread failure of a critical section, control failure where lighting cannot be reliably controlled or monitored.

      Major examples

      Multiple failed lights, repeated adjacent failures, significant brightness degradation, damaged transformer connection, water ingress affecting several fittings, damaged stop-bar section, significant PAPI alignment discrepancy.

      Minor examples

      Damaged hardware not affecting alignment, minor corrosion, localized lens contamination, non-critical sign damage, minor enclosure defects.

      Records

      Maintenance Records and Trend Analysis

      A good record holds

      • Asset: airport, runway or taxiway, system, circuit, equipment ID, fixture type, transformer ID, CCR number, location
      • Inspection: date, time, technician, conditions, type, test equipment
      • Defect: description, classification, operational impact, temporary and permanent action
      • Test: current, insulation and earth measurements, photometric and PAPI data, control test, calibration status
      • Closure: component replaced, repair, retest, supervisor verification, date

      Registers to maintain

      • Asset and maintenance registers
      • Testing and photometry registers
      • PAPI and CCR registers
      • Defect register (defect, class, impact, repair date, retest, closure)

      Approach

      Preventive, Corrective and Condition-Based Maintenance

      Preventive

      Planned work before failure: cleaning, scheduled inspection, tightening, electrical testing, alignment checks, photometry, connector, transformer and CCR checks.

      Corrective

      Work after a defect: replacing failed fittings, transformers or connectors, cable repair, PAPI realignment, CCR repair, sign replacement, repair after impact.

      Two airports with the same AGL equipment can need very different programmes because of aircraft movements, climate, rainfall, humidity, dust, salt, pavement condition, flooding, lightning exposure, installation age, LED technology, runway category, operating hours, RVR conditions and fault history. The best programmes combine time-based, condition-based and event-driven maintenance: annual photometry, find degradation, raise inspection frequency, clean, repair or replace, retest, trend.

      Airport Lighting Inspection Checklist

      The checklist above sets out the inspection items by frequency, from the daily operational check to the annual or periodic inspection. Adapt it to the aerodrome's approved programme and record the result of each inspection, including any defect and its closure.

      Airport Lighting Day Inspection

      A day inspection is the physical check: damaged or displaced fittings, cracked or contaminated lenses, water ingress, exposed wiring, damaged bases or covers, corrosion, mounting and foreign material around the fittings, plus the condition of signs and PAPI units.

      Airport Lighting Night Inspection

      A night inspection checks the lighting while it is lit. Failed or intermittent lights, abnormal brightness or colour, an abnormal lighting pattern and the PAPI indications are easier to see at night, so it complements the day inspection rather than replacing it.

      India and Contracts

      Maintenance in India and Contractor Scope

      For Indian airports, align the programme with applicable DGCA requirements, AAI requirements where they apply, current AIP/eAIP information, approved airport procedures, equipment manuals and project specifications. A current AAI eAIP entry assigns the Electrical In-charge responsibility for Category I precision-approach lighting serviceability and states the associated runway-lighting serviceability objective. AAI maintenance documents show practical schedules mixing daily cleaning and serviceability checks, periodic PAPI angle checks, brightness-stage checks, CCR operation, wiring and termination inspection and earthing records. Do not copy a generic monthly or annual checklist: build the programme from the actual configuration and requirements.

      Runway Lighting Standards India

      For runway lighting in India, the applicable requirements come from DGCA requirements, AAI requirements where they apply, current AIP/eAIP information, approved airport procedures, equipment manuals and the project specification. There is no single generic standard set for every runway, so identify the requirements from the airport's own documents. See the Standards Companion for how the references fit together.

      Airport Lighting Test Standards

      The standard used for a test depends on what is being tested. Electrical tests follow the equipment manufacturer's documentation and the project specification, and photometric tests follow the requirement the aerodrome or authority defines. Do not use one generic tolerance, and record the test equipment and its calibration status with the result.

      A contractor scope should define

      • Inspection frequency and working hours
      • Airside access, safety and permit requirements
      • Equipment to inspect, test instruments and calibration
      • Spare parts and emergency response
      • Defect response time
      • Documentation and reporting
      • Photometric testing and PAPI calibration
      • CCR maintenance and transformer inspection
      • Cable fault location and post-maintenance testing
      • Final acceptance, and what counts as routine, preventive, corrective, specialized testing or major rehabilitation

      FAQ

      Airport Lighting Maintenance FAQ

      Q1How often should airport lighting be maintained?

      There is no single universal calendar. Base AGL maintenance on the aerodrome's approved preventive-maintenance programme, operating category, equipment type, manufacturer requirements and authority requirements. A practical programme combines daily or operational checks with weekly, monthly, periodic and annual inspections.

      Q2Is airport lighting inspected every day?

      Operational inspections are commonly daily or per shift, but the exact requirement depends on the approved aerodrome procedures. Daily checks focus on serviceability, visible damage, reported faults and operational condition.

      Q3How often should PAPI be checked?

      According to the approved maintenance programme. Routine checks can cover optical cleanliness, physical condition, electrical operation and alignment. Some AAI maintenance schedules specify more frequent PAPI angle checks than a simple monthly inspection, so project-specific requirements can be more demanding than a generic schedule.

      Q4How often should airport lighting photometry be performed?

      There is no single interval for every installation. Photometry may be scheduled periodically and also triggered by commissioning, rehabilitation, alignment changes, fixture replacement, repeated defects or performance concerns. The airport specification and maintenance programme decide the interval.

      Q5What is preventive maintenance in airport lighting?

      Planned work done to reduce the probability of failure or degradation: inspection, cleaning, testing, alignment, electrical checks, photometry, component inspection and similar activities.

      Q6What is corrective maintenance in AGL?

      Work done after a defect is identified, such as replacing failed fittings, repairing cables, replacing isolating transformers, repairing CCRs and realigning PAPI.

      Q7Why is AGL cable maintenance important?

      AGL is an electrical distribution system, not independent lamps. Cable, connector and transformer problems can cause repeated light failures and circuit instability, so the programme should include the associated cable systems. FAA preventive-maintenance guidance includes cable systems.

      Q8Should LED airport lighting still be maintained?

      Yes. LED reduces lamp replacement but still needs inspection of optical output, electronics, thermal condition, connectors, housing, surge protection, moisture ingress and alignment.

      Q9What should happen after an AGL repair?

      Test the repaired equipment before closing the defect. Depending on the repair, that may be electrical testing, operational testing, control-system verification, alignment checks or photometric verification.

      Q10What is the difference between AGL maintenance and AGL testing?

      Maintenance restores or preserves equipment condition. Testing measures and verifies performance. A good programme uses both.

      Q11Why are maintenance records important?

      They show what was inspected and tested, evidence defect closure, support audits and enable trend analysis that can expose recurring problems before they become major failures.
