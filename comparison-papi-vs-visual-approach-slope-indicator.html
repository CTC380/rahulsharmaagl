Terminology & Classification

PAPI VS VISUAL APPROACH SLOPE INDICATOR

This isn't really a comparison of two separate systems — it's a terminology question. "Visual approach slope indicator" is the generic category, and PAPI is one modern member of it; the acronym VASI, strictly, names a different member — the older red/white bar system. The FAA identifies PAPI as the current standard Visual Glide Slope Indicator (VGSI), a category that also includes traditional VASI bar configurations, PVASI and tri-colour systems; ICAO's equivalent group also includes T-VASIS and AT-VASIS.

01 · The Real Relationship

PAPI and VASI Are Siblings in One Family

The clearest way to frame this: "visual approach slope indicator" is the umbrella term for systems of lights that give a pilot visual descent guidance on approach — ICAO Annex 14 uses "visual approach slope indicator systems" for the group that includes PAPI, and the FAA's equivalent umbrella is VGSI. The acronym VASI, however, conventionally names one specific member of the group: the red/white two- or three-bar system. PAPI is a sibling of VASI within the family — one that happens to have become the modern standard.

So "PAPI vs VASI" compares two siblings, while "PAPI vs visual approach slope indicator" compares one member with the whole family it belongs to — not two unrelated technologies.

The FAA notes that VASI installations can use 2, 4, 6, 12 or 16 light units arranged in near, middle and far bars, while PAPI uses a single row of two or four units.

03 · What Is VASI?

The Bar-Type System

VASI is a system of lights arranged to give visual descent guidance during an approach. In its traditional, most recognizable form it uses light bars that the pilot reads relative to one another. The same three words are also used loosely as the name of the whole category, which is where the confusion starts.

Traditional 2-Bar VASI

White / WhiteHigh
Red / WhiteOn glide path
Red / RedLow

The pilot reads the relationship between the far bar and the near bar (far over near — “red over white” is on glide path), not a count of individual lights.

04 · What Is PAPI?

A Sibling Type Within the Same Family

PAPI — Precision Approach Path Indicator — arranges its light units similarly to VASI, but installs them in a single horizontal row of either two or four units, rather than in separate near/far bars.

PAPI — 5 Principal States (4-unit arrangement)

4 WhiteToo high
3 White + 1 RedSlightly high
2 White + 2 RedOn glide path
1 White + 3 RedSlightly low
4 RedToo low

The FAA's PAPI diagram identifies 2 white + 2 red as the nominal on-glide-path indication, with progressively more white above the path and more red below it. A two-unit PAPI (FAA L-881, ICAO's APAPI) gives three indications instead: two white = high, one white + one red = on path, two red = low.

05 · The Real Difference

Comparing Bars vs Counting Units

Traditional VASI

Read the far bar over the near bar — “red over white” is on path
White / White — high
Red / White — on path
Red / Red — low

PAPI

Count white and red units across the row
4 White — high
2 White + 2 Red — on path
4 Red — low

PAPI gives more discrete positional information than a traditional 2-bar VASI. An FAA Technical Center evaluation (1983) found PAPI was preferred over the standard red/white VASI, mainly for its extra rate and position information and its quicker colour transitions — although US pilots found little fault with VASI itself.

06 · Physical Arrangement

Bars vs a Single Row

Traditional VASI

Arranged as separate near and far light bars, sometimes with a middle bar as well, spread out along the approach path.

PAPI

Arranged as a single, compact horizontal row of light units, typically installed to one side of the runway.

07 · Shared Purpose

Both Provide Vertical Approach-Slope Guidance

Whatever the specific configuration, every visual approach slope indicator — including PAPI — exists to answer the same question: is the aircraft on the correct visual descent path to the runway? Neither PAPI nor any traditional VASI configuration provides lateral navigation. The FAA notes that lateral course guidance during a visual approach comes from the runway and runway lighting, or from other navigation aids — not from the VASI/PAPI system itself.

Neither is a substitute for an ILS. An ILS localizer and glideslope provide radio-based lateral and vertical guidance; visual approach slope indicators (PAPI included) are purely visual references for the vertical path once the runway environment is in sight.

08 · Terminology Map

PAPI Is a VGSI

The FAA's umbrella term is Visual Glide Slope Indicator (VGSI). Under it sit VASI (in its various bar counts), PAPI, PVASI, tri-colour systems and alignment-of-elements systems. ICAO groups its own systems as "visual approach slope indicator systems" — PAPI/APAPI plus T-VASIS/AT-VASIS.

  • VASI — traditional near/far (or multi-bar) configuration
  • PAPI — single-row, current standard configuration
  • T-VASIS / AT-VASIS — T-shaped configuration, and its abbreviated form (an ICAO system; not among the VGSI types listed in the FAA AIM)
  • PVASI — pulsating light configuration

So "Visual Approach Slope Indicator" can mean the VASI system specifically, or it can be used loosely as the general category — PAPI is a particular implementation within that broader family, not a competing category.

09 · Why PAPI Became the Standard

Practical Advantages Over Bar-Type VASI

  • Compact, single-row installation footprint
  • Intuitive pilot interpretation — a graded white/red count rather than a two-bar colour comparison
  • More graduated vertical-position information than a traditional 2-bar VASI
  • Fewer components than many multi-bar VASI configurations
  • Easier integration into modern runway lighting and monitoring systems

The FAA identifies PAPI as the current standard VGSI, though older VASI configurations remain in service at a number of airports.

10 · Common Misconception

Myths vs Facts

Myth: PAPI and VASI are completely different, unrelated systems
Fact

Not exactly. They are different members of the same family of visual approach slope indicators — related in purpose and light-unit design, but with different geometry and indication logic.

Myth: "VASI" only ever refers to the old 2-bar system
Fact

Not strictly. VASI installations vary — 2-bar systems use 2, 4 or 12 units and 3-bar systems 6 or 16 — and "visual approach slope indicator" is also used loosely as the general category name that includes PAPI.

Myth: PAPI provides lateral or centreline guidance
Fact

No. Like the other visual approach slope indicators, PAPI's primary function is vertical approach-path guidance only.

Myth: 2 White + 2 Red on a PAPI means the aircraft is high
Fact

No — 2 White + 2 Red is the standard on-glide-path indication, not a high indication.

Myth: Because PAPI is newer, VASI is obsolete everywhere
Fact

No. PAPI is the current standard for new installations, but traditional VASI configurations remain installed and in service at many airports.

11 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI vs Visual Approach Slope Indicator

Engineering Verdict

This isn't a like-for-like comparison — it's a terminology question. "Visual approach slope indicator" is the broad family name for visual glide-slope systems; PAPI is the single-row member that has become the current standard, alongside the bar-type VASI, T-VASIS, PVASI and others. Only the acronym VASI names a specific counterpart.

Both exist to answer the same question for the pilot — is the aircraft on the correct visual descent path — but PAPI's single-row, white/red-count design has largely superseded older multi-bar VASI configurations for new installations.

PAPIA specific VGSI type — single row, 2 or 4 units, current standard.
VASIThe generic category name — and, as an acronym, the traditional bar-based member of it.

When someone asks "PAPI vs VASI," they almost always mean PAPI vs the traditional bar-type VASI — while PAPI itself is, technically, a type of visual approach slope indicator.

12 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PAPI a type of VASI, or a completely different system?

Strictly, PAPI is a type of visual approach slope indicator (the generic category) but not a type of VASI (the specific red/white bar system). It is a sibling: it uses light units similar to VASI, arranged in a single row and read by counting red and white units. Because "VASI" is sometimes stretched to cover the whole category, the question comes up often.

What does the FAA call this category of systems?

The FAA uses the umbrella term Visual Glide Slope Indicator (VGSI), under which PAPI, traditional VASI bar configurations, PVASI, tri-colour and alignment-of-elements systems sit. ICAO uses "visual approach slope indicator systems" for its own group — PAPI/APAPI and T-VASIS/AT-VASIS.

How many light units can a traditional VASI installation have?

The FAA notes VASI installations can use 2, 4, 6, 12 or 16 light units, arranged in near, middle and far bars, depending on the installation.

Why do pilots generally prefer PAPI over a traditional VASI?

PAPI's single-row white/red count gives more graduated positional information and faster colour transitions than comparing near and far bars, which FAA evaluation work found pilots tended to prefer.

Does either system provide lateral or centreline guidance?

No. Both PAPI and traditional VASI configurations provide vertical approach-path guidance only. Lateral guidance during a visual approach comes from the runway and its lighting, or from other navigation aids.

Is traditional VASI still in use today?

Yes, at a number of airports, even though PAPI is now the current standard VGSI for new installations. The applicable system should always be verified for the specific runway.
