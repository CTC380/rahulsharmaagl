Calculator #03

Runway Centreline Lights

Requirement, spacing, colour-zone and fixture-quantity tool for runway centreline lighting (RCL) — FAA AC 150/5340-30J.

About this calculator

The fixture position sequence is generated first, at nominal 50 ft (15.24 m) spacing, and colours are assigned afterward from each position's distance to the runway's stop end — never calculated as three independent 3,000/2,000/1,000 ft blocks, which can duplicate or omit endpoint fixtures. Colour is directional: the same physical fixture can present differently depending on landing direction, so this calculator reports one physical fixture count alongside two colour-sequence breakdowns. An ICAO Annex 14 equivalent is not yet incorporated — use Project-Specific for an ICAO-based specification rather than assuming the FAA figures apply.

Method & Reference

How This Calculator Works

Centreline colour zones are assigned from a single continuous position sequence, not calculated as three independent block counts — and colour is directional, so the same installed fixture is interpreted differently depending on which threshold a pilot is landing over.

Position generation before colour

Positions are generated first, at uniform spacing from the starting reference:

xi = x0 + i·S   (S = 15.24 m / 50 ft)

Only after the full position sequence exists is each position classified by colour. Calculating 3,000/2,000/1,000 ft zones independently and adding the counts can duplicate or omit a fixture at a zone boundary — this calculator avoids that by construction.

Colour-zone assignment (general form)

Measuring distance from the runway's stop end, general zone lengths (valid for any runway length, including short runways where the full 6,000 ft scheme cannot exist):

Zred = min(1000 ft, L)  →  Zalt = min(2000 ft, L − Zred)  →  Zwhite = L − Zred − Zalt

For L > 3,000 ft this is equivalent to: white until the last 3,000 ft, alternating for the next 2,000 ft, red for the final 1,000 ft — white is not capped at a fixed 3,000 ft on longer runways; it occupies the remainder.

Direction changes colour, not fixture count

Because the colour scheme is measured from the stop end in the landing direction, the same physical position is red when a small distance from Threshold B (landing A→B) but may be white when the same position is a large distance from Threshold A (landing B→A). This calculator keeps physical fixture count and optical colour interpretation as two separate outputs rather than conflating them.

Existing-installation inspection — two checks, not one

Individual interval spacing can each pass tolerance while the whole sequence still drifts from the intended layout. Both checks are run independently:

Check A: |Si − S| ≤ tol   |   Check B: |xi − xi,design| ≤ tol

FAQ

Runway Centreline Lights Calculator: Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers on centreline light positions, colour zones and the existing-installation checks.

What does the Runway Centreline Lights Calculator do?

It generates the runway centreline light positions at uniform spacing, assigns the white, alternating red and white, and red colour zones, and reports the physical fixture quantity. It also checks an existing installation's measured positions against spacing tolerance and pavement-joint clearance, and produces a PDF report.

When are runway centreline lights required?

The calculator uses FAA AC 150/5340-30J. Centreline lights are required for CAT II and CAT III. For CAT I they are required when landing RVR is below 750 m. They are also required when takeoff RVR is below 480 m, unless an FAA-approved exception applies. For CAT I they are recommended, not required, where the runway is wider than 170 ft or the approach speed is above 140 kt. These trigger values are FAA-based, and ICAO Annex 14 uses different RVR thresholds.

What longitudinal spacing and tolerances does the calculator use?

The FAA AC 150/5340-30J §3.3.1.1 basis is 50 ft (15.24 m) longitudinal spacing with a ±2 ft (0.6096 m) tolerance. The maximum uniform lateral offset from the true centreline is 2.5 ft (0.762 m). The minimum clearance from a fixture base to a pavement joint on a new runway is 2 ft (0.6096 m). Edit these only under the Project-Specific basis.

How are the colour zones laid out?

Measured from the stop end, the final 1,000 ft (304.8 m) is red, the next 2,000 ft (609.6 m) alternates red and white, and white occupies the remainder. White is never capped at a fixed 3,000 ft on runways longer than 6,000 ft. For a 6,000 ft runway the zones are 1,000 ft red, 2,000 ft alternating and 3,000 ft white. Short runways are handled by the general form Zred = min(1000 ft, L).

Why does the calculator generate positions before assigning colours?

Positions are generated first from xi = x0 + i·S, and each position is classified by colour only afterwards. Calculating the three zone lengths independently and adding the counts can duplicate or omit the fixture at a zone boundary. Generating one continuous sequence avoids that by construction.

Why can the same fixture be white from one end and red from the other?

Colour is measured from the stop end in the landing direction, so it depends on the direction of approach. The same physical position may be red when landing A to B but white when landing B to A. The calculator reports the physical fixture count and the colour breakdown for each landing direction as separate outputs, and both totals match the physical count.

How does the existing-installation inspection work?

It runs two independent checks on the measured chainages. Check A tests each interval against the spacing tolerance, |Si − S| ≤ tol. Check B tests each cumulative position against the design position, |xi − xi,design| ≤ tol. Both are needed because a run of uniform but off-nominal intervals can drift out of tolerance cumulatively even if every individual interval passes. Pavement joint positions can also be checked against the joint clearance.

Is the quantity a final bill of quantities?

No. It is a calculated nominal quantity, not a final BOQ quantity. The exact zone-boundary and alternating-sequence endpoint arrangement, and the first colour of the alternating zone, are figure-specific, so verify them against FAA Figure A-34 or the applicable project figure before issue. The calculator is an engineering reference tool and does not incorporate an ICAO Annex 14 equivalent, so use the Project-Specific basis for ICAO work.
