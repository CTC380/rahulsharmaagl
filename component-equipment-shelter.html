Equipment Shelter

A dedicated protective enclosure that houses critical AGL equipment such as Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), power distribution panels, communication gateways, and control systems. Equipment shelters protect sensitive electronics from environmental exposure, vandalism, and extreme weather, ensuring reliable operation of the airfield lighting system.

Overview

What Is It?

An Equipment Shelter (also known as an equipment enclosure or equipment hut) is a weatherproof, secure structure designed to house sensitive electrical and electronic equipment used in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems. These shelters protect critical components from environmental factors such as rain, snow, dust, extreme temperatures, and UV radiation, while also providing physical security against vandalism and unauthorised access.

Typical equipment housed in these shelters includes:

  • Constant Current Regulators (CCRs): The primary power supply units for AGL series circuits.
  • Power Distribution Panels: Circuit breakers, switchgear, and distribution boards for AC and DC power.
  • ALCMS Control Equipment: Servers, PLCs, communication gateways, and network switches.
  • Communication Equipment: Fibre optic transceivers, Ethernet switches, and radio modems.
  • UPS and Battery Systems: Uninterruptible power supplies and backup batteries for critical control systems.
  • Monitoring and Alarm Panels: Local display units and alarm annunciators.

Key characteristics of an equipment shelter include:

  • Construction: Typically made of steel, aluminium, or GRP (glass‑reinforced plastic) for durability and corrosion resistance.
  • Environmental Protection: Rated to IP54 or higher (dust and water ingress protection).
  • Thermal Management: Equipped with ventilation, heating, or air conditioning to maintain equipment operating temperature.
  • Security: Lockable doors, tamper‑proof fasteners, and sometimes CCTV or alarm systems.
  • Access: Designed for safe entry and maintenance access, with adequate lighting and workspace inside.
Purpose

Purpose & Function

The equipment shelter's purpose is to protect the equipment that the rest of the AGL system depends on to function at all, since a CCR, ALCMS server, or communication gateway failing from moisture, dust, or temperature extremes does not just take out one fixture but can affect an entire circuit or the operator's visibility into the whole lighting system. Housing this equipment together also concentrates the airfield's most safety-critical control infrastructure in a small number of physically secured locations, which makes both routine maintenance and protection against tampering or vandalism far more practical than if the same equipment were distributed unprotected across the field. Environmental control inside the shelter, ventilation, heating, or air conditioning, exists for the same underlying reason: to keep sensitive electronics operating within the temperature range their reliability depends on, regardless of what the weather outside is doing.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Equipment shelters are deployed at various strategic locations across the airfield:

  • CCR Vaults and Substations: Dedicated shelters located near the runway or taxiway edge, housing the CCRs that power the AGL series circuits.
  • ALCMS Control Rooms: Larger shelters or buildings that house the central ALCMS servers, workstations, and communication equipment.
  • Remote Equipment Sites: Shelters located near navigation aids (ILS, VOR, DME) to house local control and monitoring equipment.
  • Apron and Gate Areas: Shelters housing power distribution equipment for apron floodlighting, ground power units, and visual docking guidance systems.
  • Equipment Shelters for Obstruction Lighting: Small enclosures located at the base of towers or masts, housing the controllers and power supplies for obstruction lights.

Equipment shelters serve several critical functions:

  • Environmental Protection: Shielding sensitive electronics from rain, snow, dust, and extreme temperatures.
  • Physical Security: Preventing vandalism, theft, and unauthorised access to critical equipment.
  • Thermal Management: Maintaining equipment within its specified operating temperature range through ventilation, heating, or cooling.
  • Safety: Providing a safe environment for maintenance personnel, with proper lighting, space, and emergency access.
Placement

Installation & Location

The shelter is set on a levelled concrete plinth or foundation sized to the unit's footprint, anchored to resist wind uplift and sited far enough from the runway or taxiway edge to sit outside the obstacle-limitation surface and any frangibility-sensitive zone, since a fixed shelter is not designed to break away on impact. Cable entry points are positioned to align with the incoming duct bank from the CCR room, distribution point, or fibre backbone, with sufficient clearance around the building for vehicle access, door swing, and routine maintenance. Where the shelter serves a specific fixture group or navigation aid, it's sited as close as practical to that equipment to keep circuit cable runs short, while remaining accessible by the airfield's service road network without requiring a vehicle to cross an active movement area.

Function

How Does It Work?

An equipment shelter functions through its design, construction, and integration with the airfield infrastructure:

  • Structural Design: The shelter is designed to withstand environmental loads including wind, snow, and seismic forces. It is typically anchored to a concrete foundation or slab to prevent movement and provide a stable base.
  • Weatherproofing: The shelter is constructed with weathertight seals, gaskets, and drainage systems to prevent water ingress. Roofs are sloped to shed water, and cable entry points are sealed with cable glands or conduit.
  • Thermal Management: Equipment generates heat during operation. The shelter is equipped with:
    • Ventilation: Natural or forced ventilation with louvres and fans to exhaust hot air.
    • Air Conditioning: For heat‑sensitive equipment or in hot climates, air conditioning units maintain a stable internal temperature.
    • Heating: In cold climates, electric heaters prevent condensation and maintain minimum operating temperatures.
  • Electrical Infrastructure: The shelter includes power distribution, earthing, and surge protection systems. The equipment is mounted on racks or frames, with cable trays and trunking for organised cable management.
  • Security and Access: Doors are fitted with robust locks and often have alarms. Inside, the shelter has adequate lighting, working space, and sometimes emergency lighting for power failure.
  • Fire Protection: Many shelters are equipped with fire detection and suppression systems (e.g., smoke detectors, gas or powder extinguishers) to protect valuable equipment.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The shelter itself carries no lighting control logic — its role in the wider system architecture is to host the CCR, PLC, and ALCMS interface equipment, along with the switchgear that commands the surrounding circuits, plus the power distribution, earthing, and surge protection that supports it. Cable entries are typically segregated so that the LV power feed, outgoing series-circuit cabling to the field loop, and control/communications wiring stay on separate, clearly identified paths. This segregation means a shelter-level event — loss of shelter power, ventilation or air-conditioning failure, door alarm — is reported to ALCMS as a distinct alarm class from any field-circuit fault, keeping the health of the equipment enclosure separate from the health of the loops that equipment controls.

Construction

Physical Construction

The shelter is a prefabricated steel, aluminium, or GRP (glass-reinforced plastic) cabinet, ranging from a compact 2.0m x 2.0m x 2.5m field unit up to a 6.0m x 4.0m x 3.5m housing for larger equipment racks, bolted down on a poured concrete slab or ground anchors rather than resting freely. The enclosure seals to an IP54–IP66 rating with a lockable door (sometimes glazed with a viewing window) and internal ventilation, air conditioning, or electric heating to hold the +5°C to +40°C range the equipment inside needs regardless of outside conditions. Mains power (230V/400V AC) enters through a sealed gland with UPS backup inside, the whole structure bonds to a dedicated earth pit kept below 5Ω resistance, and the exterior finish is chosen in a grey, green, or other aviation-appropriate colour so the shelter doesn't stand out against its surroundings.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Condensation and Moisture Ingress

Temperature fluctuations can cause condensation inside the shelter, leading to corrosion and electrical failures. Proper sealing of cable entry points, installation of dehumidifiers, and maintenance of ventilation systems are essential to prevent moisture accumulation.

Cooling System Failures

Air conditioning units or ventilation fans can fail, causing the internal temperature to rise above the equipment's operating range. This can lead to equipment shutdown or permanent damage. Regular maintenance of cooling systems, including filter cleaning and refrigerant checks, is critical.

Pest Infestation

Rodents, insects, and birds can enter shelters through gaps, chewing cables and causing short circuits. Sealing all openings and periodic pest control inspections are necessary to prevent damage.

Door and Lock Deterioration

Exposure to weather can cause door seals, hinges, and locks to deteriorate, compromising security and weatherproofing. Regular inspection and replacement of worn components are required.

Earthing and Lightning Protection Degradation

Earthing connections can corrode over time, increasing resistance and compromising safety. Regular testing of earth resistance and maintenance of lightning protection systems are essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Corrosion or Electrical Faults Traced to Moisture Inside
Likely Cause
Condensation forming from temperature fluctuations, or a failed seal at a cable entry point allowing moisture ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect cable entry seals and gland plates for gaps or degradation; check for visible condensation, staining, or corrosion on equipment and enclosures; verify any dehumidifier is running and its reservoir isn't full.
Corrective Action
Re-seal cable entries, service or replace the dehumidifier, and confirm ventilation is functioning; dry out affected equipment and check it for corrosion damage before re-energising.
Internal Temperature Running High or Equipment Shutting Down on Heat
Likely Cause
Air conditioning or ventilation fan failure, or a clogged filter restricting airflow.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the cooling unit's operation and refrigerant charge; inspect and clean or replace air filters; log internal temperature against the equipment's rated operating range.
Corrective Action
Service or replace the failed cooling unit or fan, clean/replace filters, and confirm internal temperature has returned within the equipment's rated range before restoring full load.
Chewed Cables or Unexplained Short Circuits
Likely Cause
Rodents, insects, or birds have entered through an unsealed gap and damaged cabling inside the shelter.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the shelter for entry points (gaps around cable entries, vents, or door seals); look for droppings, nesting material, or gnaw marks on cables.
Corrective Action
Seal all identified entry points, replace damaged cabling, and schedule a pest control inspection; consider rodent-resistant cable conduit for future installations.
Door Won't Seal or Lock Properly
Likely Cause
Weathering has degraded door seals, hinges, or the lock mechanism, compromising security and weatherproofing.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect door seals for cracking or compression set; check hinge alignment and lock mechanism operation; confirm the door closes flush against the frame.
Corrective Action
Replace worn seals, hinges, or lock hardware, and re-test that the door seals weathertight and locks securely before leaving the site.
High Earth Resistance or Lightning Protection Concerns
Likely Cause
Corroded earthing connections have increased resistance over time, or a lightning protection component has been damaged.
Diagnostic Steps
Test earth resistance at the shelter's earthing point and compare against the required maximum; visually inspect earthing conductors and lightning protection components for corrosion or physical damage.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace corroded earthing connections, repair or replace damaged lightning protection components, and re-test to confirm resistance is within the required limit.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Entry
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out shelter power distribution before working inside on CCRs, monitoring equipment, or wiring, and verify zero energy before touching any terminal.

Fire Suppression & Extinguisher Check
Operational

Confirm the shelter's fire extinguisher or suppression system is present, in date, and unobstructed at every inspection, given the concentration of energised electrical equipment inside.

Earthing/Bonding Verification
Electrical Safety

Check the shelter's earthing and bonding connections for corrosion or looseness before working on equipment inside, since a degraded earth defeats the protection it's meant to provide.

Pest & Housekeeping Control
Operational

Keep the shelter interior clear of stored combustibles and check seals and screens for pest entry points at each visit, since rodent damage to cabling is a common cause of unexplained faults.

Door Interlock & Lockout
Personnel Safety

Follow the shelter's lockout/tagout procedure before working on any energised equipment inside, and never prop open or defeat a door interlock that is there to prevent unauthorised or unsafe entry.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Monitor Climate Control as Its Own System
Maintenance

A failed HVAC or climate control unit inside a sealed shelter causes cumulative heat stress on every piece of equipment housed inside well before any single device fails outright; treat shelter climate control as a monitored system in its own right rather than assuming it's working because nothing inside has failed yet.

A Weathertight Shelter Can Still Trap Internal Condensation
Design

A shelter sealed well enough to keep rain out can trap humidity inside if ventilation is inadequate, leading to internal condensation that a rain-tight design alone doesn't prevent; verify ventilation and humidity control separately from the shelter's weatherproofing.

Cable Entry Seals Fail Before the Main Structure Does
Inspection

The gland and entry points where cables penetrate the shelter wall are typically the first point of water ingress, well ahead of any failure in the main structure; inspect these penetrations as a distinct item rather than judging the shelter's weatherproofing from its overall condition.

Verify the Shelter Frame's Own Bonding, Not Just the Equipment Inside
Electrical

A shelter housing sensitive electronics is itself part of the site's overall bonding system; periodically verify the structure's own grounding and bonding rather than assuming it's adequate because the equipment inside is properly earthed.

Match Door Seal Inspection Frequency to Actual Access Frequency
Maintenance

A shelter accessed frequently for routine maintenance wears its door seal and lock mechanism faster than one rarely opened; adjust the door inspection interval to the site's actual access pattern rather than applying one generic schedule to every shelter.

Check Conduit Penetrations for Pest Exclusion, Not Just Weatherproofing
Durability

A shelter that's physically secure against human intrusion can still have small gaps around unsealed conduit penetrations that let rodents in, which then chew cable insulation from inside; check these penetrations specifically for pest exclusion as a separate item from the general weatherproofing check.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL equipment shelters: what they house, construction, climate control, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an AGL equipment shelter?

An equipment shelter, also called an equipment enclosure or hut, is a weatherproof, secure structure that houses sensitive electrical and electronic equipment for airfield ground lighting. It protects equipment from rain, snow, dust, temperature extremes and UV, and provides physical security against vandalism and unauthorised access.

Q2What equipment is kept in an equipment shelter?

Constant current regulators, power distribution panels, ALCMS control equipment and communication gateways, and local control and monitoring equipment. Shelters are used in CCR vaults and substations, ALCMS control rooms, navaid sites (ILS, VOR, DME) and apron and gate areas.

Q3Why does the shelter matter so much?

A CCR, ALCMS server or communication gateway that fails from moisture, dust or temperature extremes can affect an entire circuit or the operator's visibility of the whole lighting system, not just one fixture. Housing equipment together also concentrates safety-critical control equipment in a protected space.

Q4How big is an equipment shelter?

Typically from about 2.0 m × 2.0 m × 2.5 m for small shelters up to 6.0 m × 4.0 m × 3.5 m for large ones, built from steel, aluminium or GRP.

Q5What IP rating and temperature range should a shelter provide?

Typically IP54 to IP66 depending on location, keeping the interior at about +5 °C to +40 °C through ventilation, air conditioning or electric heating.

Q6Where should an equipment shelter be sited?

On a levelled concrete plinth or foundation, anchored against wind uplift, and far enough from the runway or taxiway edge to sit outside the obstacle-limitation surface and any frangibility-sensitive zone, since a fixed shelter is not designed to break away on impact. Cable entries align with the incoming duct bank.

Q7What power and earthing does a shelter have?

A 230 V or 400 V AC mains supply with UPS backup, and a dedicated earth pit with a specified earth resistance. Smoke detectors and fire extinguishers are optional additions.

Q8Which standards apply to equipment shelters?

The main references are IEC 60364 (low-voltage installations), NEC/NFPA 70, FAA AC 150/5340-30 (including CCR vault and equipment shelter guidance) and IEC 60529 (IP code). Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should an equipment shelter be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cooling system service, semi-annual for seal and cable entry inspection and pest control inspection, and annual for door, hinge and lock inspection and earth resistance testing. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What causes moisture and corrosion problems inside a shelter?

Temperature fluctuations cause condensation, and a failed seal at a cable entry lets moisture in. Re-seal cable entries, service the dehumidifier and ventilation, and dry out and inspect affected equipment for corrosion before re-energising.
