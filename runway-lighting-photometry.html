Runway Lighting Photometry: Intensity, Coverage, Colour & Testing

How photometry measures the intensity, distribution, colour and alignment of runway edge, threshold, end, centreline, touchdown zone and approach lights. A runway light can be switched on and still give too little output, the wrong beam pattern or the wrong direction.

Start Learning AGL Photometry Concepts
Overview

What Is Runway Lighting Photometry?

Runway lighting photometry is the measurement and evaluation of the optical performance of runway lighting fixtures. It shows whether installed lights give the intensity, distribution, colour and alignment their operational function needs, and it answers a different question from "is the lamp on?"

Why on/off is not enough

Two runway edge lights can look alike during a night inspection while one has much lower output or a displaced beam. Photometry finds differences that visual inspection cannot judge reliably.

When it is carried out

Commissioning, periodic inspection, preventive maintenance, rehabilitation, troubleshooting, and after replacement of runway lighting equipment.

Depending on the fixture and test requirement, measurements may examine

Luminous intensityIntensity distributionBeam coverageOptical alignmentColourRelative uniformityFixture-to-fixture consistencyChange from baselineDegradation over time
Acceptance criteria come from the governing aerodrome standard, approved project specification, equipment requirements and authority documentation. ICAO Annex 14 Volume I is the principal international reference for visual aids. FAA maintenance guidance (AC 150/5340-26C) notes that regular photometric measurement can identify reduced output and misalignment and target maintenance where it is needed.

Purpose

Why Runway Photometry Is Important

Runway lights form a visual guidance system, and their usefulness depends on the pilot receiving a consistent and unambiguous visual pattern. A photometry programme supports both compliance verification and condition-based maintenance.

Low intensity

A lamp, LED assembly or optical system can degrade while still operating.

Misalignment

The fixture produces light, but the main beam may no longer point the right way.

Contaminated optics

Rubber deposits, dirt, dust and moisture cut output, especially for in-pavement fixtures.

Incorrect colour

Lenses, filters or light sources can deteriorate and move colour out of requirement.

Incorrect installation

A replacement fixture or optical assembly may be oriented or installed wrongly.

Uneven performance

Individual fixtures can drift away from the rest of the lighting system.

      Measurement

      Intensity, Colour and Coverage

      Intensity

      Depends on fixture design, light source, optics, operating step, beam direction, measurement geometry, environment and fixture condition. Controlled, repeatable conditions are required.

      Colour

      Intensity and colour are related but separate checks. A fixture with acceptable intensity can still have degraded colour from filter, lens or light-source ageing.

      Coverage

      Whether enough output is given over the required angular region. A high peak intensity can still fail the distribution.

      Intensity at different brightness steps

      Report the operating condition used (low, intermediate or high step, where those apply). Comparing a fixture measured at one step with a specification for another gives misleading results.

      Absolute versus comparative

      Absolute: the measured value is compared directly with a specified limit. Comparative: the result is compared with baseline, neighbouring fixtures or history, which can expose a progressive loss before a formal failure.

      How Is Runway Light Intensity Measured?

      Runway light intensity is measured with calibrated photometric test equipment under controlled, repeatable conditions. The fixture is inspected and, where the procedure permits, cleaned first. The sensor position, distance, viewing direction, height and fixture orientation are then fixed and recorded, and the circuit is set to the required brightness step. The reading is compared with the applicable acceptance criterion or with baseline data, using the same geometry each time. See the measurement procedure below for the full ten stages.

      Analyse more than peak output

      AspectWhat it tells you
      Peak outputLight produced in the principal measurement direction
      Angular distributionHow output changes with viewing angle
      Useful coverageWhether intensity holds across the required beam region
      ConsistencyWhether adjacent fixtures give a comparable and appropriate pattern

      Equipment

      Runway Photometer, Calibration and Test Equipment

      What a runway photometer does

      An instrument or measurement system for the optical output of airport lighting fixtures. Depending on the equipment it measures or calculates luminous intensity, distribution, angular position, colour-related information, fixture orientation, beam characteristics and location. Mobile systems allow rapid measurement of runway installations and help locate deficient fixtures.

      A typical system may include

      Photometric sensor, optical sensor or camera, positioning, distance and angular measurement, data acquisition unit, computer or tablet, software, fixture identification database and calibration references. Mobile systems add location tracking and automated processing.

      Before testing, verify

      • Instrument identification and calibration status
      • Calibration certificate, reference or traceability requirements
      • Measurement range and sensor condition
      • Software or data-acquisition configuration
      • Date and time settings
      A result from an instrument with uncertain calibration status is not equivalent to a controlled measurement. The equipment needs accuracy and repeatability suited to the acceptance or maintenance purpose.

      Procedure

      Runway Photometry Measurement Procedure

      A controlled test follows ten stages. Do not assume the same acceptance limits apply to every runway or fixture type.

      01

      Review the test requirements

      Runway, lighting system, fixture types, scope, standards, specifications, acceptance criteria, operating settings and measurement points.

      02

      Review drawings and asset information

      Layout, runway geometry, fixture schedule, datasheets, earlier photometric reports, maintenance history and as-builts.

      03

      Verify test equipment

      Calibration, identification, software set-up, sensor configuration, range, date and time, data storage.

      04

      Inspect the runway lighting

      Damaged lenses, contamination, missing fixtures, damaged housings, wrong orientation, water ingress, loose fittings, damaged cables or connectors, abnormal illumination. Clean before final acceptance measurements where the procedure permits.

      05

      Establish measurement geometry

      Sensor position, distance, viewing direction, height, horizontal and vertical alignment, angle and fixture orientation. Use the same geometry when comparing.

      06

      Operate at the required setting

      Set the specified test intensity and record the actual operating condition.

      07

      Measure the fixtures

      Each fixture or the specified representative set, linked to runway, type, fixture ID, station, step, date and time, and result.

      08

      Evaluate the results

      Compare with acceptance criteria and classify: pass, fail, investigate, maintenance required or retest required.

      09

      Correct deficiencies

      Cleaning, optical adjustment, realignment, lens, lamp, LED module or fixture replacement, electrical troubleshooting, transformer or connector inspection, water-ingress correction.

      10

      Retest

      Remeasure corrected fixtures. A light that is visible again is not proof of the required optical performance.

      Test points depend on the fixture and specification

      Horizontal distributionVertical distributionMain beam intensityColourBeam coverage
      Where acceptance is based on a distribution, evaluate more than a single peak intensity value.

      Alignment

      Photometry and Alignment

      How alignment errors arise

      Vehicle impact, maintenance work, fixture replacement, pavement work, foundation movement, incorrect installation and mechanical loosening.

      What photometry shows

      An incorrectly aligned light can look bright from the wrong location. Photometric measurement shows whether the optical output is delivered in the intended direction, so use it together with runway geometry and fixture orientation data.

      Failed Results

      Handling a Failed Photometric Result

      A failed measurement is not automatically a failed fixture. Validate the measurement before assigning maintenance.

      Check the measurement

      • Test instrument calibration
      • Test geometry
      • Sensor alignment
      • Operating intensity
      • Fixture identification
      • Measurement repeatability

      Then check the fixture

      • Contamination
      • Physical alignment
      • Optical condition
      • Electrical condition

      Retest after

      Cleaning for low outputRealignmentRepairRe-lampingNew optical assemblyFixture replacementPavement workAny other corrective action

      Why retesting matters

      Maintenance can fix one problem and introduce another: a lens replacement may alter output, a realignment may move the beam away from another required direction, a replacement lamp may differ optically, and a replacement fixture may mount at a different orientation.

      Retest conditions

      Where practical, retest under conditions comparable with the original measurement. Show success by measurement rather than assuming it.

      Records

      Commissioning, Periodic Testing and the Photometric Report

      Commissioning baseline

      Document installed fixture type, location, equipment ID, operating condition, measurement equipment, calibration status, measured performance, acceptance criteria, pass/fail result, corrective actions and final retest. The report becomes the baseline for later tests.

      Periodic testing

      Compare current and previous intensity, fixture-to-fixture variation, repeated fault locations, maintenance frequency and trends. Frequency follows authority requirements, airport conditions, equipment condition and the approved maintenance programme.

      Airport, runway, reference number, date, conditions, organisation, personnel; photometer manufacturer, model, serial, sensor ID, calibration certificate and software version.

      Lighting information

      Fixture type, manufacturer, model, ID, location, installation type, operating intensity and relevant electrical configuration.

      What was tested, how, what was measured, what it should have been (with source), and what happened after a failure.

      A good report records the full chain: measurement, defect, corrective action, verification. Record failures fixture by fixture, not as one runway pass/fail.

      FAQ

      Runway Lighting Photometry FAQ

      Q1What is runway lighting photometry?

      Runway lighting photometry is the measurement of the optical performance of runway lighting fixtures, including parameters such as intensity, distribution, colour and alignment.

      Q2Why is runway photometric testing required?

      It verifies that installed lights continue to give the intended visual guidance, and it finds degradation or misalignment that normal visual inspection may not reveal.

      Q3What does a runway photometer measure?

      Depending on the instrument and application, it can measure light intensity, distribution and other optical characteristics, and may also determine alignment or location information.

      Q4How often should runway lights be photometrically tested?

      There is no single universal interval. Follow the applicable authority requirements and the airport maintenance programme, considering runway usage, environmental conditions, equipment reliability and historical performance. FAA guidance lists traffic, pollution, equipment reliability and observed field performance as factors.

      Q5Can photometry detect misaligned runway lights?

      Yes. Photometric measurement can show that a fixture's optical output is not being delivered in the intended direction.

      Q6Can a light pass visual inspection and fail photometry?

      Yes. A fixture can stay illuminated while giving insufficient intensity, an incorrect beam pattern or other degraded optical performance.

      Q7Why are in-pavement runway lights difficult to maintain?

      They are exposed to contamination, rubber deposits, pavement loading, moisture and mechanical stress. These conditions can degrade optical performance even while the fixture keeps operating.

      Q8Should runway lights be cleaned before photometric testing?

      Where the test procedure requires it, clean before final acceptance measurements so contamination is not mistaken for fixture degradation.

      Q9Is intensity alone enough to evaluate a runway light?

      Not always. Depending on the applicable requirement, colour, distribution, beam coverage and alignment may also need to be evaluated.

      Q10What should be included in a runway photometric report?

      Runway and fixture identification, test date, equipment and calibration information, operating condition, measurement method, measured results, acceptance criteria, pass/fail status, corrective actions and retest results.

      Q11What is the difference between absolute and comparative photometry?

      Absolute photometry compares a measured value directly with a specified limit. Comparative photometry compares the result with a baseline, neighbouring fixtures or historical data, which can show a progressive loss of output before a formal failure.

      Q12What should I do when a fixture fails a photometric measurement?

      Validate the measurement first: instrument calibration, geometry, sensor alignment, operating intensity and fixture identification. Then check contamination, alignment, optics and the electrical supply before repairing or replacing, and retest afterwards.
