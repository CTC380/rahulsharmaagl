Product Overview

Lead-On / Lead-Off Light

What It Is

Lead-On and Lead-Off lights are specialized taxiway centreline lighting configurations that provide visual guidance when aircraft transition between a runway and the taxiway system.

The colour-coded arrangement distinguishes the runway environment and can identify proximity to the runway holding position or ILS/MLS critical area, depending on the applicable airport design.

Where They Apply

  • Leaving the runway and entering a taxiway
  • Leaving the taxiway and entering the runway environment

The final configuration should always follow the approved airport lighting layout, applicable aerodrome standard and operational control philosophy.

Lead-On and Lead-Off Functions

Two Functions, Different Directional Guidance

Lead-Off Lights

Guide pilots and vehicle drivers exiting the runway. The colour-coded sequence begins at the runway and progresses toward the taxiway holding / critical-area limit.

FAA guidance specifies alternate green and yellow centreline lights from the runway centreline to one centreline-light position beyond the runway hold or ILS critical-area hold position, ending with a yellow light. Where an odd number of colour-coded fixtures occurs, the first two centreline lights on the runway should be green.

Lead-On Lights

Guide pilots entering the runway. The same green/yellow concept gives visual information about the runway environment and the applicable critical-area boundary.

The FAA arrangement uses green at the runway centreline and progresses through the applicable green/yellow sequence to one light beyond the runway hold or ILS critical-area hold position.

The colour pattern is the same as for lead-off. The fixtures are bidirectional, so one side serves the lead-off function and the other side the lead-on function.

Bidirectional Centreline Fixture

The last coloured fixture of the sequence, one centreline position beyond the runway holding position or ILS/MLS critical-area holding position (the first coloured fixture met from the taxiway side), is a bi-colour, bidirectional fixture:

  • Approached from taxiway → Green
  • Approached from runway → Yellow

This is a key product-selection parameter and should be identified explicitly on the technical submittal.

Inset Configuration

In-Pavement Centreline Fixtures

Typical Installation

Lead-On / Lead-Off lights are generally implemented using in-pavement centreline fixtures. Fixture and housing should be treated as one coordinated pavement installation.

  • Inset centreline fixture and load-bearing housing
  • Green/yellow optical assembly and LED module
  • Directional optics, gaskets and sealing system
  • Drainage, mounting hardware
  • Secondary cable, isolating transformer, primary connector

Optical Configuration

Because the system communicates through both colour and direction, the optics are critical.

  • Unidirectional / bidirectional output
  • Green and yellow beams
  • Beam width and horizontal / vertical distribution
  • Optical axis, viewing sector, intensity
Runway Interfaces

Coordination With the Runway-Interface Visual System

Potentially associated systems include runway centreline lights, taxiway centreline lights, stop bar lights, runway guard lights, taxiway edge lights, runway holding-position markings and the AGL control and monitoring system. The correct combination depends on runway category, RVR, ILS/MLS installation, airport procedures and applicable standard.

Relationship With Stop Bars

Lead-On / Lead-Off provides directional route and runway-environment information through the centreline. A stop bar provides the controlled red stop indication at the holding position. The systems can be integrated through the airport control system so indications remain consistent.

Relationship With Taxiway Centreline

Ordinary taxiway centreline is green; the Lead-On / Lead-Off transition is green + yellow. FAA AC 150/5340-30J places both under its taxiway centreline lighting requirements.

Safety

Electrical Safety — Isolation and Lock-Out

WARNING — never open a live series circuit. An open series circuit driven by a constant-current regulator can produce dangerously high voltage. Opening, cutting or disconnecting any part of an energised circuit can cause serious injury or death and damage equipment.

  • De-energise the circuit at the regulator and apply the site lock-out / tag-out (LOTO) procedure before any work
  • Where the circuit must be isolated or shorted, use the series circuit cut-out switch or approved isolation device — not an improvised bypass
  • Verify the circuit is de-energised and the output is isolated before touching conductors, connectors, splices or transformers
  • Work only under the airport's permit-to-work system, using qualified personnel and the manufacturer's instructions
  • Follow the applicable national electrical safety regulations and site procedures
Standards & References

Verify the Applicable Edition

FAA

AC 150/5340-30J (Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids) is listed as active by the FAA. It covers lead-off and lead-on lights, alternate green/yellow coding, bidirectional operation before the hold / critical-area position, and special coding where centreline lights cross a runway. Fixtures are specified in AC 150/5345-46F (L-852 taxiway centreline family). Bidirectional green–yellow combinations are provided by the straight-section, narrow-beam types (L-852A and L-852C); confirm the type against the current edition (AC 150/5345-46F) and the project centreline layout.

ICAO

Under ICAO Annex 14, Volume I (taxiway centre line lights) exit-taxiway lights alternate green and yellow from the runway centre line to the perimeter of the ILS/MLS critical/sensitive area or the lower edge of the inner transitional surface, whichever is farthest from the runway, and are green beyond. This extent differs from the FAA arrangement, so confirm which standard governs. See also ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4.

India

  • DGCA India CAR Section 4 requirements
  • Airport-specific AGL specification
  • Approved lighting layout, LVP, ILS/MLS critical areas
  • ALCMS / control philosophy
  • BOQ, Employer's requirements, Indian Standards
  • Airport operator requirements

Always verify the applicable document edition before technical approval, procurement or installation.

FAQ

Lead-On / Lead-Off Light — Frequently Asked Questions

What are lead-on and lead-off lights?

Lead-on and lead-off lights are specialized taxiway centreline lighting configurations that give colour-coded green and yellow, direction-specific guidance when aircraft transition between a runway and the taxiway system. The arrangement distinguishes the runway environment and can identify proximity to the runway holding position or the ILS/MLS critical area, depending on the airport design.

How is the lead-off and lead-on sequence arranged under FAA guidance?

For lead-off, FAA guidance specifies alternate green and yellow centreline lights from the runway centreline to one centreline-light position beyond the runway hold or ILS critical-area hold position, ending with a yellow light. Where an odd number of colour-coded fixtures occurs, the first two centreline lights on the runway should be green. The lead-on arrangement starts with green at the runway centreline and follows the same green and yellow sequence to one light beyond the hold or critical-area hold position.

Why is the last coloured fixture bidirectional?

The last coloured fixture of the sequence, one centreline position beyond the runway holding position or ILS/MLS critical-area holding position, is a bi-colour, bidirectional fixture. Approached from the taxiway it shows green, and approached from the runway it shows yellow. This is a key product-selection parameter and should be identified explicitly on the technical submittal.

How do ICAO and FAA differ on the extent of the green and yellow sequence?

Under ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, exit-taxiway lights alternate green and yellow from the runway centre line to the perimeter of the ILS/MLS critical/sensitive area or the lower edge of the inner transitional surface, whichever is farthest from the runway, and are green beyond. This extent differs from the FAA arrangement, so confirm which standard governs the project.

Which FAA documents cover lead-on and lead-off lights?

FAA AC 150/5340-30J covers lead-off and lead-on lights, alternate green and yellow coding, bidirectional operation before the hold or critical-area position, and special coding where centreline lights cross a runway. Fixtures are specified in AC 150/5345-46F, the L-852 taxiway centreline family. Bidirectional green and yellow combinations are provided by the straight-section, narrow-beam types L-852A and L-852C, and the type should be confirmed against the current edition and the project centreline layout.

How do lead-on and lead-off lights differ from taxiway centreline lights and stop bars?

Ordinary taxiway centreline lighting is green, while the lead-on and lead-off transition is green and yellow. A stop bar gives the controlled red stop indication at the holding position, whereas lead-on and lead-off lights give directional route and runway-environment information through the centreline. The systems can be integrated through the airport control system so that indications remain consistent.

How are lead-on and lead-off lights installed?

They are generally implemented using in-pavement centreline fixtures, and the fixture and its load-bearing housing should be treated as one coordinated pavement installation. The sequence, number of fixtures and location must be taken from the approved lighting layout, and the manufacturer installation drawing controls the dimensions. Incorrect orientation can display the wrong information to an approaching aircraft.

How are lead-on and lead-off lights tested?

Test in both directions of movement. From the taxiway direction the specified green indication should show, and from the runway direction the bidirectional fixture should show yellow, which is especially important for the fixture before the holding or critical-area position. Electrical tests cover circuit continuity, insulation resistance, transformer verification, series-circuit current and CCR intensity-step operation, with photometric output, beam distribution and colour sequence checked where required.

What are the common faults, and why is a wrong colour serious?

Typical faults are a fixture that is off, a wrong colour, the correct colour in one direction only (a bidirectional optical failure, a failed LED channel, a wiring fault or incorrect orientation), an incorrect colour sequence and low intensity. A wrong-colour or wrong-direction indication is safety-critical and should be reported to airport operations or ATC, following the airport AGL serviceability procedure.

What should be confirmed before ordering lead-on and lead-off lights?

Confirm the airport and project, the runway and taxiway identification, the entry or exit function, the runway holding position and ILS/MLS critical-area boundary, the low-visibility requirement, the colour sequence, whether the fixture is unidirectional or bidirectional, the fixture spacing, the series-circuit current, isolating transformer and connector type, housing, installation depth and pavement cut-out, control and monitoring requirements, the governing standard and the datasheet and photometric documentation.

Is it safe to work on lead-on and lead-off fixtures while the circuit is energised?

No. An open series circuit driven by a constant-current regulator can produce dangerously high voltage, and opening, cutting or disconnecting any part of an energised circuit can cause serious injury or death. De-energise the circuit at the regulator, apply the site lock-out / tag-out procedure, use the series circuit cut-out switch or an approved isolation device and work under the permit-to-work system. See the electrical safety warning on this page.
