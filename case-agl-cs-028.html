AGL-CS-028 — Pre-Installation Testing of an AGL Transformer

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A quality-assurance investigation where acceptance testing revealed a hidden manufacturing defect — from nameplate verification through continuity and insulation testing to rejection and replacement.

Category

Level

Intermediate to Advanced

Important: This is an engineering simulation created for training. It is not presented as an actual airport incident. All readings, values, dates and outcomes are illustrative, and no airport, organisation, person or manufacturer is identified or implied.

1Case Study Overview

A newly delivered batch of AGL isolating transformers was received for installation during a runway lighting upgrade project.

Although the transformers were supplied by an approved manufacturer, the airport engineering team required pre-installation testing before field installation.

During acceptance testing, one transformer showed abnormally low insulation resistance and inconsistent secondary continuity, despite appearing mechanically undamaged.

The defective unit was rejected before installation, preventing a likely field failure.

This case demonstrates that pre-installation testing is a critical quality-control process that can prevent premature transformer failures and operational disruptions.

  • Inspect connectors and terminations.
  • Document transformer acceptance results.
  • Determine transformer installation readiness.

3System Background

Before installation, an AGL isolating transformer should be verified for:

  • Correct current rating.
  • Correct VA rating.
  • Physical condition.
  • Winding integrity.
  • Insulation integrity.
  • Connector condition.
  • Mechanical integrity.
  • Documentation compliance.

Installation of an untested transformer may introduce:

  • Early failures.
  • Insulation problems.
  • Secondary faults.
  • Connector failures.
  • Warranty disputes.
  • Operational outages.

The reported condition was:

  • New transformer received from approved supplier.
  • Factory test certificate available.
  • No visible shipping damage.
  • Installation scheduled for runway circuit replacement.

8Acceptance Test Procedure

Step 1: Nameplate Verification

The transformer nameplate matched:

  • 6.6 A primary rating.
  • 65 VA rating.
  • Approved manufacturer.
  • Correct project specification.

Step 2: Visual Inspection

Inspection confirmed:

  • No cracks.
  • No resin damage.
  • No deformation.
  • No corrosion.
  • No loose hardware.
  • No connector damage.

Step 3: Primary Winding Continuity

Measured value: Within expected range.

Primary winding was continuous.

Step 4: Secondary Winding Continuity

Measured value: Unstable during repeated testing.

One measurement showed intermittent continuity.

This was abnormal for a new transformer.

Step 5: Insulation Resistance Testing

Results:

Primary to Earth Greater than 1000 MΩ
Secondary to Earth 35 MΩ
Primary to Secondary 42 MΩ

The secondary insulation value was significantly lower than expected for a new transformer.

Step 6: Connector Inspection

Closer inspection revealed:

  • Incomplete connector assembly.
  • Slight terminal looseness.
  • Moisture residue near one secondary termination.

These findings suggested possible manufacturing or storage contamination.

Step 7: Repeat Testing

After connector stabilization:

  • Continuity improved.
  • Insulation remained below acceptance expectation.

The transformer failed the acceptance criteria.

9Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The transformer had pre-existing insulation contamination and unstable secondary termination, which had not been detected during routine visual inspection.

The unit was unsuitable for field installation.

Pre-installation testing prevented:

  • Early transformer failure.
  • Repeated maintenance.
  • Runway lighting outage.
  • Warranty complications.

10Corrective Action

The engineering team:

  1. Rejected the defective transformer.
  2. Quarantined the unit.
  3. Documented test results.
  4. Informed the supplier.
  5. Requested replacement.
  6. Tested the remaining transformers.
  7. Updated the acceptance procedure.
  8. Verified replacement transformer.
  9. Approved installation.
  10. Archived test records.

14Why This Case Was Important

A new transformer is not automatically a healthy transformer.

Shipping, storage, manufacturing, and handling can affect:

  • Insulation integrity.
  • Connector quality.
  • Mechanical reliability.
  • Moisture condition.

Acceptance testing is significantly less expensive than field failure investigation.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You receive a new approved transformer with a factory test certificate. What should you do before installation?

A. Install immediately.
B. Test only the connector.
C. Perform complete pre-installation acceptance testing.
D. Increase CCR current after installation.
Correct answer: C. Acceptance testing verifies insulation integrity, winding continuity, and connector condition, preventing hidden defects from entering service.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about pre-installation testing of AGL transformers, based on this case study.

Q1What is AGL-CS-028?

AGL-CS-028 is an engineering training case study on pre-installation acceptance testing of a new batch of AGL isolating transformers. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2Why test a new transformer that has a factory certificate?

Acceptance testing verifies insulation integrity, winding continuity and connector condition, so hidden defects do not enter service. The transformers came from an approved manufacturer with certificates and no visible shipping damage, yet one unit was still defective.

Q3What did the acceptance test find?

One transformer showed abnormally low insulation resistance and inconsistent secondary continuity, despite appearing mechanically undamaged.

Q4What was the root cause of the rejection?

The unit had pre-existing insulation contamination and an unstable secondary termination that routine visual inspection had not detected. It was unsuitable for field installation.

Q5What did testing prevent?

It prevented early transformer failure, repeated maintenance, a runway lighting outage and warranty complications.

Q6Which test equipment was used?

The acceptance testing used a true-RMS multimeter, winding resistance meter, continuity tester and 5 kV insulation resistance tester, along with approved transformer test equipment, manufacturer specifications and an inspection checklist.

Q7What happened to the defective transformer?

The engineering team rejected and quarantined the unit, documented the test results, informed the supplier and requested a replacement. They also tested the remaining transformers, updated the acceptance procedure, verified the replacement, approved installation and archived the test records.

Q8What should you do before installing a new approved transformer with a factory certificate?

Perform complete pre-installation acceptance testing. Installing immediately, testing only the connector or increasing CCR current after installation is not the right approach.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist

Make Incoming Testing a Routine Gate

Quality

Test every unit before it leaves stores, including those taken from spares stock for emergencies.

Test Insulation and Secondary Continuity Together

Method

A unit can pass one check and fail the other. Record both results against the serial number.

Quarantine and Tag Rejected Units

Control

A failed unit left on the shelf can return to service. Tag it, record it and return it to the supplier.

Link Test Records to the Installation Record

Traceability

Knowing which tested unit went into which pit helps analysis if it fails later.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

Pre-installation testing is a critical quality-assurance process that verifies transformer insulation integrity, winding continuity, connector condition, and installation readiness before the transformer becomes part of the operational AGL system.

Systematic acceptance inspection, continuity testing, insulation resistance testing, connector verification, and documentation can prevent early-life transformer failures, reduce maintenance costs, and improve the long-term reliability of the airfield lighting network.

In AGL engineering, the most economical transformer failure is the one that is detected before the transformer is installed.
