AGL-CS-081 — Approach Light Segment Failure

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. An approach lighting system investigation where a complete segment of lights became inoperative due to a cable-joint failure — from CCR verification through failure-boundary identification, continuity testing, TDR fault localization, joint excavation, and operational restoration.

1Case Study Overview

During routine night operations, air traffic control reported that a complete segment of the approach lighting system had become inoperative, with approximately 12 consecutive approach lights not illuminated.

Pilots reported that the approach lighting pattern appeared discontinuous, making runway acquisition more difficult during reduced-visibility approaches.

The Constant Current Regulator (CCR) remained operational, circuit current was maintained, and no major CCR trip occurred.

Because the failure affected a consecutive group of approach lights, a detailed investigation was initiated covering series-circuit continuity, isolating transformers, cable joints, L-823 connectors, segment boundaries, and field fault localization.

The investigation identified failure of a cable joint within the affected approach-light segment, resulting in loss of power to the downstream portion of the series circuit.

This case demonstrates that approach-light segment failures are typically circuit-distribution problems requiring systematic fault localization rather than replacement of individual light fittings.

3System Background

An approach-light segment includes:

  • Series primary cable.
  • Isolating transformers.
  • Secondary cables.
  • L-823 connectors.
  • Approach light fittings.
  • Cable joints.
  • Junction interfaces.

Segment failures may result from:

  • Cable-joint failure.
  • Primary-cable damage.
  • Connector failure.
  • Transformer open circuit.
  • Water ingress.
  • Excavation damage.
  • Mechanical stress.
  • Insulation failure.

Approach-light reliability depends on series-circuit continuity.

7Segment Failure Principle

Series circuit healthy → Continuous power flow → All segment lights ON → Complete approach pattern
Circuit interruption → Downstream isolation → Consecutive lights OFF → Discontinuous approach pattern → Operational degradation

Consecutive failures usually indicate a common circuit interruption.

10Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The approach-light segment failure was caused by failure of the cable joint between A18 and A19, resulting from:

  • Water ingress.
  • Insulation degradation.
  • Conductor corrosion.
  • Progressive joint deterioration.

The CCR and isolating transformers remained healthy.

The primary failure mechanism was open-circuit interruption of the series cable.

11Corrective Action

The maintenance team:

  1. Isolated the circuit.
  2. Removed the damaged cable joint.
  3. Replaced the affected cable section.
  4. Installed a new approved cable joint.
  5. Replaced degraded connectors.
  6. Performed insulation testing.
  7. Verified circuit continuity.
  8. Energized the circuit.
  9. Verified segment illumination.
  10. Updated cable-joint maintenance records.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: 12 consecutive approach lights OFF. CCR healthy. Upstream lights ON. Downstream lights OFF. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace all failed light fittings.
B. Increase CCR current.
C. Identify the failure boundary, perform circuit continuity testing, verify isolating transformers, inspect cable joints, and locate the open circuit.
D. Replace the CCR.
Correct answer: C. Consecutive failures with a healthy CCR indicate a common circuit interruption. Systematic boundary identification, continuity testing, transformer verification, and cable-joint inspection will locate the fault without unnecessary equipment replacement.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about approach-light segment failure, cable joints and fault localization.

Q1What is AGL-CS-081?

AGL-CS-081 is an engineering training case study. It covers a run of about 12 consecutive approach lights that went dark while the CCR stayed healthy. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2Why do consecutive approach lights fail together?

On a series circuit, an interruption isolates everything downstream of it. Consecutive failures usually point to a common circuit interruption rather than many separate fixture failures.

Q3How was the failure boundary found in AGL-CS-081?

Field inspection showed A1–A18 ON and A19–A30 OFF. Continuity was normal upstream and absent downstream, placing the open circuit near the A18–A19 transition.

Q4Were the isolating transformers at fault?

No. The A18 and A19 transformers tested healthy and no transformer open circuit was found.

Q5What did connector and joint inspection find?

One L-823 connector showed moisture, corrosion, heating marks and higher contact resistance. The excavated cable joint between A18 and A19 showed water ingress, insulation deterioration, conductor corrosion and joint separation.

Q6How was the fault location confirmed?

TDR located the fault 146.8 m from the reference point, which matched the excavated cable joint.

Q7What was the root cause of AGL-CS-081?

Failure of the cable joint between A18 and A19 from water ingress, insulation degradation, conductor corrosion and progressive deterioration. The primary mechanism was an open-circuit interruption of the series cable.

Q8How was the circuit restored?

The team removed the damaged joint, replaced the cable section and installed a new approved joint. They also replaced degraded connectors, tested insulation, verified continuity, energized the circuit, verified segment illumination and updated cable-joint records.

Q9Why not just replace the individual dark lights?

The lights were healthy. Replacing fittings would not fix the open circuit, so systematic fault localization is needed instead.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

Approach-light segment failures are typically series-circuit continuity problems caused by cable-joint deterioration, connector failure, or primary-cable interruption, and systematic identification of the failure boundary, continuity testing, transformer verification, and TDR-based fault localization provide the most effective method for restoring complete approach-light operation.

Systematic circuit-boundary analysis, continuity testing, cable-joint inspection, TDR verification, insulation testing, and post-repair operational validation can rapidly identify segment failures and restore ICAO-compliant approach-light performance.

In AGL engineering, a dark approach-light segment is not merely a group of failed lights; it is a broken visual pathway that must be restored with the same precision as the approach itself.
