Halogen Lamp

The traditional incandescent light source used in legacy AGL fixtures. Despite being superseded by LEDs, halogen lamps remain widely deployed in many airfields due to their warm, consistent colour output, excellent colour rendering (CRI ≈ 100), and simple control via series circuits.

Overview

What Is It?

A Halogen Lamp is a type of incandescent lamp that uses a tungsten filament enclosed in a compact, high-temperature quartz envelope filled with a halogen gas (typically iodine or bromine). The halogen cycle allows the filament to operate at higher temperatures than standard incandescent lamps, producing a brighter, whiter light with higher efficacy and longer life. In AGL applications, halogen lamps are available in various wattages (e.g., 50W, 100W, 200W) and are designed to operate directly on the series circuit current (typically 6.6A) via an isolating transformer. They are used extensively in runway, taxiway, and approach lighting fixtures, and remain the primary light source in many legacy systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The halogen lamp's purpose, within an AGL fixture, is to provide a light source bright and consistent enough for pilots to see and correctly interpret from the air, in a package robust enough to survive the airfield's vibration, temperature extremes, and constant on/off cycling. Its self-cleaning halogen cycle — which redeposits evaporated tungsten back onto the filament — is what lets it sustain high brightness over a useful service life rather than dimming and blackening the envelope the way a standard incandescent lamp would, making its output stable enough to be trusted for pilot guidance throughout its rated life. While LED technology now offers longer life and lower power consumption, halogen lamps remain widely deployed because they operate directly on the series circuit's stepped current without additional driver electronics, which keeps many legacy fixtures simple, field-proven, and straightforward to maintain.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Halogen lamps are used in a wide range of AGL fixtures, particularly in older installations:

  • Runway Edge & Centreline Lights: 50W, 100W, or 200W lamps for edge and centreline guidance.
  • Threshold & End Lights: Coloured (green/red) lamps for threshold and end marking.
  • Taxiway Lights: Lower wattage (50W) lamps for taxiway edge and centreline.
  • Approach Lighting: High-intensity halogen lamps for barrettes and flashing lights.
  • PAPI & VASI: Specialised halogen lamps with filters to produce the required red/white transition.
  • Obstruction Lights: Halogen lamps for obstruction marking, often with red filters.
  • Beacons & Wind Cones: Halogen lamps for rotating beacons and illuminated windsocks.
Placement

Installation & Location

The lamp is fitted into its holder inside the fixture housing, pushed and (where the base type requires it) twisted home so the contacts seat fully and squarely, since a partially seated lamp can arc, overheat, or fail prematurely. Handling is done without bare-hand contact on the glass envelope, using a cloth or gloves, as skin oils left on a halogen bulb create a hot spot that shortens its life or causes early failure. Once fitted, the lamp is centred in the reflector or prism's optical focus using the holder's locating features or a shim, so the beam pattern the fixture produces matches its design photometrics, and the fixture is resealed with a fresh gasket before the top cover is replaced.

Function

How Does It Work?

A halogen lamp works on the principle of incandescence: an electric current passes through a tungsten filament, heating it to a temperature of about 2,500–3,000°C, causing it to emit light. The halogen gas (iodine or bromine) within the quartz envelope reacts with tungsten atoms that evaporate from the filament, forming tungsten halide compounds. These compounds are then transported back to the filament where they decompose, redepositing tungsten onto the filament. This halogen cycle extends lamp life and keeps the quartz envelope clean, maintaining high light output. The lamp operates on the series circuit through an isolating transformer, with intensity controlled by the CCR's current steps (typically 5 steps: 2.8A to 6.6A).

System Architecture

The Halogen Cycle & Control Logic

Current Heats the Tungsten Filament

Temperature reaches roughly 2,500°C – 3,000°C

Filament Emits Light

Incandescence produces the visible output

Tungsten Atoms Evaporate

Hot filament sheds tungsten into the quartz envelope

Halogen Gas Reacts With the Vapour

Iodine or bromine forms a tungsten halide compound

Compound Decomposes at the Filament

Tungsten redeposits back onto the filament surface

The Cycle Repeats Continuously — Extending Lamp Life While Keeping the Quartz Envelope Clean
Construction

Physical Construction

The lamp is built around a coiled tungsten filament suspended on molybdenum support wires inside a small-volume fused-quartz envelope, which is pinch-sealed at one or both ends to hold the halogen fill gas under pressure. Quartz is used rather than ordinary glass because it withstands the high envelope-wall temperature the halogen cycle depends on without softening or devitrifying. Filament position within the envelope is held to a tight tolerance, since AGL fixtures rely on precise, repeatable filament placement relative to the reflector or prism to hit their certified beam pattern — a lamp with the filament even slightly off-axis will shift the beam. The lamp base — typically a prefocus or bipost type matched to the fixture's lamp holder — locates the envelope at a fixed height and orientation the moment the lamp is inserted, so no field adjustment of the light source itself is normally required after a lamp change.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lamp Blackening & Depreciation

Over time, the halogen cycle may become less effective, leading to tungsten deposition on the quartz envelope (blackening) and reduced light output. Also, lamp lumen depreciation is inherent. Regular photometric checks and replacement of aged lamps is essential.

Filament Breakage from Vibration

AGL fixtures are subject to significant vibration from aircraft and ground vehicles. The tungsten filament can break due to mechanical stress, causing premature lamp failure. Use of shock-resistant lamps and proper mounting is recommended.

Quartz Envelope Contamination

Oil or dirt from fingers can contaminate the quartz envelope, causing hot spots and cracking. Always handle halogen lamps with clean gloves or use a protective sleeve during installation.

Incorrect Dimming Response

Halogen lamps have a non-linear response to current changes; at low currents, colour temperature drops and light output decreases rapidly. Ensure the CCR steps are correctly calibrated for the lamp type.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Lamp Fails Well Before Rated Life
Likely Cause
Filament breakage from vibration, incorrect CCR current causing overdriving, or contamination on the quartz envelope from bare-hand handling during installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the failed lamp's filament for a clean break versus melted appearance; check CCR output current against the lamp's rated value; inspect the quartz envelope for oily fingerprints or hot-spot discolouration.
Corrective Action
Replace with a shock-resistant lamp rated for the application, correct CCR current setting if overdriving was found, and handle replacement lamps only with clean gloves or a protective sleeve.
Reduced or Dimming Light Output
Likely Cause
Tungsten blackening of the quartz envelope from normal ageing, or inherent lumen depreciation as the lamp approaches end of rated life.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against the rated output; visually inspect the envelope for visible dark tungsten deposits; check lamp age/hours against rated life.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp if photometric output has fallen below the acceptable threshold; schedule replacement proactively for lamps approaching end of rated life.
Cracked Quartz Envelope
Likely Cause
Contamination from finger oils creating a localised hot spot that stresses the quartz, or thermal shock from environmental conditions.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crack location for correlation with a visible contamination mark; review installation records for handling practices used.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp, and reinforce clean-handling procedure (gloves or protective sleeve) for all future halogen lamp installations.
Light Appears Dim or Off-Colour at a Reduced Intensity Step
Likely Cause
Halogen lamps respond non-linearly to reduced current — colour temperature and output both drop sharply at low CCR steps, which can be mistaken for a lamp fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare observed colour/output against the manufacturer's published current-versus-output curve for that lamp type at the given CCR step; confirm the CCR step calibration matches the lamp's specified response.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the CCR step settings for the installed lamp type if they don't match the expected curve; this is often a calibration correction rather than a lamp replacement.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

No Bare-Hand Contact with the Envelope
Handling

Always handle the lamp by its base, using a clean cloth or gloves if the quartz envelope must be touched; finger oils create a localised hot spot that stresses the quartz and can cause premature cracking or failure.

Allow the Lamp to Cool Before Handling
Personnel Safety

Halogen lamps run at high surface temperature during operation; allow the lamp and fixture housing to cool fully before relamping to avoid burns.

Electrical Isolation Before Relamping
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR or supply circuit before removing or installing a lamp, and verify zero energy at the fixture before opening the housing.

Shattered Envelope Hazard
Personnel Safety

Wear eye protection when installing or removing a lamp, since a contaminated or thermally stressed quartz envelope can crack or shatter under normal handling; treat any lamp with visible surface marks or a prior overdrive event as higher risk.

Match Lamp Rating to CCR Output
Electrical Safety

Confirm the CCR is set to the correct current step for the installed lamp type before re-energising; overdriving a halogen lamp beyond its rated current shortens filament life and increases the risk of envelope failure.

Disposal of Failed Lamps
Operational

Dispose of failed or removed lamps according to site waste procedures, and avoid discarding a cracked or partially shattered lamp loose where broken glass could become foreign object debris.

Verify Output Before Closing Out
Compliance

After relamping, confirm the fixture strikes correctly and reaches expected intensity at the commissioned CCR step; a lamp that appears dim or off-colour at a low intensity step may be functioning correctly given halogen's non-linear response to reduced current.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture in service with an incorrect lamp type or CCR mismatch; verify photometric output meets the fixture's rated requirements before closing out the work.

Quick Reference

Airport Lighting Halogen Fixture

An airport lighting halogen fixture uses a halogen lamp behind the lens and filter. It works well with older series circuits but has a much shorter life than LED, so lamp replacement is a regular maintenance task. Replace with the correct wattage and type, and check the lens, seal and filter at the same time. See the LED technology page for the alternative.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Single Fingerprint Can Cause a Later, Unrelated-Looking Failure
Handling

Skin oil contamination on the quartz envelope creates a localised hot spot that devitrifies the quartz over subsequent burn hours; the resulting failure can occur weeks after the contaminating handling event, which makes it easy to blame the wrong maintenance visit when investigating a premature lamp failure.

Non-Linear Dimming Response Is Normal, Not a Fault
Electrical

Halogen lamps don't dim linearly with current, so a lamp running noticeably dimmer or more amber at a low CCR step than expected is often behaving correctly rather than failing; compare against the lamp's published output curve for that step before condemning it as a bad lamp.

Repeated Thermal Cycling Embrittles the Lead Wires
Failure Mode

Fixtures that cycle frequently between on and off, rather than running continuously, subject the lamp's internal lead wires and seal to more thermal cycling stress than steady operation does; where nuisance failures cluster on frequently-switched circuits, consider that duty cycle itself as a contributing factor, not just lamp quality.

Overdriving Trades Short-Term Output for Long-Term Life
Electrical

Running a halogen lamp above its rated current to compensate for a perceived dimness elsewhere in the circuit shortens filament life disproportionately to the output gained; trace the actual cause of low brightness (loop balance, transformer sizing, ageing lamps elsewhere) rather than compensating by overdriving the replacement lamp.

Storage Conditions Affect Shelf Life
Handling

Spare lamps stored in humid or high-temperature conditions, or exposed to UV through packaging, can degrade before they're ever installed; keep spares in their original sealed packaging in a stable environment and rotate stock so older spares are used first.

Consider LED Retrofit Economics Before a Like-for-Like Replacement
Retrofit

On fixtures nearing an overhaul cycle anyway, compare the ongoing relamping and CCR-step-matching burden of halogen against a properly step-matched LED retrofit before defaulting to another like-for-like lamp order; the right answer depends on remaining fixture life and loop composition, not on lamp cost alone.
