WGS Sign

A sign indicating the World Geodetic System (WGS) reference point or coordinates used for navigation, positioning, and calibration of aircraft systems. Typically displays latitude and longitude information for the airport reference point or specific survey markers.

Overview

What Is It?

A WGS Sign (World Geodetic System Sign) is a ground-based sign that displays the precise geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) of a reference point on the airfield. These signs are typically located at the Airport Reference Point (ARP) or at other critical survey points used for navigation, instrument calibration, and aircraft positioning systems. The coordinates are based on the WGS‑84 standard, which is the global reference frame used by GNSS (GPS, GLONASS, etc.) and modern navigation systems. WGS signs provide a visual confirmation of the reference location for surveyors, engineers, and pilots, and are an essential tool for maintaining the accuracy of airfield navigation databases.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The WGS sign exists to anchor the airfield's physical surface to its published coordinate data, giving surveyors and maintenance engineers a fixed, visually verifiable point against which GNSS-based survey equipment and navigation database updates can be checked. Because the ARP and other charted reference points feed directly into instrument approach design, FMS databases, and RNAV/RNP procedures, an error at the source coordinate propagates into every system that relies on it; the sign's role is to make that coordinate physically findable on the ground so it can be re-surveyed, audited, or cross-checked without ambiguity. For pilots and ground personnel it serves a secondary confirmation function, visually corroborating the reference location displayed in charts and navigation systems.

Application

Where Is It Used?

WGS signs are installed at specific locations across the airfield where precise coordinate reference is required, including:

  • Airport Reference Point (ARP): The designated geographic centre of the airport, as defined by ICAO, used for overall airport location reporting.
  • Survey Control Points: Fixed survey markers used for airfield mapping, construction, and maintenance.
  • Navigation Aid Sites: Near ILS localizer, glide slope, DME, or VOR installations where precise coordinates are needed for calibration.
  • Apron and Gate Areas: At gates or stands where aircraft docking systems require precise positioning data.
  • Approach Path Points: At specific locations along the approach path for instrument approach procedure verification.
Placement

Installation & Location

WGS reference markers are founded on a fixed, non-frangible survey monument rather than the collapsible post used for operational signage, since the marker's coordinate value is only meaningful if its physical position never moves; the monument is set below or at grade with a durable engraved or plated reference point rather than a readable panel at cockpit height. Siting for each marker type follows its specific reference role — the ARP monument at the surveyed airport centre, control points at stable ground clear of future construction, navaid-adjacent markers close enough to support calibration surveys without fouling the protected navaid area. Because these are geodetic references, positional accuracy is re-verified by survey rather than by the visual inspection used for other airfield signage; where a marker is specified with internal illumination, a light-duty power feed is run to the pedestal or pole in the same manner as other illuminated signs, but this has no bearing on the coordinate value itself.

Function

How Does It Work?

WGS signs serve as a physical reference for the published coordinates of the location. The sign face displays the latitude and longitude in degrees, minutes, and seconds (or decimal degrees), along with the elevation if applicable. The coordinates are derived from official surveys and are used to verify the accuracy of aircraft navigation systems, including GPS, and to cross-check with flight management system databases. The sign provides a visual cue for ground personnel and can be used during pre‑flight checks or maintenance activities to confirm that onboard systems are correctly aligned with the airfield’s reference frame. The sign is typically weather‑resistant and may be illuminated for visibility in low‑light conditions.

Construction

Physical Construction

A WGS sign is built as a rigid metal plate, typically aluminium or stainless steel, fabricated with the coordinate text and any accompanying markings applied through engraving, UV-resistant enamel, or a durable acrylic overlay bonded to the substrate to resist fading from prolonged sun exposure. The plate is mounted on a ground-level pedestal, pole, or wall bracket depending on the survey point's location, with the mounting hardware set to hold the sign rigid and precisely oriented so it is not disturbed by wind loading or routine airfield activity, since any movement could call the sign's positional reference into question. Where illumination is specified, an internal LED strip or edge-lit panel is integrated behind a translucent face, sealed within a weatherproof housing to prevent moisture ingress while keeping the text legible at night; non-illuminated signs instead rely on a retroreflective sheeting laminated to the face. The pedestal or mounting frame is corrosion-resistant, typically galvanised or stainless steel, and the overall assembly is engineered to remain dimensionally stable over years of outdoor exposure so the displayed coordinates continue to accurately represent the physical survey point.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Sign Face Fading

Prolonged exposure to UV radiation and weather can cause the coordinate text to fade, making it illegible. Regular inspection and replacement of the sign face or coating are necessary to maintain readability.

Physical Damage

Ground vehicles, construction equipment, or snowploughs can damage WGS signs. Siting the monument clear of routine traffic and marking it visibly reduces risk — a frangible mount is not used here, since the marker's entire value depends on its position never shifting.

Coordinate Accuracy

Over time, the published coordinates may become outdated due to tectonic plate movement or new surveys. Regular verification and update of the sign's coordinates are essential to maintain navigation accuracy.

Obstruction

Signs can be obscured by vegetation, parked aircraft, or ground equipment. Periodic site clearing and visibility checks are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Coordinate Text Illegible or Faded
Likely Cause
Prolonged UV exposure and weathering have faded the printed or engraved coordinate text.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the sign face for fading, chalking, or peeling coating from a normal viewing distance.
Corrective Action
Recoat or replace the sign face with UV-stable materials, verifying the coordinate text is re-applied accurately from the current survey record.
Sign Damaged, Bent, or Missing
Likely Cause
Impact from ground vehicles, construction equipment, or snowploughs operating near the sign.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the sign and mount for physical damage; confirm the sign is still present at its documented location.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged sign, and consider a frangible mount or relocation to a protected area if repeated strikes occur.
Displayed Coordinates Don't Match Current Survey
Likely Cause
Tectonic plate movement or a newer survey has shifted the reference coordinates since the sign was last updated.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the sign's displayed coordinates against the current published survey record for the reference point.
Corrective Action
Update the sign face with the current coordinates from the latest survey, and record the update date for future reference.
Sign Not Visible from the Intended Viewing Position
Likely Cause
Vegetation growth, parked aircraft, or ground equipment is obstructing the line of sight to the sign.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the sightline from the typical survey or verification position, and identify what is blocking the view.
Corrective Action
Clear the obstructing vegetation or equipment, and add the sightline to routine site-clearing checks.
Sign Location Doesn't Match Documented Reference Point
Likely Cause
The sign or its mount has shifted from ground settlement, or was reinstalled slightly off-position after nearby works.
Diagnostic Steps
Survey the sign's actual position against the charted Airport Reference Point or survey marker coordinates.
Corrective Action
Re-position the sign to the correct surveyed location, or update records if the survey marker itself has been formally re-established elsewhere.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Do Not Relocate Without Survey
Data Integrity

Never move, reset, or reinstall the sign at a different point without a fresh survey; the sign's value depends entirely on it marking the exact coordinate recorded in the navigation database.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations before working near the sign if it sits within the movement area, and follow standard vehicle and FOD control procedures.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools and fasteners accounted for during cleaning or repainting, and secure loose material before leaving the site to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Verify Against Survey Record
Compliance

After any ground disturbance nearby (excavation, paving, drainage work), confirm the sign's physical position still matches the recorded WGS-84 coordinate before it is relied upon again.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Wear standard airside PPE (high-visibility clothing, hearing protection near active movement areas) appropriate to the sign's location on the airfield.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the sign is legible, correctly oriented, and undisturbed before closing out any nearby work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Treat the Coordinate as the Asset, Not the Post
Data Integrity

The physical sign is only a visible marker for a surveyed WGS-84 point that may feed navigation or obstacle databases elsewhere; if the post is damaged or removed, flag the underlying coordinate record for a data-currency check rather than treating a simple replacement as the end of the job.

Nearby Groundwork Is the Real Risk to Position
Survey

Drainage, paving, or excavation work near a reference point is far more likely to shift or disturb it than routine airfield operations are; build a position re-check into the sign-off procedure for any ground disturbance within its immediate vicinity, not just for work that visibly touches the sign itself.

Settlement Happens Slowly Enough to Go Unnoticed
Survey

Frost heave and soil settlement can shift a post's position by small increments over several seasons without any single event standing out; a periodic re-survey on a fixed interval catches this kind of drift that a purely reactive, disturbance-triggered check would miss.

Legibility Fades Before Structural Failure Does
Durability

UV exposure degrades a sign's face contrast and any retroreflective treatment well before the post or mounting shows visible wear; judge replacement timing against legibility standards rather than structural condition alone.

Close the Loop Between Field Record and Database
Compliance

A re-survey that corrects the physical position but never gets reflected back into the navigation or asset database recreates the same discrepancy it was meant to fix; confirm the updated coordinate has actually reached every system of record before closing out the work.

Avoid Disturbing the Base During Cleaning or Repainting
Operational

Routine cleaning or repainting is a lower-risk task than most airside work, but it's still an opportunity for a post to be knocked or reset slightly out of position by well-meaning ground crew; brief personnel to leave the base undisturbed and report any accidental contact rather than quietly re-seating it themselves.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about WGS signs: purpose, placement, coordinates, power, standards, verification and maintenance.

Q1What is a WGS sign?

A WGS sign (World Geodetic System sign) is a ground-based marker that displays the precise latitude and longitude of a reference point on the airfield, based on the WGS-84 coordinate frame. It is typically placed at the Airport Reference Point (ARP) or at other survey control points, giving surveyors, engineers and ground personnel a visible confirmation of the published coordinate.

Q2Why does an airfield need a WGS sign?

The ARP and other charted reference points feed instrument approach design, flight management system databases and RNAV/RNP procedures, so an error at the source coordinate propagates into every system that relies on it. The sign makes that coordinate physically findable on the ground so it can be re-surveyed, audited or cross-checked without ambiguity.

Q3Where are WGS signs installed?

Typical locations are the Airport Reference Point, survey control points used for mapping and construction, sites near ILS localizer, glide slope, DME or VOR installations where calibration needs precise coordinates, apron and gate areas where docking systems need positioning data, and selected points along the approach path for procedure verification.

Q4What information is shown on a WGS sign, and what does it look like?

The face shows latitude and longitude in degrees, minutes and seconds (or decimal degrees), plus elevation where applicable. Typical sign sizes range from 300 mm x 200 mm to 600 mm x 400 mm, usually black text on a white background (or blue for an ARP designation), on an aluminium or stainless steel plate protected to IP54-IP65.

Q5How is a WGS sign mounted, and is it frangible?

The marker is founded on a fixed, non-frangible survey monument, pedestal or rigid pole rather than the collapsible post used for operational signage, because its coordinate value is only meaningful if its position never moves. It is set on stable, compacted ground and fixed rigidly so wind loading and routine airfield activity cannot shift it.

Q6Does a WGS sign need electrical power?

Only if it is specified with internal LED illumination. Retroreflective (non-illuminated) signs carry no electrical load. Illuminated signs use a low-voltage LED driver circuit (24 V DC, or a local 230 V AC feed with an integral driver) switched by a dusk-to-dawn photocell or ALCMS output, protected by an MCB, with the frame bonded to site earth. It is a simple on/off circuit, not a CCR intensity-stepped series loop.

Q7What are the photometric requirements for an illuminated WGS sign?

Typical values for an illuminated face are an average luminance of at least 2 cd/m2, at least 1 cd/m2 at any point, and a max/min uniformity ratio of no more than 4:1, with enough text-to-background contrast for night legibility. Retroreflective signs are assessed for reflectivity rather than luminance.

Q8Which standards apply to WGS signs?

The page references ICAO Annex 14 (airport reference point and coordinate reporting), ICAO Annex 15 (aeronautical information services, coordinate accuracy and presentation), ISO 19111 (spatial referencing by coordinates) and FAA Order 8260.19 (flight procedures and coordinate reference). The sign's coordinates must match the official survey record.

Q9How is a WGS sign verified at installation and commissioning?

Check the coordinates to be engraved against the official survey certificate before fabrication, confirm the installed monument position with an independent GNSS or total-station check, and verify the finished face matches the certified data digit for digit. Also confirm foundation stability, test illumination where fitted, take an independent GNSS cross-check reading, and file the survey certificate and photos in the commissioning dossier.

Q10How often should a WGS sign be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for visibility and obstruction clearance; semi-annual for face legibility (UV fading) and physical damage; and annual for coordinate accuracy against the latest survey, LED output on illuminated signs, mounting and base condition, weatherproof seals and electrical connections.

Q11Why can the coordinates on a WGS sign become out of date?

Tectonic plate movement or a newer survey can change the reference coordinates after the sign was produced. An annual cross-check against the current published survey record catches this, and if the coordinates no longer match the sign face should be updated from the latest survey and the update date recorded.

Q12What should be done if a WGS sign is damaged, moved or disturbed by nearby works?

Never move, reset or reinstall the sign at a different point without a fresh survey. After any nearby excavation, paving or drainage work, confirm the sign's position still matches the recorded WGS-84 coordinate before it is relied on again. If it has shifted, re-position it to the surveyed location or update the records if the marker was formally re-established elsewhere.
