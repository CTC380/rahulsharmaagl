Threshold/Runway End Combination Fixture

A bi‑colour light fixture that displays green light from the approach side (threshold) and red light from the opposite side (runway end). Installed at the runway ends where the threshold and end coincide, this single fixture serves dual function, simplifying installation and reducing infrastructure costs while providing clear visual guidance for both approach directions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Threshold/Runway End Combination Fixture (also known as a combination threshold/end light) is a single light unit that incorporates both a green threshold light (visible from the approach side) and a red runway end light (visible from the opposite side). This dual‑colour design is used at runways where the same physical location serves as the threshold for one direction and the end for the other – common on runways with displaced thresholds or where the runway is used in both directions. The fixture typically contains two optical systems or a bi‑colour dichroic filter that produces green light when viewed from the approach side and red light when viewed from the opposite side. This eliminates the need for separate green and red fixtures at the same location, reducing installation complexity, cable runs, and maintenance costs.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

This combination fixture exists to solve a specific geometric problem: at a displaced threshold or a runway used in both directions, one exact physical location has to mean two completely different things depending on which way you're looking at it — the start of usable landing surface to one pilot, and the end of pavement to another approaching from the opposite direction. Using two separate fixtures at that single point would be redundant and would double the cabling, mounting, and maintenance burden for guidance that has to occupy the same physical spot anyway.

By producing green in one direction and red in the other from a single housing, the fixture gives each approaching pilot exactly the colour their situation calls for, without any risk of the two messages being visible from the wrong side and causing confusion. This precision matters because threshold and end lighting carry very different implications — land here is the opposite message from stop here — and the whole point of the dual-colour design is making sure each pilot only ever sees the one that applies to them.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at the ends of runways where the threshold and the runway end are co‑located – typically at both ends of a runway that is used for take‑off and landing in both directions (i.e., non‑directional runways). They are also used at displaced thresholds, where the actual landing threshold is located further down the runway, and the area before it serves as a stopway or overrun area. Combination fixtures are used wherever the threshold coincides with the runway extremity, on precision (CAT I, II, III) and non-precision runways alike. They are not appropriate where the threshold is displaced — there the green threshold row and the red runway end row sit at different locations and must be separate fixtures. They are arranged in a row across the runway width, with the green side facing the approaching aircraft and the red side facing the opposite direction. The spacing and mounting (elevated or inset) follow the same standards as individual threshold and end lights.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out in a single row across the full runway width at the runway end or displaced threshold, using the same base can and spacing convention as separate threshold/end light installations — an inset base can where the surface is paved and load-rated, or an elevated frangible mount where conditions allow. Because a single fixture serves both directions (green facing the approach, red facing the departure/overrun side), its orientation is critical during setting-out: the fixture is aligned squarely across the runway centreline rather than skewed, so neither colour is compromised for the direction it serves. Cabling and isolating transformer arrangements follow the same convention as standard threshold and runway end lights, with position tied to the threshold or displaced-threshold survey rather than a generic runway-end offset.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each combination fixture is powered from the series circuit via an isolating transformer. The fixture contains a single lamp or LED array whose light is split or filtered to produce two distinct colours. A common design uses a dichroic reflector or prism that reflects green light towards the approach side and transmits red light towards the opposite side, or vice versa. Alternatively, the fixture may have two separate optical cavities with independent colour filters. The intensity is controlled by the CCR's current steps, with the green and red outputs adjusted simultaneously to match visibility conditions. The fixture is uni‑directional for each colour: green is visible only from the approach side, and red only from the opposite side. This ensures that pilots on approach see the green threshold, while pilots on the opposite end see the red end light.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The combination fixture is not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit it is wired into, generally the same runway edge lighting loop that continues along the pavement on both sides of it. An ALCMS, operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step for that loop based on prevailing RVR or ATC instruction, and this command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop — and therefore both the green and red outputs of every combination fixture on it — to a single fixed current. Because the fixture's colour split is achieved optically (by the dichroic reflector or separate filtered cavities) rather than electrically, the CCR has no way to command green and red independently: raising or lowering the loop's current step changes the intensity of both colours together, in lock-step with every other edge or threshold fixture sharing the circuit. Fault detection is likewise loop-based: the CCR monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise — typically an open-circuit source or a failed isolating transformer — is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault, isolated to the specific fixture only through sectionalised field testing; because a single lamp or LED array feeds both colour outputs, a failure at the source extinguishes green and red simultaneously rather than one colour independently of the other.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is a die-cast aluminium alloy housing, anodised or powder-coated against runway de-icing fluid and UV exposure, with a single lamp module viewed through two separately masked lens segments — one green, one red — rather than two independent optics, since the colour separation here is optical rather than electrical. The lens assembly is gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring or silicone seal for an IP67/IP68 rating, and the unit mounts either on a frangible coupling seated on an L-867 base can for elevated installations, or directly into an L-868 base can for flush/inset units rated for aircraft wheel loading. Internal wiring terminates at an L-823 primary connector feeding the isolating transformer's secondary leads in its own adjacent base can, with stainless-steel fasteners and a drainage provision at the base to keep water off the transformer and cable entries.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Colour Cross‑contamination

Optical seals or dichroic filters can degrade over time, causing green light to bleed into the red side or vice versa. This can confuse pilots as they may see the wrong colour from a specific angle. Regular photometric testing of both colours and inspection of optical compartments is essential.

Uneven Intensity Between Colours

Due to different optical efficiencies for green and red paths, one colour may appear dimmer than the other. This can affect the visual balance of the threshold/end indication. Calibration and adjustment of the optical system (or selecting matched LED outputs) are required.

Lens Contamination on Both Sides

Combination fixtures have optical windows on two sides, both exposed to runway debris, rubber, and de‑icing fluids. Cleaning protocols must address both sides to maintain photometric performance for both colours.

Moisture Ingress Affecting Both Cavities

A single point of seal failure can allow moisture into both optical cavities, causing corrosion and colour shift. Routine seal inspection and pressure‑vent maintenance are critical for long‑term reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Wrong Colour Visible From an Approach or Departure Angle
Likely Cause
Degraded optical seal or dichroic filter allowing colour bleed between the red and green compartments.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform photometric testing of both colours from the relevant viewing angles; inspect the internal optical divider and filter seals for gaps or degradation.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded filter or reseal the optical divider, then re-verify colour separation with a follow-up photometric check.
One Colour Appears Dimmer Than the Other
Likely Cause
Differing optical efficiency between the red and green paths, or mismatched LED output between the two sides.
Diagnostic Steps
Take photometric intensity readings for both colours and compare against the specified ratio; check that LED modules on both sides are from matched bins.
Corrective Action
Calibrate or adjust the optical system, or replace mismatched LED modules with correctly matched-output units to restore visual balance.
Reduced Light Output on One or Both Optical Faces
Likely Cause
Contamination from runway debris, rubber, or de-icing fluid on one or both optical windows.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect both optical windows for visible contamination; run a photometric check on each face independently.
Corrective Action
Clean both optical windows with approved solvents, addressing both sides on a consistent cleaning schedule since the fixture has exposure on two faces.
Colour Shift or Corrosion Inside Either Optical Cavity
Likely Cause
A single point of seal failure has allowed moisture into both optical cavities.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect for condensation, corrosion, or discolouration inside both cavities; check the fixture's pressure-vent and main body seals for integrity.
Corrective Action
Dry out and clean affected cavities, replace failed seals, and service the pressure-vent to prevent recurring moisture ingress into both compartments.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the series circuit, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling cable connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near an active runway requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance, since this single unit serves as both the threshold light for one direction and the end light for the other.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for whenever the base can is open, and secure loose material before reinstating the fixture to prevent foreign object debris on the runway.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the fixture and base are bonded/grounded before reconnecting the loop.

Verify Both Colour Aspects
Compliance

Confirm both the green approach-side aspect and the red opposite-side aspect meet photometric and chromaticity requirements before closing out the work, since a single-optic fault can affect one colour while leaving the other normal.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Chromaticity Drifts Before Intensity Does
Optical

On a bi-colour combination fixture, the dichroic filter or coloured lens on one face typically loses chromaticity accuracy well before the LED itself loses meaningful output, so a fixture can pass a simple "is it lit" check while quietly drifting out of colour-box tolerance on one side. Schedule chromaticity spot checks independently of intensity checks rather than assuming one implies the other.

Confirm Orientation From Both Ends of the Runway
Commissioning

Because this fixture shows green to one direction of approach and red to the other, a single fixture installed with its optical axis rotated 180° will look correct from a casual ground inspection but present the wrong colour to pilots on one runway direction. Verify the visible colour by physically walking or driving both approach directions at commissioning, not just checking the fixture's physical alignment against the plan.

One Seal Failure Threatens Both Colours
Durability

Unlike a single-colour fixture, this unit has two optical cavities sharing one body and often one set of seals, so a single gasket or pressure-vent failure can compromise both the red and green sides simultaneously. Prioritise this fixture type in seal inspection rotations, since the failure consequence is effectively doubled compared with a single-optic unit.

Balance Optical Efficiency, Not Just Rated Lumens
Design

Red and green LEDs rarely have identical luminous efficacy for the same drive current, so specifying "equal wattage" for both sides does not guarantee equal perceived brightness. Select or bin LED modules against the actual candela requirement for each colour in the applicable standard, not against a shared electrical spec.

Rubber Deposits Build Unevenly Across the Two Faces
Maintenance

Because the two optical faces point in opposite directions along the runway, they accumulate rubber, dirt, and de-icing residue at different rates depending on local traffic patterns and prevailing wind. Clean and inspect both faces on their own schedule rather than treating the fixture as a single maintenance item, or one side will silently under-perform between visits.

Log Which Side Faults When Troubleshooting
Troubleshooting

An ALCMS alarm on a combination fixture doesn't tell you which optic failed. Record the reported colour fault against the specific approach direction in your maintenance log; over time this history narrows down whether a recurring fault is tied to one optical assembly, one connector pin, or the shared power feed.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about combination threshold/end lights: green and red colours, where they are used, spacing, photometrics, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a combination threshold/end light?

It is a single light unit that shows green to pilots on the approach side (threshold) and red to those on the opposite side (runway end). It is used where one physical location acts as the threshold for one direction and the runway end for the other.

Q2Where are combination threshold/end lights used?

At the ends of runways used in both directions and at displaced thresholds where the area before the threshold serves as a stopway or overrun. They are used on precision (CAT I, II, III) and non-precision runways wherever the threshold coincides with the runway extremity. They are not appropriate where the threshold is displaced away from the runway end.

Q3How does a combination fixture show two colours?

Either a dichroic reflector or prism splits a single lamp or LED array into green towards the approach side and red towards the opposite side, or the fixture has two separate optical cavities with independent colour filters. Both outputs follow the CCR brightness step together.

Q4How are combination threshold/end lights spaced?

Threshold lights are uniformly spaced at intervals of not more than 3 m between the rows of runway edge lights. Runway end lights are at least six, spread across the runway uniformly or symmetrically in two groups, on a line perpendicular to the runway axis and no more than 3 m outside the runway end.

Q5Are the green and red sides the same intensity?

No. The green threshold side follows the ICAO Annex 14 Appendix 2 isocandela diagram for the runway type and is substantially higher than the red side on a precision approach runway. A fixture that meets the red figure does not automatically meet the green one. Red end-light average main beam intensity is at least 2,500 cd on a precision runway.

Q6What are the brightness steps?

Intensity steps are 100%, 30%, 10%, 3% and 1% of rated peak intensity, matched across both optical chambers, and driven by the CCR's 5-step current settings (2.8 A to 6.6 A).

Q7How is a combination fixture powered?

From the constant current series circuit through an isolating transformer, typically 45 W or 65 W, which feeds both the green and red optical chambers. Cable is L-824 single-conductor with L-823 submersible primary connectors.

Q8Which standards apply to combination threshold/end lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 and Appendix 2, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (including the bidirectional green/red threshold and runway end fixtures), FAA AC 150/5345-42 (L-867 and L-868 base cans), IEC/TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief #67. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should combination threshold/end lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cleaning both lenses, and annual for bi-directional photometric checks, colour cross-contamination inspection and seal and moisture ingress checks. Each side should be tested separately, because moisture in one cavity does not always show in the other. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does a combination light show the wrong colour or one colour dimmer than the other?

A wrong colour from a given angle usually means a degraded optical seal or dichroic filter letting colour bleed between the red and green compartments. One dimmer colour usually means different optical efficiency or mismatched LED output between the two sides. Photometrically test both colours from the relevant angles and replace or reseal as needed.
