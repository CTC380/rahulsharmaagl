Bidirectional Runway Centreline Light

A flush‑mounted runway centreline light that emits light in both directions along the runway axis. Provides continuous longitudinal guidance to pilots during take‑off, landing, and roll‑out on runways used from both ends. Features the standard white‑red‑white colour coding to indicate remaining runway distance.

Overview

What Is It?

A Bidirectional Runway Centreline Light is an inset light fixture installed along the exact centre of the runway, engineered to project light in two opposite directions along the runway centreline. This design ensures that the light is visible to pilots approaching from either end of the runway, as well as to crews during the landing roll and take‑off run. The colour coding is identical to standard centreline lights: white from the threshold up to 900 m from the runway end, alternating red and white between 900 m and 300 m, and red in the last 300 m. This provides pilots with a clear visual indication of remaining runway length, regardless of the direction of travel.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The bidirectional design exists because many runways genuinely operate in both directions depending on the day's wind conditions, and a centreline light visible from only one end would leave the opposite-direction traffic without the same precise remaining-distance guidance. By projecting light both ways from a single fixture, the runway gets full centreline tracking and distance-remaining information regardless of which direction is currently active, without needing to install and maintain two separate light strings.

The same white/red-white/red colour progression works from either end because the pattern is symmetric relative to each threshold, meaning a pilot lands or departs and always sees the same meaningful sequence of colour transitions leading toward the runway end they're approaching, no matter which physical direction that happens to be on a given day.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Bidirectional centreline lights are installed on runways that are used for take‑off and landing in both directions (i.e., non‑directional runways). They are common on precision approach runways (CAT I, II, and III) where centreline guidance is required for both approach directions. The lights are spaced at 15‑metre intervals for CAT I runways and 7.5‑metre intervals for CAT II/III runways. They are used in conjunction with bidirectional runway edge lights and, where applicable, with touchdown zone lights that are also bi‑directional. The bidirectional optical design eliminates the need for separate unidirectional fixtures for each runway end, simplifying installation and maintenance.

Placement

Installation & Location

Bidirectional centreline lights are set flush into the runway pavement along the physical centreline, tied into the runway centreline survey the same way as unidirectional fixtures, at 15 m intervals for CAT I runways and 7.5 m intervals through the CAT II/III zone. Because each fixture projects light both ways along the runway, only a single row of lights is needed regardless of which direction is in use, simplifying the setting-out compared with runways that would otherwise need separate fixtures or optics for each landing direction. Each unit seats into an inset base can sealed against water and jet-blast erosion, fed by cable run in pavement ducting back to its isolating transformer, with installation following the same colour-zone layout (white body, red/white transition, solid red) used for the equivalent unidirectional centreline lighting.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each bidirectional centreline light is powered from the constant current series circuit via an isolating transformer. The fixture contains two optical systems – one facing each direction – or a single bi‑directional optical assembly (often using a prism or dual‑reflector design) that splits the light output into two equal beams along the runway centreline. Each beam typically has a narrow vertical spread (4°) and a horizontal spread (6°) to ensure visibility over a long distance. The colour transition is achieved using dichroic filters or coloured lenses that are selected based on the fixture's position along the runway. The intensity is controlled by the CCR's current steps, with higher intensities (up to 6.6 A) used during low‑visibility operations. The bidirectional nature is particularly useful during runway changes, as the same fixtures provide guidance for both approach directions without requiring reconfiguration.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Bidirectional centreline lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or (at smaller aerodromes) pilot-controlled lighting input. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A). Every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, draws from the same current and burns at the same intensity simultaneously in both directions — there is no way to intensify or dim one beam of a fixture, or one fixture, independently of the rest of the circuit. Colour is fixed at manufacture by the dichroic filter or lens selected for each fixture's position, so it is set by physical location on the loop rather than by any command from the ALCMS. Fault detection remains loop-based: the CCR monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise signals increased circuit impedance — typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer — reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture or beam direction without further sectionalised testing.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Bidirectional Fixture (with loop-level feedback)

System Integration

Circuit & Beam Overview

A single isolating transformer still feeds one fixture, but that fixture splits its output into two opposing beams rather than one — the same current draw as a unidirectional fixture now serves pilots at both ends of the runway at once, avoiding the need for a second fixture per position.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture uses a die-cast aluminium body, precision-machined and anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, set flush into an L-867 base can (fixture qualified under the L-850 series, FAA runway centreline light designation) recessed into the runway pavement. Because the unit must project light in two opposite directions along the centreline, it incorporates two separate lamp/LED and prism assemblies (or a single bi-lobed reflector arrangement) arranged back-to-back within a common housing, each independently aimed along the runway axis. The optical elements sit beneath a toughened borosilicate glass or polycarbonate top lens, load-rated for aircraft wheel loading and gasket-sealed with an O-ring to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating. Colour changes along the fixture's length are achieved with coloured filter segments or dual-colour LED arrays (white/red) built into each optical path rather than by physically relocating fixtures. Internal wiring from both optical assemblies terminates at sealed L-823 primary connectors mating to the secondary leads of a nearby isolating transformer housed in its own base can, with stainless-steel fasteners and a drainage channel provided to keep the base can free of standing water.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Optical Misalignment of Bidirectional Beams

Because the fixture has two optical paths, any slight misalignment during installation or after pavement settlement can cause one beam to deviate, reducing visibility from one direction. Regular alignment checks using a precision goniophotometer or digital level are essential to maintain both beams within the required tolerance (±0.5°).

Uneven Luminous Intensity Between the Two Beams

Dirt, moisture, or aging of optical components can cause unequal light output between the two beams. This can be particularly confusing for pilots as one direction may appear dimmer. Routine photometric testing of each beam individually is recommended, along with cleaning and replacement of prisms or lenses as needed.

Colour Filter Degradation on Both Sides

Bidirectional fixtures have colour filters for both directions, which are subjected to UV and thermal stress. Over time, the red and white filters can fade or shift, compromising the colour coding. Regular filter replacement and photometric colour measurements are required to maintain the integrity of the colour‑distance indication.

Moisture Ingress Affecting Both Optical Cavities

Bidirectional fixtures have two separate optical cavities or a shared cavity with two optical windows. A single point of ingress (e.g., a failed O‑ring or pressure vent) can affect both beams. Routine seal inspection and pressure‑vent maintenance are critical to prevent condensation and corrosion inside the fixture.

Troubleshooting Guide

One Beam Direction Dim or Not Visible
Likely Cause
Optical misalignment of one of the two beam paths, from installation error or pavement settlement, has pushed that beam outside its required tolerance.
Diagnostic Steps
Check both beam alignments with a precision goniophotometer or digital level against the ±0.5° tolerance.
Corrective Action
Re-align the affected beam to bring it within tolerance, and re-survey after any pavement work near the fixture.
Unequal Intensity Between the Two Beams
Likely Cause
Dirt, moisture, or aging optical components affecting one beam's prism or lens more than the other.
Diagnostic Steps
Take independent photometric readings for each beam direction and compare against each other and against spec.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the prism/lens on the underperforming beam, and re-verify both beams match within tolerance.
Colour Fade Differing Between Red and White Sides
Likely Cause
UV and thermal degradation of the colour filters, progressing unevenly between the two directional filters.
Diagnostic Steps
Run photometric colour measurements on both the red and white sides and compare against the colour-distance indication spec.
Corrective Action
Replace the faded filter(s) and re-verify colour-distance indication integrity on both sides.
Both Beams Affected by Moisture or Condensation
Likely Cause
A single point of ingress, such as a failed O-ring or blocked pressure vent, affecting the shared or dual optical cavity.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the O-ring seal and pressure vent for damage or blockage; check for internal condensation or corrosion.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed seal and clear or replace the pressure vent, then dry the cavity and confirm no further condensation before re-sealing.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Open Circuit / Failed Isolating Transformer Detected Only at Loop Level
High Severity
Root Cause
LED array or transformer failure at an individual fixture, which the CCR's loop-level voltage monitoring flags as a voltage rise without identifying which fixture on the loop has failed.
Effect
A dark fixture on the centreline is confirmed to exist somewhere on the loop, but pinpointing which one requires field testing rather than a control-system readout, since control and colour are managed at loop level rather than per fixture.
Detection Method
Loop voltage trend review against the CCR's baseline, followed by a sectionalised field walk or fixture-by-fixture continuity check to localise the fault.
Uneven Wear Between the Red and White Optical Halves
Medium Severity
Root Cause
One direction's optical half seeing disproportionately more landing or take-off traffic than the other, depending on prevailing wind and runway usage patterns, ageing that half's lens and LEDs faster.
Effect
One colour direction shows earlier photometric degradation than the other on the same physical fixture, an asymmetric wear pattern this bidirectional design is specifically exposed to.
Detection Method
Photometric testing of both optical halves independently rather than assuming symmetric condition from a single-direction test.
Bidirectional Lens / Prism Misalignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Settlement or an impact event shifting the fixture's dual-direction optic out of its commissioned alignment.
Effect
Beam confinement degrades on one or both directions, weakening the centreline cue for approach or take-off traffic from that end.
Detection Method
Fixture aim and orientation check against the runway centreline survey for both directions during scheduled inspection.
LED Array Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or a manufacturing defect in the LED array serving either optical half.
Effect
That fixture goes dark for one or both directions, breaking the continuous centreline reference at that point on the runway.
Detection Method
Test activation from both directions, and loop-resistance testing to help localise a suspected fixture.
Series Circuit Insulation Breakdown at Close Fixture Spacing
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress or cable damage along the circuit, more consequential here given the tight 7.5–15 m fixture spacing on CAT I/II/III bidirectional runs.
Effect
Intermittent operation or a partial ground fault on the circuit segment, potentially affecting several closely spaced fixtures at once.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit segment during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Centreline light circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening the inset housing or handling cable connectors.

Active Runway Work Coordination
Operational

The fixture sits directly on the landing/take-off path and load-bearing surface; obtain ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance, and never work on it with the runway active without an approved closure or protected work window.

Aircraft Wheel-Load Integrity
Mechanical

The fixture and its inset housing are directly trafficked by aircraft tyres; confirm the top plate, lens, and housing seat flush with the pavement surface and are rated for the design wheel load before reinstating, since a proud or cracked fixture becomes an FOD and load-bearing hazard.

FOD Control at the Fixture
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the housing is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the fixture, to prevent foreign object debris on the runway.

Hot Lamp / Housing Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the fixture housing and lamp/LED module to cool before handling after an active period; incandescent units in particular retain significant heat and can cause burns during lens or lamp replacement.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the fixture and housing are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture out of aim tolerance, at reduced intensity, or with the wrong colour segment showing after maintenance; verify photometric, positional, and colour-zone conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Colour Changeover Position Needs Periodic Re-Verification
Compliance

The point along the runway where centreline lighting transitions from white to red/white to red is a defined design distance from the runway end; confirm this changeover position is still correct after any relocation, repaving, or fixture replacement rather than assuming the original layout persists.

Colour Consistency Must Hold in Both Directions of Travel
Verification

A bidirectional fixture's two colour outputs can age or drift at different rates from each other; verify both directions independently during testing rather than confirming one direction and assuming the other matches.

Wheel-Load Integrity Is More Critical Here Than on Elevated Fixtures
Mechanical

Bidirectional centreline fixtures are inset and take direct, repeated wheel loading along the most heavily trafficked line on the runway; prioritise housing and lens integrity checks on these fixtures over a uniform inspection interval applied runway-wide.

Dual-Colour LED Modules Can Age Asymmetrically
Reliability

Where a fixture uses separate LED elements for each colour, one colour's elements can lumen-depreciate faster than the other depending on drive current and duty cycle, gradually shifting the relative brightness balance between directions even though each colour individually still passes minimum output.

Inset Housing Seal Integrity Matters More at High-Traffic Centreline Positions
Durability

Centreline positions see concentrated wheel traffic and de-icing fluid exposure; seal degradation here has a shorter runway to failure than at lower-traffic inset positions. Prioritise these fixtures in gasket and seal inspection scheduling.

Verify From an Approaching Pilot's Actual Viewing Angle, Not Just Overhead
Verification

A fixture's colour and brightness can measure correctly on axis while looking noticeably different from the shallow viewing angle of an approaching or departing pilot. Where practical, verify from a representative low-angle viewpoint rather than relying solely on direct overhead or bench measurement.
