Auxiliary Control Panel

A secondary control console that provides dedicated, localised control of specific AGL subsystems — such as stop bars, approach lighting, apron floodlights, or guidance signs. The Auxiliary Control Panel complements the Master Control Panel by offloading routine or subsystem‑specific tasks, improving operator efficiency and providing redundant control points for critical functions.

Overview

What Is It?

An Auxiliary Control Panel (ACP) is a dedicated operator interface designed to manage a specific subset of airfield lighting functions, independent of the Master Control Panel. It is typically smaller, less complex, and focused on a particular subsystem — for example, stop bar control, approach lighting sequencers, apron lighting, or runway status light (RWSL) systems. The ACP provides localised control and monitoring, allowing operators to manage these subsystems without cluttering the main control console.

Key characteristics of the Auxiliary Control Panel include:

  • Function‑Specific Design: Tailored to the needs of a particular subsystem, with controls and indicators optimised for that task (e.g., stop bar on/off, brightness steps for approach lights, or mode selection for RWSL).
  • Compact Form Factor: Smaller than the Master Control Panel, often mounted in a 19" rack or as a wall‑mounted unit, saving valuable control room space.
  • Dedicated I/O: Hardwired or networked connections to the specific field devices it controls, reducing the load on the main ALCMS communication network.
  • Redundancy & Backup: Can serve as a backup control point for critical subsystems if the Master Control Panel or ALCMS becomes unavailable.
  • Local/Remote Selector: Often includes a mode switch to allow the panel to operate in "local" (manual) or "remote" (ALCMS‑controlled) mode, providing flexibility and safety.

Auxiliary Control Panels are deployed in various locations, including control towers, substations, approach lighting shelters, and apron control rooms. They are typically connected to the ALCMS via hardwired I/O or a serial/Ethernet link, and they may include a small display or status indicators to show real‑time subsystem status.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The auxiliary control panel exists to keep a demanding, safety-critical subsystem — stop bars, an approach lighting sequencer, RWSL — from being just one more set of controls buried among everything else on the Master Control Panel, where the sheer volume of information could make a subsystem-specific fault harder to spot quickly. Giving that subsystem its own dedicated, purpose-built interface means an operator managing it doesn't have to navigate through unrelated controls to find what they need in a time-sensitive moment.

Its role as a backup control point is just as important: if the Master Control Panel or the central ALCMS becomes unavailable, having a subsystem's controls already isolated onto their own panel means that specific function can keep operating independently, rather than the whole subsystem going dark along with everything else that depends on the central system.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Auxiliary Control Panels are deployed in a wide range of airfield lighting applications, including:

  • Stop Bar Control: Dedicated panels for managing runway stop bars and clearance bars, providing quick on/off control and status indication for ground controllers.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Panels for controlling approach light sequencers, flashing lights, and barrette intensity levels, often located in approach control shelters or the tower.
  • Apron & Gate Lighting: Panels for managing apron floodlights, docking guidance systems, and gate illumination, typically located in apron control rooms or at the gate.
  • Runway Status Lights (RWSL): Dedicated panels for RWSL system monitoring and control, including mode selection (automatic/manual) and override functions.
  • Taxiway Guidance Systems: Panels for controlling taxiway centreline and edge lighting segments, particularly at complex intersections or during construction.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Panels for monitoring and controlling obstruction lights on airport structures, providing status and fault indication.
  • Weather & Visibility Systems: Panels for displaying weather data and controlling associated lighting (e.g., RVR‑dependent lighting steps).
  • Maintenance & Testing: Portable or fixed panels used by maintenance crews for testing and troubleshooting specific lighting circuits without affecting the main system.
Placement

Installation & Location

Auxiliary control panels are installed close to the subsystem they manage rather than centrally, so a stop bar panel sits in or near the relevant taxiway control cabinet, an approach lighting panel sits in the approach control shelter or tower, and an RWSL or apron lighting panel sits in the relevant operations or gate control room. Wiring runs directly to the sequencer, relay cabinet, or lighting circuit it operates, with a separate feed into the ALCMS network where status and fault reporting are integrated centrally. Because these panels are meant to provide independent, subsystem-level control that keeps working even if the broader ALCMS is unavailable, they're positioned so that link to the ALCMS is a reporting connection rather than the only path by which the panel can issue commands.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Auxiliary Control Panel operates as a specialised interface, providing focused control and monitoring for its assigned subsystem. Key functional aspects include:

  • Dedicated Control Logic: The panel contains hardwired or programmable logic tailored to the specific subsystem. For example, a stop bar control panel may include interlock logic to prevent conflicting commands (e.g., not clearing a stop bar if an aircraft is on the runway).
  • Direct Field Device Interface: The panel communicates directly with the field devices (e.g., stop bar controllers, approach light sequencers) via hardwired discrete I/O or a dedicated communication link. This allows the panel to operate independently of the ALCMS if needed.
  • Status Indication: The panel provides clear visual feedback of the subsystem state using LED indicators, alphanumeric displays, or small touchscreens. This includes status (ON/OFF), fault conditions, and mode (Local/Remote).
  • Mode Selection (Local/Remote): A key‑operated or software‑selectable switch determines whether the panel is in Local mode (manual control enabled) or Remote mode (control delegated to the ALCMS). In Remote mode, the panel's controls are locked out, but status is still displayed.
  • Alarm Annunciation: The panel includes visual and audible alarms for faults specific to its subsystem (e.g., stop bar failure, sequencer fault). These alarms are also forwarded to the ALCMS for central logging.
  • Communication with ALCMS: The panel exchanges status and control data with the ALCMS, ensuring that the Master Control Panel and other systems have a consistent view of the subsystem. This communication is typically via Modbus, Profibus, or Ethernet.

The Auxiliary Control Panel is designed for high reliability, with industrial‑grade components and protection against environmental factors. It is powered from a dedicated supply and often includes surge protection and filtering to ensure stable operation.

Construction

Physical Construction

The panel is housed in a compact wall-mount enclosure, small rack-mount chassis (1U to 4U), or desktop unit, sized to sit alongside — rather than replace — the main L-821 lighting control panel, since it is dedicated to a single subsystem such as stop bars, approach lighting, or RWSL. The front face carries LED status indicators or a small LCD display, a local/remote selector (a key-operated switch on more sensitive subsystems, or a software toggle on networked panels), and any subsystem-specific pushbuttons, laid out so the on-duty controller can confirm the subsystem's state without cross-referencing the main panel. Internally, terminal blocks and a small relay or I/O board handle the discrete wiring back to field equipment, with a separate section for the communication interface card where the panel talks to the main ALCMS over RS-485 or Ethernet. The enclosure is rated IP20 for indoor cabinet use as standard, with a gasketed, weatherproof (IP54) version available where the panel must sit in an unconditioned equipment room or outdoor cabinet.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Local/Remote Selector Test
Semi-annual

Cycle the key-operated or software selector through both positions and confirm control authority transfers cleanly, so the panel is never accidentally stuck out of remote reach.

ALCMS Communication Link Test
Quarterly

Verify the RS-485 or Ethernet link to the ALCMS reports status and accepts commands without dropouts, and confirm the panel's own "communication lost" alarm triggers correctly on a simulated break.

Switch & Contact Inspection
Annual

Check switch and pushbutton contacts for oxidation or wear, particularly on panels in humid or coastal locations, and clean or replace contacts before intermittent operation appears.

Interlock Logic Verification
Annual

Force each interlock condition for the subsystem under control and confirm the panel blocks the unsafe command, re-verifying against the documented interlock settings after any modification.

Alarm & Buzzer Test
Semi-annual

Trigger a test fault and confirm the flashing LED and audible buzzer both activate at the correct volume, since a subsystem-dedicated panel's alarm is often the only local warning an operator gets.

Indicator Lamp Check
Quarterly

Run the lamp-test function and visually confirm every ON/FAULT/STANDBY indicator or LCD display reads correctly, replacing any failed LEDs promptly to avoid operator confusion.

Enclosure & Seal Inspection
Annual

Check gaskets, cable glands, and enclosure hardware on outdoor or weatherproof installations, since moisture ingress or corrosion is the leading cause of failure for exposed auxiliary panels.

Control Power Supply Test
Annual

Confirm the 24 VDC or AC control supply stays within tolerance under load, and verify any backup or secondary supply path transfers cleanly on a simulated primary-supply loss.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Mode Selector Malfunction

The Local/Remote selector switch can fail or be left in an unintended position, causing loss of control or unexpected behaviour. Regularly test the selector and ensure clear labelling. Consider using a key‑operated switch with a tamper‑proof mechanism.

Communication Loss with ALCMS

If the panel loses communication with the ALCMS, it may not receive remote commands or forward status updates. Use redundant communication paths and implement a "communication lost" alarm on the panel itself.

Switch Contact Oxidation

In humid or coastal environments, switch contacts can oxidise, leading to intermittent operation. Use gold‑plated or sealed switches and perform periodic maintenance.

Incorrect Interlock Configuration

If the panel's interlock logic is not properly configured, unsafe operations may be possible. Verify interlock logic during commissioning and after any modifications. Document all interlock settings.

Environmental Damage

Outdoor panels can suffer from moisture ingress, corrosion, or temperature extremes. Ensure proper enclosure sealing and use heaters/coolers as needed. Regularly inspect gaskets and cable glands.

Indicator Lamp Failure

LED indicators can fail due to surges or manufacturing defects. Include a lamp test feature and use high‑quality components. Replace failed indicators promptly to avoid operator confusion.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Panel Does Not Respond as Expected to Local or Remote Commands
Likely Cause
The Local/Remote selector switch has failed or been left in an unintended position.
Diagnostic Steps
Physically verify the selector's position against the panel's actual control mode, and cycle the switch while monitoring circuit response.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed selector switch, and consider upgrading to a key-operated, tamper-proof version to prevent unintended repositioning.
Panel Not Receiving Remote Commands or Forwarding Status
Likely Cause
Communication between the panel and the ALCMS has been lost.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the communication link status and any "communication lost" alarm on the panel; verify redundant paths if fitted.
Corrective Action
Restore the communication link, and implement redundant communication paths and a local communication-lost alarm if not already in place.
Switch Operation Intermittent in Humid or Coastal Sites
Likely Cause
Switch contacts have oxidised due to humid or coastal environmental exposure.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect switch contacts for oxidation or corrosion, and test for intermittent operation under normal use.
Corrective Action
Replace with gold-plated or sealed switches suited to the environment, and add periodic contact maintenance to the schedule.
Unsafe Operating Sequence Possible
Likely Cause
The panel's interlock logic was not correctly configured, or a modification was made without re-verifying the logic.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the interlock configuration against the documented design, and test all interlock conditions systematically.
Corrective Action
Correct the interlock configuration to match the verified design, re-test fully, and update the interlock documentation to reflect the change.
Corrosion or Moisture Found Inside an Outdoor Panel
Likely Cause
Enclosure sealing has degraded, allowing moisture ingress, or the panel is experiencing temperature extremes beyond its rating.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect gaskets and cable glands for damage, and check for internal condensation or corrosion.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded gaskets/glands, restore proper enclosure sealing, and fit or service heaters/coolers as needed for the site's climate.
Indicator Lamp Fails to Illuminate
Likely Cause
An LED indicator has failed due to a surge event or a manufacturing defect.
Diagnostic Steps
Run the panel's lamp test feature to identify the failed indicator, and check for evidence of a recent surge event.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed indicator promptly with a high-quality component to avoid operator confusion, and consider surge protection if failures recur.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Mode Selector Malfunction
High Severity
Root Cause
Worn contacts or a mechanical fault in the local/remote selector switch after repeated operation over its service life.
Effect
The panel does not respond as expected to local or remote commands, and can leave the subsystem stuck in whichever mode the switch last registered.
Detection Method
Local/remote selector test cycling the switch through each position and confirming the panel responds correctly in each.
Communication Loss with ALCMS
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Network cable damage, a failed communication card, or a configuration change breaking the panel's reporting link to the ALCMS.
Effect
The panel is not receiving remote commands or forwarding status, forcing reliance on the panel's local controls without central visibility.
Detection Method
ALCMS communication link test and review of the connection status log.
Switch Contact Oxidation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Humid or coastal site conditions building an oxide layer on switch and relay contacts inside the panel.
Effect
Switch operation becomes intermittent, particularly noticeable in humid or coastal sites where this is a known recurring issue.
Detection Method
Switch and contact inspection, with particular attention at sites known for high humidity or salt exposure.
Incorrect Interlock Configuration
High Severity
Root Cause
A wiring error or configuration change during commissioning or later modification that doesn't correctly enforce the intended interlock logic.
Effect
An unsafe operating sequence becomes possible, since the interlock that should prevent it is not actually enforcing the intended constraint.
Detection Method
Interlock logic verification test performed by deliberately attempting the prohibited sequence and confirming the panel blocks it.
Environmental Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A degraded enclosure seal or gasket allowing moisture and dust ingress into an outdoor-mounted panel.
Effect
Corrosion or moisture is found inside an outdoor panel during inspection, ahead of it causing an actual functional fault.
Detection Method
Enclosure and seal inspection as part of scheduled maintenance, checking gasket condition and looking for internal condensation.
Indicator Lamp Failure
Low Severity
Root Cause
Ordinary end-of-life bulb or LED failure, or a blown fuse in the indicator circuit.
Effect
The indicator lamp fails to illuminate, denying the operator a status cue even though the underlying subsystem may be functioning normally.
Detection Method
Indicator lamp check performed by cycling through each status and confirming the corresponding lamp illuminates.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Confirm Mode Before Leaving the Panel
Operational

Always return the local/remote selector to its normal operating position (typically remote/ALCMS) before leaving the panel unattended, so the subsystem does not remain stranded in manual control.

De-Energise Before Internal Work
Electrical Safety

Isolate and lock out supply and control power before opening the panel to service switches, indicators, or internal wiring.

Airside Coordination for Affected Subsystems
Operational

Where the panel controls stop bars, RWSL, or other safety-critical subsystems, coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM as needed before taking the subsystem out of automatic control for testing.

Do Not Bypass Interlocks
Compliance

Never defeat or bypass hardwired interlocks to work around a fault; diagnose and correct the underlying condition, since interlocks exist to prevent conflicting or unsafe circuit operations.

PPE for Live Testing
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate PPE and insulated tools if any testing must be performed with control or indication circuits energised.

Verify Correct Operation Before Handover
Compliance

After maintenance, confirm each switch, indicator, and interlock functions correctly and that the panel is left in its normal operating mode before signing off the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Panel Left in the Wrong Mode Is a Silent Risk
Operational

A panel left in local or maintenance mode after work is completed can leave a subsystem unresponsive to the master control system without any immediate alarm, and the gap may only surface the next time that subsystem is needed. Make confirming mode selection back to normal a distinct, checked step at every handover, not an assumed part of closing up.

A Bypassed Interlock Rarely Gets Un-Bypassed on Schedule
Safety Design

An interlock bypassed temporarily to work around a fault has a strong tendency to stay bypassed once the immediate problem is resolved and attention moves elsewhere. Track any interlock bypass as an open item with an owner and a close-out date, not just a note left at the panel.

Map Panel Functions to Operational Impact Before Working on Them
Design

Not every switch on an auxiliary panel carries the same operational weight; some affect a single non-critical circuit while others affect systems ATC is actively relying on. Know which functions require airside coordination before starting work, rather than treating every panel the same regardless of what it actually controls.

Live Testing Needs a Plan, Not Just PPE
Electrical Safety

Wearing correct PPE for live testing addresses the injury risk but not the operational risk of an unplanned trip or state change during the test. Agree the test sequence and rollback plan with anyone relying on the panel's outputs before energising it for testing, not just with the person doing the work.

Indicator Lamps Can Lie About Actual State
Troubleshooting

A failed indicator lamp or a wiring fault upstream of it can show a status that doesn't match the actual state of the equipment it's meant to represent, which is a common source of confusion during fault-finding. Verify actual equipment state independently when an indicator's reading is central to a diagnosis, rather than trusting the lamp at face value.

Functional Verification Beats a Visual Check on Handover
Compliance

Confirming a panel "looks correct" after maintenance doesn't catch a switch that's mechanically fine but wired to the wrong function, or an interlock that resets incorrectly. Exercise every switch, indicator, and interlock through its actual function before signing off, not just a visual inspection of the panel face.
