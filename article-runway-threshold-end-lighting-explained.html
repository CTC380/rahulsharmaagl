Runway Threshold and Runway End Lighting System Explained: ICAO Annex 14 Requirements and Practical Engineering Guide

Every successful landing begins with a clear visual reference. Threshold lights mark where the runway available for landing begins; end lights mark where the runway runs out. Both look simple — both are engineered to precise ICAO Annex 14 photometric and electrical requirements.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

Published July 7, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

For pilots approaching an airport at night or in reduced visibility, one of the first visual cues identifying the start of the runway available for landing is the Runway Threshold Lighting System. As the aircraft completes its landing roll, another equally important visual aid comes into view: the Runway End Lighting System, indicating the end of the available runway.

Although these lighting systems appear simple, they are designed according to precise engineering and photometric requirements defined in ICAO Annex 14. Their purpose extends beyond illumination — they provide pilots with unmistakable visual information about where the runway begins, where it ends, and where it is safe to operate.

Key Takeaway

Threshold lights (green) mark the start of the runway available for landing; end lights (red) mark the end of the runway. Both are engineered to ICAO Annex 14 spacing, colour, and intensity requirements — and both depend on a single integrated electrical and photometric system to actually deliver that guidance.

01

Understanding the Runway Threshold

A runway threshold is the beginning of the portion of the runway available for landing. It may be:

  • A threshold located at the physical beginning of the runway
  • A displaced threshold, where the landing area starts further down the runway due to obstacles, pavement limitations, or operational requirements

Regardless of its location, the threshold marks the beginning of the runway available for landing. It is not the touchdown point: aircraft cross the threshold on approach and touch down further along, in the touchdown zone. Providing a clear visual indication of the threshold is critical for flight safety, especially during night operations and low-visibility conditions.

Where a displaced threshold exists, the threshold lighting must be located at the declared threshold — not the physical beginning of the pavement. Runway edge lights on the pavement before the displaced threshold show red to approaching aircraft.

02

Runway Threshold Lights & ICAO Requirements

Runway Threshold Lights are green lights installed across the landing threshold of a runway. Their primary purpose is to indicate:

  • The beginning of the landing surface
  • The point where the runway available for landing begins
  • The usable runway available for landing
  • A lateral reference across the full runway width during the final approach

To an approaching pilot, these lights form a continuous line of green lights extending across the runway width, confirming that the aircraft is correctly aligned with the landing surface. Per ICAO Annex 14, threshold lights shall be provided for a runway equipped with runway edge lights, except on a non-instrument or non-precision approach runway where the threshold is displaced and wing bar lights are provided. They are fixed, unidirectional green lights showing in the direction of approach, and the system should:

  • Extend across the full width of the runway, either equally spaced between the edge-light rows or in two symmetrical groups
  • Be placed in a row at right angles to the runway axis, as near to the threshold as possible and, if outside it, not more than 3 m outside
  • Clearly define the landing threshold
  • Use a number and spacing of lights that depends on the approach category (non-precision, CAT I, CAT II/III), with closer spacing for the precision categories — take the exact figures from Annex 14 section 5.3.10
  • Provide adequate intensity for the intended operational category

Green threshold wing bars — short groups of lights either side of the threshold — add conspicuity. They are used, for example, on precision approach runways and where a threshold is displaced; confirm the exact requirement for your category in Annex 14.

This ensures pilots receive accurate visual guidance regarding the available landing distance, even where the threshold has been displaced.

03

Runway End Lights & ICAO Requirements

Runway End Lights mark the extremity of the runway. Unlike threshold lights, runway end lights are fixed, unidirectional red lights showing in the direction of the runway — warning pilots that no further runway is available beyond this point. Annex 14 provides runway end lights for a runway equipped with edge lights and specifies that they shall:

  • Be placed in a row at right angles to the runway axis, as near to the end as possible and not more than 3 m outside it
  • Consist of at least six lights, either equally spaced between the edge-light rows or in two symmetrical groups with a limited central gap
  • On CAT III runways, be closely spaced (not more than about 6 m between lights, apart from any central gap)
  • Show red toward the runway, so they are visible to aircraft operating on it

Their arrangement ensures that pilots can easily identify runway termination during the landing roll, take-off roll and rejected take-off.

A row of red lights signifies one thing to a pilot: end of the runway — no usable pavement beyond, so stop or turn off at a taxiway before reaching it.

04

The Colour Logic: Why Green, Why Red

The colour of these lights carries a specific, internationally standardised operational meaning — one that eliminates ambiguity regardless of airport location.

Green — ThresholdMarks the start of the runway available for landing. It is not a landing clearance — that comes from ATC
Red — Runway EndUniversally recognised warning colour: the runway ends here, with no usable pavement beyond

Where the threshold is at the runway extremity, the same fittings can serve as threshold lights for one direction and end lights for the other: combined threshold/end fittings show green toward the approach and red toward the runway. This bi-directional design reduces the number of fittings while giving pilots the correct information from each direction. Combined units are normally used only where the threshold coincides with the runway end; at a displaced threshold the arrangement differs and bi-directional units are rare.

Proper installation orientation is essential — an incorrectly installed combined fitting may display the wrong colour, creating a significant operational hazard.

05

Photometric Requirements

Runway threshold and runway end lights must satisfy stringent photometric requirements to ensure consistent visibility under varying weather and ambient lighting conditions:

Beam SpreadCorrect horizontal and vertical spread of the light cone
Light IntensitySpecified candela output for the operational category
Colour ChromaticityGreen and red within the Annex 14 Appendix 1 colour limits
Horizontal DistributionIntensity profile across azimuth
Vertical DistributionIntensity profile by elevation angle
Minimal GlareControlled output that doesn't dazzle the pilot

Annex 14 Appendix 2 gives isocandela diagrams for these light types, and performance is judged against the diagram rather than a single candela value. Modern LED fittings use precision optics to meet these requirements while reducing energy consumption and maintenance.

For AGL engineers, photometric compliance is not simply a testing activity at the end of installation — it is a consideration throughout design, fixture selection, installation, commissioning, and maintenance.

06

Electrical Configuration

Both threshold and runway end lights are typically connected in an airport series lighting circuit. A standard installation includes:

01CCRConstant Current Regulator
02Primary CableSeries feeder cable
03Isolating TransformerPer-fixture isolation (FAA: isolation transformer)
04Secondary CableTransformer to fitting
05Connectore.g. FAA L-823 type, or equivalent
06Light FittingThreshold or end light

This configuration ensures every light receives correct operating current while maintaining electrical isolation between fixtures — the electrical system and the lighting fixture must be considered as one integrated AGL system, not independent components.

07

Installation Best Practices

Successful installation requires more than simply mounting the fittings. Engineers should verify:

  • Correct orientation, accurate spacing, and proper elevation
  • Secure frangible mounting and correct beam direction
  • Waterproof cable connections and tight connector assembly
  • Proper earthing and circuit continuity
  • Photometric performance verified after installation

A final night inspection should always be performed to verify the visual appearance from both approach and runway directions — particularly important for combined fittings, where the correct colour must be visible from the intended direction, and for displaced thresholds, where the red edge-light zone must face approaching aircraft.

08

Common Field Issues & Maintenance

During inspections, engineers frequently encounter:

Incorrect orientation of combined fittings
Water ingress inside fixtures
Dirty optical lenses reducing light output
Loose secondary connectors
Failed isolating transformers
Damaged frangible couplings
LED driver failures
Incorrect installation after runway resurfacing

Most of these issues can be prevented through routine preventive maintenance and periodic inspections. A structured maintenance program should include:

  • Routine visual inspections and cleaning of optical surfaces
  • Tightening of mounting hardware
  • Insulation resistance testing and electrical continuity checks
  • Photometric verification and functional testing at all CCR brightness steps
  • Replacement of damaged frangible components

Preventive maintenance not only improves system reliability but also reduces unexpected outages during airport operations.

09

Threshold vs. Runway End Lights at a Glance

Where the threshold is at the runway end they can share combined fittings, yet threshold and end lights communicate entirely different operational messages depending on the viewing direction.

Pilot ActionUse as the lateral and longitudinal reference on approachStop or turn off before the end

Key Takeaways

  • Threshold lights are green and mark where the runway available for landing begins; end lights are red and mark where the runway terminates
  • A displaced threshold requires the threshold lighting to be at the declared threshold, not the physical pavement edge
  • Where the threshold is at the runway end, combined fittings show green toward approach and red toward the runway — orientation errors are a genuine operational hazard
  • Photometric compliance (beam spread, intensity, chromaticity, distribution, glare control) must be verified, not assumed
  • The CCR, primary cable, isolation transformer, secondary cable, connector, and fitting must be treated as one integrated electrical system
  • A structured preventive maintenance program materially reduces unexpected outages during airport operations

Final Thoughts

Runway Threshold and Runway End Lighting Systems are among the most fundamental components of an Airfield Ground Lighting system. Their standardised colours, precise placement, and carefully controlled photometric characteristics enable pilots to identify the beginning and end of the runway instantly, regardless of the airport or operating environment.

For AGL engineers, achieving compliance with ICAO Annex 14, as applied through your State’s regulations, requires careful attention to design, installation, testing, and maintenance. Every correctly installed light contributes to safer approaches, smoother landings, better pilot situational awareness, and more reliable airport operations.

As airports continue to modernise with LED technology, intelligent monitoring systems, and advanced airfield control solutions, the primary objective remains unchanged: provide pilots with clear, reliable, and internationally standardised visual guidance for every take-off and landing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a runway threshold?

The runway threshold is the beginning of the portion of the runway available for landing. It may be at the physical beginning of the runway or displaced further along it because of obstacles, pavement limitations or operational requirements. It is not the touchdown point, because aircraft cross the threshold on approach and touch down further along in the touchdown zone.

What colour are runway threshold lights and what do they indicate?

Threshold lights are fixed, unidirectional green lights showing in the direction of approach. They mark the start of the runway available for landing and form a line of green across the runway width. Green is not a landing clearance, which comes from ATC.

Where are threshold lights placed if the threshold is displaced?

They must be located at the declared threshold, not at the physical beginning of the pavement. Runway edge lights on the pavement before a displaced threshold show red to approaching aircraft.

How many threshold lights are required and how are they spaced?

The number and spacing depend on the approach category (non-precision, CAT I, CAT II/III), with closer spacing for the precision categories, so take the exact figures from Annex 14 section 5.3.10. The lights extend across the full runway width in a row at right angles to the runway axis, as near to the threshold as possible and, if outside it, not more than 3 m outside.

What are runway end lights?

Runway end lights are fixed, unidirectional red lights that warn pilots that no further runway is available. They are placed in a row at right angles to the runway axis, as near to the end as possible and not more than 3 m outside it, and consist of at least six lights. On CAT III runways they are closely spaced, at not more than about 6 m apart, apart from any central gap.

What are threshold wing bars?

Threshold wing bars are short groups of green lights on either side of the threshold that add conspicuity. They are used, for example, on precision approach runways and where a threshold is displaced, so confirm the exact requirement for your category in Annex 14.

Can one light fitting serve as both a threshold and an end light?

Yes. Combined threshold/end fittings show green toward the approach and red toward the runway, and are normally used only where the threshold coincides with the runway end. Orientation is critical, because an incorrectly installed combined fitting may show the wrong colour and create a significant operational hazard.

How are threshold and end lights judged photometrically?

They must meet the intensity, beam spread and distribution requirements for the operational category, with green and red chromaticity within the Annex 14 Appendix 1 colour limits. Annex 14 Appendix 2 gives isocandela diagrams for these light types, and performance is judged against the diagram rather than a single candela value.
