Primary Cable Joint/Splice Connector

A specialised connector used to join or splice L‑824 primary AGL cables in series circuits. Available in mechanical, compression, and resin‑filled types, it provides a reliable, waterproof, and electrically sound connection that maintains the integrity of the constant‑current loop in buried, duct, and elevated installations.

Overview

What Is It?

The Primary Cable Joint/Splice Connector (also known as a primary splice connector or series cable joint) is a specialised termination device used to make permanent or semi‑permanent connections between two sections of L‑824 primary AGL cable. These connectors are essential for creating cable runs of the required length, repairing damaged cable sections, and making tap‑offs or branch connections in the series circuit.

Key characteristics include:

  • Conductor Connection: Provides a low‑resistance mechanical or compression connection between the copper conductors, ensuring minimal voltage drop and heat generation.
  • Insulation and Sealing: Restores the electrical insulation and provides a waterproof seal (IP68) to prevent moisture ingress, which is critical for buried and submerged installations.
  • Screen Continuity: For shielded cables (L‑824), the connector includes provisions to reconnect the metallic screen (shield) to maintain EMI protection and earth continuity.
  • Mechanical Strength: Provides mechanical strain relief to prevent the joint from pulling apart under tension or during backfilling.
  • Types: Available in several configurations, including compression‑type (crimp), bolted mechanical connectors, heat‑shrink splice kits, and resin‑filled joints.

The connector is typically installed in a joint pit, handhole, or directly buried, and is covered with a protective housing or filled with encapsulating compound for additional mechanical and environmental protection.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The splice connector exists because a real airfield cable run almost never comes from a single continuous length of cable — damaged sections need repair, circuits need extending, and branch taps need to be added — and any of these situations requires a way to join two cable ends that performs as reliably as the continuous cable itself, at the full 5kV operating stress of the primary circuit. A poorly made joint is a likely point of future failure, whether from resistance heating, insulation breakdown, or moisture ingress at exactly the seam where protection is hardest to maintain.

Because these joints are typically buried or installed in handholes where they're difficult to inspect or access after installation, getting the connection, insulation, and screen continuity right the first time matters enormously — a fault discovered years later at a buried splice means locating and excavating a specific point along a long cable run, which is far more costly than a proper joint made correctly during installation.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The primary cable joint/splice connector is used throughout the airfield wherever primary cable splices are required, including:

  • Runway and Taxiway Lighting Circuits: Splicing L‑824 cable to extend the series loop around the airfield, including connections at circuit transitions and turns.
  • Repair of Damaged Cable: Cutting out damaged sections of cable and splicing in new cable, restoring circuit continuity with minimal disruption.
  • Tap‑Offs and Branch Connections: Creating a tap‑off point to feed a separate series circuit or to connect to an isolating transformer (though L‑823 connectors are more common for transformer connections).
  • Transition Points: Joining cable from different installation methods (e.g., underground to elevated) or connecting cable to equipment such as CCR output panels.
  • Junction Boxes and Handholes: Installing splices within junction boxes or handholes to allow for future maintenance and testing.
  • System Expansion: Adding new sections of primary cable during airport expansion or runway extension projects.

The connector is available for cable sizes from 6 AWG to 2 AWG (6mm² to 35mm²) and is rated for continuous operation at the full primary current (6.6A or 20A) and voltage (5kV).

Placement

Installation & Location

Splices are made in an accessible splice pit or handhole set along the cable duct route, rather than buried directly in an unmarked trench, so the joint can be located and re-opened for future fault-finding without excavating blind. The pit is backfilled with sand or a marker tape laid above it once the splice is tested and potted/sealed, and its location is logged on the as-built cable route drawing since a splice is otherwise invisible once buried. Where the splice makes a tap-off or transition (underground to elevated, or into a CCR output panel), the pit or enclosure is sized to keep minimum bend radius on both the incoming and outgoing cable runs, and is sited clear of the pavement's frost/frangibility zone where the route runs near a runway or taxiway edge.

Function

How Does It Work?

The primary cable splice connector provides a reliable electrical and mechanical joint between two cable ends. The specific method varies by connector type:

  • Compression (Crimp) Connectors: The stripped conductor ends are inserted into a cylindrical connector (barrel) and crimped with a hydraulic or manual crimping tool. The crimp deforms the barrel, creating a gas‑tight, low‑resistance connection. The joint is then insulated with heat‑shrink tubing or covered with a resin‑filled sleeve.
  • Bolted Mechanical Connectors: The conductor ends are clamped into the connector using set screws or bolts. These are easier to install but require careful torque control to ensure low resistance. They are often used in junction boxes where future disconnection may be needed.
  • Heat‑Shrink Splice Kits: After the conductors are joined (often with a crimp connector), a dual‑wall heat‑shrink tubing with adhesive liner is slid over the joint and heated. The tubing shrinks and the adhesive melts, creating a watertight seal and restoring insulation.
  • Resin‑Filled (Cold‑Shrink) Joints: The conductor connection is placed inside a mould, and a two‑part epoxy or polyurethane resin is poured in. The resin cures to form a solid, waterproof, and mechanically robust insulation block. This method provides the highest level of protection for buried splices.
  • Screen Connection: For shielded cables, the screen (copper tape or braid) is reconnected using a screen connector or by twisting and soldering the braid, and then it is earthed at one end of the splice (or both ends, depending on design).

The splice must also restore the cable's overall diameter and mechanical strength to withstand handling and backfilling. Proper installation using the correct tooling and materials is essential for long‑term reliability.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The splice connector carries no control logic of its own — like the cable it joins, it is a passive link in the CCR's series loop, and its only "function" from the loop's point of view is to be electrically indistinguishable from an unbroken run of L-824 cable. Whatever the ALCMS commands and the CCR regulates, the splice simply has to pass the full loop current through with negligible added resistance and no leakage path through the screen or insulation. Because the loop is monitored only at its ends (loop voltage against commanded current), a degrading splice — rising contact resistance in a crimp, a compromised heat-shrink seal — shows up to the CCR as the same gradual voltage rise or eventual open circuit as any other loop fault, with no way to tell from the ALCMS console alone whether the fault is at a splice, a fixture, or a length of cable; locating it falls to insulation-resistance testing and sectionalised loop testing, then physically inspecting the suspect joint.

A correctly installed splice restores the cable electrically as well as mechanically, so it disappears into the loop; a poor one only reveals itself the same way any other loop fault would — as a voltage anomaly, not a named location.
Operating Principle

Four Ways to Join Two Cable Ends

All four methods create the electrical joint differently, but each still has to pass the same two tests afterward: restoring the cable's original insulation and outer diameter, and — for shielded cable — re-establishing screen continuity to earth.
Construction

Physical Construction

A primary splice connector kit typically comprises a crimp or compression connector body to join the two conductor ends, layers of self-amalgamating rubber and vinyl electrical tape (or heat-shrink insulating tubing) to rebuild the insulation and voltage-grading layers around the splice, and a copper braid or mesh sleeve to restore electrical continuity of the cable's shield across the joint. The completed splice is then encapsulated in a moulded, resin-filled splice kit body or a heat-shrink outer sleeve, forming a continuous, void-free seal rated for direct burial and continuous submersion. Some designs use a pre-moulded rubber or silicone splice sleeve that stretches over the prepared joint, relying on its own elastomeric memory to maintain a watertight, high-dielectric-strength seal without a resin pour. Stress cones or grading layers are built into the kit to control the electric field at the point where the shield is interrupted, preventing partial discharge and premature insulation failure at the splice.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Improper Crimping or Compression

Under‑crimping or using the wrong die can result in a high‑resistance joint that overheats. Always use the correct crimping tool and die set, and perform a pull‑test to verify the connection. Follow the manufacturer's crimp specifications.

Inadequate Insulation Restoration

If the insulation is not properly restored (e.g., incomplete heat‑shrink or resin voids), the joint may fail due to partial discharge or moisture ingress. Ensure the joint is clean, dry, and that the insulation layers are applied without air gaps.

Screen Continuity Failure

If the metallic screen is not properly reconnected, the cable's shielding effectiveness is lost, leading to EMI issues. Use the screen connector provided in the kit and ensure the screen is bonded to earth at the splice (usually at one end).

Moisture Ingress at Splice

If the splice is not properly sealed, moisture can enter and cause corrosion of the conductors and screen. For buried splices, use resin‑filled or heat‑shrink kits with adhesive liner, and ensure the splice is installed in a dry environment.

Mechanical Damage During Installation

The splice can be damaged by sharp edges in the trench, excessive pulling tension, or impact during backfilling. Protect the splice with a mechanical cover or place it on a bed of sand, and avoid placing heavy objects directly on the splice.

Incorrect Cable Preparation

Stripping the cable jacket and insulation incorrectly (e.g., nicking the conductor, leaving screen fragments) can lead to premature failure. Use the correct stripping tools and follow the manufacturer's strip length instructions.

Failure to Test After Installation

Not performing insulation resistance and continuity tests after splicing can leave undetected faults. Always test the splice with a 5000V megger and a continuity tester before backfilling.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Splice Runs Hot or Shows High Resistance
Likely Cause
Under-crimping or the wrong crimp die used during installation, producing a high-resistance connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure resistance across the splice and compare against an equivalent length of sound cable; if accessible, inspect the crimp for correct die profile.
Corrective Action
Cut out and remake the splice using the correct crimping tool and die set, then verify with a pull-test and resistance check.
Insulation Resistance Test Fails at the Splice
Likely Cause
Incomplete insulation restoration, such as an incomplete heat-shrink seal or a void in the resin fill.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger-test the splice at 5000V; if accessible, visually inspect for incomplete shrink, air gaps, or resin voids.
Corrective Action
Remake the splice with a fresh kit, ensuring the joint is clean and dry and the insulation is applied without air gaps, then re-test before backfilling.
Screen Continuity Lost Through the Splice
Likely Cause
The metallic screen was not properly reconnected using the kit's screen connector.
Diagnostic Steps
Test screen continuity across the splice; if accessible, inspect the screen connector for a missed or loose connection.
Corrective Action
Remake the screen connection using the kit's screen connector, and confirm it is bonded to earth at the correct end before re-sealing.
Moisture Found Inside the Splice Housing
Likely Cause
The splice was not properly sealed, or was installed in wet conditions without a resin-filled or adhesive-lined heat-shrink kit.
Diagnostic Steps
Excavate and open the splice housing to check for moisture or corrosion on the conductors and screen.
Corrective Action
Dry and remake the splice using a resin-filled or adhesive-lined kit appropriate for buried service, installed in dry conditions.
Splice Physically Damaged After Backfill
Likely Cause
Sharp edges in the trench, excessive pulling tension, or impact from backfilling equipment damaged the splice.
Diagnostic Steps
Excavate carefully to inspect the splice housing for cracking or crushing; test insulation resistance and continuity.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged splice, install a mechanical protective cover or bed of sand, and avoid placing heavy objects directly on the splice during backfill.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The splice joins primary cable carrying constant-current series loop power that can reach several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the cable before cutting, splicing, or handling the joint.

Confirm the Correct Cable Before Cutting
Operational

Positively identify the correct cable and circuit before cutting into it to make a repair or tap-off, since cutting into the wrong run can leave live conductors exposed while the intended circuit stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After making a splice, megger test the affected cable run before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the loop.

Correct Compression and Screen Continuity
Compliance

Use the correct die size and crimp/compression tooling for the connector and conductor size, and reconnect the cable screen where the cable is shielded, since a poor crimp or a broken screen continuity can cause a high-resistance joint or lost EMI protection.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when making or working near a series-circuit splice, and confirm the screen and any shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Seal to IP68 Before Burial
Electrical Safety

Complete the full insulation and IP68 sealing process (heat-shrink, resin, or moulded kit as specified) before burying or reinstating the joint; moisture ingress at a splice is one of the most common causes of an eventual ground fault on the series loop.

FOD Control at the Excavation
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and offcuts accounted for at all times when a splice location is exposed for work, and reinstate the surrounding ground or duct before leaving the area, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a splice unsealed, uninsulated, or with a residual insulation fault after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and correct burial depth before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Compression Tool Calibration Drifts Silently
Installation

A hydraulic crimping tool that's slightly out of calibration can still produce a splice that looks correctly formed while delivering insufficient compression for a reliable long-term connection. Include crimp tool calibration verification in the routine tooling maintenance schedule, not just when a splice failure prompts an investigation.

An IP68 Rating Only Holds If Every Interface Is Correct
Sealing

The overall seal rating of a splice is only as good as its weakest interface; a correctly sealed connector body with a poorly prepared cable jacket entry point still lets moisture in over time. Inspect and prep every sealing surface to spec rather than treating the kit's rating as a guarantee independent of workmanship.

Screen Continuity Failures Often Trace Back to the Splice
Troubleshooting

When a loop shows a screen continuity fault, the joint or splice location is one of the most likely points of failure, since it's the one place the screen is physically interrupted and reformed. Check splice locations first when troubleshooting a screen fault before assuming damage elsewhere along the run.

Mixed Conductor Sizes Need the Right Connector, Not a Workaround
Design

Splicing between cables of different conductor sizes with a connector rated for a single size range, using packing or improvisation to make it fit, is a common source of a marginal connection that passes an initial test but fails prematurely. Use a connector rated for the actual size range present, or replace the mismatched cable section instead.

Backfill Material Choice Affects Splice Longevity
Durability

Backfilling directly with sharp or rocky excavated material around a splice can abrade or puncture the outer protection over time as ground settles and shifts. Use appropriate bedding material immediately around the splice even where it adds a step to the excavation work.

Test Immediately After Sealing, Not Just Before
Compliance

An insulation test performed before the splice is fully sealed and backfilled can miss a problem introduced during the final sealing or burial steps. Where practical, re-verify insulation resistance after the splice is complete and before covering it, not only at the intermediate compression stage.
