High-Intensity Runway Centreline Light

A precision visual guidance aid with enhanced luminous output, installed along the runway centreline for CAT II/III low-visibility operations. Features the same colour coding (white, alternating red/white, red) as standard centreline lights but with significantly higher intensity for reliable visibility in fog, rain, and snow.

Overview

What Is It?

A High-Intensity Runway Centreline Light is a flush-mounted inset light installed along the exact centre of the runway, designed specifically for CAT II and CAT III precision approach operations. These lights provide the same longitudinal guidance as standard centreline lights but with a significantly higher luminous intensity (typically 2x to 3x higher) to ensure visibility in very low visibility conditions (RVR as low as 75 metres for CAT IIIB). The colour coding is identical: white lights from the threshold to 900 metres from the runway end, alternating red and white from 900 to 300 metres, and all red from 300 metres to the runway end. This colour transition gives pilots a visual cue of the remaining runway length, even in the most challenging weather conditions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

This fixture exists to solve a specific problem: at RVR 75 m, the slant visual range available to a pilot on the runway is barely longer than the aircraft itself, so any light that isn't intense enough simply won't be seen in time to be useful. The higher luminous output is not a general upgrade for its own sake — it is what keeps the centreline visible at the exact distance and contrast level a CAT IIIB landing or rollout demands, when a standard centreline light would already have fallen below what the eye can pick out through fog. The white-to-red colour transition then carries the added burden of telling the pilot how much runway remains using colour alone, since at that visibility there is effectively no other way to judge remaining distance visually. In this way the fixture's purpose is inseparable from the specific low-visibility regime it is built for: it is the difference between centreline guidance that works down to CAT I minima and guidance that continues to work when there is almost nothing else to see by.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed exclusively on CAT II and CAT III precision approach runways where low-visibility operations are conducted. The lights are spaced at approximately 15-metre (50 ft) intervals, the ICAO/FAA centreline spacing for CAT II/III (30 m is accepted on lower-category runways), giving the dense visual cue pattern low-visibility guidance needs. They are used in conjunction with high-intensity runway edge lights, touchdown zone lights, and approach lighting systems (ALSF-2) to provide comprehensive visual guidance during all phases of the approach and landing in RVR conditions down to 75 metres (CAT IIIB).

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are recessed into the runway pavement along the centreline at the 15 m (50 ft) spacing required for CAT II/III operations, using a load-rated inset base can set flush with the surface to withstand full aircraft wheel loads at the runway's declared load classification. Because these fixtures sit on the primary runway surface, setting-out is tied directly to the runway centreline survey used for the associated ALSF-2 and touchdown zone lighting, with base can position checked against pavement joints and the runway's grooving/friction-course pattern so the fixture doesn't compromise surface drainage; cabling runs in duct beneath the pavement back to isolating transformers on the runway centreline series circuit.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each high-intensity centreline light is powered from the constant current series circuit via an isolating transformer. The lights use a bi-directional optical system with high-output LEDs or halogen lamps to project a narrow, high-intensity beam (typically 4° vertical x 6° horizontal) both ahead and behind the fixture, ensuring visibility to pilots on approach and during roll-out. The colour transition is achieved using precision dichroic filters or coloured lenses. The intensity is controlled by the CCR's current steps, with the highest intensity step (6.6A) delivering up to 20,000 cd or more to ensure visibility in the lowest RVR conditions. The fixtures are designed with advanced thermal management to handle the higher power dissipation of high-intensity operation.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

High-intensity centreline lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit, which on precision approach runways is typically sectionalised so the ALCMS can command different current steps (or switch sections off entirely) as an aircraft progresses down the runway during roll-out, rather than running the full centreline row at one fixed setting throughout. The ALCMS, driven by tower instruction or an automated RVR-linked routine, selects the required brightness step for each active section; this command passes to the CCR, which regulates that section of the loop to the corresponding fixed current, up to 6.6A for the lowest RVR conditions the fixture is rated to serve. Every fixture within an active section, via its own isolating transformer, draws the same current and burns at the same intensity simultaneously — the colour transition along the centreline (white, alternating red/white, solid red) is fixed by each fixture's dichroic filtering at installation, not by anything the CCR commands, so loop-level current control and the fixed colour-zone layout operate independently of one another. Fault detection remains loop-based: the CCR monitors loop voltage against commanded current, and a voltage rise — typically an open-circuit lamp or failed isolating transformer — is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault pending sectionalised field testing to isolate the exact fixture.

Colour Zones & the Bi-Directional Beam

Two design features work together at CAT II/III intensities: the three-zone colour coding (white, alternating red/white, then solid red) lets a pilot read remaining runway length even in RVR down to 75 m, while each fixture's bi-directional optics project a beam both toward the approaching aircraft and back toward one on roll-out — so the same 15 m-spaced row serves both landing and taxi-out guidance without needing separate fixtures for each direction.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is a die-cast aluminium housing set flush into an L-868 base can rated for 100,000kg wheel loading, with a bi-directional lens assembly — one high-output LED array or 100W halogen lamp viewed through separately masked white, red/white, and red zones depending on the fixture's position along the runway. The lens is glass or high-strength polycarbonate, gasket-sealed to the housing for an IP67/IP68 submersible, pressure-vented rating that copes with the hydraulic loading of aircraft tyres passing directly overhead. Internal wiring runs to an L-823 primary connector mating to the isolating transformer's secondary leads in its own base can, with stainless-steel fasteners and a drainage provision preventing water pooling at the cable entries — the same base-can construction as the standard high-intensity runway light, just built to the higher output and pressure-venting spec that CAT II/III precision runways require.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Thermal Overload of LED Modules

High-intensity centreline lights dissipate significant heat, especially in hot climates. Inadequate thermal management can lead to LED junction temperature exceeding limits, causing premature lumen depreciation or complete failure. Regular inspection of heat sinks and thermal pads is essential, and active cooling (such as heat pipes) may be required for some designs.

Optical Degradation from High Luminous Flux

The high luminous flux output accelerates the degradation of optical components (prisms, lenses, and dichroic filters). UV radiation and thermal stress can cause yellowing or crazing of the optical surfaces, reducing light output and altering beam shape. Regular photometric testing and replacement of optical assemblies at recommended intervals is critical.

Colour Filter Fading in Red Zone

The red filters used in the last 300 metres of the runway are subjected to high thermal and UV stress due to the high-intensity operation. Over time, the red colour can fade or shift toward orange, compromising the colour coding that pilots rely on for distance estimation. Regular photometric testing and filter replacement are essential to maintain colour fidelity.

CCR Current Ripple Effects

High-intensity LED centreline lights are sensitive to current ripple from the CCR. Excessive ripple can cause visible flickering, which is particularly distracting in low-visibility conditions. Ensuring that the CCR output meets the ripple specifications (typically < 5% for LED loads) is critical for stable light output.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

LED Module Runs Excessively Hot or Degrades Rapidly
Likely Cause
Inadequate heat sink performance or degraded thermal pads under sustained high-intensity operation, especially in hot climates.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure LED junction/case temperature under normal load against the rated maximum; inspect heat sinks and thermal pads for contamination or degradation; check whether an active-cooling heat pipe (if fitted) is functioning correctly.
Corrective Action
Clean and restore heat sink performance, replace degraded thermal pads, and service or replace a faulty heat pipe if active cooling is part of the design.
Beam Output Reduced or Distorted at High Flux
Likely Cause
UV radiation and thermal stress have caused yellowing or crazing of prisms, lenses, or dichroic filters.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity and beam-pattern check against the applicable standard; visually inspect optical assemblies for yellowing, haze, or surface crazing.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded optical assemblies at the recommended interval and re-verify photometric compliance with a follow-up test.
Red Zone Lights Appear Orange or Faded
Likely Cause
Red colour filters in the final 300 metres have faded from high thermal and UV stress specific to high-intensity operation.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour check on red-zone fixtures against the applicable chromaticity requirement; compare filter appearance to a new reference filter.
Corrective Action
Replace faded red filters and re-verify colour fidelity; consider shortening the inspection interval for fixtures in this zone.
Visible Flicker, Especially in Low Visibility
Likely Cause
Excessive current ripple from the CCR exceeding the LED load's tolerance (typically above ~5%).
Diagnostic Steps
Measure CCR output current ripple under normal operation; compare against the LED load's ripple specification; check for a failing CCR filter stage.
Corrective Action
Service or replace the CCR filtering stage to bring ripple back within specification, and re-verify with a visual and instrumented flicker check.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the runway centreline circuit and verify zero energy at the fixture before opening the inset base can or disturbing connectors.

LVP / CAT II-III Coordination
Operational

These lights are integral to CAT II/III minima; a degraded or dark fixture can force suspension of low-visibility procedures, so coordinate with ATC, raise a NOTAM, and complete any work before low-visibility operations resume.

Correct Colour-Zone Lamp Installation
Compliance

Confirm the replacement lamp or LED module matches the fixture's zone (white, alternating red/white, or all-red) before closing up; an incorrect colour miscommunicates remaining runway length to a pilot in near-zero visibility.

Hot Lamp / Housing Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the fixture to cool before handling after an active period; high-intensity lamp assemblies retain significant heat during lens or lamp replacement.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for while the base can is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture on the runway surface.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage other fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE on series-circuit connections, and confirm the base can is bonded/grounded before reconnecting the loop.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Colour-Zone Boundaries Are a System Property, Not Per-Fixture
Compliance

The transition points between white, alternating red/white, and all-red zones are set by distance-to-go, so a single mis-zoned lamp doesn't just look wrong locally, it shifts the perceived remaining-runway cue for that whole stretch. Keep an as-built zone map on hand during relamping.

High-Drive LEDs Shorten Their Own Service Life If Under-Cooled
Durability

CAT II/III intensity levels often mean the LED module runs near its thermal limit; a fixture reinstalled with a damaged or missing heat-sink gasket path can run hotter than spec and degrade faster even though it passes an initial intensity check.

Flush Fit Matters as Much as Photometrics
Mechanical

An inset fixture sitting even slightly proud of grade becomes a snag or FOD risk for aircraft tyres well before it fails photometrically; verify flush seating against the surrounding runway grade at every reinstall, not just light output.

Base Can Seal Is the Long-Term Failure Point on Inset Fixtures
Durability

Standing water and de-icing fluid pooling in a recessed base can degrade gaskets and cable glands faster than sun and weather alone; specify seal materials rated for glycol contact and inspect gasket condition at overhaul, not only at initial install.

Trend Loop Readings, Don't Just Spot-Check
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at an L-823 connector usually shows up first as a slow drift in loop insulation resistance, not a hard fault. Logging megger readings over successive maintenance cycles catches a failing splice months before it takes the loop down.

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven brightness runway-to-runway even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about high-intensity runway centreline lights: layout, colour zones, intensity, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a high-intensity runway centreline light?

A high-intensity runway centreline light is a flush-mounted inset fixture installed along the exact centre of the runway for CAT II and CAT III precision approach operations. It gives the same longitudinal guidance as a standard centreline light but at much higher luminous intensity, and its bi-directional optics serve aircraft landing and rolling out.

Q2Why do CAT II/III runways need high-intensity centreline lights?

In very low visibility, such as an RVR of 75 m, a pilot's slant visual range on the runway is barely longer than the aircraft itself. Lower-intensity lights would not be seen in time to be useful, so the higher output is what keeps the centreline usable for roll-out and take-off.

Q3What are the colour zones of runway centreline lights?

The centreline is white from the threshold until 900 m before the runway end, alternating red and white from 900 m to 300 m before the end, and solid red for the last 300 m. This lets a pilot judge remaining runway length without consulting an instrument.

Q4How far apart are runway centreline lights spaced?

ICAO Annex 14 and FAA guidance place centreline lights at approximately 15 m (50 ft) intervals, with 30 m accepted on runways intended for lower-category operations. Confirm the exact spacing against your regulator's requirements and the aerodrome's certification basis.

Q5How bright is a high-intensity runway centreline light?

Typical output is up to 20,000 cd for LED and 15,000 cd for halogen at 6.6 A. The CCR steps the series circuit through 2.8 A, 3.4 A, 4.1 A, 5.2 A and 6.6 A, giving intensity steps of 100%, 30%, 10%, 3% and 1% of rated peak, selectable by RVR.

Q6Which standards apply to high-intensity runway centreline lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (fixture specification), IEC TS 61827 (inset and elevated aerodrome luminaires) and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q7How often should high-intensity centreline lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are monthly lens cleaning to remove tyre rubber, semi-annual thermal performance and red-zone filter checks, and annual photometric output and CCR output waveform checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q8What are the common faults on high-intensity centreline lights?

The usual problems are thermal overload of LED modules, optical degradation from high luminous flux, fading of the red filters in the last 300 m, and flicker caused by CCR current ripple. For LED loads, ripple should typically stay below 5%.
