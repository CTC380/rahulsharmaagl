Approach Lighting System (Simple / Precision CAT I / II-III)

What It Provides

Approach lighting systems provide the principal visual transition between the instrument approach environment and the runway visual environment. Visual references extending outward from the threshold help pilots identify:

  • Runway alignment and approach direction
  • Horizontal reference and roll attitude
  • Threshold location and runway orientation
  • Visual transition during landing

What We Support

Precision AGL Solutions supports selection, engineering, supply and integration of simple and configured systems, including ICAO-style simple approach arrangements and FAA families:

  • MALS, MALSF, MALSR
  • ALSF-1, ALSF-2
  • SSALS / SSALF where applicable
  • Other authority-approved configurations

Select by approach category, runway configuration, applicable authority, approved layout, visibility requirements and project specification.

What Is an Approach Lighting System?

A Coordinated Field of Visual Aids Before the Threshold

Located Along the Extended Centreline

An ALS is a coordinated field of visual aids before the runway threshold, along and around the extended runway centreline. It may contain:

  • Approach centreline lights
  • Crossbar and side-row lights
  • Threshold lights
  • Sequenced flashing lights and RAIL
  • Supporting structures
  • Electrical circuits, CCRs / power supplies
  • Control and monitoring equipment

A Complete Engineered System

The exact combination depends on the system type. An ALS should be treated as a complete engineered system, not a collection of unrelated light fixtures.

This avoids a common procurement error: specifying a generic “approach light” without identifying the actual system configuration.

SALS — Simple Approach Lighting System

ICAO-Style Simple Approach Arrangement

Context

In FAA usage SALS and SALSF mean Short Approach Lighting System (with sequenced flashers), whereas ICAO uses “simple approach lighting system”; confirm which meaning your specification uses. Where SALS describes a Simple Approach Lighting System, it refers to the simpler arrangement in ICAO-style standards, associated with:

  • Non-instrument runways
  • Non-precision approach runways
  • Simple visual approach guidance

ICAO material describes a row of lights on the extended runway centreline with a crossbar 300 m from the threshold. Current ICAO-derived provisions specify a minimum practicable length of 420 m, with an 18 m or 30 m crossbar at 300 m. Confirm against national implementation and project specification.

Centreline & Crossbar

Centreline: individual point-source lights, barrettes, fixed light bars or system-specific modular fixtures. ICAO-derived guidance permits a single source or a barrette of at least 3 m, with point-source lights in a barrette spaced at not more than 1.5 m.

Crossbar: the primary transverse reference. It should be straight, horizontal as far as practicable, at right angles to the centreline and bisected by it.

MALS & MALSF

FAA Medium-Intensity Approach Lighting

MALS

FAA identifies MALS as an economy approach-lighting aid for non-precision approaches, giving enhanced visual recognition of the runway end. AC 150/5340-30J lists it among the economy systems.

  • Green threshold lights
  • White steady-burning centreline light bars
  • Seven five-light centreline bars at 200 ft intervals, from 200 ft to 1,400 ft
  • Two additional five-light bars at the 1,000 ft station, forming a crossbar effect
  • Supporting approach-light structure

The MALS steady-burning field is the same as that of MALSR; MALSR adds the RAIL flashers beyond it. Verify dimensions against the applicable FAA drawing.

MALSF — MALS With Sequenced Flashers

A MALS configuration supplemented by three sequenced flashers. The flashers give a dynamic effect that improves identification of the approach-light field where surrounding lights make a steady-burning system harder to distinguish.

  • Green threshold lights
  • White steady-burning light bars
  • Three sequenced flashers in the outer portion
  • Seven centreline bars at 200 ft (approximately 61 m) intervals

Installation dimensions and tolerances follow the current approved FAA drawing.

MALSR

MALS With Runway Alignment Indicator Lights

FAA Category I Standard

FAA identifies MALSR as an economy system used as the FAA standard for Category I precision runways.

  • 18–33 green steady-burning threshold lights, depending on runway width
  • Nine steady-burning light bars
  • Typically five sequenced flashing lights (RAIL), up to eight, starting 1,600 ft from the threshold
  • 200 ft spacing for principal bars and flashers

MALSR and RAIL

The Runway Alignment Indicator Light (RAIL) gives a dynamic indication along the approach axis. FAA defines RAIL as sequenced flashing lights installed in combination with other light systems.

Treat RAIL as part of the complete MALSR system, not a generic flashing-light product.

ALSF-1 & ALSF-2

FAA High-Intensity Approach Lighting

ALSF-1 — Category I

Approach Light System with Sequenced Flashers in a Category I configuration, identified by FAA separately from ALSF-2, MALS, MALSF and MALSR. It gives a much more extensive field than an economy MALS.

  • Green threshold lights
  • White steady-burning centreline lights and light bars
  • Crossbar and red terminating-bar elements (the red side-row lights belong to ALSF-2)
  • Sequenced flashers
  • Supporting structures, control and power infrastructure

Layout comes from the applicable FAA system drawing.

ALSF-2 — Category II / III

Designed for Category II/III instrument approaches, giving information on runway alignment, height perception, roll guidance and horizontal reference.

Typical FAA scope:

  • 49 green threshold lights
  • Red side-row lighting
  • 144 high-intensity white steady-burning lights
  • 15 sequenced flashers on a typical 2,400 ft system (up to 21 on a 3,000 ft system)
  • 100 ft spacing between principal light stations

Flashers run sequentially from the outermost station toward the threshold, creating an apparent moving light. These numbers describe the FAA typical configuration and should not be applied automatically to non-FAA projects.

ICAO precision approach (Category I): a row of centreline lights extending, wherever possible, 900 m from the threshold, with a 30 m crossbar at 300 m. Category II/III systems add side-row barrettes and further crossbars in the inner portion. Confirm the arrangement in the applicable Annex 14 edition.

Approach-Light Plane

FAA MALSF guidance identifies the optimum position as a horizontal plane at runway-end elevation, with permitted longitudinal slope adjustments for obstacles and supports, and beam aiming toward an assumed 3° glide path.

This affects light elevation, support height, vertical alignment, beam aiming, terrain and obstructions. The project survey controls final installation.

FAQ

Approach Lighting System — Frequently Asked Questions

What is an approach lighting system?

It is a coordinated field of visual aids before the runway threshold, along and around the extended runway centreline, giving the visual transition between the instrument approach and the runway environment. It may contain centreline lights, crossbars, side rows, threshold lights, sequenced flashers and RAIL, plus supports, circuits, CCRs, control and monitoring, and should be treated as a complete engineered system.

What do SALS, MALS, MALSF, MALSR, ALSF-1 and ALSF-2 mean?

They are not interchangeable terms. SALS is an ICAO-style simple concept (in FAA usage SALS means Short Approach Lighting System, so confirm which your specification uses), MALS is an FAA medium-intensity economy system, MALSF adds three sequenced flashers, MALSR adds RAIL and is the FAA standard for Category I precision runways, ALSF-1 is high-intensity Category I, and ALSF-2 is high-intensity Category II/III.

What is a simple approach lighting system under ICAO?

A row of lights on the extended runway centreline with a crossbar 300 m from the threshold. Current ICAO-derived provisions specify a minimum practicable length of 420 m, with an 18 m or 30 m crossbar at 300 m. Confirm against the national implementation and project specification.

What does a MALSR consist of?

18 to 33 green steady-burning threshold lights depending on runway width, nine steady-burning light bars, and typically five sequenced flashing lights (RAIL), up to eight, starting 1,600 ft from the threshold, with 200 ft spacing for principal bars and flashers. The steady-burning field is the same as MALS, and MALSR adds the RAIL flashers beyond it.

What is RAIL?

The Runway Alignment Indicator Light is a set of sequenced flashing lights installed in combination with other light systems, giving a dynamic indication along the approach axis. Treat it as part of the complete MALSR system, not a generic flashing-light product.

What does an ALSF-2 include?

A typical FAA ALSF-2 includes 49 green threshold lights, red side-row lighting, 144 high-intensity white steady-burning lights and 15 sequenced flashers on a typical 2,400 ft system (up to 21 on a 3,000 ft system), with 100 ft spacing between principal light stations. These numbers describe the FAA typical configuration and should not be applied automatically to non-FAA projects.

How does an ICAO precision approach lighting system compare?

ICAO precision approach (Category I) has a row of centreline lights extending, wherever possible, 900 m from the threshold, with a 30 m crossbar at 300 m. Category II/III systems add side-row barrettes and further crossbars in the inner portion. Confirm the arrangement in the applicable Annex 14 edition.

How should an approach lighting system be selected?

Begin with the runway and approach requirement, not the product: identify the runway, approach category, authority (ICAO, FAA, DGCA or contract specification), then select the system and develop the layout of centreline, crossbars, side rows, threshold and flashers, then the electrical system and fixtures. See the approach centreline light and approach threshold light pages for the components.

What site and structural factors matter?

Terrain, roads, drainage, buildings, trees, ILS/MLS equipment, utilities and available land. FAA MALSF guidance identifies minimum land of about 1,600 ft by 400 ft for its typical installation. Supports must suit height, wind loading, foundations and frangibility, and the approach-light plane affects elevation, vertical alignment and beam aiming toward an assumed 3° glide path.

How is the system controlled and powered?

Through a CCR or power supply, approach lighting circuit, transformer or driver and fixtures, with a control system, sequencer and flasher controller. Systems may operate at low, medium and high intensity with multiple steps, and fixtures must suit the actual CCR and control configuration. Interfaces can include the AGL control panel, ALCMS and the ATC interface.

What are common faults?

An entire system off (power, CCR, control command or primary circuit failure), a centreline section off (circuit, transformer, cable, connector or fixture), uneven crossbars, an incomplete side row, flashers not operating (sequencer, flasher controller or channel failure), a wrong sequence from wiring or channel configuration errors, and incorrect visual geometry from survey error or support displacement.

What data is needed before a configuration can be recommended?

The runway designation, length, width, threshold coordinates and elevation, approach category, instrument procedure and RVR minima, ILS or other navigation, existing approach lighting, available land, terrain, obstructions, drainage, utilities, existing CCRs, cables, fixtures and transformers, the control system and ALCMS, the BOQ or technical specification, and authority and certification requirements.

Engineering Note

An approach lighting system should be selected as a complete operational configuration, not assembled by combining individual products. SALS/simple approach, FAA MALS, MALSF, MALSR and ALSF configurations differ in purpose, approach category, light population, spacing, intensity, optical geometry, flashing/RAIL functions and electrical/control architecture.

FAA currently identifies MALS, MALSF, MALSR, ALSF-1 and ALSF-2 as separate system types. For example, MALSR has a green threshold array, nine steady-burning white light bars and five sequenced flashers, while a typical ALSF-2 uses a much larger field with green threshold lights, red side rows, white steady-burning lights and 15 sequenced flashers (up to 21 on a 3,000 ft system).

Select from the approved approach procedure, applicable aviation authority standard, runway/site constraints and project technical specification, with all fixtures, supports, power, control and monitoring engineered as one system.
