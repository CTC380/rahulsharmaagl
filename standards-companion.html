DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I — Aerodrome Design & Operations

India's national aerodrome design standard, issued under the Aircraft Rules, 1937

DGCA Mandatory — the controlling national requirement 🟡 Verify current revision
Clause / Section: Mirrors ICAO Annex 14, Vol. I structure (Physical Characteristics, Visual Aids, Electrical Systems chapters)
Edition: Current published edition — confirm revision/amendment number with DGCA; a draft amendment (definitions, apron ground-handling criteria) was issued 4 Oct 2025, comments closed 3 Nov 2025 and had not been confirmed incorporated at last check
Requirement Type: Mandatory — Level 1 in the standards hierarchy above
Verification Status: 🟡 Summary – Confirm Current Edition (last checked for newer revisions: Sep 2026)

India's actual controlling aerodrome design requirement — not ICAO Annex 14 itself — substantially adopting Annex 14, Volume I provisions (including AGL spacing, colour, siting and obstacle-lighting criteria) as binding domestic requirements.

Standard

DGCA Civil Aviation Requirements, Section 4 – Aerodromes, Series B, Part I – Aerodrome Design & OperationsIssued under Rule 90 of the Aircraft Rules, 1937; a draft amendment covering definitions and apron ground-handling criteria was issued 4 Oct 2025

Requirement / Summary

Sets out India's national physical, visual-aid and electrical-system requirements for aerodrome design, substantially mirroring ICAO Annex 14, Volume I provisions as binding domestic requirements enforceable by DGCA.

Practical Meaning

For an Indian aerodrome project, this CAR — not Annex 14 directly — is the actual Level 1 controlling document in the hierarchy above. Where India has filed a difference against an Annex 14 provision, the CAR text governs domestically, not the ICAO text.

Field Application

Confirm project compliance against the current CAR text and any open draft amendments rather than assuming Annex 14 compliance alone satisfies DGCA requirements — cite the CAR paragraph, not just the Annex 14 clause, in Indian compliance documentation.

Reference: DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I
Source: Directorate General of Civil Aviation, Government of India — this page is a summary, not the standard itself. Confirm current revision and any published amendments directly with DGCA (dgca.gov.in) before specifying.

Volume I, Aerodrome Design and Operations

ICAO Mandatory 📘 Annex 14, 9th Ed. (2022)
Clause / Section: Chapter 5
Edition: 9th Edition, July 2022 (supersedes the 8th Ed. as of 3 Nov 2022) — not yet re-checked against later amendments
Requirement Type: Design criterion
Verification Status: 🔵 Summarized – ICAO Annex 14, 9th Edition (2022)

Edge lights sit no more than 60m apart — the ceiling that keeps the light row looking continuous to a pilot's eye at approach speed.

Standard

ICAO Annex 14, Chapter 5 — Visual Aids for NavigationClause reference: verify exact numbering against the 9th Edition (numbering has shifted across recent editions)

Requirement / Summary

Runway edge lights shall be spaced at intervals of not more than 60m.

Practical Meaning

The 60m ceiling sets the maximum gap a pilot's eye will see between light points at typical approach speeds — closer spacing is permitted, wider is not.

Field Application

Verify spacing during design review and again at as-built survey; a common installation error is exceeding spacing on curved or non-standard-length runways.

FAA AC 150/5345-10H — Constant Current Regulators & Regulator Monitors

Constant Current Regulator equipment specification

FAA Equipment specification / Test standard 🟢 Current
Clause / Section: Chapter 3 (Equipment Requirements), Chapter 4 (Qualification Testing)
Edition: Revision H (supersedes revision dated 29 Sep 2010) — confirm no later revision
Requirement Type: Equipment specification / Test standard
Verification Status: 🔵 Verified – Controlled Technical Source

The current FAA CCR/regulator-monitor spec — qualification testing includes humidity cycling (five 48-hour cycles at up to 55°C) followed by CCR input voltage tests, in addition to output regulation accuracy.

Standard

FAA AC 150/5345-10H, Specification for Constant Current Regulators and Regulator MonitorsRevision H — Table 1 updated for CCR conformance testing; para 3.3.2 updated on maximum operating point

Requirement / Summary

Output current must be held within the tolerance specified in Chapter 3 across the full connected load range, and the CCR/monitor assembly must pass the Chapter 4 qualification tests, including environmental cycling (5 × 48-hour humidity cycles, max chamber temp 131°F/55°C) followed by input voltage testing per para 4.2.1.1.

Practical Meaning

The humidity-cycling requirement exists because CCR cabinets sit in outdoor equipment enclosures for decades — a unit that only passes a bench test at room conditions can still fail in the field from condensation-driven insulation breakdown.

Field Application

When reviewing a CCR type-test report, confirm it references revision H specifically (not the superseded 2010 edition) and that the humidity-cycling sequence is documented as five distinct 48-hour cycles, not a single extended soak.

FAA AC 150/5345-47C — Series to Series Isolation Transformers

L-830 (60Hz) / L-831 (50Hz) isolation transformer specification

FAA Equipment specification / Test standard 🟢 Current
Clause / Section: Table 1 (transformer types), hi-pot / insulation spec
Edition: Revision C, issued 22 Jul 2011 — cancels 47B (30 Jun 2005)
Requirement Type: Mandatory for AIP/PFC funded work
Verification Status: 🔵 Verified – Controlled Technical Source

Covers L-830 (60Hz) and L-831 (50Hz) series isolation transformers, encapsulated in a watertight case; transformers must withstand a specified hi-pot test between primary and secondary windings (confirm exact test voltage/duration against your copy).

Standard

FAA AC 150/5345-47C, Specification for Series to Series Isolation Transformers for Airport Lighting SystemsCancels AC 150/5345-47B, dated 30 Jun 2005

Requirement / Summary

Transformers are designated Type L-830 (60Hz operation) or L-831 (50Hz operation), with a numerical suffix identifying wattage/current rating per Table 1. They must consist of electrically separate primary and secondary coils wound on a core, encapsulated in a watertight case with rubber (or equivalent) connectors, and must withstand a specified high-potential (hi-pot) test between primary and secondary windings without breakdown.

Field Application

Hi-pot testing should be part of incoming goods inspection, not assumed from the manufacturer's factory test certificate alone; also confirm the L-830/L-831 frequency designation matches the project's supply frequency before ordering, not just the wattage rating.

Stop bars are unidirectional red lights installed across a taxiway at a runway-holding position, with provision for selective switching and, where used for low-visibility operations, interlocking with the taxiway centre line lights beyond them.

Keyword Reference

Aerodrome Lighting Standards, Requirements & Testing

Airport lighting design standards

Airport lighting design standards set what the lighting system must provide and how it is arranged: which lights are needed, their layout, colours, intensity levels and power supply. Design starts from the aerodrome's operating category and the standards its authority applies, then follows the project specification.

ICAO Annex 14 aerodrome lighting

ICAO Annex 14 aerodrome lighting requirements are the international baseline for runway, taxiway and approach lighting, stop bars, PAPI and signs. They describe what visual aids must do, and States may adopt or adapt them in their own rules. Always use the edition and amendments your authority has adopted.

ICAO airfield lighting standards

ICAO airfield lighting standards are the Standards and Recommended Practices in Annex 14, supported by ICAO design and maintenance manuals. They set the performance and arrangement of the lights, not the product design, so equipment specifications come from other documents.

FAA airfield lighting requirements

FAA airfield lighting requirements are set in advisory circulars on installation and maintenance and in equipment specifications for fixtures, regulators, transformers and cables. They are more product-specific than ICAO, and apply where a project or tender names them.

DGCA India aerodrome lighting

DGCA India aerodrome lighting requirements come from the Civil Aviation Requirements for aerodromes, which adopt ICAO provisions with national differences. Indian aerodrome projects should follow the current CARs together with the airport operator's specification.

BCAS airport lighting requirements

BCAS airport lighting requirements relate to lighting for airport security, such as perimeter and security areas, rather than aviation visual aids. They are separate from DGCA and ICAO rules on AGL. Check the current BCAS circulars for the airport concerned.

Airport lighting inspection standards

Airport lighting inspection standards define what must be checked, how often and how defects are classified and recorded. The aviation authority's rules and the airport's maintenance programme set the schedule, and the manufacturer's instructions add detail.

AGL testing standards

AGL testing standards cover electrical tests such as circuit resistance and insulation, photometric tests of light output, and functional tests of control and monitoring. The test method, instruments and acceptance criteria should be taken from the specified standard and approved test plan.

Airport lighting specification standards

Airport lighting specification standards are the documents a project specification points to for equipment and installation, such as equipment specifications and IEC standards. A specification should name each standard with its edition, so bids can be compared.

Airfield lighting standards comparison

An airfield lighting standards comparison shows how ICAO, FAA, IEC and Indian Standards differ in purpose and detail: ICAO sets the operational requirement, FAA documents specify products and installation, and IEC and IS cover equipment performance and safety. They are often used together on one project.

ICAO vs FAA lighting requirements

ICAO vs FAA lighting requirements differ in structure and wording, and in some colours, intensities and arrangements. Where a project mixes both, the specification must say which one governs each item, so the installed system is consistent.

Runway lighting testing

Runway lighting testing checks that edge, threshold, centreline and touchdown zone lights work correctly after installation and during service. It includes circuit tests, brightness step checks and light output measurements, using the methods in the applicable standard.

Taxiway lighting testing

Taxiway lighting testing covers edge, centreline and stop bar lights: circuit and insulation tests, correct colours and operation, and photometric checks where required. Results are recorded against the approved test plan.

Airfield lighting control system

The airfield lighting control system lets controllers switch circuits and set brightness, and reports the status of regulators and circuits. Standards cover its functions, response and monitoring, and the system should be tested together with the CCRs and the lights.

Q13What is IS 12290 and what does it cover for airport lighting?

IS 12290:1987 is the Bureau of Indian Standards specification for isolating transformers for airport lighting systems. It covers construction, ratings and testing, including insulation resistance and a 20-cycle endurance test, and was prepared with reference to FAA AC 150/5345-47.

Q14What should I check on an IS 12290 isolating transformer test certificate?

Confirm that it reports the 20-cycle test result and both primary and secondary insulation resistance. A certificate showing only a single hi-pot pass/fail is incomplete against this standard.

Q15Which standards apply to apron lighting design?

Apron lighting design is guided by the apron floodlighting provisions of ICAO Annex 14, the equivalent national requirements such as DGCA in India, and the airport's own specification. These set illuminance, uniformity and glare expectations for the apron. Check the current edition for exact values, and see the apron floodlighting design page for how the calculation is done.
