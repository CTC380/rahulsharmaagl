How Often Should Airfield Ground Lighting Photometry Be Performed?

An AGL system can look perfectly healthy — lights on, circuits fine, CCR silent — while its photometric performance quietly falls out of compliance. A practical guide for airport engineers on when photometry should actually be carried out.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

July 21, 2026 Updated October 27, 2026

An Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) system can appear to be functioning normally while its photometric performance is gradually deteriorating. The lights may illuminate. The electrical circuits may be healthy. The Constant Current Regulator (CCR) may operate without alarms. Yet the light reaching the pilot may no longer meet the photometric requirements specified by ICAO Annex 14 or FAA standards.

This is why photometric testing is one of the most important verification activities in airport lighting engineering. It confirms not only that a light works, but that it provides the correct intensity, colour, and beam distribution required for safe aircraft operations. One of the most common questions from airport operators is: how often should AGL photometry actually be carried out? The answer is not a fixed annual interval — it depends on the operational condition of the airfield, maintenance activities, regulatory requirements, and the airport's maintenance strategy.

Key Takeaway

Photometry should be planned according to engineering risk, not a fixed calendar. Electrical operation does not guarantee photometric compliance — the two must be verified separately.

01

There Is No Universal Testing Interval

Unlike routine electrical inspections, ICAO Annex 14 does not prescribe a single mandatory photometric testing frequency applicable to every airport. It does, however, expect preventive maintenance on precision approach runways (CAT II/III) to include field measurement of the intensity, beam spread and orientation of approach and runway lights, and it treats certain lights as unserviceable when their main-beam average intensity falls below 50% of the specified value. Beyond that, airport operators are responsible for ensuring that all visual aids continue to meet the required performance throughout their service life. In India, check the photometric and maintenance provisions of DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I and the conditions of the aerodrome licence, since national rules and the approved maintenance programme can set specific intervals.

This means photometry should be planned according to engineering risk rather than waiting for visible failures. The objective is straightforward:

Verify that the lighting system still delivers the visual performance expected by the pilot.

How often should airport lighting be inspected?

There is no single fixed interval. For photometric inspection, ICAO Annex 14 does not prescribe one mandatory frequency for every airport, so the interval is set by the airport operator as part of its preventive maintenance programme. It depends on traffic volume, environmental conditions, fitting age, LED vs. halogen technology, local procedures and regulatory oversight. In addition, inspect photometrically after new installation, after major maintenance, after realignment of fixtures, and before regulatory audits or operational upgrades. Confirm routine visual and electrical inspection intervals against your own maintenance procedures and regulator.

02

When Should Photometric Testing Be Performed?

Rather than a fixed calendar, photometric testing is triggered by specific events and conditions across the lifecycle of the lighting system:

After New Installation Every newly installed lighting system should undergo photometric verification before being placed into operational service — confirming light intensity, beam distribution, colour, installation alignment, and ICAO/FAA compliance. Commissioning without photometric verification leaves uncertainty about actual performance.
After Major Maintenance Whenever maintenance could influence optical performance — LED module or lamp replacement, prism or optical assembly replacement, fixture refurbishment, driver replacement, major electrical modifications, or optical cleaning after severe contamination. Even small changes can alter beam characteristics enough to affect compliance.
After Realignment of Fixtures If a fitting has been re-levelled, repositioned, reinstalled, or adjusted after pavement work, its beam should be re-verified. A few degrees of misalignment can significantly reduce the light intensity visible to an approaching pilot.
During Periodic Compliance Verification Many airports include scheduled photometric inspections within their preventive maintenance programmes. The aim is to identify degradation before it becomes an operational problem, with the interval set by the factors described earlier and confirmed against the current Annex 14 edition, FAA AC 150/5340-26 and your regulator's requirements.
Before Regulatory Audits or Operational Upgrades Ahead of regulatory inspections, ICAO compliance assessments, airport certification activities, runway category upgrades, or operational acceptance testing. Verifying compliance beforehand reduces the risk of unexpected findings and provides technical evidence of performance.

These are the moments that matter — not a date on a calendar, but a change in condition, configuration, or scrutiny.

03

Why Waiting for a Failure Is the Wrong Strategy

Many lighting systems continue to operate electrically even while their optical performance degrades. Common causes include:

Ageing LEDsGradual lumen depreciation over service life
Reflector DeteriorationReduced reflectivity over time
Prism ContaminationDirt and residue distorting the beam
UV AgeingLens yellowing and material fatigue
Water IngressMoisture affecting optics and electronics
Optical MisalignmentGradual drift in beam direction

Without photometric testing, these issues may remain unnoticed until they affect operational safety or trigger audit observations. By that stage, corrective maintenance becomes more disruptive and expensive.

Electrical operation does not necessarily mean photometric performance is acceptable.

04

The Benefits of Regular Photometry

Routine photometric testing provides much more than a simple pass-or-fail result. It enables airports to:

  • Establish baseline performance after installation
  • Monitor gradual degradation over time
  • Identify underperforming fittings early
  • Verify maintenance quality
  • Support predictive maintenance programmes
  • Reduce unexpected failures and optimise maintenance budgets

Photometric data transforms maintenance decisions from reactive to evidence-based.

05

The Cost of Not Testing

Skipping scheduled photometric verification can lead to a range of operational and financial consequences:

RiskConsequence
Undetected degradationOperational restrictions or reduced runway availability
Reactive maintenanceEmergency maintenance and unexpected fitting replacements
No performance historyIncreased lifecycle costs and non-conformities during audits
No verification dataReduced confidence in the visual guidance system

The cost of a structured photometric inspection programme is generally far lower than the cost of operational disruption caused by undetected performance degradation.

06

Photometry Is an Investment in Safety

Some organisations still view photometric testing as an optional compliance exercise. In reality, it is an essential engineering tool. Electrical tests confirm that the system is powered. Photometric tests confirm that the system performs its intended operational function. Both are equally important for safe aircraft operations.

Photometry is a risk-control measure that helps ensure pilots receive the visual guidance they depend on during every take-off and landing.

When Photometry Should Be Triggered

Event-driven, not calendar-driven

Conclusion

There is no single timetable that applies to every airport. However, the engineering principle is universal: photometry should be performed whenever there is a possibility that lighting performance has changed, and at planned intervals to verify continued compliance.

Airfield lighting is a safety-critical system. Its performance should never be assumed. It should be measured.

  • Perform photometry after installation and commissioning
  • Re-test after major maintenance or fixture replacement
  • Verify performance after alignment adjustments
  • Include periodic photometric inspections in preventive maintenance
  • Confirm compliance before audits and operational upgrades
  • Use photometric data to support predictive maintenance

07

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should AGL photometry be performed?

There is no universal interval. ICAO Annex 14 does not prescribe a single mandatory photometric testing frequency for every airport, so photometry should be planned according to engineering risk and the operator's preventive maintenance programme, not a fixed calendar. Factors include traffic volume, environmental conditions, fitting age, LED or halogen technology, local procedures and regulatory oversight.

Does Annex 14 say anything about photometric checks at all?

Annex 14 expects preventive maintenance on precision approach runways (CAT II/III) to include field measurement of the intensity, beam spread and orientation of approach and runway lights. It also treats certain lights as unserviceable when their main-beam average intensity falls below 50% of the specified value. In India, check the provisions of DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I and the aerodrome licence conditions.

When should photometric testing be triggered?

Testing is event-driven: after new installation and before operational service, after major maintenance that could affect optical performance, after realignment of fixtures, during periodic compliance verification and before regulatory audits or operational upgrades. A change in condition, configuration or scrutiny is the trigger, not a date on a calendar.

Why is waiting for a failure the wrong strategy?

Many lighting systems keep operating electrically while optical performance degrades. Common causes include ageing LEDs, reflector deterioration, prism contamination, UV ageing of lenses, water ingress and optical misalignment. These issues can go unnoticed until they affect safety or trigger audit observations.

Does a lit light with a healthy CCR mean photometry is fine?

No. Electrical operation does not guarantee photometric compliance, and the two must be verified separately. The lights may illuminate and the CCR may run without alarms, yet the light reaching the pilot may no longer meet Annex 14 or FAA requirements.

What are the benefits of regular photometry?

It establishes baseline performance after installation, monitors gradual degradation, identifies underperforming fittings early and verifies maintenance quality. It also supports predictive maintenance and helps reduce unexpected failures and optimise maintenance budgets.

What are the risks of not testing?

Undetected degradation can lead to operational restrictions or reduced runway availability, reactive maintenance leads to emergency fitting replacements, and no performance history increases lifecycle costs and audit non-conformities. The cost of a structured photometric programme is generally far lower than the cost of operational disruption.
