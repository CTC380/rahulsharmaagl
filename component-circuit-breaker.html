Circuit Breaker

An automatically operated electrical switch designed to protect an AGL circuit from damage caused by overload or short circuit. Unlike an isolation switch, a circuit breaker detects a fault condition and interrupts current flow, preventing equipment damage and ensuring the safety of personnel and infrastructure. Circuit breakers are essential components in Airfield Ground Lighting power distribution and control systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Circuit Breaker is an automatically operated electrical switching device that protects an electrical circuit from damage caused by excess current due to overload, short circuit, or ground fault. In Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) systems, circuit breakers are used in both primary (series) and secondary (control and distribution) circuits to protect the CCRs, transformers, cables, and lighting fixtures. Unlike fuses, which must be replaced after a single fault, circuit breakers can be reset (either manually or automatically) after the fault is cleared, making them reusable and more convenient for maintenance.

AGL circuit breakers are typically designed for:

  • Series Lighting Circuits: Used at the CCR output to protect against short circuits in the series loop.
  • Control and Monitoring Circuits: Protecting the ALCMS, PLCs, and communication equipment.
  • Distribution Panels: Protecting feeder cables to various airfield sectors.
Purpose

Purpose & Function

The circuit breaker's purpose, in general, is the same one that its MCB and MCCB variants specialise in at different current ranges: to sense an overload or short circuit and interrupt the faulted circuit automatically before it can damage cables, transformers, CCRs, or lighting fixtures, while allowing that circuit to be safely restored once the fault is cleared. In an AGL series circuit specifically, the breaker at the CCR output exists to protect the entire loop of fixtures and isolating transformers from a short circuit anywhere along its length, converting what could otherwise be a sustained overcurrent event into a brief, contained interruption. Its reusability compared to a fuse is itself part of its purpose — it lets maintenance crews restore power quickly after clearing a fault without needing replacement parts, minimising how long a runway or taxiway section is left without lighting.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Circuit breakers are used throughout the AGL power and control infrastructure:

  • In CCR Panels: Protecting the output of each Constant Current Regulator from short circuits in the series loop.
  • In Distribution Boards: Providing overcurrent protection for sub‑circuits feeding different airfield sectors (runways, taxiways, aprons).
  • In ALCMS Racks: Protecting control and monitoring equipment (PLCs, servers, communication modules).
  • In Lighting Control Panels: Protecting individual lighting circuits and stop bar circuits.
  • In Substations: As the main incoming protection for the AGL power supply.
Placement

Installation & Location

Circuit breakers are DIN-rail or panel-mounted inside CCR cabinets, distribution boards, ALCMS racks, and substation switchgear, positioned so the protected cable enters and leaves the enclosure with a short, well-supported run to the breaker's terminals. Mounting orientation and spacing follow the manufacturer's derating tables, since breakers mounted close together or in a sealed enclosure need extra clearance or ventilation to avoid nuisance tripping from internal heat build-up. Each breaker is labelled to identify the circuit it protects, and its position within the panel is chosen to keep discrimination and access logical — main incomers at the top or one side, feeder breakers grouped by the section of the airfield they serve — so a fault can be traced and isolated quickly.

Function

How Does It Work?

A circuit breaker operates using a combination of thermal and magnetic (or electronic) trip mechanisms:

  • Overload Protection (Thermal): A bimetallic strip heats up and bends when current exceeds the rated value for a sustained period. The bending triggers the trip mechanism, opening the contacts after a time delay that is inversely proportional to the overcurrent (trip time decreases as current increases). This protects against moderate, prolonged overcurrents.
  • Short‑Circuit Protection (Magnetic): A magnetic coil generates a force proportional to the current. Under high fault currents (short circuits), the magnetic force is sufficient to instantly open the contacts, providing near‑instantaneous interruption.
  • Electronic Trip Units: Modern circuit breakers (especially MCCBs) use microprocessor‑based trip units that provide programmable protection curves, including long‑time, short‑time, instantaneous, and ground‑fault protection.
  • Arc Extinction: When the contacts open, an arc is formed. Circuit breakers use arc chutes, de‑ionizing plates, or vacuum/SF6 interrupter chambers to extinguish the arc quickly, ensuring safe interruption.
  • Reset: After a fault is cleared, the circuit breaker can be reset manually (by pushing the handle to the ON position) or automatically (in some remote‑controlled breakers).
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Circuit breakers in an AGL network are arranged in a protection hierarchy rather than operating in isolation: an incomer or MCCB at the distribution board sits above a series of downstream MCBs or MCCBs feeding individual CCRs and circuits, and the trip curves are graded (discrimination) so that the breaker closest to a fault opens before any breaker upstream of it, limiting an outage to the smallest possible section of the network. Where a breaker includes an auxiliary or alarm contact, its open/closed and tripped status can be wired back to a local annunciator panel or, on larger sites, polled by the ALCMS or a building management system, giving remote visibility of which circuit has tripped without a technician having to inspect each panel physically. Breakers without such contacts remain purely local protection devices — their trip is visible only at the breaker handle, and diagnosing which breaker opened after a wider outage is done by physical inspection rather than remote reporting.

Construction

Physical Construction

The breaker's current-carrying path runs through a set of silver-alloy contacts operated by a spring-loaded toggle mechanism, housed within an arc chamber of ceramic or high-temperature thermoset plastic designed to split and extinguish the arc generated when the contacts open under fault current. A bimetallic strip provides thermal overload protection while a solenoid or electromagnetic element delivers instantaneous magnetic tripping on short-circuit currents, both acting on a common trip bar to open the contacts regardless of which mechanism triggers. The MCCB's moulded case, or the MCB's compact insulated housing, is built from flame-retardant, high-dielectric-strength thermoset or thermoplastic material to contain internal arcing and prevent case rupture under maximum fault conditions. External terminals are shrouded to prevent accidental contact, and the assembly snaps onto a standard DIN rail or bolts to a panel via base-mounting holes, with a clear, positive on/off/tripped indicator visible on the operating handle.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Nuisance Tripping

Circuit breakers may trip due to temporary overloads (e.g., inrush current during CCR start‑up) or harmonic currents. Selecting the correct trip curve (C‑curve or D‑curve) and adjusting the setting (if adjustable) is essential to avoid nuisance tripping.

Contact Wear and Arcing

Repeated fault interruptions cause contact erosion and degradation of the arc‑chute. Regular inspection and replacement of breakers after a specified number of fault operations (or at scheduled intervals) is recommended.

Thermal Derating

Circuit breakers are rated at a specific ambient temperature (typically 30°C or 40°C). Higher ambient temperatures (common in outdoor enclosures) reduce the current‑carrying capacity. Derating factors must be applied or higher‑rated breakers selected.

Inadequate Breaking Capacity

If the short‑circuit current at the installation point exceeds the breaker's breaking capacity (Icu), the breaker may fail to interrupt the fault safely, leading to catastrophic failure. Always verify the available fault current and select an appropriate breaker.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Breaker Trips Immediately on Reset
Likely Cause
A persistent downstream short circuit or ground fault, or the breaker's internal trip mechanism has failed in the tripped state.
Diagnostic Steps
Isolate downstream circuits and reset with the load disconnected to confirm whether the breaker holds; megger downstream cabling and equipment to locate a fault.
Corrective Action
Repair the downstream short/ground fault before re-energising, or replace the breaker if it will not hold with all downstream circuits isolated.
Nuisance Tripping Under Normal Load
Likely Cause
Inrush current at CCR start-up or harmonic currents exceeding the breaker's instantaneous trip setting, or an incorrectly selected trip curve.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the trip curve type (B/C/D-curve) against the actual inrush profile; log load current during start-up to compare against the breaker's rating and curve.
Corrective Action
Select a breaker with the correct trip curve for the load profile, or adjust the trip setting (if adjustable) to accommodate normal inrush without compromising protection.
Breaker Fails to Trip Under a Confirmed Fault
Likely Cause
Worn or welded contacts, a degraded arc-chute, or the breaker's rated breaking capacity has been exceeded by the available fault current.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a primary current injection test to verify trip time and threshold against the breaker's rating; inspect contacts and arc-chute condition; verify available fault current against the breaker's Icu rating.
Corrective Action
Replace a breaker that fails injection testing or shows contact/arc-chute wear, and upgrade to a breaker with adequate breaking capacity if the fault level exceeds its rating.
Overheating at Breaker Terminals
Likely Cause
Loose or under-torqued terminal connections, or the breaker is operating above its thermal derating limit for the ambient enclosure temperature.
Diagnostic Steps
Thermal-image the breaker and terminals under load; check terminal torque against the manufacturer's specification; verify ambient enclosure temperature against the breaker's rated derating curve.
Corrective Action
Re-torque terminal connections to specification, and apply appropriate derating or upgrade to a higher-rated breaker if enclosure temperatures exceed the rated ambient.
Breaker Will Not Reset / Mechanically Jammed
Likely Cause
Mechanical damage to the toggle mechanism, internal contact welding from a prior high-current fault, or accumulated dirt/corrosion in the mechanism.
Diagnostic Steps
Attempt to reset with load fully isolated; inspect the toggle for physical damage; check for signs of overheating or arcing damage inside the enclosure.
Corrective Action
Replace the breaker if the mechanism is damaged, welded, or will not reset cleanly with the load isolated — do not force a jammed mechanism.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Persistent Downstream Fault or Mechanism Lockout
High Severity
Root Cause
A persistent downstream short circuit or ground fault, or the breaker's internal trip mechanism has failed in the tripped state.
Effect
The breaker trips immediately on reset, preventing the circuit from being restored to service.
Detection Method
Isolating downstream circuits and resetting with the load disconnected, plus a megger test of downstream cabling and equipment.
Trip Curve Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Inrush current at CCR start-up or harmonic currents exceeding the breaker's instantaneous trip setting, or an incorrectly selected trip curve.
Effect
Nuisance tripping under normal load even though no genuine fault is present.
Detection Method
Review of the trip curve type against the actual inrush profile, and load current logging during start-up compared to the breaker's rating.
Worn Contacts or Insufficient Breaking Capacity
High Severity
Root Cause
Worn or welded contacts, a degraded arc-chute, or the breaker's rated breaking capacity exceeded by the available fault current.
Effect
The breaker fails to trip under a confirmed fault, leaving the circuit unprotected.
Detection Method
Primary current injection testing to verify trip time and threshold, inspection of contacts and arc-chute condition, and a check of available fault current against the breaker's Icu rating.
Loose Terminal Connection
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Loose or under-torqued terminal connections, or the breaker operating above its thermal derating limit for the ambient enclosure temperature.
Effect
Overheating at breaker terminals that can progress to a terminal failure if left unaddressed.
Detection Method
Thermal imaging of the breaker and terminals under load, and a terminal torque check against specification.
Mechanical Jamming or Contact Welding
High Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical damage to the toggle mechanism, internal contact welding from a prior high-current fault, or accumulated dirt/corrosion in the mechanism.
Effect
The breaker will not reset or is mechanically jammed, leaving the circuit without a functioning protective device.
Detection Method
Attempting a reset with the load fully isolated, and inspecting the toggle for physical damage or signs of internal arcing.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Upstream Isolation Before Removal
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the upstream supply before removing or replacing a circuit breaker, and verify zero energy at its terminals before disconnecting wiring, even though the breaker's own contacts are open.

Correct Rating on Replacement
Compliance

Fit a replacement breaker with the same rated current and breaking capacity as the original, verified against the protected circuit's design load and prospective fault current; an undersized breaking capacity can fail to safely interrupt a fault.

Investigate Before Resetting a Tripped Breaker
Personnel Safety

Investigate the cause of a trip before resetting a breaker; repeatedly forcing it closed against a persistent fault can damage the breaker and leave the circuit inadequately protected.

Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any work downstream of a breaker, verify insulation resistance and correct load connection before closing it, since a residual fault at re-energisation can cause a nuisance trip or equipment damage.

PPE for Panel Work
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working inside a distribution board or CCR panel, and be aware of adjacent live circuits on other breakers in the same enclosure.

FOD Control at the Panel
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and breaker blanks accounted for at all times when a panel is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, particularly in panels serving critical control or lighting circuits.

Check for Thermal Damage on Replacement
Personnel Safety

Inspect the busbar and adjacent breakers for discolouration or heat damage when replacing a breaker that tripped repeatedly or showed signs of overheating, since a hot connection can damage neighbouring equipment.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a panel with an incorrectly rated breaker, an unlabelled circuit, or a loose termination after maintenance; verify correct labelling and torque before closing out the work order.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Series-Circuit Breakers Need Fault-Current Coordination, Not Just a Rating
Coordination

A breaker protecting a CCR series loop needs its trip settings coordinated against the constant-current regulator's own fault response, not just sized to the loop's steady-state current; a mismatch can leave the CCR's internal protection racing the breaker on every fault, with inconsistent results.

Match Trip Curve to Inrush Behaviour on Control Circuits
Selection

Nuisance tripping on control and distribution circuits is very often a trip-curve mismatch against inrush current from PLCs, transformers, or contactors, not an undersized breaker; check the curve type before resizing the breaker upward.

Intermittent Tripping With No Fault Found Warrants a Load Study
Troubleshooting

A breaker that trips occasionally with nothing obviously wrong on inspection is often responding to a marginal cumulative load condition rather than a genuine fault; a short load-logging exercise usually resolves it faster than repeated fault-finding on the wiring.

Exercise Standby Breakers on a Schedule
Maintenance

Breakers protecting rarely-used or standby circuits can develop a stiff or sluggish mechanism from years without operation; build periodic manual operation into the maintenance plan so a mechanism that won't trip or close cleanly is caught before it's needed for real.

Panel Ambient Temperature Shifts the Effective Rating
Design

Thermal-element trip characteristics are rated at a reference ambient; a tightly packed, hot-running panel can cause a breaker to trip below its nameplate current. Where panels run warm, derate selection accordingly rather than relying on the nameplate figure alone.

Record the Exact Replacement Spec, Not Just "Equivalent"
Documentation

When a failed breaker is replaced, record the curve type, breaking capacity, and settings actually fitted; a spare that's superficially equivalent on current rating alone can silently undermine the coordination the original design engineer calculated.
