Visual Approach Slope Guidance Comparison

PAPI VS T-VASIS

A compact 4-light row against a 20-light T-shaped array. PAPI condenses vertical approach-path guidance into a simple white/red count; T-VASIS spreads it across a much larger wing-bar and fly-up/fly-down arrangement. Both are ICAO-recognised visual approach slope systems — but only one of them is still the current standard.

02 · Definitions

What Is PAPI & What Is T-VASIS?

PAPI

PAPI consists of four light units arranged in a single horizontal row, generally on one side of the runway. Each unit is set at a different vertical aiming angle, so the aircraft's position produces a different white/red combination.

Pilot interpretation

The pilot counts how many units are white and how many are red across the row — more white means higher, more red means lower.

T-VASIS

T-VASIS is a large T-shaped arrangement of 20 light units, symmetrical about the runway centreline: two wing bars of four units each (8 units), each bisected by a longitudinal line of six units — three fly-down and three fly-up (12 units).

Pilot interpretation

The pilot reads the wing-bar and fly-up/fly-down light pattern — a different logic from simply counting coloured lights.

03 · Physical Layout

Compact Row vs T-Shaped Array

This is one of the biggest engineering differences between the two systems — PAPI is a single row, T-VASIS is a large symmetrical array built from wing bars and a centreline stem.

PAPI Layout

Four units, single horizontal row, one side of the runway.

T-VASIS Layout

Two 4-unit wing bars, one either side of the centreline, each bisected by a 6-unit longitudinal line (3 fly-down + 3 fly-up) — 8 + 12 = 20 units in total.

04 · Visual Indication Logic

Counting Lights vs Reading the T-Pattern

PAPI reduces vertical position to a light count. T-VASIS uses different optical light-unit types — wing-bar, fly-up and fly-down units — to build a richer picture of vertical deviation.

PAPI

Read across the row — white = high, red = low
4 White — too high
2 White + 2 Red — on path
4 Red — too low

T-VASIS

Read the wing bar together with the fly-up/fly-down cues

Above path: one, two or three fly-down lights show above the wing bar — the more lights, the higher the aircraft.

On path: the wing bar alone is visible.

Below path: one, two or three fly-up lights show below the wing bar; when well below, the wing bar and all three fly-up lights turn red.

05 · Deviation Indications

4 Discrete Indications vs 7

PAPI — 5 Principal States

4 WhiteToo high
3 White + 1 RedSlightly high
2 White + 2 RedOn approach path
1 White + 3 RedSlightly low
4 RedToo low

One normal approach-path indication plus four discrete deviation indications.

T-VASIS — More Discrete States

Under the ICAO Annex 14 specification, T-VASIS provides one normal approach-path indication plus seven discrete deviation indications, built from the wing-bar plus fly-up/fly-down light units: one, two or three fly-down lights above the path (3), one, two or three fly-up lights below it (3), and the all-red “well below” indication (1).

Seven deviation states versus PAPI's four means T-VASIS can convey a finer-grained picture of vertical deviation — at the cost of a much larger installation.

06 · The Main Difference

Number of Lights vs T-Shaped Pattern

PAPI

The pilot essentially counts the white and red lights: "how many are white, how many are red?"

T-VASIS

The pilot interprets the wing-bar and fly-up/fly-down light pattern: "what does the T-shaped indication tell me about my vertical position?"

Both answer the same operational question — is the aircraft on the desired vertical approach path? PAPI answers it with a light count; T-VASIS answers it with a pattern read.

07 · Scope of Guidance

What Neither System Replaces

PAPI and T-VASIS both provide visual vertical approach-slope guidance only. Neither replaces an ILS, and neither provides lateral guidance on its own.

PAPI and T-VASIS can complement an ILS. The localizer gives lateral guidance and the glide slope gives vertical radio guidance; the visual system is designed so its indications can correlate with the non-visual glide path signal.

08 · Electrical System

Series Circuit vs Multiple-Circuit Array

PAPI Electrical System

CCR / power → series circuit → isolation transformers → 4 PAPI units.

  • Constant-current supply
  • Series circuit
  • Isolation transformers
  • Light-source monitoring, intensity control, alignment

T-VASIS Electrical System

CCR / power → multiple circuits → isolation transformers → 20 T-VASIS light units.

  • Substantially more light units and optical components
  • More cabling and isolation transformers
  • More structural elements and maintenance points

10 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: T-VASIS is just a bigger PAPI
Fact

No. T-VASIS uses a different indication logic entirely — wing-bar and fly-up/fly-down cues rather than a white/red light count.

Myth: T-VASIS is still the ICAO-recommended standard
Fact

No. ICAO has recommended discontinuing T-VASIS and AT-VASIS as standard visual approach-slope systems from 1 January 2020. PAPI remains a current standard system.

Myth: More light units always means better guidance
Fact

T-VASIS's 20 units do provide more discrete deviation indications than PAPI's 4, but that comes with a much larger physical footprint, higher installation complexity and a heavier maintenance burden.

Myth: PAPI and T-VASIS must use the same approach angle
Fact

No. The normal approach slope is commonly 3°, but another slope can be selected for a specific runway depending on considerations such as obstacle clearance or harmonisation with a non-visual glide path.

Myth: PAPI and T-VASIS are interchangeable in every installation
Fact

No. They have different configurations, visual indication logic, design criteria and installation requirements — one is not a drop-in substitute for the other.

12 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI vs T-VASIS

Engineering Verdict

T-VASIS is not simply an older PAPI — it is a different optical logic that trades a compact footprint for finer deviation resolution. Both answer the same operational question, but T-VASIS spreads that answer across 20 units and seven deviation states instead of PAPI's 4 units and four states.

Given the larger footprint, higher installation complexity and heavier maintenance burden, ICAO has moved away from T-VASIS as a standard system in favour of PAPI's compact, simpler-to-maintain design.

PAPI4-light row, white/red count, compact footprint, current ICAO standard.
T-VASIS20-light T-shaped array, wing-bar/fly-up-down pattern, legacy system.

T-VASIS installations remain in service at some airports, but ICAO recommended discontinuing it as a standard system from 1 January 2020 — always verify the actual system installed at a given runway rather than assuming.

13 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest difference between PAPI and T-VASIS?

PAPI uses 4 light units in a single row, read as a white/red count. T-VASIS uses 20 light units in a T-shaped array symmetrical about the runway centreline, read as a wing-bar plus fly-up/fly-down pattern.

Is T-VASIS still an ICAO standard system?

ICAO has recommended discontinuing T-VASIS and AT-VASIS as standard visual approach-slope systems from 1 January 2020. PAPI remains a current standard.

Does T-VASIS give more information than PAPI?

T-VASIS provides one normal approach-path indication plus seven discrete deviation indications, versus four for PAPI — so it can convey finer vertical deviation detail, at the cost of a much larger installation.

Why is T-VASIS physically so much larger than PAPI?

T-VASIS's 20 units are arranged as two 4-unit wing bars (one either side of the runway centreline), each bisected by a 6-unit longitudinal line — 8 + 12 = 20 units, versus PAPI's single compact 4-unit row.

Do PAPI and T-VASIS provide lateral guidance?

No. Both are purely vertical visual approach-slope references. Neither provides lateral navigation, range information or the full precision guidance of an ILS.

Can PAPI or T-VASIS be installed alongside an ILS?

Yes. Both are designed so their visual indications can correlate with a non-visual glide-path signal, giving the pilot a visual cross-check once the runway environment is in sight.

Do PAPI and T-VASIS always use the same approach slope angle?

Not necessarily. A 3° slope is common, but another angle can be selected for a specific runway based on considerations like obstacle clearance or harmonisation with a non-visual glide path.

14 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations, §5.3.5 — visual approach slope indicator systems: PAPI/APAPI and T-VASIS/AT-VASIS configuration, siting, and the recommendation to discontinue T-VASIS/AT-VASIS as standard systems from 1 Jan 2020.

ICAO Doc 9157, Aerodrome Design Manual Part 4

Visual Aids — design guidance for PAPI and T-VASIS light-unit types, optical cut-off characteristics and deviation-indication counts.

DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I

India's aerodrome design and operations requirements, implementing ICAO Annex 14 — the primary regulatory reference for visual aids on Indian airport projects. Read alongside ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4 (Visual Aids) for design guidance.

FAA AC 150/5345-28 & AC 150/5340-30

PAPI equipment specification and installation details (L-880 four-box, L-881 two-box). T-VASIS is specified through ICAO Annex 14 and Doc 9157 rather than by FAA equipment ACs.

IS 12290 & IS 7785 (BIS)

IS 12290 covers isolating transformers for airport lighting systems; IS 7785 is the multi-part series for elevated-type aerodrome lighting fittings. Neither is an equipment specification for PAPI or T-VASIS light units — for those units, follow ICAO Annex 14 / Doc 9157 Part 4 or the FAA PAPI specification, as the project requires.

Do not design a PAPI or T-VASIS installation from a generic web diagram. The final siting, unit count and alignment must always be developed from the current governing standard, approved airport design and project-specific requirements.
