Runway Occupancy Sensor

A high‑reliability detection system that monitors runway occupancy status in real time. Providing critical input to Runway Status Light (RWSL) systems, A‑SMGCS, and ATC surveillance, these sensors enable automated conflict detection, runway incursion prevention, and enhanced safety in all visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Runway Occupancy Sensor is a sophisticated detection device that continuously monitors a designated runway zone to determine whether the runway is occupied by an aircraft or vehicle. It is a core component of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system, providing the real‑time occupancy data required to activate runway entrance lights (RELs), take‑off hold lights (THLs), and runway guard lights. The sensor uses advanced technologies—typically radar, infrared, or inductive loop—to detect targets with high accuracy and reliability, even in adverse weather conditions (fog, rain, snow) and low visibility. Unlike stop bar sensors that detect crossing at a specific point, runway occupancy sensors monitor an entire runway segment (typically several hundred metres) to determine if any target is present on the runway. The sensor's output is fed to the RWSL processing unit, which uses it to determine runway status and control the associated warning lights. By providing precise, continuous occupancy detection, these sensors play a vital role in preventing runway incursions, reducing the risk of runway collisions, and supporting safe, efficient airfield operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The runway occupancy sensor's purpose is to give the RWSL system a source of truth about the runway that does not depend on a controller noticing and acting on a specific traffic situation. Because RWSL is designed as an automatic layer that operates alongside, not instead of, standard ATC clearances, the sensor has to detect occupancy continuously across the whole monitored runway segment rather than at a single point, so that any aircraft or vehicle present anywhere in the zone is reflected in the light state without a gap in coverage. This continuous, always-on monitoring is what allows REL and THL lights to illuminate automatically the moment a runway becomes occupied, giving pilots and vehicle operators an independent visual cue that functions even if a clearance was misunderstood or a controller's attention was elsewhere.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Runway Occupancy Sensors are deployed at airports requiring enhanced runway safety surveillance, particularly:

  • RWSL (Runway Status Light) Systems: As the primary detection input for runway entrance lights (RELs), take‑off hold lights (THLs), and runway guard lights.
  • A‑SMGCS (Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control Systems): Integrating with A‑SMGCS for comprehensive surface surveillance and conflict detection.
  • ATC Towers: Providing controllers with automated runway occupancy status, enhancing situational awareness.
  • Airport Operations Centers: Monitoring runway usage for operational planning and resource allocation.
  • Low Visibility Operations (CAT II/III): Enabling safe runway occupancy management when RVR is below 400m.
  • Busy Hub Airports: Managing high‑density traffic with multiple simultaneous runway movements to prevent conflicts.
  • Runway Crossing Points: Monitoring taxiway/runway intersections to prevent incursions.
  • Intersecting Runways: Detecting occupancy on intersecting runways to prevent cross‑runway conflicts.
Placement

Installation & Location

Radar- and video-based sensors are pole-mounted beside the runway or at intersection entrances, sited and angled to give the required detection zone coverage over the runway surface or the crossing point being monitored, with spacing between poles set by each sensor's field-of-view range. In-pavement variants use inductive loops or magnetometers embedded beneath the surface at the detection points, wired back to a roadside pull box in the same way as other in-ground sensors. Power and data cabling from each sensor or loop is run in duct to a local equipment hut, from which a fibre or copper backbone carries occupancy status to the RWSL logic controller, A-SMGCS processor, or tower display, with sensor siting kept clear of the runway strip's frangibility and obstacle-clearance requirements.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Runway Occupancy Sensor uses a combination of detection technologies to provide continuous, reliable runway occupancy status. Key functional aspects include:

  • Detection Technologies: Sensors typically use one or a combination of:
    • FMCW Radar (24 GHz or 77 GHz): Detects moving and stationary targets across a wide area, providing range, velocity, and position information, immune to weather and lighting conditions.
    • Infrared (thermal or active): Uses thermal signatures or reflected IR to detect targets, effective in darkness, fog, and smoke.
    • Inductive Loop Arrays: A series of buried wire loops that detect metallic targets passing over them, providing precise position and direction information.
    • Fusion Sensors: Combine multiple technologies (e.g., radar + IR) for improved detection reliability and false‑alarm rejection.
  • Coverage Zone: The sensor is positioned to cover a specific runway segment, typically 300‑900 metres or the entire runway length (using multiple sensors). The coverage zone is precisely calibrated to the runway geometry.
  • Target Tracking: The sensor processor tracks multiple targets simultaneously, maintaining their position, speed, and heading within the coverage zone.
  • Occupancy Determination: The system determines runway occupancy status based on the presence of any tracked target within the runway zone, with configurable dwell times and hysteresis to prevent false triggers from transient targets.
  • Output Signal: The sensor provides a clean, isolated output—typically a dry contact, transistor output, or digital message (e.g., via Ethernet/Modbus)—that indicates occupied or clear status. The output is connected to the RWSL logic processor.
  • Self‑Diagnostics: The sensor continuously monitors its own health (power, communication, signal quality, internal faults) and provides diagnostic outputs to alert maintenance staff of any degradation or failure.
  • Integration: The sensor interfaces with the RWSL processor, A‑SMGCS, and ATC display systems via standard protocols (Ethernet, serial, hardwired).

The sensor is typically mounted on a pole adjacent to the runway, on a dedicated mast, or integrated into existing runway infrastructure (e.g., approach lighting masts). Its position and orientation are carefully engineered to provide optimal coverage while minimising false alarms from adjacent taxiways or apron areas.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The occupancy sensor's control logic sits between the sensing element and the airfield lighting/ATC systems it feeds. The sensor (radar, magnetic loop, or video analytics depending on technology) continuously reports a raw occupied/clear state to a local processing unit, which applies a confirmation and debounce logic — requiring the state to hold for a minimum dwell time — to filter out false triggers before declaring a validated occupancy change. The validated state is passed to the ALCMS, which can automatically illuminate runway status lights, or to ATC's surface surveillance display; the sensor also reports its own health continuously, and a dropout or self-test failure is flagged as a distinct sensor fault so controllers don't mistake an offline sensor for a confirmed-clear runway.

Construction

Physical Construction

The sensor head is housed in a sealed, weatherproof enclosure — typically die-cast aluminium or glass-reinforced polymer — rated IP65/IP67, with a radome or IR-transparent window covering the antenna or optical aperture to protect it from rain, dust, and de-icing overspray while remaining transparent to the sensing wavelength. Pole-mounted units bolt to an adjustable bracket that allows azimuth and elevation aiming to be set and locked during commissioning, with the bracket itself mounted on a frangible or shear-pin base where the pole sits within an obstacle-limitation surface. Internal electronics are conformally coated and built around a sealed connector interface (typically M12 circular connectors) for power and signal cabling, avoiding open terminal strips that could admit moisture. Built-in transient voltage suppression components are mounted close to the cable entry point to absorb lightning-induced surges before they reach the sensing and processing circuitry, and the enclosure includes a grounding lug for bonding to the site's lightning protection system.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

False Alarms from Adjacent Taxiways

Radar and IR sensors may detect aircraft on adjacent taxiways or apron areas if the field of view is too wide or the range gate is not properly configured. Careful alignment and the use of advanced filtering (range‑gating, Doppler filtering) are essential to prevent false alarms.

Sensor Obscuration (Radar/IR)

Snow, ice, heavy rain, or dirt accumulation on the sensor window can significantly reduce detection performance. Regular cleaning and the use of heated housings, wipers, or air purges can prevent obscuration.

Clutter from Runway Surface

Wet or snow‑covered runways can create clutter returns that interfere with target detection. Advanced clutter mapping and adaptive thresholding are required to maintain reliable detection in varying surface conditions.

Multi‑Path Interference

Reflections from large aircraft, buildings, or terrain can cause ghost targets or shadowing. Proper sensor siting and the use of multi‑path mitigation techniques (e.g., frequency diversity) are recommended.

Power Supply Instability

Voltage fluctuations or spikes can cause sensor resets or damage. Use a regulated power supply with surge protection and UPS backup to ensure reliable operation.

Alignment Drift

Over time, sensor mounts can shift due to vibration, thermal expansion, or ground movement, altering the detection zone. Regular site surveys and alignment checks using calibration targets are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

False Alarms from Adjacent Taxiway or Apron Traffic
Likely Cause
Field of view too wide or the range gate not configured for the intended runway/taxiway segment.
Diagnostic Steps
Review alarm logs against known adjacent traffic movements, check the current range-gate and field-of-view settings, and run a test target through the defined boundaries.
Corrective Action
Re-align the sensor and tighten the range-gate/field-of-view configuration, applying Doppler or range filtering as needed.
Missed or Delayed Detection (Sensor Obscuration)
Likely Cause
Snow, ice, heavy rain, or dirt accumulation on the sensor window.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the sensor window/radome, check heater/wiper/air-purge operation, and compare detection performance before and after cleaning.
Corrective Action
Clean the sensor window, repair or activate the heated housing/wiper/air purge, and schedule more frequent cleaning in adverse weather periods.
Persistent Detections on a Clear Runway (Clutter)
Likely Cause
Clutter returns from a wet or snow-covered runway surface not adequately filtered.
Diagnostic Steps
Review clutter map settings against current surface conditions, check adaptive thresholding configuration, and correlate false detections with weather reports.
Corrective Action
Update the clutter map for current conditions, adjust thresholding parameters, and re-test after the configuration change.
Ghost Targets or Shadowed Detection Zones
Likely Cause
Multi-path reflections from large aircraft, buildings, or terrain.
Diagnostic Steps
Map reported ghost-target positions against known reflective structures and review sensor siting relative to large reflective surfaces.
Corrective Action
Apply multi-path mitigation (frequency diversity) if available, and re-site or re-aim the sensor where practical.
Sensor Reset or Data Loss During Voltage Events
Likely Cause
Power supply instability — voltage fluctuations or spikes.
Diagnostic Steps
Log supply voltage over time, inspect surge protection device status, and correlate resets with known power events.
Corrective Action
Install or replace a regulated power supply with surge protection, and add UPS backup.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Sensor Window Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Dust, insects, ice, or condensation accumulating on the sensor's optical or radar window between cleaning cycles.
Effect
Reduced detection sensitivity or a slow drift toward missed detections as the window's transmission degrades.
Detection Method
Scheduled sensor window cleaning combined with a periodic power and output signal check.
Sensor Obscuration in Adverse Weather
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Heavy rain, snow, or fog attenuating the radar or infrared signal path across the monitored zone.
Effect
Missed or delayed detection of an occupying aircraft or vehicle, undermining the safety logic that depends on this input.
Detection Method
Correlating missed-detection events with weather conditions, and reviewing the false-alarm/miss log for weather-linked patterns.
Alignment / Aim Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Mounting bracket loosening from vibration, thermal cycling, or incidental contact shifting the sensor's field of view.
Effect
The monitored zone no longer matches the design boundary, causing false alarms from adjacent taxiways or missed detections at the true zone edge.
Detection Method
Alignment check against the design field of view, and mount/bracket inspection for looseness or damage.
Clutter Map Drift / Outdated Baseline
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Physical changes near the sensor — new signage, parked equipment, or vegetation growth — that the stored clutter map no longer accounts for.
Effect
Persistent false detections on a clear runway as the sensor flags stationary objects it was never trained to ignore.
Detection Method
Clutter map review against current site conditions, with an update triggered by any nearby physical change.
Heater / Wiper / Air-Purge Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Failed heating element, wiper motor, or air-purge system meant to keep the sensor window clear in icing or heavy-precipitation conditions.
Effect
Rapid re-contamination of the sensor window in poor weather, compounding obscuration-related missed detections.
Detection Method
Functional test of the heater, wiper, or air-purge system as part of scheduled maintenance.
Power Supply / UPS Instability
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing UPS batteries or a marginal power feed causing brief interruptions that the sensor doesn't ride through cleanly.
Effect
Sensor reset or data loss during voltage events, producing a gap in occupancy status right when it may matter most.
Detection Method
Power supply and UPS test under simulated load-shedding, and review of sensor reset logs against known voltage events.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Do Not Take Offline Without a Procedural Fallback
Operational

Never take a runway occupancy sensor out of service without first agreeing a procedural fallback with ATC, since the RWSL system depends on its data to control runway entrance and take-off hold lights.

RF/Radar Exposure Precaution
Personnel Safety

For radar-based sensors, avoid standing directly in front of the antenna aperture while the unit is transmitting, and follow the manufacturer's minimum safe distance for RF exposure during servicing.

Airside Work Coordination & NOTAM
Operational

Any work on or near the sensor requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM reflecting the RWSL system's degraded status for the duration of the work.

Alignment Verification After Servicing
Operational

Re-verify the sensor's aim and detection zone against the as-commissioned baseline after any servicing that disturbs its mounting, since a small alignment shift can create a gap in runway coverage.

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the sensor's power supply before opening the housing, and verify zero energy before touching any internal wiring.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify Detection Zone Boundaries in the Field, Not Just on the Drawing
Commissioning

A detection zone that looks correct on the design drawing can end up offset in practice due to as-built survey error or a mounting position adjusted during installation. Walk and mark the actual zone boundary against a test target before sign-off, rather than relying on the design coordinates alone.

Radar and Infrared Sensors Fail Differently in Weather
Reliability

Heavy rain and fog degrade infrared and radar sensing in different ways and to different degrees, so a site using a mix of technologies for redundancy needs its alarm and health monitoring logic to distinguish a genuine target-detection failure from a known weather-related sensitivity reduction rather than treating both the same way.

Nuisance Triggers Cluster at the Zone Edge
Troubleshooting

False detections from wildlife, service vehicles, or blowing debris tend to concentrate near the boundary of the detection zone rather than being evenly distributed through it; reviewing logged nuisance events by location, not just by count, usually points straight to a boundary or sensitivity setting that needs adjustment.

Mounting Vibration Shifts Alignment Over Time
Mechanical

Sensors mounted on masts or structures near the movement area experience continuous low-level vibration from aircraft and ground vehicle traffic, which can gradually shift aim on a sensor that isn't mechanically over-specified for the application. Include an alignment check, not just a functional check, in the periodic maintenance routine.

Know the Full Latency Budget, Not Just the Sensor's Own Response Time
Performance

The sensor's datasheet response time is only one part of the total delay between an aircraft entering the zone and a runway status light actually illuminating; processing, communication, and light drive latency all add to that figure, and the end-to-end number is what matters for the safety case, not the sensor spec alone.

Cross-Check Against Surface Surveillance Data During Acceptance Testing
Verification

Where the airfield has ASDE-X or another surface surveillance system, correlating occupancy sensor detections against it during acceptance testing catches disagreements between the two systems' pictures of the runway that a stand-alone functional test of the sensor wouldn't surface.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the runway occupancy sensor: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a runway occupancy sensor?

A runway occupancy sensor continuously monitors a designated runway zone to determine whether an aircraft or vehicle is on it. It is a core component of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system, supplying the real-time occupancy data needed to activate runway entrance lights, take-off hold lights and runway guard lights.

Q2How is it different from a stop bar sensor?

A stop bar sensor detects crossing at a specific point. A runway occupancy sensor monitors an entire runway segment, typically several hundred metres, to determine whether any target is present.

Q3What detection technologies are used?

Typically FMCW radar (24 GHz or 77 GHz), infrared (thermal or active), or inductive loop arrays, used singly or in combination. Radar detects moving and stationary targets regardless of weather and lighting, infrared works in darkness, fog and smoke, and buried loops detect metallic targets with precise position and direction.

Q4What coverage range does a sensor have?

Typical coverage is 300 m to 900 m for radar, 100 m to 300 m for infrared and 5 m to 50 m per zone for inductive loops. Radar range resolution is 1 m to 5 m.

Q5How fast does the sensor respond?

Response time from detection to output change is typically 200 ms or less, and targets can be detected from parked up to about 100 km/h.

Q6How reliable is detection?

A typical false alarm rate is no more than one per 24 hours with proper calibration. Typical minimum target size is 1 m² for radar and 2 m² for infrared.

Q7Where are runway occupancy sensors used?

At airports needing enhanced runway safety surveillance: as the primary detection input for RWSL systems, integrated with A-SMGCS for surface surveillance and conflict detection, and providing information to ATC towers.

Q8How does the sensor connect to the control system?

It is a standalone powered sensor (24 V DC or 120/230 V AC) with outputs by dry contact relay, opto-isolated transistor, Ethernet (Modbus TCP) or serial RS-485. Output isolation keeps the sensor circuit separate from controlled loads, and built-in surge protection is typically up to 6 kV.

Q9Which standards apply?

Relevant references are ICAO Annex 14 (the runway safety context), FAA AC 150/5340-30 (visual aid design and installation, including RWSL provisions) and EUROCAE ED-87 (MASPS for A-SMGCS). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q10How often should a runway occupancy sensor be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for sensor window cleaning and false-alarm log review, semi-annual for range gate and field-of-view calibration, alignment and power supply and UPS tests, and annual for clutter map update, heater, wiper and air-purge checks and mount inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11What are the common problems with runway occupancy sensors?

Common problems are false alarms from adjacent taxiways, sensor obscuration in adverse weather, clutter from the runway surface, multipath interference, power supply instability and alignment drift. Persistent detections on a clear runway usually point to clutter, and missed or delayed detection to obscuration.
