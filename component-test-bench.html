Test Bench

A dedicated workbench and testing station used for the maintenance, repair, and performance verification of AGL fixtures, isolating transformers, connectors, and other components. Equipped with power supplies, photometric instruments, and diagnostic tools, the test bench enables thorough off‑airfield testing, reducing downtime and improving maintenance efficiency.

Overview

What Is It?

A Test Bench is a purpose‑built workstation that provides a controlled environment for testing, diagnosing, and repairing AGL components removed from the airfield. It typically includes:

  • Variable Power Supply: A stabilised DC and AC power source with adjustable voltage and current, often including a constant current regulator (CCR) simulator to replicate the 6.6 A series circuit conditions.
  • Photometric Measurement Equipment: A lux meter, photometer, or colorimeter mounted on an adjustable stand, allowing the operator to measure light output and colour of fixtures at a fixed distance.
  • Electrical Test Instruments: Multimeters, insulation resistance testers, earth testers, and loop resistance meters for checking electrical parameters.
  • Mechanical Fixtures and Mounts: Adjustable clamps, bases, and adapters to securely hold various fixture types (inset, elevated, different manufacturers) during testing.
  • Optical Inspection Tools: Magnifying lamps, borescopes, or microscopes for inspecting lenses, prisms, and optical surfaces.
  • ESD Protection: Anti‑static work surfaces and grounding points to protect sensitive LED electronics.

The test bench is typically located in a workshop or maintenance facility, away from the operational airfield. It allows technicians to perform comprehensive diagnostics, identify faults, and verify repairs before reinstalling components, significantly improving maintenance efficiency and reducing airfield downtime.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The test bench exists because a component pulled from the field cannot be trusted to be either faulty or fixed based on appearance alone, and it cannot safely be evaluated at full operating current out on the airfield. Recreating the exact series-circuit current steps and measuring photometric output under controlled conditions lets a technician confirm precisely what is wrong — a degraded LED module, a marginal transformer, an out-of-tolerance colour or intensity — rather than guessing and swapping parts. Just as importantly, it verifies a repair before the component ever goes back into the ground: a fixture that leaves the bench having demonstrably met its intensity, colour, and electrical specifications is far less likely to fail again shortly after reinstallation, which reduces repeat callouts, unplanned outages, and the airfield downtime each one causes.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The test bench is deployed across various maintenance and quality assurance scenarios:

  • Workshop Maintenance Facilities: Airports with in‑house maintenance teams use test benches for routine repair and overhaul of AGL fixtures, transformers, and connectors.
  • AGL Service Centres: Specialised repair workshops and third‑party service providers use test benches to offer component repair and refurbishment services to multiple airports.
  • Manufacturing Quality Control: AGL manufacturers use test benches in their QC labs to perform sample testing of production batches, verifying performance before shipment.
  • Training and Education: Test benches are used in training facilities to teach technicians how to diagnose and repair AGL components in a controlled, safe environment.
  • Research and Development: Engineers use test benches to evaluate new components, test prototypes, and conduct failure analysis.
Care

Storage & Handling

The test bench is a fixed installation within a workshop, service centre, or QC lab rather than field-portable equipment, so its "location" is chosen at commissioning rather than moved job to job — typically bolted or wheel-locked in place on a level floor near mains power and clear of foot traffic, with enough surrounding clearance to open panel covers and manoeuvre fixtures or transformers on and off the bench. Cabling, test leads, and adaptor fixtures are stored on the bench's integral racks or a nearby tool board so the correct interface is always at hand, and the unit is kept covered or powered down between test sessions to protect its meters and connectors from workshop dust. Periodic calibration checks against a reference standard are carried out in place, without needing to relocate the bench itself.

Function

How Does It Work?

A typical test bench operation follows a structured workflow:

  • Receiving and Inspection: The component (e.g., an inset light, transformer, or connector) is cleaned and visually inspected for physical damage, corrosion, or signs of overheating.
  • Electrical Testing: The component is connected to the test bench's power supply and test instruments. For fixtures, the supply is set to simulate the series circuit current (e.g., 2.8 A to 6.6 A). Electrical parameters (voltage, current, insulation resistance) are measured and recorded.
  • Photometric Testing (for light fixtures): The fixture is powered at the required current steps, and its luminous intensity and colour are measured at a fixed distance (often 10 m or 30 m) using a photometer or colorimeter mounted on the test bench. The measured values are compared against the manufacturer's specifications and ICAO/FAA requirements.
  • Diagnostic Analysis: Based on the test results, the technician identifies the fault. Common issues include failed LED modules, degraded optical filters, burnt‑out lamps, faulty transformers, or damaged connectors.
  • Repair and Re‑testing: The faulty component is repaired (e.g., replacing an LED driver, cleaning the lens, replacing a connector). After repair, the component is tested again to verify that it meets the required performance standards.
  • Reporting and Documentation: All test results are documented, and the component is either returned to stock or reinstalled on the airfield with a record of the repair.

The test bench environment allows for repeatable, controlled testing conditions that are not possible in the field. This ensures that repaired components perform reliably when reinstalled, reducing the likelihood of premature failures.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The test bench replicates the AGL series-circuit control chain in miniature so that a fixture can be exercised exactly as it would be in the field, without needing a live runway loop. Its own power supply takes the place of the CCR, stepping through the same fixed current levels (2.8A to 6.6A) on command from the bench's control software, while the mounted photometer or colorimeter takes the place of the field verification process, logging intensity and colour at each step. The control logic here is entirely local to the bench: a technician or automated test sequence selects the current step, the supply regulates to that value, and the measurement instruments capture the fixture's response, with the pass/fail comparison against specification acting as the gate that decides whether the unit is returned to service or sent back for further repair. None of this communicates with the live ALCMS or field CCR — the bench is a self-contained, offline replica of that control relationship used purely for verification.

Diagram

The Test–Repair–Verify Loop

What separates the test bench from a field check is the closed repair loop: a component that fails photometric or electrical test is repaired and fed straight back into the same test sequence, not just re-installed on trust. Only a component that verifies within spec — first time or after repair — reaches the documentation step, giving every returned unit a repeatable, controlled pass/fail record.

Construction

Physical Construction

The bench is typically a welded steel-frame workstation with a conductive, ESD-safe laminate worktop bonded to a single grounding point, mounted on lockable castors or bolted feet depending on whether it serves a mobile workshop or a fixed lamp room. Instruments — the variable DC supply, CCR simulator, photometer/lux meter, insulation tester, and multimeter — are rack-mounted or panel-built into the bench frame with their displays and controls facing the operator, wired through a common distribution panel with individually fused outputs so a fault on one test circuit doesn't take down the others. A fixed optical rail or adjustable stand at the front of the bench sets the photometer at a repeatable measurement distance from the fixture under test, with clamps and bases sized to hold inset, elevated, and pole-mounted AGL fixtures securely during testing. Overhead task lighting, a fume-extraction hood above the soldering station, and a lockable cabinet for spares and calibration certificates complete the layout.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Photometer Calibration Drift

The photometer or lux meter on the test bench must be calibrated regularly (annually) against a traceable reference. If the calibration is off, all intensity and colour measurements will be inaccurate, leading to incorrect pass/fail decisions. Use a calibration standard lamp to verify the photometer before each test session.

Inconsistent Mounting and Alignment

The fixture under test must be mounted consistently and aligned precisely with the photometer's optical axis. Even small misalignments (1–2°) can cause significant errors in intensity readings. Use precision mounting adapters and laser alignment tools to ensure repeatable positioning.

Stray Light and Ambient Reflections

The test bench area should be shielded from external light sources and painted in matte black to minimise reflections. Stray light can inflate lux readings and corrupt colour measurements. Use a dark curtain or enclosure for critical tests.

Fixture Warm‑up and Thermal Stabilisation

Halogen lamps and LED modules change their output as they warm up. For halogen lamps, allow 10–15 minutes of stabilisation before taking photometric measurements. For LEDs, allow at least 2–3 minutes. Document the warm‑up time in the test procedure.

ESD Damage to LED Components

LED drivers and modules are sensitive to electrostatic discharge (ESD). Without proper ESD protection (wrist straps, conductive mats), handling can damage the electronics, leading to premature failures. Ensure that all technicians are properly earthed and that the test bench has a grounded work surface.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Test Results Inconsistent Between Sessions for the Same Fixture Type
Likely Cause
Photometer calibration has drifted since the last check, or fixture mounting/alignment against the photometer's optical axis is not being repeated precisely between sessions.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a calibration standard lamp through the bench before the next test session to confirm the photometer reads within tolerance; check mounting adapter positioning and alignment against the documented setup for that fixture type.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the photometer against a traceable reference if drift is confirmed; use precision mounting adapters and laser alignment tools to restore repeatable 1-2° positioning, then re-run the affected test batch.
Lux Readings Higher Than Expected for a Known-Good Fixture
Likely Cause
Stray light or ambient reflections entering the test area are inflating the reading, since the bench relies on shielding from external light and matte-black surfaces to prevent this.
Diagnostic Steps
Take a baseline (zero-fixture) reading with the bench dark to check for a non-zero stray-light offset; inspect the enclosure or dark curtain for gaps or degraded matte-black coating.
Corrective Action
Seal light leaks in the enclosure or curtain and refresh matte-black surfaces where reflective wear is visible; re-verify with a baseline zero reading before resuming test sessions.
Fixture Fails Intensity Test Immediately After Power-Up
Likely Cause
The fixture hasn't been allowed to thermally stabilise before measurement — halogen lamps and LED modules both change output as they warm up, and a measurement taken too early will read low against the specified value.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the elapsed warm-up time against the documented requirement (10-15 minutes for halogen, 2-3 minutes for LED) before the reading was taken.
Corrective Action
Re-run the test after the correct warm-up period, and document the warm-up time actually used in the test record to prevent recurrence.
LED Fixture Fails to Power On or Shows Erratic Output During Test
Likely Cause
Electrostatic discharge during handling has damaged the LED driver or module, particularly if the fixture was handled without ESD protection prior to testing.
Diagnostic Steps
Check whether ESD protection (wrist strap, grounded mat) was in use when the fixture was last handled; substitute a known-good driver module to confirm whether the fault is in the driver or elsewhere in the fixture.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged driver/module; reinforce ESD protocol for technicians handling LED components going forward, since ESD damage is often not immediately visible.
Colour Measurement Results Appear Shifted or Inconsistent
Likely Cause
Ambient reflections or stray light contaminating the colour measurement, or the photometer's colour calibration has drifted independently of its intensity calibration.
Diagnostic Steps
Repeat the measurement with the enclosure fully sealed to rule out stray light; check the photometer's colour calibration record separately from its intensity calibration.
Corrective Action
Address any stray-light path found; recalibrate the photometer's colour measurement function against a certified reference standard if drift is confirmed there.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Photometer Calibration Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Sensor ageing, optical component degradation, or handling shock since the last traceable calibration.
Effect
Intensity and colour readings shift away from true values, producing inconsistent pass/fail results between test sessions for the same fixture type.
Detection Method
Running a calibration standard lamp through the bench before each test session and comparing against its certified output.
Stray-Light Enclosure / Seal Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wear or gaps developing in the dark curtain, enclosure seams, or matte-black interior coating over repeated use.
Effect
Ambient light or internal reflections inflate lux and colour readings, particularly on known-good fixtures that should read within a tight band.
Detection Method
A baseline zero-fixture reading with the bench dark, checked periodically for a non-zero stray-light offset.
Reference / Calibration Standard Lamp Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Normal lumen depreciation and filament or LED ageing in the certified reference lamp used to validate the bench.
Effect
The reference lamp itself drifts out of its certified tolerance, masking or mimicking a photometer calibration fault.
Detection Method
Tracking reference lamp output against its calibration certificate and retiring it at the manufacturer's rated service life.
Mounting Adapter / Alignment Jig Wear
Low Severity
Root Cause
Repeated clamping and unclamping of fixtures wearing the adapter's registration features or loosening its locating pins.
Effect
Fixture positioning against the photometer's optical axis is no longer repeatable between sessions, adding scatter to results.
Detection Method
Laser alignment check of adapter positioning against the documented setup for each fixture type.
Bench Power Supply / Regulated Rail Instability
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing power supply components or a marginal regulated output feeding the fixture under test at an incorrect voltage or current.
Effect
Fixtures under test receive an out-of-tolerance supply, producing intensity or warm-up readings that don't reflect real-world performance.
Detection Method
Periodic verification of the bench's regulated output voltage/current against a calibrated meter under load.
Data Acquisition / Firmware Fault
Low Severity
Root Cause
Software bug, corrupted configuration, or firmware drift in the bench's data-logging and control system.
Effect
Recorded results don't match what the photometer displayed, or test sequences fail to run to completion.
Detection Method
Cross-checking logged results against the photometer's direct display output, and reviewing firmware version history.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Bench Power Isolation
Electrical Safety

Verify the bench's supply matches the fixture under test (series current or fixed voltage) before connecting it; landing a series-circuit fixture on the wrong output, or vice versa, can damage the fixture or the bench.

Direct Viewing During Testing
Personnel Safety

Avoid looking directly at a fixture under test at close range inside the enclosure, particularly high-intensity or rotating-beacon units on the bench.

Hot Fixture / Calibration Lamp Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow a fixture or the reference calibration lamp to cool before handling after a test run, particularly halogen and xenon sources that retain heat inside the enclosure.

ESD Precautions
Component Handling

Use anti-static precautions when handling LED driver boards, controllers, or other electronic components on the bench.

Calibration Traceability
Compliance

Run the reference calibration standard lamp before each test session and log the result; test data collected on a drifted or unverified bench is not a reliable basis for a pass/fail decision.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for electrical connections and standard workshop PPE (eye protection, gloves) appropriate to the fixture type being tested.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the stray-light enclosure is properly sealed and the bench reads zero on a baseline check before relying on its results for a fixture disposition.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
An Uncalibrated Bench Produces Confident, Wrong Answers
Calibration

A bench that's drifted out of calibration doesn't announce itself; it keeps producing pass/fail readings that look perfectly normal while being systematically off, which is more dangerous than an obvious fault because nobody questions the result; running the reference standard lamp before every session, not just periodically, is what actually catches this.

Stray Light Leakage Skews Low-Intensity Readings the Most
Photometric

A minor seal or seam failure in the enclosure that lets in a small amount of ambient light barely affects a high-intensity fixture reading but can meaningfully distort a low-intensity or dimmed-step measurement; verify the baseline zero reading is genuinely zero before trusting results near the low end of a fixture's dimming range specifically.

Bench Output Characteristics Should Match the Fixture's Real Circuit
Verification

A bench that supplies a clean, well-regulated current can produce a better result than the fixture would ever see on an actual series loop with real-world voltage drop and interference; where a fixture passes bench testing but underperforms in the field, consider whether the bench's supply characteristics are more forgiving than the installation it's meant to represent.

Traceability Requires the Calibration Standard's Own History
Compliance

The reference calibration lamp itself has a service life and its own gradual output drift; keep a separate record of the standard's own calibration history and replacement schedule, since a bench "verified" against a drifted standard gives a false sense of traceability.

Let the Bench and Fixture Reach Thermal Stability Before Reading
Photometric

Many light sources shift output somewhat during their first minutes of operation as they reach thermal equilibrium; taking a reading too early in a test cycle can record a transient value rather than the fixture's actual steady-state performance, so allow adequate warm-up time before logging a result.

Keep Test Records Tied to the Bench's Calibration State at the Time
Documentation

If a calibration issue is discovered later, being able to identify which test results were recorded before versus after the last verified-good calibration check lets you scope exactly which fixture dispositions might need re-testing, rather than having to question every result the bench has ever produced.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the test bench: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an AGL test bench?

A test bench is a purpose-built workstation that provides a controlled environment for testing, diagnosing and repairing AGL components removed from the airfield, such as fixtures, isolating transformers and connectors.

Q2What equipment does an AGL test bench include?

Typically a variable power supply that may include a CCR simulator for 6.6 A series circuit conditions, photometric equipment such as a lux meter or colorimeter on an adjustable stand, electrical test instruments (multimeters, insulation testers, earth testers and loop resistance meters), and mechanical fixtures and mounts for different fixture types.

Q3How is a component tested on a test bench?

The component is cleaned and inspected, then connected to the bench supply and instruments. For fixtures, the supply simulates series circuit current (for example 2.8 A to 6.6 A) while voltage, current and insulation resistance are recorded. Light fixtures are then powered at the required current steps and luminous intensity and colour are measured at a fixed distance.

Q4Where are test benches used?

In airport workshop maintenance facilities, AGL service centres and third-party repair workshops, and in manufacturing quality assurance.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

A variable DC supply of 0-30 V and 0-10 A, a CCR simulator of 0-6.6 A up to 200 V, a photometer covering 0.01 lx to 200,000 lx, a measurement distance of typically 10 m or 30 m, and an insulation tester at 500 V or 1,000 V up to 2 GΩ. The work surface is roughly 2.0 m x 1.0 m.

Q6Is a test bench part of the airfield lighting circuit?

No. It is standalone mains-powered test equipment (230 V AC at 50 Hz or 110 V at 60 Hz), not part of the airfield series circuit. Its CCR simulator only replicates the series circuit for the component under test.

Q7Which standards apply to test benches?

Relevant references are ISO/IEC 17025 (competence of testing laboratories), IEC/TS 61827 as the test reference for photometric benches, FAA AC 150/5340-26 (maintenance of airport visual aid facilities) and the manufacturer's service manuals. Always verify the current edition and your requirements.

Q8How is a test bench maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for photometer calibration and ESD protection checks, semi-annual for an ambient light baseline check, and mounting and alignment verification before each test series. Your facility's quality procedures take precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with test benches?

Common problems are photometer calibration drift, inconsistent mounting and alignment, stray light and ambient reflections, insufficient fixture warm-up and thermal stabilisation, and ESD damage to LED components. Inconsistent results between sessions for the same fixture type often point to one of these.
