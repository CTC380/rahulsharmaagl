Why AGL Photometry Is Important

A practical field reference on AGL photometry — what is measured, how and where it is measured, what the results mean, when testing is required, and what happens when a fitting does not perform.

RS

Rahul Sharma

AGL Photometry Specialist · Airfield Ground Lighting

Published January 15, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) is not just about installing lights. It is about ensuring that every light delivers the right intensity, in the right direction, with the required performance characteristics defined by applicable standards and design requirements. This is where AGL photometry becomes critical.

Photometry is the measurement of light weighted to the sensitivity of the human eye — in AGL, chiefly intensity (in candela) and its distribution in angle — and it is normally carried out together with a check of chromaticity (colour). In an airfield environment, these measurements directly affect flight safety, pilot decision-making, and regulatory compliance.

Key Takeaway

AGL photometry converts the visual performance of an airfield light into measurable engineering data that can be evaluated against the applicable ICAO, FAA, and project-specific requirements.

01

What Is AGL Photometry?

AGL photometry is the controlled, instrument-based measurement of the light a fitting actually produces, checked against the values defined in its design and in the applicable standard. Rather than relying on how a light looks to the eye, photometry uses calibrated equipment — a photometer, goniophotometer, or distribution measurement bench — to record intensity, direction, and colour under known conditions.

In simple terms: photometry answers the question every AGL engineer eventually has to ask — does this light actually perform the way it is supposed to, or does it just look like it does?

02

What Does AGL Photometry Actually Measure?

Photometric testing is not a single reading. It covers a defined set of optical parameters, each of which is evaluated against the applicable standard, equipment specification, design requirement, or project acceptance criteria:

Light IntensityPeak and average candela output of the fitting
Angular DistributionHow intensity varies across the full beam pattern (isocandela diagram)
Horizontal Beam SpreadCoverage angle across the horizontal plane
Vertical Beam SpreadCoverage angle across the vertical plane
Colour / ChromaticityColour coordinates against the ICAO/FAA chromaticity boundaries
UniformityConsistency of output within the beam and across the fitting population
Beam OrientationToe-in, elevation setting, and aiming accuracy
Intensity at Defined AnglesCandela values at the specific angles standards require
Rated Operating ConditionsPerformance at the fitting's rated current and at each CCR brightness step

Together, these parameters define whether a fitting is genuinely fit for purpose — not just visually similar to one that is.

03

Why Photometry Matters for Flight Safety

Pilots rely on visual cues from runway, taxiway, and approach lighting systems, especially during night operations and low visibility conditions. If the photometric performance of a fitting is incorrect:

  • Light may appear dim or washed out
  • Glare may affect pilot vision
  • Beam alignment may mislead aircraft positioning
  • Colour outside the chromaticity limits may be misread, for example white against yellow

Accurate photometry ensures that each AGL fitting provides clear, unambiguous visual guidance during critical phases of flight.

04

ICAO & FAA Standards

Applicable ICAO provisions, FAA advisory circulars, equipment specifications, and project or tender requirements define the photometric performance criteria for a given AGL application. Depending on the fitting and the contract, this can draw on several distinct layers:

  • ICAO Annex 14, Volume I — Appendix 1 (colours) and Appendix 2 (light characteristics and isocandela diagrams), with guidance in the ICAO Aerodrome Design Manual (Doc 9157)
  • FAA advisory circulars and equipment specifications — for example AC 150/5345-46 for runway and taxiway light fixtures, and the AC 150/5345-53 equipment certification programme
  • Product-specific photometric requirements from the manufacturer's design data
  • Applicable project or tender-specific requirements

Photometric testing verifies that AGL products meet the relevant combination of these requirements before installation and, where required, while in service. Without validated photometry, a fitting may fail inspection, delay commissioning, or lead to non-compliance findings during audits.

05

Visual Inspection vs Photometric Testing

One of the most common misunderstandings on site is treating a visual check and a photometric test as interchangeable. They are not — they answer different questions.

AspectVisual InspectionPhotometric Testing
What it checksPhysical condition of the fittingOptical performance of the fitting
What it looks forDamage, corrosion, cracked lensesActual light output and distribution
What it can detectObvious, visible defectsPerformance deviations invisible to the eye
Nature of resultUsually qualitativeQuantitative, instrument-based
Where it happensField observation, walk-roundLab, goniophotometer, or calibrated field kit

A light can look bright and still fail its required photometric performance.

06

AGL Photometry Testing Workflow

Whether the test is lab-based type testing or a field verification, the underlying workflow follows the same broad sequence (the methods and acceptance limits differ):

01PreparationSample selection, cleaning, warm-up
02EquipmentPhotometer/goniophotometer setup & traceable calibration
03Test ConditionsRated voltage/current, ambient control
04MeasurementIntensity readings across defined angles
05Data AnalysisPlotting distribution, checking uniformity
06Compliance CheckComparing results to the applicable limits (ICAO/FAA, design, tender)
07ReportFormal photometric test report
08Corrective ActionRework, re-alignment, or rejection if needed

Skipping or rushing any single step weakens confidence in the entire test result — a result is only as reliable as the calibration chain and measurement uncertainty behind it.

07

What Photometric Results Tell an Engineer

The real value of photometry is diagnostic, not just pass/fail. Each type of deviation points an engineer toward a different root cause:

Low intensity
Investigate optical or electrical condition — LED/lamp degradation, lens contamination or moisture ingress, incorrect circuit current (CCR brightness step or calibration), or isolating-transformer and connector problems.
Incorrect beam distribution
Investigate alignment and optics — fitting tilt, damaged prism/lens, or misaligned reflector.
Colour deviation
Investigate the light source and filter — LED chromaticity shift with temperature or ageing, filter or lens degradation, or a wrong LED batch or bin.
Non-uniform performance
Investigate fitting-to-fitting variation, mixed batches or lamp/LED types, transformer mismatch, or installation and aiming consistency.
Progressive degradation
Establish a data-driven maintenance or replacement strategy instead of reacting after failure.

Photometry does not just tell you something is wrong. Read correctly, it tells you where to look.

08

Verification of Design and Manufacturing Quality

Photometric results reflect the true quality of:

  • Optical design
  • Prism or lens accuracy
  • Lamp or LED alignment
  • Thermal and electrical stability

Two fittings may look identical physically, but only photometric testing reveals whether their optical performance matches the design intent.

09

Long-Term Performance and Maintenance Planning

Photometric data is not only for initial approval. It helps in:

  • Benchmarking light output degradation over time
  • Comparing new fittings with installed ones
  • Planning maintenance or replacement cycles

This supports data-driven decisions instead of reactive maintenance.

10

When Should AGL Photometry Be Performed?

Photometry is not a one-time event tied only to product approval. In practice, it recurs across the entire lifecycle of an AGL system:

Product qualification / type testing
Factory Acceptance Test (FAT)
Installation verification
Commissioning
Periodic performance monitoring
Troubleshooting reported lighting issues
Post-maintenance verification
LED replacement or upgrade
Investigation of abnormal lighting performance

If photometry is treated only as a one-time approval step, degradation between those points goes unnoticed until it becomes a safety finding.

11

Illustrative Field Example: Detecting Hidden Photometric Degradation

The scenario below is a representative, illustrative example built from common failure patterns seen in AGL photometry work — not a citation of a specific project or audit.

Picture a taxiway edge lighting circuit where a row of fittings from the same batch looks identical during a routine walk-round — same brightness, same colour, no visible damage. Periodic photometric monitoring tells a different story: one sub-group has drifted below the minimum intensity threshold (for example, ICAO Annex 14 treats a light as unserviceable when its main-beam average intensity falls below 50% of the specified value) due to gradual LED degradation, while sitting right next to fittings performing well within limits.

Visually, nothing separated the two groups. Photometrically, one group had already crossed into non-compliance. That single test turned a hidden, escalating risk into a scheduled replacement — before it became a safety finding during a night inspection or, worse, a pilot report.

This is what validated photometry gives airport operators, DGCA and safety auditors, and consultants: confidence that the lighting system is safe, compliant, and fit for purpose — not just installed to specification and assumed to stay that way.

12

Key Takeaways

  • Photometry measures intensity, distribution, colour, uniformity, and orientation — not just "how bright it looks"
  • Visual inspection and photometric testing answer different questions and cannot substitute for each other
  • Testing follows a repeatable workflow, from preparation through to compliance verification and reporting
  • Specific deviations in the data point toward specific root causes — intensity, alignment, colour, or uniformity
  • Photometry recurs across the AGL lifecycle: qualification, FAT, commissioning, monitoring, and troubleshooting
  • Without it, a fitting can look perfectly fine and still be operating outside its required performance envelope

AGL Photometry at a Glance

Measurement → Analysis → Compliance

Conclusion

AGL photometry is not an optional laboratory exercise. It is a core part of safety assurance and compliance for modern airfields, and it helps verify that the visual guidance presented to pilots matches the defined design and performance requirements. The extent and frequency of testing depend on the aerodrome’s regulator, approved design and maintenance programme.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is AGL photometry?

AGL photometry is the controlled, instrument-based measurement of the light a fitting actually produces, checked against the values in its design and the applicable standard. It uses calibrated equipment such as a photometer, goniophotometer or distribution measurement bench to record intensity, direction and colour, and it answers whether a light actually performs as it should or only looks like it does.

What does AGL photometry measure?

It covers light intensity, angular distribution (the isocandela diagram), horizontal and vertical beam spread, colour or chromaticity, uniformity, beam orientation (toe-in, elevation and aiming accuracy), intensity at defined angles and performance at the rated current and at each CCR brightness step.

Why does photometry matter for flight safety?

If photometric performance is incorrect, light may appear dim or washed out, glare may affect pilot vision, beam alignment may mislead aircraft positioning and colour outside chromaticity limits may be misread, for example white against yellow. Accurate photometry gives clear, unambiguous visual guidance during critical phases of flight.

Which standards define photometric requirements?

They can draw on ICAO Annex 14 Volume I (Appendix 1 for colours and Appendix 2 for light characteristics and isocandela diagrams) with guidance in Doc 9157, FAA advisory circulars and equipment specifications such as AC 150/5345-46 and the AC 150/5345-53 certification programme, the manufacturer's design data and project or tender requirements. Without validated photometry a fitting may fail inspection, delay commissioning or lead to audit findings.

How does visual inspection differ from photometric testing?

Visual inspection checks physical condition and usually gives a qualitative result, while photometric testing checks optical performance and gives a quantitative, instrument-based result. A light can look bright and still fail its required photometric performance.

How can photometric results help diagnose problems?

Each type of deviation points to different causes. Low intensity suggests LED or lamp degradation, lens contamination, moisture, incorrect circuit current or transformer and connector problems, while incorrect beam distribution points to tilt, damaged optics or misalignment. Colour deviation suggests source or filter degradation or a wrong LED batch, and non-uniformity suggests mixed batches, transformer mismatch or inconsistent aiming.

When should AGL photometry be performed?

It recurs across the lifecycle: product qualification and type testing, factory acceptance testing, installation verification, commissioning, periodic performance monitoring, troubleshooting, post-maintenance verification, LED replacement or upgrade and investigation of abnormal performance. The extent and frequency depend on the regulator, approved design and maintenance programme.

Why isn't a walk-round enough to catch degraded fittings?

The article gives an illustrative example of a taxiway edge circuit where a row of fittings looked identical on a walk-round, yet periodic photometry showed one sub-group had drifted below the minimum intensity threshold because of gradual LED degradation. Annex 14 treats a light as unserviceable when its main-beam average intensity falls below 50% of the specified value.
