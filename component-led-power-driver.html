LED Power Driver

An electronic power supply that provides a precisely regulated constant current or voltage to LED modules in AGL fixtures. Essential for ensuring stable light output, long LED life, and reliable operation under varying environmental conditions and input voltage fluctuations.

Overview

What Is It?

An LED Driver is an electronic device that regulates the power supplied to an LED module or array. Unlike traditional lamps that operate directly from the AC mains or a constant current source (like a CCR), LEDs require a precisely controlled current or voltage to operate efficiently and reliably. The LED driver converts the input power (which may be AC or DC, and may vary in voltage) into a stable, regulated output that matches the LED's requirements.

Key functions of an LED driver include:

  • Current Regulation: Providing a constant current (e.g., 350 mA, 700 mA, 1 A) to the LED module, regardless of input voltage fluctuations or temperature changes.
  • Voltage Regulation: Providing a constant voltage (e.g., 24 V, 48 V) when driving multiple LEDs in parallel.
  • Protection: Incorporating over‑current, over‑voltage, short‑circuit, and thermal protection to prevent damage to the LEDs and the driver itself.
  • Dimming Control: Supporting PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) or analogue dimming to adjust the brightness of the LED, often used in conjunction with the CCR's intensity steps.
  • Power Factor Correction (PFC): Ensuring that the driver draws power efficiently from the mains, minimising harmonic distortion.

In AGL applications, LED drivers are typically potted or encapsulated to withstand moisture, vibration, and temperature extremes, and they are designed to meet the stringent reliability requirements of airfield lighting.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The LED driver's purpose is to bridge the gap between whatever power is actually available — mains AC, a CCR's stepped current, a DC supply — and the precisely regulated current an LED module needs to produce stable, compliant light output, a translation that has no equivalent requirement for a simple incandescent or halogen lamp. Because an unregulated or fluctuating current would cause an LED's brightness and colour to drift and could shorten its life dramatically, the driver's current-regulation feedback loop is what allows LED fixtures to actually achieve their advertised advantages of long life, colour stability, and low maintenance. Its protection circuitry serves the same purpose from a different angle, shielding the comparatively delicate LED module from the overvoltage and short-circuit events that a hardier halogen lamp would simply tolerate.

Application

Where Is It Used?

LED drivers are used in virtually all LED‑based AGL fixtures, including:

  • Runway Edge Lights: Driving the LED arrays in elevated and inset runway edge lights.
  • Runway Centreline Lights: Supplying power to the multi‑LED modules in centreline fixtures.
  • Taxiway Edge and Centreline Lights: Providing regulated current to LED‑based taxiway guidance lights.
  • Approach Lighting: Driving the high‑intensity LED arrays in approach light fixtures.
  • Obstruction Lights: Powering the LEDs in obstruction warning beacons.
  • Apron Floodlights: Supplying the high‑power LED modules used in apron lighting.
  • PAPI and VASI Units: Driving the LED elements in visual approach slope indicators.

LED drivers are also used in:

  • Retrofit Projects: When upgrading existing halogen or incandescent AGL fixtures to LED, the driver is an essential component of the conversion.
  • New LED Fixtures: Integrated into the fixture housing or provided as a separate module.
  • Maintenance and Repair: Replacing faulty drivers in the field to restore fixture operation.
Placement

Installation & Location

The driver board mounts inside the fixture's base or lamp housing, positioned close to the LED PCB it feeds so the low-voltage DC leads stay short, with its own compartment or potting sealed against the same moisture and vibration environment as the rest of the fixture. Where the fixture is powered from a CCR series circuit, the driver's AC/series input terminates on the fixture's isolating transformer secondary rather than being wired directly to the field cable; where used in a mains-fed apron or building-mounted fixture, the driver's input wiring is kept on its own terminal block separate from the LED output leads. No separate field installation is required beyond what the host fixture's own housing and cabling already provide.

Function

How Does It Work?

An LED driver typically operates through several stages:

  • Input Stage: The driver accepts an input voltage, which may be from the AC mains (e.g., 230 V AC), from the AC output of a constant current regulator via the fixture's isolating transformer, or from a genuine DC source (e.g., a battery or DC bus in some retrofit designs). For AGL applications, drivers often accept a wide input range (e.g., 100–277 V AC) to accommodate varying supply conditions.
  • Power Conversion (AC‑DC): If the input is AC, the driver rectifies it to DC and uses a switching converter (e.g., flyback, buck, boost) to produce a stable, isolated DC output. This stage also includes power factor correction to improve efficiency and reduce harmonic currents.
  • Current/Voltage Regulation: The driver uses a feedback loop to maintain a constant output current (for series LED strings) or constant voltage (for parallel arrays). This is achieved using a controller IC that monitors the output and adjusts the switching duty cycle.
  • Protection Circuits: The driver includes over‑voltage protection (OVP), over‑current protection (OCP), short‑circuit protection (SCP), and overtemperature protection (OTP). These safeguards shut down or limit the output to prevent damage.
  • Dimming Interface: Many LED drivers support dimming via PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) or 0–10 V analogue signals. In AGL systems, dimming is often controlled by the CCR's current steps, which adjust the input voltage or current to the driver, or by a separate dimming bus.
  • Output Connection: The driver's output is connected to the LED module(s) via wires or connectors. The output voltage and current are matched to the LED specifications.

In AGL fixtures, the driver is typically potted (encapsulated in resin) to provide environmental protection and thermal management. The driver may be integrated into the fixture's base or housed in a separate compartment.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The LED driver sits between the airfield's loop-level control decision and the physical LED module, converting a system-level command it doesn't originate into a locally-regulated output. In a conventional series-circuit fixture, the driver's input is the loop current the CCR is already regulating, set by the ALCMS's chosen step; in a mains- or DC-bus-fed installation, the driver's dimming interface receives that same step translated into a PWM or 0–10 V signal instead. Either way, the driver has no independent authority over brightness — it only ever converts and regulates toward whatever intensity the upstream control layer has already decided on, using its own fast internal feedback loop (constant current or constant voltage regulation via the controller IC) to hold that output steady regardless of variation in its input supply. Its protection circuits (OVP, OCP, SCP, OTP) operate autonomously at this local level: a fault trips the driver's own shutdown or current-limit response immediately, without waiting for or reporting through the ALCMS, which is why a fixture's failure is generally first visible to the wider control system only indirectly, as a loop voltage anomaly at the CCR rather than as a driver-level fault message. This layered structure — system-level step command, driver-level conversion and fast local regulation, module-level output — is what lets the same ALCMS command scale correctly across many different driver designs and LED module ratings across the airfield.

Construction

Physical Construction

The driver is a compact power-conversion PCB — rectifier, PFC stage, switching regulator, and output filter built around a transformer or inductor core — typically 50–150mm long and 30–60mm wide, sized to fit inside the fixture housing alongside the LED array it feeds. For field fixtures the finished board is fully potted in epoxy resin inside its own small enclosure to reach an IP67/IP68 submersible rating and to isolate the primary and secondary sides to a 3kV standard, since a driver failure that bridges isolation is a safety fault, not just a lighting one. The potting also anchors the board against vibration from aircraft and vehicle traffic and conducts heat from the switching components out to the case, which is what the case-temperature-rated -40°C to +70°C operating range actually describes.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Overheating and Thermal Degradation

LED drivers generate heat during operation. Inadequate heat sinking or poor thermal contact with the fixture housing can cause the driver to overheat, leading to reduced efficiency, flickering, or premature failure. Ensure drivers are properly mounted with thermal paste or pads, and that the fixture provides adequate thermal management.

Moisture Ingress

Despite high IP ratings, moisture can penetrate through cable entry points, damaged potting, or connector seals, causing corrosion and short circuits. Regular inspection of seals and cable glands, and replacement of compromised units, is essential.

Surge and Transient Damage

Lightning strikes or switching transients on the power line can damage the driver's input stage. Installing external surge protection devices (SPDs) on the power supply line can help, but the driver itself should have robust surge protection (typically 2–4 kV).

Incompatibility with CCR Output

Some LED drivers are designed to operate directly from a constant current regulator (CCR) output (e.g., 6.6 A series circuit). If the driver is not compatible with the CCR's current steps and voltage compliance, it may not regulate properly or may overheat. Verify driver specifications before installation.

Dimming Control Issues

When dimming is used, improper PWM frequency or 0–10 V signal levels can cause flicker or incorrect brightness. Ensure the driver's dimming input matches the control signal from the ALCMS or local controller.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the CCR feeding the series circuit and verify zero energy before disconnecting or replacing the driver; the driver sits between the isolating transformer and the LED module and both sides must be confirmed de-energised.

ESD Precautions
Component Handling

Use anti-static precautions when handling the driver board; its regulation and diagnostic circuitry is sensitive to electrostatic discharge in a way a simple halogen lamp circuit is not.

Surge Exposure
Electrical Safety

Confirm surge protection is intact before re-energising, since the driver is a common failure point on circuits exposed to lightning-induced transients on the series loop.

Dimming Signal Verification
Compliance

Confirm the PWM frequency or 0-10V dimming signal level matches the driver's control input specification before returning the fixture to service; a mismatched signal can cause flicker or incorrect brightness even when the driver itself is healthy.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any driver replacement, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power to avoid tripping the CCR or damaging other fixtures on the circuit.

PPE

Restore to Compliant State

Confirm correct dimming response across all CCR intensity steps and any ALCMS diagnostic feedback before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Step Curve Matching Matters More Than Peak Output
Retrofit

Two drivers can both deliver correct full-intensity output yet still produce a visibly uneven runway if their brightness-vs-current curve at intermediate CCR steps doesn't track the original design. Check the whole step curve during commissioning, not just the top step.

Thermal Derating Is Often the Real Lifespan Limiter
Reliability

A driver rated for the fixture's electrical load can still underperform on service life if the housing's thermal design doesn't move heat away from it fast enough. Check the driver's actual operating temperature in situ against its rated derating curve rather than trusting the datasheet current rating alone.

Flicker Complaints Aren't Always the Driver's Fault
Troubleshooting

Visible flicker can originate upstream at the CCR's waveform or a mismatched dimming signal just as often as from the driver itself. Scope the actual dimming input signal at the driver terminals before swapping the part, since a healthy driver fed a bad signal will flicker too.

Drivers Absorb Surge Energy the Old Lamp Never Saw
Design

Incandescent lamps were largely indifferent to line transients; the electronics inside an LED driver are not. On circuits with a known lightning exposure history, verify the surge protection design was actually re-evaluated for the driver's sensitivity rather than carried over unchanged from the lamp-based design.

Diagnostic Feedback Is Only Useful If ALCMS Actually Reads It
Integration

Many drivers report richer fault/status data than a simple loop-continuity CCR check can see, but that data is wasted if the ALCMS integration was never configured to poll it. Confirm the diagnostic feedback path is actually wired into the monitoring system, not just present at the driver's terminals.

Don't Mix Driver Generations on One Runway
Maintenance

Replacing failed drivers one at a time over several years with whatever the current model happens to be can leave a runway with subtly different dimming curves fixture-to-fixture. Where possible, batch-replace by runway section rather than spot-replacing, so brightness uniformity doesn't slowly drift apart.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL LED drivers: what they do, input and output, dimming, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an LED driver?

An LED driver is an electronic device that regulates the power supplied to an LED module or array. LEDs need a precisely controlled current or voltage, so the driver converts whatever input is available, whether AC mains, DC or the stepped current from a CCR via an isolating transformer, into a stable regulated output.

Q2Why does an LED fixture need a driver when a halogen lamp does not?

An unregulated or fluctuating current would make an LED's brightness and colour drift and could shorten its life. A simple incandescent or halogen lamp has no equivalent requirement, so the driver bridges the gap between the available supply and the regulated current the LED needs.

Q3Which AGL fixtures use LED drivers?

Virtually all LED-based AGL fixtures: runway edge and centreline lights, taxiway edge and centreline lights, approach lighting, obstruction lights, apron floodlights and PAPI/VASI units.

Q4What are the typical driver specifications?

Input 100–277 V AC or 12–48 V DC, constant-current output of 350 mA, 700 mA, 1 A or 1.5 A (or constant-voltage 12/24/48 V), power factor of 0.95 or better, efficiency of 85–92% and 3 kV isolation. It carries OVP, OCP, SCP and OTP protection, with a case temperature range of −40 °C to +70 °C and IP67/IP68 potting.

Q5How is the driver powered on a CCR series circuit?

The driver's input terminates on the fixture's isolating transformer secondary rather than being wired directly to the series circuit. Because the CCR output is stepped current, the driver must be compatible with the CCR's output characteristics, which is checked during maintenance.

Q6How are LED drivers dimmed?

By PWM (above 200 Hz, per the FAA EB-67D flicker note for single-propeller aircraft) or by 0–10 V dimming where fitted. The dimming control should respond correctly across its full range.

Q7Where is the driver installed?

Inside the fixture's base or lamp housing, close to the LED PCB it feeds so the low-voltage DC leads stay short. It has its own compartment or potting sealed against the same moisture and vibration environment as the rest of the fixture.

Q8Which standards apply to LED drivers?

The main references are IEC 61347 (lamp controlgear), IEC 62384 (electronic control gear for LED modules), FAA Engineering Brief 67D (light sources other than incandescent and xenon, including driver and PWM dimming requirements) and ANSI C82.18. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should an LED driver be checked?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for thermal performance, and annual for enclosure seal inspection, surge protection check, CCR compatibility verification and dimming control check. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does my LED driver overheat, flicker or fail early?

Inadequate heat sinking or poor thermal contact between the driver and the fixture housing is the usual cause. Compare case temperature under load with the rated maximum, then re-apply thermal interface material, restore full mounting contact and improve fixture-side thermal management.

Q11Why is there corrosion or a short circuit despite a high IP rating?

Moisture has entered through cable entry points, damaged potting or a compromised connector seal. Inspect glands, potting and connector seals, replace damaged seals or the driver itself if potting has failed, and re-verify ingress protection after repair.
