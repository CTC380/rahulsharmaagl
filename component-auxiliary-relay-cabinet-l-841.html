Auxiliary Relay Cabinet (L-841)

A rugged, weatherproof enclosure housing relays, contactors, and control logic for remote switching and monitoring of AGL lighting circuits. Widely referred to in the field as an "L-841-style" cabinet after FAA's original relay-panel type, the modern Auxiliary Relay Cabinet provides a scalable, field‑deployable interface between the central ALCMS and distributed lighting loads, enabling reliable circuit control, status feedback, and fault detection in airfield environments.

Overview

What Is It?

An Auxiliary Relay Cabinet — commonly called an L-841 Relay Cabinet or Remote Relay Cabinet after FAA's original relay-panel type — is a standardised, industrial‑grade enclosure that houses the relay‑based switching and monitoring logic for airfield ground lighting circuits. It is typically installed in remote substations, runway edge cabinets, or equipment shelters, where it acts as the local control hub between the central ALCMS (or PLC) and the field lighting circuits.

Note on terminology: FAA's original L-841 specification (AC 150/5345‑13B) covers a much narrower device — a 20‑relay, DC‑powered pilot relay panel used specifically for Pilot‑Controlled Lighting (PCL) at unstaffed airports, with no networked communications or PLC logic. The industry has since extended the "L‑841" name to the broader family of networked, PLC/CCR‑integrated relay cabinets described on this page. Where strict FAA type‑approval to the 2007 spec is required, confirm the specific cabinet is qualified against AC 150/5345‑13B rather than assuming the features below apply.

The cabinet contains a combination of electromechanical relays, solid‑state contactors, interposing relays, and auxiliary control devices that perform the following functions:

  • Circuit Switching: Energising and de‑energising lighting circuits (runway edge, centreline, taxiway, approach) in response to commands from the ALCMS or local control panels.
  • Status Monitoring: Providing dry‑contact feedback to the ALCMS indicating the actual state of each circuit (ON/OFF), enabling confirmation that commands have been executed.
  • Brightness Step Control: Switching between multiple brightness levels (typically 3 to 5 steps) by selecting the appropriate CCR tap or contactor combination.
  • Interlocking & Safety: Implementing hardwired interlock logic to prevent conflicting operations (e.g., preventing a circuit from being energised when maintenance access is required).
  • Fault Detection: Monitoring auxiliary contacts of circuit breakers and contactors to detect and report faults such as overloads, short circuits, or contactor failures.

The cabinet is designed for harsh outdoor environments, featuring a robust metallic enclosure (typically stainless steel or powder‑coated mild steel) with an IP66 or NEMA 4X rating, providing protection against dust, water jets, and ice formation. It is equipped with terminal blocks for field wiring, clearly labelled to simplify installation and maintenance, and often includes a local HMI (or status indication panel) for manual override and diagnostics.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The L-841 cabinet exists to give the central ALCMS a physical point of execution and confirmation out in the field, since a control command issued from the control room has no real effect until something local actually switches the circuit and reports back that it did so correctly. By combining switching, status feedback, brightness-step control, interlocking, and fault detection in one cabinet, it closes the loop between "the operator asked for this" and "the field confirms this is actually happening," which matters because a command that silently fails to execute is arguably more dangerous than an obvious failure — it gives the false impression that lighting is correctly configured when it isn't. Its hardwired interlock logic serves a distinct purpose within that same cabinet: some safety conditions (like preventing energisation during maintenance access) are critical enough that they need to be enforced at the local hardware level, independent of software or communication links back to the ALCMS that could themselves fail or be delayed.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The L-841 Auxiliary Relay Cabinet is deployed in various locations across the airfield where distributed control of lighting circuits is required, including:

  • Remote CCR Substations: Acting as the local switching interface for CCRs, enabling remote control of circuit energisation and brightness selection.
  • Runway Edge Lighting Cabinets: Controlling individual runway edge lighting circuits, with status feedback to the ALCMS.
  • Taxiway Lighting Nodes: Managing taxiway edge and centreline lighting in specific zones, enabling selective switching for maintenance or operational flexibility.
  • Approach Lighting Control Shelters: Providing the switching and interlocking logic for approach light barrettes and sequenced flashing systems.
  • Apron & Gate Lighting: Controlling apron floodlights and gate guidance lights, with local override capability for ground crew.
  • Isolated Intersection Lighting: Providing independent control of runway‑taxiway intersection lighting, ensuring proper sequencing and interlocking.
  • Legacy System Modernisation: Retrofitting to replace obsolete relay logic with a modern, maintainable cabinet while preserving existing field wiring.
Placement

Installation & Location

The L-841 cabinet is installed close to the circuits it switches — inside or beside a remote CCR substation, in a runway edge or taxiway lighting cabinet, or in an approach lighting control shelter — so the heavy relay and contactor wiring to the field circuits stays short while a lighter control and feedback cable runs back to the ALCMS or panel commanding it. It's floor- or wall-mounted in a dedicated cabinet with enough internal space and ventilation for its relay bank, and positioned for technician access to individual relays during fault-finding or maintenance. Where it's retrofitted to replace obsolete relay logic, it's installed in the same physical location as the equipment it replaces so the existing field wiring can be reconnected without rerouting cable through the site.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Auxiliary Relay Cabinet operates as a distributed control node, receiving commands from the central ALCMS (or PLC) and translating them into physical switching actions on the field lighting circuits. Key functional aspects include:

  • Command Reception: The cabinet receives digital commands from the ALCMS via a communication link (typically serial RS‑485, Modbus, or Ethernet). These commands include circuit ON/OFF requests, brightness step selections, and mode changes.
  • Relay Logic Execution: The received commands are processed by the relay logic — either through hardwired relay circuits or a programmable logic controller (PLC) within the cabinet. The logic evaluates interlocks, safety conditions, and sequencing requirements before energising the appropriate output relays.
  • Switching Action: The output relays (or contactors) switch the control voltage to the CCRs or directly to the lighting circuit contactors, energising or de‑energising the circuits as commanded. For brightness control, the cabinet selects the appropriate CCR tap or auxiliary contactor to achieve the desired current level (e.g., Step 1 through Step 5).
  • Status Feedback: Auxiliary contacts on the relays and contactors provide a dry‑contact status signal back to the ALCMS, confirming that the switching action has been successfully executed. This feedback enables the ALCMS to display the actual circuit state and to detect failures (e.g., a relay that did not change state).
  • Fault Monitoring: The cabinet continuously monitors for abnormal conditions, such as contactor failure, loss of control power, or communication loss. These faults are reported to the ALCMS as alarms, enabling rapid intervention.
  • Local Override: The cabinet typically includes a local control panel (or pushbuttons) that allows maintenance personnel to manually override the automatic control, enabling testing and troubleshooting without ALCMS involvement.

The cabinet is powered from a dedicated 120/230 VAC supply (or 24 VDC for low‑power versions) and includes surge protection, circuit breakers, and fusing to protect both the cabinet and the field circuits. The internal wiring is colour‑coded and clearly labelled, following standard airfield wiring practices to facilitate troubleshooting.

Construction

Physical Construction

The cabinet is a free-standing or wall-mounted enclosure in stainless steel (304/316) or powder-coated mild steel, gasketed and rated IP66/NEMA 4X so it can be sited outdoors or in an unconditioned equipment room close to the circuits it switches. Inside, contactors or solid-state relays sized to the cabinet's output current rating are mounted on a back-panel or DIN rail, one per controlled circuit, wired through screw- or spring-clamp terminal blocks rated for the heavier AWG 14-8 field cabling typical of lighting circuit feeders. A local HMI — LED indicators, pushbuttons, or an optional touchscreen — is fitted to the door or a front sub-panel for on-site status checks and manual override, while surge arresters on the power and communication lines protect the contactors and any networked controller card from switching transients and induced surges. The cabinet's door is gasketed and secured with cam latches or a lockable handle, and an optional internal heater and thermostat keep condensation and cold-start contactor issues under control in exposed, cold-climate installations.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Contactor Coil Burnout

Frequent switching or extended holding periods can cause contactor coils to overheat and fail, especially in poorly ventilated cabinets. Use of solid‑state relays or contactors with auxiliary interlocking can reduce wear. Regularly inspect contactors for signs of overheating (discolouration, burnt odour).

Communication Loss with ALCMS

Loss of communication with the central ALCMS can leave the cabinet in an unknown state, potentially locking out control. Implement a "fail‑safe" or "last‑state" logic and ensure the cabinet can continue operating in a degraded mode. Use redundant communication paths (e.g., dual RS‑485 or Ethernet) where possible.

Corrosion of Terminals and Wiring

In coastal or high‑humidity environments, terminal screws and wiring can corrode, leading to poor connections and intermittent faults. Use plated terminals and anti‑oxidant grease. Periodically check and re‑torque all terminal connections.

Incorrect Relay Configuration

Relays that are mis‑wired or mis‑configured (e.g., wrong voltage, incorrect contact arrangement) can cause circuits to fail to switch or to switch in the wrong sequence. Always verify wiring against the schematic during installation, and perform thorough functional testing before commissioning.

Overheating in Enclosures

Relay cabinets generate significant heat, especially in warm climates. Inadequate ventilation or cooling can cause relays to fail prematurely. Ensure proper cabinet ventilation (louvers, fans) and install heaters for cold climates to prevent condensation and icing.

Lightning Induced Surges

Lightning strikes or power surges can damage relays, contactors, and control electronics. Ensure that the cabinet includes adequate surge protection and that the earthing system is properly installed and maintained. Regularly inspect surge arresters for signs of degradation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Contactor Fails to Energise or Trips Out Repeatedly
Likely Cause
Coil burnout from overheating in a poorly ventilated cabinet, or excessive switching/holding duty on the contactor.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coil for discolouration or a burnt odour; check cabinet ambient temperature and ventilation; review switching frequency and holding duration against the contactor's rating.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed coil/contactor, improve cabinet ventilation, and consider a solid-state relay where switching duty is high.
Cabinet Locks Out or Behaves Unpredictably After a Comms Drop
Likely Cause
Loss of communication with the central ALCMS leaving the cabinet without defined fail-safe logic.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the communication link status and recent loss-of-comms events; review whether fail-safe/last-state logic is configured and active.
Corrective Action
Restore the communication path, implement or verify fail-safe/last-state logic so the cabinet degrades gracefully, and add redundant communication paths where possible.
Intermittent Faults or No-Response on a Specific Circuit
Likely Cause
Corroded terminal screws or wiring, common in coastal or high-humidity environments.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect terminals and wiring for corrosion, discolouration, or looseness; measure connection resistance across suspect terminals.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace corroded terminals, apply anti-oxidant grease, and re-torque all terminal connections to specification.
Circuit Fails to Switch or Switches in the Wrong Sequence
Likely Cause
Relay mis-wiring or misconfiguration — wrong voltage rating or incorrect contact arrangement.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify wiring against the as-built schematic for the affected relay; check the relay's voltage rating and contact configuration against the design.
Corrective Action
Correct the wiring or replace the relay with the correctly specified type, then re-run a full functional sequence test.
Cabinet Interior Running Hot
Likely Cause
Inadequate ventilation or cooling for the heat generated by relays and contactors, especially in warm climates.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure interior cabinet temperature under normal load; inspect louvers, fans, and filters for blockage or failure.
Corrective Action
Clear or repair ventilation paths, add or service cooling fans, and fit heaters in cold climates to prevent condensation and icing on the same system.
Relays or Electronics Damaged Following a Storm
Likely Cause
Lightning-induced surge or power transient exceeding the cabinet's surge protection capacity.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect surge arresters for signs of degradation or having triggered; check the earthing system's continuity and resistance.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded surge arresters, verify and repair the earthing system, and replace any relays/electronics confirmed damaged by the surge.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Contactor Coil Burnout
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated switching cycles, an undervoltage condition, or a mechanically sticking contactor forcing the coil to hold under stall conditions.
Effect
The contactor fails to energise or trips out repeatedly, taking the circuit it switches out of service.
Detection Method
Contactor and relay inspection, and an undervoltage/overcurrent trip test to confirm the coil and its protection are within tolerance.
Communication Loss with ALCMS
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Network cable damage, a failed communication card, or a configuration change breaking the cabinet's reporting link to the ALCMS.
Effect
The cabinet locks out or behaves unpredictably after a comms drop, depending on how its fail-safe logic is configured to respond to lost supervisory contact.
Detection Method
Communication link test and review of the connection status log against the ALCMS.
Corrosion of Terminals and Wiring
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Humid conditions or a degraded enclosure seal allowing moisture into the cabinet over time.
Effect
Intermittent faults or no-response on a specific circuit that traces back to a corroded terminal rather than a relay or contactor fault.
Detection Method
Terminal torque and corrosion check as part of scheduled maintenance.
Incorrect Relay Configuration
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A wiring error or configuration change during commissioning or later modification that doesn't match the intended relay sequence logic.
Effect
A circuit fails to switch or switches in the wrong sequence, even though every individual relay and contactor tests healthy on its own.
Detection Method
Relay sequence functional test confirming the actual switching order matches the documented design sequence.
Overheating in Enclosures
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Inadequate ventilation or a failed cabinet heater/cooling provision allowing internal temperature to rise beyond the equipment's rated range.
Effect
The cabinet interior runs hot, accelerating component ageing and increasing the risk of a heat-related contactor or relay failure.
Detection Method
Cabinet ventilation and heater check, combined with a thermal check of the interior under normal operating load.
Lightning-Induced Surges
High Severity
Root Cause
A transient overvoltage entering the cabinet through field wiring during a nearby lightning strike, exceeding the installed surge protection's capacity.
Effect
Relays or electronics are damaged following a storm, sometimes in a way that isn't obvious until the affected circuit is next commanded.
Detection Method
Surge arrester inspection after any significant storm event, and a functional check of circuits fed through the cabinet.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Lock Out Before Opening the Cabinet
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out incoming supply and control circuits before opening the cabinet; relay and contactor coils, and the lighting circuits they switch, can carry high voltage series-circuit energy.

Arc Flash Awareness at Contactors
Personnel Safety

Treat contactor and relay compartments as a potential arc-flash source when energised; use appropriate PPE and avoid working inside the cabinet while it is live.

Do Not Bypass Interlocks
Compliance

Never jumper or defeat hardwired interlock logic to clear a fault or restore service; correct the root cause instead, since interlocks prevent conflicting circuit operations and unsafe access conditions.

Surge Protection Must Remain in Place
Electrical Safety

Do not remove or bypass surge suppression devices after a lightning event; replace them per the manufacturer's rating and verify protection is restored before re-energising.

Enclosure Integrity After Service
Operational

Reinstate door seals and gland plates correctly and confirm the enclosure rating (IP66/NEMA 4X) is not compromised before closing out the work, to keep moisture and pests out of the relay logic.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger the affected lighting circuit before restoring power after any wiring or relay work, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy equipment.

Confirm Status Feedback Matches Actual State
Compliance

After maintenance, verify that ALCMS status feedback for each circuit matches its actual energised state before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Arc Flash Risk Doesn't Track Cabinet Size
Electrical Safety

A compact relay cabinet feeding a small number of circuits can still present a serious arc flash hazard at its contactors depending on the upstream fault current available, regardless of how modest the cabinet itself looks. Base PPE and approach distance on the actual arc flash study for the panel, not on a visual impression of its size.

Interlock Bypasses Tend to Outlive Their Original Reason
Safety Design

An interlock bypassed to work through a specific fault condition often stays in place well after that condition is resolved, simply because removing it isn't anyone's explicit follow-up task. Log every bypass with an owner and a planned removal date rather than relying on someone remembering to undo it.

Surge Protection Removed "Temporarily" Rarely Gets Reinstated Quickly
Reliability

Pulling a surge protection device to troubleshoot an unrelated fault, then closing up the cabinet without reinstating it, leaves the whole relay circuit exposed to the next transient event with no obvious external sign that protection is missing. Treat SPD reinstatement as a mandatory line item on the close-out checklist, not an assumed step.

Status Feedback Mismatches Point to Wiring, Not Just Logic
Troubleshooting

When ALCMS status doesn't match a circuit's actual energised state, the fault is at least as likely to be a feedback contact or wiring issue inside the cabinet as a software or PLC logic problem. Verify feedback wiring physically before spending time troubleshooting the control system logic.

Enclosure Integrity Affects Contactor Life, Not Just Weatherproofing
Durability

A compromised door seal or gasket doesn't just risk moisture ingress; dust and humidity ingress into a relay cabinet accelerates contact wear and can cause nuisance tripping well before an obvious fault develops. Verify enclosure seal integrity as part of routine inspection, not only after visible water ingress is found.

Insulation Testing After Relay Work Catches What Functional Testing Misses
Compliance

A relay that switches correctly during a functional test can still have a marginal insulation fault introduced during the work, one that a simple energise-and-observe check won't reveal. Run an insulation test before re-energising after any internal work, even when the functional check already looks clean.
