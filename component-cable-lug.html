Cable Lug

A copper or aluminium termination device used to securely connect cable conductors to bus bars, transformers, earth bars, and other equipment in AGL power distribution systems. Available in crimp, solder, and bolted variants, it ensures low‑resistance, reliable electrical connections that withstand the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Cable Lug (also known as a cable terminal or termination lug) is a metallic connector used to terminate the end of a cable conductor, providing a secure interface for bolting, soldering, or crimping to a bus bar, terminal block, isolating transformer, earthing system, or other electrical equipment. In airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems, cable lugs are essential for creating reliable, low‑resistance connections in both primary and secondary circuits.

Cable lugs are typically manufactured from high‑conductivity copper (tinned or bare) or aluminium, and are designed to match the conductor size and termination method. They feature a barrel for cable insertion (which is crimped or soldered) and a flat tongue or pad with a hole for bolting to the equipment. They are available in a wide range of sizes, configurations, and plating options to suit the diverse requirements of AGL installations, including compatibility with copper, aluminium, and bi‑metallic connections.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

A cable lug's purpose is to preserve the conductor's own low resistance all the way through the point where it ends and bolts to something else, a transition that would otherwise be the weakest link in the circuit. A crimped or soldered lug creates a gas-tight, high-surface-area joint between the stranded conductor and the terminal pad, so that current can cross the interface without concentrating heat at any single strand. Getting the metal pairing right, matching copper to copper or using a bi-metallic lug where copper meets aluminium, matters because a poorly matched termination corrodes over time through galvanic action, gradually raising resistance at exactly the point where a failure is hardest to spot during a routine visual inspection.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Cable lugs are used throughout the airfield wherever cable terminations are required, including:

  • CCR Output Terminals: Terminating primary cables at the output of Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) using heavy‑duty lugs.
  • Isolating Transformer Connections: Connecting secondary cables from the transformer to the light fixture or sign.
  • Earthing and Bonding: Terminating bare copper earth cables to earth pits, earth electrodes, and equipment grounding points.
  • Distribution Panels: Terminating incoming and outgoing cables at terminal blocks and bus bars in junction boxes and distribution panels.
  • Receptacle and Connector Wiring: Connecting wires inside L‑823 connectors and other primary/secondary connectors.
  • Fixture Wiring: Internal wiring inside light fixtures, signs, and beacons, connecting the transformer secondary to the lamp or LED driver.

The lug is selected based on the cable size (conductor cross‑section), material (copper or aluminium), termination method (crimp, solder, or bolt), and the equipment interface (stud size, orientation, and plating). Proper selection and installation are critical for maintaining low contact resistance and preventing overheating or failure.

Placement

Installation & Location

The lug is installed at the point where a cable conductor terminates — a CCR output stud, transformer terminal, earth bar, or terminal block — with the conductor stripped to the length specified for the barrel and crimped (or soldered) using tooling and a die matched to the lug and conductor size, since an incorrect crimp is one of the more common causes of a high-resistance joint. The lug's tongue is then bolted to its terminal pad at the torque specified for the stud size and plating, with a flat washer and, where dissimilar metals meet, an anti-oxidant compound or bi-metallic barrier used to limit corrosion. Sufficient cable slack is left on the approach to the lug so the termination isn't placed under mechanical strain, and lugs used outdoors or below grade are generally covered with heat-shrink or a boot to keep moisture off the crimped joint. Positioning keeps the finished termination clear of adjacent live parts and accessible for the periodic resistance or thermal checks used to catch a degrading connection before it fails.

Function

How Does It Work?

A cable lug provides a secure, low‑resistance electrical termination for a cable conductor. Its design and installation ensure a reliable connection that can carry the required current without excessive heating or voltage drop. The typical functional elements include:

  • Cable Barrel: A cylindrical section that accepts the stripped conductor. The barrel is crimped (using a hex‑shaped or indenting crimp tool) or soldered to the conductor, creating a gas‑tight, low‑resistance joint. For aluminium conductors, the barrel may be filled with a corrosion‑inhibiting compound.
  • Flat Tongue/Pad: A flattened section with a hole for bolting to the equipment. The hole size matches the stud or bolt diameter, and the pad provides a large surface area for low contact resistance and mechanical strength.
  • Plating: Copper lugs are often tin‑plated to resist corrosion and prevent oxidation, which can increase resistance. Silver or nickel plating is used for high‑temperature or high‑reliability applications. Aluminium lugs are typically unplated but may be coated with a protective layer.
  • Insulation (optional): Some lugs are supplied with a pre‑moulded insulating sleeve or can be fitted with heat‑shrink tubing after termination to provide electrical insulation and moisture sealing.
  • Bi‑Metallic Design (optional): For connecting aluminium cables to copper bus bars, bi‑metallic lugs are used to prevent galvanic corrosion. These lugs have an aluminium barrel and a copper tongue, welded or friction‑welded together.

When properly installed with the correct crimping tool and torque, a cable lug connection can carry its rated current indefinitely with minimal resistance, ensuring the integrity and reliability of the AGL circuit.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A cable lug is a termination point in the series circuit loop — typically where the primary cable meets an isolating transformer tail, a CCR output terminal, or a bus bar — and it carries the full loop current at that junction. Like every other passive element in the loop, it is not individually addressable and generates no signal of its own; the ALCMS and CCR only see the loop as a whole. A correctly crimped lug adds negligible resistance and is electrically transparent to the control system. Over time, however, a loose bolt, a poorly crimped barrel, or galvanic corrosion at a mismatched metal pairing increases contact resistance at that single point, and because the CCR regulates current rather than voltage per node, that added resistance shows up as a rise in overall loop voltage at the commanded current step rather than as a fault flagged at the terminal itself. In more severe cases the termination heats under load, accelerating its own degradation, until the connection opens entirely and the CCR reports a standard open-circuit loop fault — again with no indication of which of the loop's many terminations caused it until the loop is sectionalised and each termination is checked in the field.

Construction

Physical Construction

A cable lug is a two‑part shape formed from a single piece of metal: a hollow barrel at one end, sized to accept the stripped conductor, and a flat tongue or pad at the other, punched or drilled with a bolt hole for connection to the equipment terminal. The barrel is either crimped with a hydraulic or mechanical tool, which deforms the metal inward to grip the conductor strands and squeeze out air gaps, or soldered, where molten solder is drawn into the barrel to bond it to the conductor; some designs allow either method. Material and plating are matched to the conductor and connection type, with tinned copper used for corrosion resistance in earthing and general power applications, bare copper for pure copper‑to‑copper joints, and aluminium or bi‑metallic (copper‑aluminium transition) lugs specified wherever an aluminium conductor must terminate onto copper equipment, since a direct copper‑aluminium joint would otherwise corrode galvanically.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Lug Size Selection

Using a lug with a barrel that is too large or too small for the conductor will result in a poor crimp, high resistance, and potential overheating. Always match the lug's barrel size to the conductor cross‑section, and use the manufacturer's recommended crimping tool and die set.

Improper Crimping Technique

Under‑crimping or over‑crimping can cause a high‑resistance joint or damage the conductor. Use the correct die set, ensure full closure of the crimping tool, and perform a pull‑test to verify the connection. Follow the tool manufacturer's instructions.

Corrosion and Oxidation

Untinned copper lugs can oxidise over time, increasing resistance. In coastal or industrial environments, corrosion is accelerated. Use tin‑plated or silver‑plated lugs, apply anti‑oxidant compound, and ensure the connection is properly torqued to prevent moisture ingress.

Insufficient Torque on Bolted Connections

Loose bolted connections can cause arcing, overheating, and eventual failure. Use a torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value (typically 10‑30 N·m for M6‑M12 studs) and use lock washers or thread‑locking compound to prevent loosening.

Galvanic Corrosion (Bi‑Metallic Connections)

Connecting aluminium cables directly to copper bus bars without a bi‑metallic lug can cause galvanic corrosion. Always use bi‑metallic lugs for aluminium‑to‑copper connections and apply anti‑oxidant compound.

Inadequate Cable Stripping

Stripping too much or too little insulation from the cable can cause the conductor to enter the lug incorrectly, leading to a poor connection. Use the correct stripping tool and follow the manufacturer's strip length instructions.

Mechanical Stress on the Lug

If the cable is not properly supported, mechanical stress on the lug can cause it to fracture or pull out. Use cable ties, clamps, or strain relief brackets to support the cable near the termination point.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Lug Runs Hot or Shows Discoloration in Service
Likely Cause
Incorrect lug size for the conductor, or under/over-crimping, has produced a high-resistance connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the lug barrel size against the conductor cross-section on the datasheet; inspect the crimp indent against the tool manufacturer's spec; check for heat discoloration.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with a correctly sized lug and the specified crimping tool/die, then verify with a pull test.
Loose or Arcing Bolted Connection
Likely Cause
Insufficient torque at installation, or thermal cycling over time, has allowed the bolted connection to loosen.
Diagnostic Steps
Check bolt torque against the manufacturer's specified value with a torque wrench; look for arc marks or pitting at the contact face.
Corrective Action
Re-torque to spec using lock washers or thread-locking compound, and replace the lug if contact-face pitting is present.
Green or White Corrosion Product at the Lug
Likely Cause
Untinned copper oxidation, or galvanic corrosion from an aluminium-to-copper connection made without a bi-metallic lug.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the lug and conductor interface for corrosion product; confirm whether dissimilar metals are joined directly.
Corrective Action
Clean and replace with a tin- or silver-plated lug (or a bi-metallic lug for aluminium-to-copper joints), and apply anti-oxidant compound.
Lug Pulls Out or Fractures
Likely Cause
Lack of strain relief has let mechanical stress from cable movement act directly on the lug, or the cable was stripped incorrectly leaving the conductor poorly seated.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the failed lug and conductor end for signs of incomplete insertion or stress fracture; check for cable support near the termination.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with correct stripping and a fresh lug, and add cable ties, clamps, or strain-relief brackets to support the cable near the joint.
Conductor Not Fully Seated in Barrel
Likely Cause
Cable stripped to the wrong length, so the conductor did not reach the full depth of the lug barrel before crimping.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect strip length against the manufacturer's instructions; if the lug can be safely removed, check for exposed conductor short of full barrel depth.
Corrective Action
Re-strip to the correct length and re-crimp with a new lug, confirming full conductor insertion before crimping.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Terminating
Electrical Safety

Never crimp or bolt a lug onto a live series-circuit conductor; de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy before stripping or terminating cable.

Correct Tool, Die & Torque
Workmanship

Use the crimp tool and die matched to the lug and conductor size, or torque bolted connections to the manufacturer's specified value; an incorrect crimp or under/over-torqued bolt is a common source of hot-spot failures.

Dissimilar Metal Connections
Workmanship

Never join aluminium and copper conductors directly at a lug; use a bi-metallic lug and appropriate anti-oxidant compound to prevent galvanic corrosion at the joint.

Sharp Edge & Pinch Hazard
Personnel Safety

Handle lug barrels and crimping tools with care to avoid cuts or pinch injuries, and wear appropriate gloves suited to the crimping or bolting operation.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Check crimp integrity, torque, and conductor seating before restoring power; a marginal termination found after re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit terminations, and confirm bonding/grounding is intact per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a termination without adequate strain relief or with an unrecorded crimp/torque value; verify and document the connection before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Hot-Spot Failures Often Start as a Fine Crimp
Durability

A technically-in-spec crimp made with a worn or slightly mismatched die can still pass initial resistance checks yet develop a hot spot months later as the crimp relaxes under thermal cycling; inspect and calibrate crimp dies on a schedule, not just when a failure prompts it.

Anti-Oxidant Compound Needs Reapplication, Not Just Presence
Workmanship

Anti-oxidant compound on an aluminium lug can migrate or dry out if the connection sits open too long before final crimping; apply it as the last step before crimping, not early in the prep sequence.

Strip Length Errors Are Invisible After Crimping
Workmanship

A conductor stripped too short leaves insulation trapped under the crimp barrel, while too long leaves bare copper exposed beyond it; both defects are hidden once the lug is crimped, so verify strip length against the lug's spec before crimping, not after.

Track Termination Resistance Over Successive Visits
Troubleshooting

A marginally high-resistance termination often measures within tolerance at any single visit but trends upward across visits before it fails; logging resistance readings over time catches this pattern that a single spot-check cannot.

Match Lug Barrel Length to Conductor Stranding, Not Just Gauge
Design

Fine-stranded and coarse-stranded conductors of the same nominal gauge can need different barrel lengths or crimp profiles to achieve full contact area; check the lug manufacturer's stranding chart rather than gauge alone.

Uninsulated Lugs Need Their Own Seal Plan
Durability

A bolted, uninsulated lug connection buried or in a wet base can needs a separate moisture-exclusion method such as heat-shrink or a grease cap; the lug itself provides no seal, and assuming the enclosure alone is enough overlooks the connection's own corrosion risk.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about cable lugs: function, materials, bi-metallic use, torque, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a cable lug?

A cable lug, also called a cable terminal or termination lug, is a metallic connector that terminates a cable conductor so it can be bolted, soldered or crimped to a bus bar, terminal block, isolating transformer, earthing system or other equipment. It has a barrel that takes the conductor and a flat tongue with a hole for bolting.

Q2What is the purpose of a cable lug in an AGL system?

It preserves the conductor's low resistance through the point where the cable ends and bolts to something else, which would otherwise be the weakest link. A crimped or soldered lug forms a gas-tight, high-surface-area joint so current crosses the interface without concentrating heat at individual strands.

Q3Where are cable lugs used in AGL?

At CCR output terminals, isolating transformer connections, earthing and bonding terminations, distribution panels and terminal blocks, wiring inside L-823 connectors, and internal wiring in light fixtures, signs and beacons.

Q4What are cable lugs made of?

Copper (EC grade, tinned or bare), aluminium, or bi-metallic. Copper lugs are often tin-plated to resist corrosion, with silver or nickel plating for high-temperature or high-reliability use. Tin-plated lugs are rated -40°C to +105°C and silver-plated up to +200°C.

Q5When do I need a bi-metallic lug?

When an aluminium cable connects to a copper bus bar or terminal. A bi-metallic lug has an aluminium barrel and a copper tongue, joined by welding or friction welding, to prevent galvanic corrosion. Joining aluminium and copper directly with a standard lug causes rising joint resistance over time.

Q6How do I select the right cable lug?

Match it to the conductor cross-section, material (copper or aluminium), termination method (crimp, solder or bolted) and the equipment interface (stud size, orientation and plating). Typical ranges are 1.5 mm² to 300 mm² conductors and M4 to M20 stud holes, with straight, 45° or 90° orientation.

Q7What torque and contact resistance should a lug termination have?

Contact resistance should be 0.5 mΩ or less per properly crimped or bolted termination. Bolted connections must be torqued to the manufacturer's value for the stud size (typically 10 to 30 N·m for M6 to M12 studs), since both under- and over-torque raise resistance.

Q8Which standards apply to cable lugs?

The main references are IEC 61238-1 (compression and mechanical connectors for power cables), UL 486A-486B (wire connectors and soldering lugs) and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program). Always verify the current edition.

Q9How often should lug connections be checked?

Annually: re-torque bolted connections, check corrosion and anti-oxidant compound, verify bi-metallic connections, thermally scan under load and check strain relief. Perform a pull-test on crimped connections immediately after crimping. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What causes a cable lug to overheat or fail?

The usual causes are the wrong lug size for the conductor, under- or over-crimping, insufficient torque on bolted joints, corrosion or oxidation of untinned copper, galvanic corrosion from unprotected aluminium-to-copper joints, incorrect stripping length, and mechanical stress from missing strain relief.
