AGL Connectors

The electrical connections that join AGL primary cable to isolating transformers, and secondary cable to light fixtures. Small, often overlooked, and one of the most common sources of series-circuit faults in the field.

Overview

What Are They?

AGL connectors are the plug-and-socket or crimped/moulded joints that make and break electrical continuity between sections of AGL cable and equipment. On the primary side, they join the primary series-circuit cable to the isolating transformer's primary leads. On the secondary side, they join the transformer's secondary leads to the light fixture, most commonly through the L-823-style connector family referenced in FAA specifications and widely adopted internationally.

Because every fixture in a series circuit sits behind at least one primary connector and one secondary connector, connector quality and installation workmanship have an outsized effect on overall circuit reliability compared to their size and cost.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Connectors exist to make the countless individual joints in an AGL series circuit as reliable as the cable and equipment they connect, which matters more than their small size and low cost would suggest — because every single fixture in a series loop depends on at least two of them working perfectly, a single degraded connector can take out one light or, in a worse case, interrupt the whole loop. Their watertight sealing and low-resistance contact design exist specifically to survive being buried or submerged in a pit for years without a maintenance visit, since a connector that lets in moisture or develops resistance at the joint is quietly generating heat and degrading insulation long before it causes an obvious failure. In effect, connectors are the weak-link risk in an AGL circuit that good design and correct installation are meant to eliminate.

Application

Where Are They Used?

Connectors appear at every equipment interface in an AGL series circuit:

  • Primary Connectors: Join primary series-circuit cable to the primary leads of each isolating transformer, typically inside a below-grade pit or handhole.
  • Secondary Connectors: Join the transformer's secondary cable to the light fixture, sign, or sensor being powered.
  • Cable-to-Cable Joints: Splice or extend primary cable runs between equipment positions where a continuous run isn't practical.
  • Elevated and Inset Fixtures: Both configurations rely on the same connector principle, differing mainly in housing and sealing method.

Connector types and part numbers vary by specification family — L-823 primary and secondary connectors are the most widely referenced in FAA-derived AGL systems, with equivalent designs used under ICAO- and IEC-based specifications.

Placement

Installation & Location

Primary connectors are made up inside a below-grade pit or handhole adjacent to each isolating transformer, with enough cable slack looped in on both sides of the joint to allow it to be unmated and pulled clear for servicing without straining the termination. Secondary connectors are made up inside the fixture base or a dedicated junction chamber at the transformer's secondary side, kept short and direct given how directly transformer-to-fixture voltage drop affects light output. Regardless of which side of the circuit a connector sits on, its coupling ring or locking mechanism is threaded or engaged fully home so the internal seals are properly compressed, since a partially mated connector is one of the most common sources of later water ingress. Connector type and part number are matched to the specific cable gauge and current rating of the circuit at each interface, with mismatched connectors avoided even where they might physically mate, since compatibility beyond just fit is what keeps contact resistance and sealing performance within specification.

Function

How Do They Work?

An AGL connector's job is simple in principle: maintain a low-resistance, weatherproof electrical joint under buried or submerged conditions, repeated thermal cycling, and years of unattended service. Most primary connectors use a moulded rubber or silicone boot with mated pin-and-socket contacts, compressed by cable tension or a locking mechanism to exclude moisture. Secondary connectors follow the same principle at lower current.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Watertight Sealing: The connector must resist moisture ingress even when submerged in a flooded pit — this is the single most common failure mode in the field.
  • Low Contact Resistance: A poor mechanical connection increases resistance, generating heat and accelerating insulation degradation at the joint.
  • Mechanical Retention: The connector must resist being pulled apart by cable tension, vibration, or routine maintenance handling.
  • Compatibility: Primary and secondary connectors must match the cable gauge, current rating, and connector family specified for the installation.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Connectors have no control logic of their own — they are passive electrical joints, not active components. Their place in the system's architecture is structural rather than functional: every fixture's connection to the CCR's control and fault-detection logic runs physically through a primary connector, the isolating transformer, and a secondary connector, so the condition of those joints determines whether the real control layer above them (the CCR and, above that, the ALCMS) can actually reach and monitor the fixture at all.

Because the CCR detects faults by watching loop voltage against commanded current, a connector that has developed resistance from a compromised seal shows up in exactly the same way as a failed lamp or transformer — as a loop-level fault — with no way for the CCR to distinguish a bad joint from a bad fixture without further sectionalised testing.

CCR → Primary Connector → Isolating Transformer → Secondary Connector → Fixture (loop-level feedback)

Function

Circuit & Fault Overview

Both connector types share the same failure logic — the joint's job is to stay watertight and low-resistance for years underground, so almost every field fault traces back to which side of this fork the joint fell on at installation.

Construction

Physical Construction

Construction follows the connector's role rather than a single standard design. L-823-style plug and receptacle bodies are moulded or machined from a UV-stabilised, glass-filled thermoplastic (or cast metal for heavier-duty variants), each housing gold- or tin-plated brass pin or socket contacts field-terminated to the cable conductor by crimping or soldering. A threaded coupling ring draws the mating halves together, compressing internal elastomeric O-rings at the contact face and at the cable entry to achieve an IP68 seal once mated. Simpler crimp or moulded joints dispense with the threaded shell altogether, instead sealing the bare splice with heat-shrink tubing or an encapsulating resin poured into a surrounding sleeve. Whichever form is used, the connector's plating, seal count, and shell material are chosen to withstand continuous outdoor exposure, submersion in flooded ducts, and repeated mate/unmate cycles without a rise in contact resistance.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test through each mated connector pair before backfill, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Continuity & Contact Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity through every mated connector and measure contact resistance, ruling out a poorly seated pin or corroded contact.

Mating & Locking Engagement Check
Pre-Energisation

Confirm each connector is fully mated and any locking feature is engaged, preventing vibration-induced disconnection.

Waterproof Seal Verification
Pre-Energisation

Inspect the O-ring or gasket seal at each mating face for correct seating before the connector is buried or placed in a handhole.

Series Circuit Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm voltage is present and correctly regulated across each connector in the series loop.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, and voltage readings alongside each connector's as-built location in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Moisture Ingress at the Joint

The most common connector failure — a degraded or improperly seated seal lets groundwater into the connector body, causing insulation breakdown and, eventually, an earth fault or open circuit. Correct seating, boot condition, and pit drainage are the usual root causes.

Corroded or Loose Contacts

Repeated thermal cycling and years of buried service can loosen or corrode pin-and-socket contacts, increasing resistance and generating localised heating that accelerates further failure.

Incorrect Connector-to-Cable Matching

Using a connector rated for the wrong current or cable gauge creates a poor mechanical and electrical fit, undermining the seal and the contact quality from day one.

Mechanical Damage During Maintenance

Connectors are frequently disturbed during transformer or fixture replacement. Repeated disconnection without proper technique degrades the seal over time, even when the connector itself is undamaged.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Connector Fails Insulation Resistance Test
Likely Cause
A degraded or improperly seated seal has let groundwater into the connector body, breaking down insulation.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the connector; inspect the seal, boot condition, and pit drainage at the connection point.
Corrective Action
Replace the connector and correct pit drainage if standing water is present, then re-test insulation resistance before reinstating.
Connector Running Warm or Showing Heat Damage
Likely Cause
Corroded or loose pin-and-socket contacts, often from years of buried service and thermal cycling, have increased resistance at the joint.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect contacts for corrosion or looseness where the connector can be safely opened; check for heat discoloration on the housing.
Corrective Action
Replace the connector and mating half if contacts are corroded or pitted, ensuring full engagement on reassembly.
Poor Mechanical or Electrical Fit at Installation
Likely Cause
The connector is rated for the wrong current or cable gauge for this application.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the connector's rating against the cable gauge and circuit current it is intended to carry.
Corrective Action
Replace with the correctly rated connector matched to the cable and circuit.
Repeated Seal Failure at a Frequently-Serviced Connector
Likely Cause
Repeated disconnection during transformer or fixture maintenance, without proper technique, is gradually degrading the seal even though the connector itself is undamaged.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the seal and boot condition at connectors known to be disturbed frequently during maintenance.
Corrective Action
Reinstruct maintenance staff on correct disconnection/reconnection technique, and replace the seal or connector if degradation is already present.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Seal / Gasket Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Improperly seated or aged seal, damaged boot, or poor pit drainage allowing standing water to sit against the joint.
Effect
Groundwater ingress into the connector body, breaking down insulation resistance and leading to an earth fault or open circuit.
Detection Method
Megger testing of the connector and visual inspection of the seal, boot condition, and pit drainage during maintenance.
Contact Corrosion / Loosening
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Years of buried service combined with repeated thermal cycling, gradually corroding or loosening the pin-and-socket contacts.
Effect
Increased contact resistance generates localised heating, which accelerates further degradation and can eventually cause the joint to fail.
Detection Method
Inspection of contacts for corrosion or looseness where the connector can be safely opened, and a check for heat discoloration on the housing.
Connector-to-Cable Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A connector rated for the wrong current or cable gauge is fitted, creating a poor mechanical and electrical fit from the point of installation.
Effect
Undermined seal integrity and contact quality from day one, shortening service life and raising the risk of early insulation or thermal failure.
Detection Method
Verification of the connector's current and gauge rating against the cable and circuit during installation or subsequent audits.
Maintenance-Induced Seal Wear
Low Severity
Root Cause
Repeated disconnection and reconnection during transformer or fixture maintenance, carried out without proper technique.
Effect
Gradual seal degradation at frequently-serviced connectors, eventually allowing moisture ingress even though the connector itself is undamaged.
Detection Method
Targeted seal and boot inspection at connectors known to be disturbed frequently, plus a review of maintenance technique.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Before Opening
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the circuit before opening, splicing, or replacing a buried connector, and verify zero energy before separating the pin-and-socket joint.

Confirm Pit Drainage Before Opening
Mechanical

Check that the pit or handhole has drained before exposing the connector, since standing water against an open joint is the leading path to the seal-degradation and earth-fault failure mode.

Match Connector to Cable Rating
Verification

Confirm the replacement connector is rated for the correct current and cable gauge before fitting it; a mismatched connector undermines seal integrity and contact quality from the point of installation.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice or connector work, megger test the circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same series loop.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on a connector serving an active runway or taxiway fixture requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance during the work.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when handling connectors on a series circuit, and confirm bonding/grounding continuity is maintained through the joint before reinstating.

FOD Control at the Pit
Operational

Keep tools, boots, and removed seal material accounted for while the pit is open, and secure or remove any loose material before backfilling or closing the handhole, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a connector unsealed, unverified, or improperly rated after maintenance; confirm insulation resistance and seal integrity before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Most Loop Faults Trace Back to a Connector, Not the Cable
Troubleshooting

Given how many connectors sit in a typical series loop compared to the length of cable itself, a disproportionate share of insulation-resistance and continuity faults originate at a connector joint rather than the cable run; when a loop reading drifts, check connector condition before assuming cable degradation.

A Connector Can Look Correctly Seated and Still Be Compromised
Installation

An L-823-style connector that visually appears fully mated can still have a partially seated O-ring or a contaminated sealing surface that lets moisture in over time; wipe and inspect the sealing surfaces before every mating rather than relying on the connector "clicking" into place as confirmation of a good seal.

Grease Type and Application Matter More Than People Assume
Field Practice

The wrong type or excessive amount of connector grease can trap contaminants or interfere with contact pressure rather than protecting the joint as intended; use the specific grease specified for the connector type and apply it according to the manufacturer's procedure, not just "a bit of grease" as a general habit.

Primary and Secondary Connectors Fail Differently — Inspect Accordingly
Maintenance

Primary-side connectors carry the full series circuit current and are more sensitive to contact resistance and heating, while secondary-side connectors are more exposed to physical handling during fixture changeout; tailor inspection focus to each connector type's actual failure mode rather than applying one generic checklist to both.

Mixing Connector Manufacturers on the Same Loop Invites Subtle Mismatches
Design

Connectors from different manufacturers that both claim compliance with the same specification don't always mate with identical contact pressure or sealing behaviour; where practical, keep a consistent connector manufacturer within a given loop or fixture family to avoid a marginal mating that passes visual inspection but underperforms electrically.

Track Connector Age and Mate Cycles, Not Just Cable Age
Documentation

Connector seals and contact plating degrade with mating cycles and environmental exposure on a different timeline than the cable they're attached to; maintaining connector-specific service records rather than lumping them into the cable's maintenance history makes it easier to plan proactive replacement before a seal fails in the field.
