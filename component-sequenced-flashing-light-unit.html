Sequenced Flashing Light Unit

A high‑intensity light fixture designed to flash in a precise sequence as part of a Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL) system. These units create the distinctive "rolling" or "chasing" effect that gives pilots a dynamic alignment cue during final approach, helping them acquire and interpret the approach lighting pattern quickly in low‑visibility conditions. Sequenced flashers provide azimuth (alignment) guidance only — vertical guidance comes from the ILS glide path or PAPI, not from the SFL.

Overview

What Is It?

A Sequenced Flashing Light Unit is an individual high‑intensity light fixture that operates as part of a Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL) system — called Runway Alignment Indicator Lights (RAIL) where the flashers extend outboard beyond the end of the steady‑burning centreline, as in MALSR and SSALR, and simply called sequenced flashers where they sit at the same stations as the centreline barrettes, as in ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2. The equipment is the same; the name follows the position in the system. These units are installed along the approach lighting system and are triggered in rapid succession from the outer end toward the runway threshold, creating a dynamic "rolling" or "chasing" effect that provides pilots with directional cues during final approach. Each unit is controlled by an SFL controller that precisely times the flash sequence, ensuring a consistent and seamless visual pattern. The units are high‑intensity unfiltered‑white condenser‑discharge (xenon) or, increasingly, LED flash heads, elevated on frangible mountings, and are a component of precision (ALSF‑1/ALSF‑2), medium‑intensity (MALSF/MALSR, SSALF/SSALR) and omnidirectional (ODALS) approach lighting systems.

The flash itself is a very short, very bright pulse — a xenon discharge lasts well under a millisecond — so the light is rated in effective intensity (the steady intensity that would give the same visual range), not in raw peak candela. The sweep from the outermost unit to the innermost repeats twice per second in ALSF and MALSR/RAIL installations, and once per second in ODALS, producing the ball of light that appears to travel toward the threshold. That apparent motion is what makes the pattern conspicuous in fog, rain and snow, where a field of steady lights alone can be slow to interpret.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The purpose of the sequenced flashing light unit is to give a pilot's eye something a steady light cannot: a sense of motion and direction. In marginal visibility, a field of steady lights can be difficult to parse quickly, especially when the pilot is still transitioning from instruments to outside visual reference on short final. The rolling flash draws the eye and unmistakably points toward the runway, effectively saying "this way" rather than simply "here is a light," which shortens the time it takes a pilot to acquire and interpret the approach lighting pattern at the moment visual contact is first made. That is why the unit is concentrated in precision and medium-intensity approach systems — it earns its place by adding a directional cue exactly where and when visual guidance is most marginal, complementing rather than duplicating the steady centreline and crossbar lights around it.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Sequenced Flashing Light Units are deployed as part of approach lighting systems that require dynamic visual guidance. They are used in:

  • ALSF‑1 – high‑intensity approach lighting with sequenced flashers, serving CAT I precision approaches.
  • ALSF‑2 – the CAT II/III configuration; typically 15 flashers at 100 ft (30 m) stations from 2,400 ft out to the 1,000 ft decision bar, where the sequence terminates.
  • MALSR and SSALR – five sequenced flashers form the RAIL section outboard of the steady‑burning centreline, supporting non‑precision and CAT I approaches.
  • MALSF and SSALF – three flashers added at the outer end where approach‑area identification is difficult (bright surrounding lighting, confusing terrain).
  • ODALS – seven omnidirectional flashers (five on the extended centreline plus a REIL pair), an economy system for runways without a full approach lighting installation.
  • ICAO precision approach category I/II/III lighting – Annex 14 permits sequenced flashing lights to be added to the approach lighting system where an operational need is identified; they are not a mandatory element of the basic Calvert‑type configuration.
  • Low‑visibility operations and military airfields – where fast, unambiguous acquisition of the approach pattern at the transition to visual reference is the deciding requirement.
Placement

Installation & Location

Individual SFL units are set out at the same longitudinal stations as the approach lighting centreline barrettes — 100 ft (30 m) spacing in ALSF systems, 200 ft (60 m) for the RAIL section of a MALSR — each flash head on its own frangible mounting adjacent to the steady‑burning fixture at that station, rather than sharing the fixture’s pole. Each flasher head sits atop its pole alongside the steady-burning fixture, fed from a dedicated sequencing controller rather than the CCR series circuit, so a separate control cable run is needed back to the approach lighting control cabinet in addition to the series-circuit cabling. On approaches over water or uneven terrain, SFL poles are carried on the same raised platform or bridge structure as the adjacent centreline piers, with pier setout tied to the same extended-centreline survey as the rest of the approach lighting system.

Function

How Does It Work?

A Sequenced Flashing Light Unit operates in a precisely timed sequence with other units in the SFL system:

  • Sequence Control: Each unit receives a trigger pulse from the system controller at its allotted point in the cycle, firing in order from the outermost unit inward. The interval between adjacent units is a few tens of milliseconds — ODALS, for example, fixes it at 1/15 second (≈67 ms) between centreline units — so that the entire sweep completes well inside one cycle.
  • Flash Rate: The complete sequence repeats twice per second (120 cycles per minute) in ALSF and MALSR/RAIL installations. ODALS repeats at 60 cycles per minute, with the two REIL‑configuration lights firing together 4/15 second after the last centreline flash. Tolerance on these intervals is typically ±10%.
  • Flash Duration: The light pulse from a condenser‑discharge (xenon) head lasts well under a millisecond; LED flash heads use a slightly longer pulse or a burst of pulses that the eye reads as one flash. Because the pulse is far shorter than the eye’s integration time, performance is specified as effective intensity using the Blondel–Rey relationship (0.2 s visual constant), not as instantaneous peak candela.
  • Rolling Effect: The sequential firing creates the "rolling" or "chasing" ball of light travelling toward the runway, giving a dynamic alignment cue that a steady light cannot.
  • Intensity Control: Discharge flashing light equipment provides three brightness steps — high, medium and low — not the five steps used for steady‑burning runway lighting. Where the flasher is fed from a series circuit, a series‑circuit adapter maps the regulator’s current steps onto those three flash levels (on a 5‑step 6.6 A regulator: 6.6 A and 5.2 A → high, 4.1 A → medium, 3.4 A and 2.8 A → low).
  • Electrical Interface: The flash energy comes from a local power supply/control unit, typically fed at 120/240 V AC, which charges the discharge capacitor and delivers the trigger pulse to the optical head — the head is not energised directly from the series loop. Where the installation is series‑fed, an optional series‑circuit adapter takes 6.6 A through a series‑to‑series isolation transformer so the flashers switch with the approach/runway circuit.
  • Mounting: Flash heads are elevated on frangible mountings (frangible couplings to FAA drawing C‑6046 or equivalent), on low‑impact‑resistant approach light towers where the terrain falls away. In‑pavement flash heads are offered by some manufacturers for specific applications but are not the normal arrangement.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

SFL units are not commanded individually — timing authority sits with a dedicated SFL sequencer/flasher controller that fires each unit's capacitor-discharge trigger in turn along the row, independent of the steady-burn approach lighting circuit the row is embedded within. The ALCMS enables or disables the sequenced flasher function as a whole (based on RVR category or ATC instruction) and selects one of the flasher system’s three brightness steps — high, medium or low — which the controller applies uniformly to every unit in the row. The rolling effect is a timing offset layered on top of a single system-level brightness, not independent per-unit dimming. Note that the flasher’s three steps are coarser than the five steps of the steady-burning approach lighting they sit within: where the flashers track a 5-step constant current regulator through a series-circuit adapter, two regulator steps map onto each of the outer flash levels, so an ATC brightness change does not always produce a visible change in flash intensity. Fault detection is correspondingly split: the CCR/SFL controller watches overall loop current for a suppressed-circuit fault, while the sequencer separately monitors trigger continuity to each position, so a single lamp or capacitor failure can often be isolated to its place in the row even though intensity control itself remains loop-wide.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each SFL unit is built around a high-intensity xenon strobe tube or high-output LED cluster housed in a rugged die-cast aluminium enclosure engineered to withstand the heat cycling and mechanical stress of repeated high-energy flashes. The lens is a hardened, UV-stabilised polycarbonate or tempered glass dome, gasket-sealed to the housing to keep the unit watertight, with a narrow, forward-directed optical system concentrating the flash toward the approach path. Elevated units mount on a frangible coupling (FAA drawing C‑6046 Type 1/1A or equivalent) on a concrete foundation or on the low-impact-resistant approach light structure serving that station, while the discharge capacitor bank and trigger electronics sit in a separate weatherproof power supply enclosure — NEMA 4 or equivalent, with a hinged lockable door — at the base or in an adjacent equipment case, linked to the lamp head by heavy-gauge cable rated for the high peak currents of a xenon discharge. This separation keeps the failure-prone electronic components accessible for servicing without disturbing the elevated optical head. Door interlocks and bleeder resistors are built into the power supply and control enclosures so that opening a door disconnects input power and bleeds voltages above 150 V down to 50 V within 30 seconds, with the bleeder resistors alone doing so within five minutes as a back-up. Internal wiring terminates at sealed L-823 connectors where the unit is series-fed, and all fasteners and brackets are stainless or otherwise corrosion-resistant given the fixture's exposed siting along the approach lighting corridor.

Energisation

Confirm the unit fires in correct order and timing relative to its neighbours in the row, producing the rolling motion toward the runway that the SFL system is designed to create.

Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Select each of the three flash intensity steps in turn, locally and from the remote/ALCMS interface, and confirm the unit changes level within the specified switching time. On series-fed installations, step the CCR through its full range and check the flash level follows the published current-to-step mapping, remembering that two regulator steps map onto one flash step at each end.

Measure effective intensity (minimum five-flash average), colour and beam coverage with a calibrated photometer against the applicable tolerance at each brightness step prior to operational handover. Record effective intensity, not instantaneous peak candela.

Confirm the frangible coupling, base can depth, and fixture height meet as-built tolerances before the site is finally backfilled.

Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, trigger, sequence timing, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination and Light Output

SFL units are exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits, and de‑icing fluids. Contamination can significantly reduce the peak intensity and flash visibility. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is essential, especially before low‑visibility seasons.

Flash Timing Drift

Over time, the SFL controller or individual unit timing can drift, causing the sequence to become irregular or out of sync. This can disrupt the rolling effect and confuse pilots. Regular calibration and testing of the SFL system is critical, with timing checks using oscilloscopes or dedicated test equipment.

Individual Unit Failure

A single failed SFL unit can break the sequence, creating a "gap" in the rolling effect. Prompt replacement is essential to maintain system integrity. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty units are recommended.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated SFL units are subject to wind loading and thermal cycling, which can fatigue the frangible couplings. Regular inspection and replacement per manufacturer schedules ensure the units remain safe and properly positioned.

Control Circuit and Communication Failures

SFL units rely on control signals for precise timing. Cable damage, connector corrosion, or communication failures can cause timing errors or complete loss of flash. Regular insulation testing and visual inspection of control cables are essential.

LED Driver Failure

In LED‑based SFL units, driver failure can cause individual units to go out or dim. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers are essential to maintain the flashing sequence and intensity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced Peak Intensity or Dim Flash Output
Likely Cause
Jet blast, rubber deposits, or de-icing fluid have contaminated the lens, scattering and absorbing light before it can be seen.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for film or residue; take a photometric peak-intensity reading and compare against specification.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and soft brushes per the manufacturer's procedure, prioritising this ahead of low-visibility seasons; re-verify photometric output after cleaning.
Rolling Sequence Appears Irregular or Out of Sync
Likely Cause
The SFL controller or an individual unit's flash timing has drifted out of specification over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Use an oscilloscope or dedicated SFL test equipment to check flash timing against the sequence specification for each unit in question.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the controller or replace the drifting unit, then re-verify the full sequence timing before returning the system to service.
Visible Gap in the Rolling Light Sequence
Likely Cause
A single SFL unit in the chain has failed completely, breaking the visual continuity of the sequence.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the sequence during a test activation to identify the dark unit; check its power supply and driver status.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed unit promptly to restore the sequence, and log it for inclusion in the next scheduled inspection round.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Wind loading and thermal cycling have fatigued the frangible coupling on an elevated SFL unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically inspect the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against the design tolerance.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling per the manufacturer's schedule and re-level and re-aim the fixture.
Timing Errors or Complete Loss of Flash on One or More Units
Likely Cause
Cable damage, connector corrosion, or a communication failure is disrupting the control signal that drives precise SFL timing.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform an insulation resistance test on the control cable and visually inspect connectors for corrosion; check the communication link status at the controller.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable/connector and restore the communication link, then re-verify timing across the full sequence.
Individual LED-Based Unit Dims or Goes Dark
Likely Cause
The LED driver in that unit has failed, unable to maintain the required drive current.
Diagnostic Steps
Check driver output and temperature on the affected unit against specification during a test activation.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED driver and confirm the unit rejoins the sequence at the correct intensity and timing.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Capacitor Discharge Hazard
Electrical Safety

SFL units store energy in a discharge capacitor to produce the high-peak flash; de-energise and lock out the unit, then verify the capacitor has fully discharged before opening the housing. Door interlocks and bleeder resistors are designed to bring voltages above 150 V down to 50 V within 30 seconds (five minutes on the bleeder alone), but never treat that as proof — measure before touching, and never defeat an interlock without following the manufacturer’s procedure, since a charged capacitor can deliver a fatal shock long after supply power is removed.

High-Intensity Flash Exposure
Personnel Safety

Avoid looking directly into an SFL unit during a test activation and warn nearby personnel before triggering the sequence; the flash intensity is designed to be visible at long range in low-visibility conditions and can cause temporary flash blindness at close range.

Approach Path Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on the SFL chain requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM, since these units are typically installed along the approach lighting and a gap or timing fault directly affects the visual cue pilots rely on in low-visibility conditions.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair on an elevated unit; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

Verify Sequence Timing After Any Unit Change
Compliance

After replacing or repairing any unit in the chain, run a full sequence test and check timing against specification before returning the system to service; a single unit out of time can make the whole rolling sequence unreliable as a directional cue.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or driver work, insulation resistance test the affected control and power wiring before restoring power, since a residual fault can propagate timing errors across the chain.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on power or control connections, and confirm each unit and its mounting structure are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a unit out of aim, dark, or with unverified timing after maintenance; confirm peak intensity, sequence timing, and positional conformance across the chain before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Flash Tube Life Is Cycle-Based, Not Calendar-Based
Reliability

Flash tube life is qualified in operating hours at the rated flash rate — typically a 1,000-hour minimum with no more than 30% loss of effective intensity and under 1% flash skipping. What that means in practice is that a tube ages by flashes, not by calendar months: a system left energised through long low-visibility seasons reaches end of life well before an install-date-based schedule expects. Use the elapsed time meter fitted in the power supply, and treat rising misfire rate as the end-of-life signal rather than waiting for total failure.

Capacitor Bank Condition Degrades Gradually Before Visible Flash Weakening
Electrical

Electrolytic capacitors in the discharge bank lose capacitance gradually with age and heat cycling; flash intensity can fall measurably before it's obviously dim to the eye. Where the controller supports it, log discharge energy or capacitor health rather than relying on a visual check alone.

Sequence Timing Drift Is Easier to Catch Across the Full Run
Troubleshooting

A single flasher unit's own timing can appear correct in isolation while drifting slightly out of step with the rest of the sequence; verify sequence timing by observing the full run of units together, not by testing a suspect unit on its own.

Condensation Inside the Flash Head Shortens Tube and Reflector Life
Durability

Temperature cycling inside a sealed flash head can draw in moisture through even a minor seal degradation, fogging the reflector and accelerating tube-socket corrosion. Check head seal integrity at overhaul rather than only after a reported dim or intermittent flash.

High-Voltage Discharge Can Interfere With Nearby Navigation Equipment
Electrical

The high-voltage discharge pulse from a flash unit can radiate EMI that affects sensitive navigation or communication equipment sited nearby; if interference is reported, check flasher shielding and grounding before assuming an unrelated source.

Approach Light Failure Patterns Differ From Runway Lighting Failure Patterns
Operational

Because sequenced flashers are typically the outermost part of an approach lighting system, a failed or degraded unit is the last thing a pilot sees before transitioning to visual runway cues; prioritise fault response on outer flasher units accordingly rather than treating every unit in the approach system as equal priority.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the Sequenced Flashing Light Unit: what it is, how it works, typical specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit?

A Sequenced Flashing Light Unit is an individual high‑intensity light fixture that operates as part of a Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL) system — called Runway Alignment Indicator Lights (RAIL) where the flashers extend outboard beyond the end of the steady‑burning centreline, as in MALSR and SSALR, and simply called sequenced flashers where they sit at the same stations as the centreline barrettes, as in ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2. The equipment is the same; the name follows the position in the system.

Q2What is the purpose of a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit?

The purpose of the sequenced flashing light unit is to give a pilot's eye something a steady light cannot: a sense of motion and direction. In marginal visibility, a field of steady lights can be difficult to parse quickly, especially when the pilot is still transitioning from instruments to outside visual reference on short final.

Q3Where is a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit used and installed?

Sequenced Flashing Light Units are deployed as part of approach lighting systems that require dynamic visual guidance. They are used in: ALSF‑1 – high‑intensity approach lighting with sequenced flashers, serving CAT I precision approaches. ALSF‑2 – the CAT II/III configuration; typically 15 flashers at 100 ft (30 m) stations from 2,400 ft out to the 1,000 ft decision bar, where the sequence terminates. MALSR and SSALR – five sequenced flashers form the RAIL section outboard of the steady‑burning centreline, supporting non‑precision and CAT I approaches.

Q4How does a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit work?

A Sequenced Flashing Light Unit operates in a precisely timed sequence with other units in the SFL system: Sequence Control: Each unit receives a trigger pulse from the system controller at its allotted point in the cycle, firing in order from the outermost unit inward. The interval between adjacent units is a few tens of milliseconds — ODALS, for example, fixes it at 1/15 second (≈67 ms) between centreline units — so that the entire sweep completes well inside one cycle.

Q5What are the typical technical parameters of a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit?

Typical values are — Light Source: Xenon condenser‑discharge flash tube (traditional) or high‑output LED flash head (retrofit standard); Colour: White, unfiltered — equivalent to an unfiltered xenon discharge, approx. 4,000–8,000 K; Effective Intensity (high step): Unidirectional: 15,000 cd. Omnidirectional: 5,000 cd. (Per FAA L‑849/L‑859 discharge flashing light equipment; tolerance ±50%); Effective Intensity (medium / low): 1,500 cd / 300 cd on three‑step (Style E and F) equipment; Flash Duration: Sub‑millisecond discharge pulse; rated as effective intensity via Blondel–Rey (0.2 s constant), not as a long "on" period.

Q6What are the electrical requirements for a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit?

Typical requirements are — Primary Supply: Control unit designed for 120/240 V AC (other standard commercial voltages optional); this feeds the power supply that charges the discharge capacitor; Series-Circuit Option: Optional series-circuit adapter allows the system to be fed from a 6.6 A (or 20 A) constant current circuit so flashers switch with the runway/approach lighting; Isolation: Series-to-series isolation transformer where series-fed, output 6.6 A to the circuit adapter — the optical head is never connected directly across the series loop.

Q7What photometric requirements apply to a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit?

Typical requirements are — Measured Quantity: Effective intensity, per the Blondel–Rey integral with a 0.2 s visual constant — peak instantaneous candela of a discharge pulse is not a meaningful acceptance figure; Effective Intensity (high step): 15,000 cd unidirectional / 5,000 cd omnidirectional, tolerance ±50% (FAA L‑849/L‑859); Effective Intensity (medium / low): 1,500 cd / 300 cd on three-step equipment; Beam Coverage: Unidirectional styles: 10° vertical × 30° horizontal (corners may be rounded on a 5° radius). Omnidirectional styles: 2–10° vertical × 360°. Output below the vertical cut-off is minimised.

Q8Which standards apply to a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, Vol I — Chapter 5 approach lighting system requirements, including sequenced flashing lights; Appendix 1 (colours) and Appendix 2 (light characteristics); ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4 — Aerodrome Design Manual, Visual Aids — design guidance for approach lighting and sequenced flashers; FAA AC 150/5345-51 — Specification for Discharge-Type Flashing Light Equipment (L-849 REIL, L-859 ODALS) — intensity, flash rate, styles and qualification; FAA AC 150/5340-30 — Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids — ALSF, MALSR, SSALR and ODALS layouts and flasher siting; FAA EB 67 — Engineering Brief: light sources other than incandescent and xenon — the route for LED flash heads; IEC 61827 — Characteristics of inset and elevated luminaires used on aerodromes and heliports; EASA CS-ADR-DSN — Certification specifications for aerodrome design — Book 1 visual aids, for EASA-regulated aerodromes. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should a Sequenced Flashing Light Unit be inspected and maintained?

Typical maintenance tasks and intervals are: Lens Cleaning (quarterly); Flash Timing Calibration (annual); Individual Unit Inspection (monthly); Frangible Coupling Inspection (annual); Control Cable & Insulation Test (annual); Flash Tube Life & Misfire Check (per operating hours); LED Driver Inspection (annual). Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10How do I troubleshoot common Sequenced Flashing Light Unit faults?

Common symptoms covered are: Reduced Peak Intensity or Dim Flash Output; Rolling Sequence Appears Irregular or Out of Sync; Visible Gap in the Rolling Light Sequence; Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned; Timing Errors or Complete Loss of Flash on One or More Units; Individual LED-Based Unit Dims or Goes Dark. For example, for "Reduced Peak Intensity or Dim Flash Output": Likely Cause: Jet blast, rubber deposits, or de-icing fluid have contaminated the lens, scattering and absorbing light before it can be seen. Diagnostic Steps: Visually inspect the lens for film or residue; take a photometric peak-intensity reading and compare against specification. Corrective Action: Clean the lens with approved solvents and soft brushes per the manufacturer's procedure, prioritising this ahead of low-visibility seasons; re-verify photometric output after cleaning.
