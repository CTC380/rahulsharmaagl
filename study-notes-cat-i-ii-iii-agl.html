CAT I, II AND III: WHAT THEY MEAN

CAT I, II and III are categories of precision instrument approach and landing operations in progressively poorer visibility. They involve aircraft systems, navigation, crew qualification, operator approval, aerodrome infrastructure, visual aids, RVR, decision height, power supply and maintenance. They are not a classification of lamps. In current ICAO Annex 6 (Part I) these categories sit under Type B instrument approach operations (DH below 75 m / 250 ft); Type A operations (MDH or DH at or above 75 m / 250 ft) sit outside CAT I to III.

CategoryDecision height (DH)RVR / visibility
CAT IDH ≥ 200 ft (60 m)Visibility ≥ 800 m or RVR ≥ 550 m
CAT II100 ft ≤ DH < 200 ftRVR ≥ 300 m
CAT IIIDH < 100 ft, or no DHDepends on sub-category: ≥ 175 m (IIIA), 50 m to < 175 m (IIIB), no limitation (IIIC)
Do not memorise only 550 / 300 / below 300 m. Category also depends on DH, aircraft certification, crew qualification, operator authorisation, navigation performance, aerodrome facilities and approved procedures. Where DH and RVR fall in different categories, the more demanding category is generally used, subject to approvals.

CAT IIIA, IIIB, IIIC

Sub-categoryDH (ICAO)RVR (ICAO)
IIIA< 100 ft (30 m) or no DH≥ 175 m
IIIB< 50 ft (15 m) or no DH< 175 m but ≥ 50 m
IIICNo DHNo RVR limitation

ICAO Annex 6 defines these sub-categories with the 175 m and 50 m RVR limits shown. Older documents and FAA practice use 200 m (700 ft) for IIIA, and some regulators no longer subdivide CAT III, so manuals, AIPs and LVPs may differ. Before using IIIA/B/C values as acceptance criteria, check: ICAO standard, State regulation, operator approval, AIP/LVP and the approved project design.

RVR

RVR (Runway Visual Range) is the distance over which a pilot on the centreline can see runway markings or lights of adequate intensity. It matters far more than ordinary visibility for CAT II/III. In India it may be reported at TDZ, MID and END positions.

Key idea
  • The lower the visibility/RVR, the more critical continuity, intensity, geometry, power, control, monitoring and maintenance become.

Exact provision depends on runway configuration, authority and approved design. ICAO allows the CAT II/III side rows to be 240 m instead of 270 m where the serviceability objectives in Section 5 can be demonstrated. The numbers above follow ICAO; check the applicable State and project standard. Note that several ICAO provisions (stop bars, runway guard lights, taxiway centreline lights) are triggered by RVR values such as 550 m and 350 m rather than by the category name, so confirm the exact clause in your edition.

Why each aid matters

  • Side-row barrettes: lateral reference, roll guidance, orientation.
  • RCL: longitudinal alignment when visibility is poor. ICAO: required on CAT II/III runways; about 15 m spacing (30 m allowed in specified conditions).
  • TDZ: touchdown-zone information when the runway appears late.
  • Stop bars: red, at holding positions; part of AGL + ATC control + interlocking + LVP + incursion prevention.
  • Taxiway centreline: pilot may lose reference to surrounding pavement.
  • Runway guard lights: yellow, extra conspicuity at holds.
RCL colours: take the colour transition, spacing and photometry from the applicable Annex/State/project specification, not from a simplified memory diagram.
3

POWER SWITCH-OVER AND UPS

CAT I aids generally allow a switch-over of up to 15 s. CAT II/III is stricter for critical aids (ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I, Table 8-1):

CAT II/III facilityMax switch-over
Inner 300 m approach lighting1 s
Other approach lighting15 s
Runway threshold, end, centreline, TDZ1 s
All stop bars1 s
Runway edge, essential taxiway, obstacle lighting15 s
Normal power → UPS / fast source → critical loads (RCL, TDZ, threshold, end, stop bars)
              + DG / standby source for longer outages

Zero interruption vs 1-second switch-over: a UPS can give no visible interruption. A generator-only chain (start, ATS, restore) must still meet the approved changeover time. UPS-supported critical circuits with automatic generator takeover are a common way to meet the 1 s requirement, subject to the approved design.

Check the footnotes to Table 8-1: for example, runway edge lights also need 1 s where the runway is used for take-off in RVR below 800 m.

INTERLEAVING AND CONTROL

Interleaved:  A B A B A B A B     Circuit A fails → X B X B X B X B  (pattern kept)
Not:          A A A A B B B B     Circuit A fails → X X X X B B B B  (dark section)

Interleaving preserves the basic visual pattern after a circuit failure. The control and monitoring system (ALCMS) should let ATC/operators select configuration and intensity, show circuit status, flag critical failures, coordinate with LVP and control stop bars. Aim: detect, identify, report, act.

Utility failUPS carries loadDG startsATS transfersAlarm / recovery

CCR brightness steps (typically 3 or 5) depend on the approved design. Too dim means guidance cannot be identified; too bright causes glare. Correct output, correct step and correct aiming together make guidance usable.

5

PHOTOMETRY AND SERVICEABILITY

For CAT II/III, ICAO recommends preventive maintenance with visual inspection, in-field intensity, beam spread, orientation and electrical measurement, and verification of intensity settings, using mobile units accurate enough to assess individual lights. Frequency should reflect traffic, pollution and reliability; guidance indicates at least twice a year for in-pavement lights and once a year for others.

Three different numbers: ICAO 50% = an individual light is unserviceable when main-beam average intensity is below 50% of the specified value. FAA 70% = maintenance target for in-pavement runway lights (AC 150/5340-26C), not a universal acceptance limit. Percent serviceability of a system is a third concept. Do not interchange them.
ElementCAT II/III objective
Approach lighting, inner 450 m95%
Runway centreline, threshold, edge95%
Touchdown zone90%
Approach beyond 450 m85%
Runway end75%

CAT I objective is about 85% for approach, threshold, edge and end lights, while keeping the pattern intact. These objectives are in ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I, Chapter 10 (paragraph 10.5.7 for CAT II/III and 10.5.10 for CAT I in recent editions).

Worked example

240 RCL lights (about 3.6 km at 15 m spacing) with 6 failed: 234 / 240 = 97.5%, above 95%. But ask: are failures adjacent? Concentrated in one section? Inside a critical element? Does the LVP restrict operations? Percentage alone never tells the full story; where the lights fail matters. ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 10 also protects continuity of guidance: the allowed unserviceable lights must not be adjacent (unless the spacing is much closer than standard), and stop bars used in RVR below 350 m have their own limit (no more than two unserviceable, and not adjacent). A high percentage can therefore still fail the pattern test. Check the exact clause in your edition.

Isolated:  ● ● ● X ● ● ● ●
Adjacent:  ● ● X X ● ● ● ●   ← pattern damaged
Cluster:   X X X X X ● ● ●

Electrical health is not photometric health: a circuit can be electrically sound yet low in intensity, mis-aimed, wrong in colour or contaminated. Record date, weather, RVR, runway status, lighting step, instrument and calibration. LED fixtures still need photometric maintenance (driver, optics, thermal, colour stability). Identify the actual fixture type and standard (for example AC 150/5345-46F), never just "CAT III light". See Note 15 AGL Photometry.

6

LVP, FAULTS AND COMMISSIONING

LVP (Low Visibility Procedures) are airport procedures, not an AGL setting. A typical LVP sequence (check your aerodrome AIP/LVP) covers: RVR/MET trigger, safeguarding, AGL inspection, movement-area restrictions, critical-area protection, CAT II/III lighting selection, ATC coordination, LVP active, then periodic AGL inspection and reporting. Routine maintenance and LVP inspection are different activities.

When a light fails, ask

  1. Which location and which circuit?
  2. Is an adjacent light out?
  3. What percentage has failed?
  4. Is the basic pattern affected?
  5. What does the LVP manual say, and is CAT II/III restricted?
  6. Has ATC been told?
Alarm / field checkCCR · cable · transformer · connector · fixtureRepairFunction testPhotometryRecord · notify ATC

Commissioning: design approval, installation, IR/continuity/CCR/circuit/fixture/control tests, failover test, photometry and alignment, then operational demonstration (power failure, automatic transfer, alarm, recovery). Verify every required intensity step, not only maximum. See Note 14.

7

ICAO, FAA AND LOCAL STANDARDS

ICAO is not FAA is not DGCA or State implementation. They differ in category definitions, RVR terms, approach configurations, fixture classes, photometric criteria and power requirements. Use the contractually and regulatorily applicable standard, never a mix of numbers.

Annex 6 (CAT definition) → Annex 14 Vol I (visual aids, power, maintenance) → Annex 10 (ILS)
→ Doc 9157 → State regulation (DGCA) → AIP / LVP → approved project specification

FAA treats ALSF-2 as the CAT II/III system giving alignment, height perception, roll guidance and horizontal reference.

8

QUICK REVISION

CAT I   = approach + edge + threshold + end (PAPI where provided)
CAT II  = CAT I + RCL + TDZ + low-vis taxi guidance + stop bars (RVR < 550 m) + 1 s critical power + interleaving + stronger control
CAT III = same aids and power as CAT II, with the most stringent reliability, monitoring, photometry and LVP control
Remember
  • CAT I: see the runway earlier. CAT II: later. CAT III: rely heavily on the system and approved procedures.
  • CAT III is not just more lights: it is reliable guidance, power, control, monitoring, photometry and procedure.
  • 50% (ICAO, per light) ≠ 70% (FAA target) ≠ 95% (system objective).
  • Percentage and failure distribution both matter.
Exam

EXAM QUESTIONS

What is CAT I?

DH ≥ 200 ft and visibility ≥ 800 m or RVR ≥ 550 m (ICAO Annex 6).

What is CAT II?

DH 100 to below 200 ft and RVR ≥ 300 m.

What is CAT III?

DH below 100 ft or no DH. RVR is not less than 175 m for IIIA, 50 m to below 175 m for IIIB, and no limitation for IIIC.

Main AGL difference between CAT I and CAT II/III?

More comprehensive, reliable guidance: RCL, TDZ, low-visibility taxi guidance, stop bars, critical power, control and monitoring.

Why is RCL important in CAT II/III?

It gives longitudinal alignment guidance in poor visibility.

Why TDZ lighting?

Extra touchdown-phase information when external visibility is limited.

Why are stop bars critical?

Conspicuous red stop instruction supporting runway-incursion prevention, tied to ATC, interlocking and LVP.

Why UPS for CAT II/III?

Critical aids such as RCL, TDZ, threshold, end and stop bars need very fast restoration (1 s).

What is interleaving?

Arranging circuits so one circuit failure does not destroy the basic visual pattern.

What is the ICAO individual-light criterion?

Unserviceable when main-beam average intensity is below 50% of the specified value.

Is 95% serviceability enough?

No. Failure distribution and preservation of the pattern also matter.

FAA 70% vs ICAO 50%?

70% is an FAA maintenance targeting criterion for in-pavement lights; 50% is ICAO's per-light unserviceability criterion. Do not interchange.

Is electrical testing enough?

No. Photometry, visual and functional checks are also needed.

At what RVR must stop bars be provided?

ICAO requires a stop bar at every runway-holding position serving a runway intended for use in RVR below 550 m, with limited exceptions where aids and procedures already limit the risk of incursion.

Final Study Takeaway

FINAL STUDY TAKEAWAY

CAT I, II and III are progressively more demanding operations, and AGL must provide progressively more reliable, continuous and accurately controlled visual guidance as external visual reference decreases.
