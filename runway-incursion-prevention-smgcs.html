Runway Incursion Prevention & SMGCS

Runway incursion prevention is not the responsibility of a single light, sign, marking or control system. It is a layered safety system combining aerodrome design, markings, lighting, signs, stop bars, runway guard lights, ATC, procedures, surface movement guidance, monitoring and pilot/vehicle operator awareness. SMGCS provides the framework for guiding and controlling aircraft and vehicles on the aerodrome surface.

Overview

What Is a Runway Incursion?

A runway incursion is an occurrence involving the incorrect presence of an aircraft, vehicle or person on the protected area of a surface designated for the landing and take-off of aircraft.

Runway incursions can have multiple contributing factors: navigation errors, communication errors, poor visibility, complex taxiway geometry, inadequate signage, marking deficiencies, lighting failures, incorrect routing, human factors, ATC coordination problems, vehicle driver errors and inadequate surface movement guidance. Prevention therefore requires a system approach.

A Layered System

No single light, sign, marking or control system is responsible for incursion prevention — it's a coordinated whole.

The Engineering Objective

Know Where You Are → Know Where You Need to Go → Know Where You Must Stop → Know When You May Proceed → Verify the Movement.

Command ≠ Field State

An ALCMS command being correct does not automatically prove the actual field condition matches it.

The Framework

What Is SMGCS?

Surface Movement Guidance and Control System (SMGCS) is the aerodrome system used to support the safe, orderly and efficient movement of aircraft and vehicles on the aerodrome surface. It combines physical infrastructure, visual guidance, control procedures and operational arrangements.

Depending on airport complexity, it may include taxiway and runway markings, taxiway and runway lighting, airfield signs, runway guard lights, stop bars, taxiway centreline lighting, holding positions, ATC procedures, surface movement surveillance, A-SMGCS, and control and monitoring systems.

SMGCS

Provides the overall surface movement guidance and control framework.

A-SMGCS

Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control System extends surface movement capabilities through additional technologies and functions — surveillance, routing, guidance and conflict-related support depending on implementation. A-SMGCS can form part of an airport's broader SMGCS architecture.

These terms should not be treated as identical.

Geometry & Holding Positions

Aerodrome Geometry & Runway Holding Positions

Taxiway and runway geometry directly influence surface movement risk — number of intersections, taxiway complexity, runway crossings, high-speed exits, parallel taxiways, multiple runway configurations, apron interfaces, hot spots and visibility limitations. Good visual guidance begins with good aerodrome geometry.

Holding Positions

Provide a defined boundary between taxiing movement and runway operations, identifiable through markings, signs, lighting and operational procedures — both a physical and operational control point.

Hot Spots

Areas with an identified increased risk of collision or runway incursion, resulting from complex geometry, confusing routing, limited visibility or high workload.

Runway Entry & Exit

Entry points must present consistent information from holding-position markings, signs, guard lights, stop bars and ATC instruction; exits require clear identification to avoid unintended routing.

Warning vs Control

Runway Guard Lights vs Stop Bars

Runway Guard Lights provide an additional conspicuous visual warning of a runway environment. Stop bars provide a red visual indication associated with a runway holding position, built from Red Lighting + Control + Operational Authorization + Monitoring. They should never be confused with each other — exact configurations must follow the applicable aerodrome standard.

Visual Layers

Lighting, Signs & Markings Working Together

Lighting and signs should reinforce rather than contradict the markings. The pilot receives information through multiple visual channels, and these channels must not disagree with each other.

Taxiway Centreline & Edge Lighting

Centreline lighting provides guidance along taxi routes and becomes particularly important in complex or low-visibility operations. Edge lighting defines lateral boundaries and complements markings and signs.

Airfield Signs

Communicate information lighting alone cannot — taxiway location, direction, destinations, runway identification, holding positions and mandatory instructions.

Markings

Provide fixed visual information: taxiway centreline, runway holding position markings, runway designation, edge and apron markings, and vehicle roadway markings.

Colour is part of the operational language of the aerodrome. Incorrect colour can create ambiguity even when the light source is functioning correctly.

Low Visibility

Visibility, Recognition & Low-Visibility Operations

Runway-incursion prevention depends on rapid recognition. Visual guidance must remain effective under night conditions, rain, fog, haze, reduced visibility, glare and background lighting: Intensity + Colour + Contrast + Location + Alignment + Visibility.

As Visibility Decreases

Pilot visual reference decreases Sign recognition harder Taxiway geometry less obvious Holding positions more critical Lighting more important Control & monitoring more important

Low-Visibility Protection Elements

Taxiway centreline lighting, stop bars, runway guard lights, enhanced signs, surface movement surveillance, A-SMGCS, ATC procedures, controlled routing and lighting intensity control — the exact configuration depends on the airport's operational category and approved procedures.

Advanced Capability

A-SMGCS, Surveillance & ALCMS

A-SMGCS

May incorporate surveillance, identification, routing, guidance, conflict alerting, tracking, controller displays and vehicle identification — part of the airport's broader operational architecture, not simply another lighting system.

Surveillance

Provides information on aircraft and vehicle position, movement, routing and location, improving situational awareness for operational personnel.

ALCMS

Provides control and monitoring of airport lighting infrastructure — interfacing with CCRs, stop bars, guard lights, taxiway and runway lighting and signs. ALCMS does not replace ATC or A-SMGCS.

Commanded State vs Actual Field State. ALCMS Command = Stop Bar ON does not automatically prove every stop-bar fixture is illuminated correctly. ALCMS Command = OFF does not by itself prove no electrical or optical abnormality exists.

Failure Behaviour

Control Interlocking & Fail-Safe Design

Interlocking helps prevent unsafe combinations of system states — involving stop bars, taxiway centreline segments, runway crossing controls, control commands, route authorization, A-SMGCS and ATC interfaces. The exact logic must be based on the approved airport control philosophy.

The critical engineering question: what does the system do when something goes wrong? A safe design must define the intended response — to power failure, communication failure, control failure, monitoring failure, CCR failure, circuit failure, ALCMS failure or A-SMGCS failure — rather than relying on operator assumptions.

Troubleshooting

Runway Incursion System Troubleshooting

When an abnormal condition occurs, work systematically through the chain rather than assuming the cause.

Verify the system after correction before returning to service.

Stop Bar Shows ON, One Fixture Dark

Investigate individual fixture, secondary connector, isolating transformer, connector and local wiring — not the CCR. ALCMS may correctly report the circuit ON while one fixture has failed.

Multiple Fixtures Fail Together

Investigate common causes first: CCR, series circuit, common cable, common control, emergency power, ALCMS, a surge event, or recent maintenance activity.

Change Management

Human Factors & Change Management

Human Factors

Runway-incursion prevention is not purely electrical engineering. Workload, familiarity, distraction, fatigue, communication, expectation, visibility, route complexity and sign interpretation all matter. Engineering should make the correct route and holding point as clear and unambiguous as reasonably possible — this applies to vehicle drivers as well as pilots.

Change Management

Changes to runway layout, taxiway geometry, holding positions, signs, lighting, ALCMS, A-SMGCS or ATC procedures can affect runway-incursion risk.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is a runway incursion?

A runway incursion is an occurrence involving the incorrect presence of an aircraft, vehicle or person on the protected area of a surface designated for the landing and take-off of aircraft.

Q2What causes runway incursions?

Contributing factors can include navigation errors, communication errors, poor visibility, complex taxiway geometry, inadequate signage, marking deficiencies, lighting failures, incorrect routing, human factors, ATC coordination problems, vehicle driver errors and inadequate surface movement guidance. Prevention therefore requires a system approach.

Q3What is SMGCS?

The Surface Movement Guidance and Control System is the aerodrome system supporting the safe, orderly and efficient movement of aircraft and vehicles on the aerodrome surface. It combines physical infrastructure, visual guidance, control procedures and operational arrangements.

Q4What is the difference between SMGCS and A-SMGCS?

SMGCS provides the overall surface movement guidance and control framework. A-SMGCS extends capabilities through additional technologies and functions such as surveillance, routing, guidance and conflict-related support, depending on implementation, and can form part of a broader SMGCS architecture. The terms should not be treated as identical.

Q5Why is incursion prevention a layered system?

No single light, sign, marking or control system is responsible. The layered model runs from aerodrome layout, markings, signs and lighting through stop bars and runway guard lights, ATC control, surface movement surveillance and ALCMS or A-SMGCS to pilot and vehicle operator response. Every layer has gaps, so safety comes from independent or complementary layers.

Q6What is the engineering objective of incursion prevention?

Know where you are, know where you need to go, know where you must stop, know when you may proceed, and verify the movement.

Q7What is the difference between runway guard lights and stop bars in incursion prevention?

Runway guard lights are a warning layer, typically yellow or amber, conveying runway presence. Stop bars are a controlled holding layer, typically red, conveying not to proceed beyond the holding position, with strong control integration. Exact configurations must follow the applicable aerodrome standard.

Q8What are runway holding positions and hot spots?

A holding position is a defined boundary between taxiing movement and runway operations, identifiable through markings, signs, lighting and procedures. A hot spot is an area with an identified increased risk of collision or runway incursion, from complex geometry, confusing routing, limited visibility or high workload.

Q9Why must stop bars and centreline lighting not conflict?

Where stop bars are integrated with controlled taxiway centreline lighting, the visual indications must not provide conflicting information. The control design should define which lights illuminate or extinguish, when centreline segments become available, and what happens during failure or loss of communication.

Q10Does a correct ALCMS command prove the field condition is correct?

No. An ALCMS command being correct does not automatically prove the actual field condition matches it, which is why monitoring and field verification remain necessary.
