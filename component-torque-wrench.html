Torque Wrench

A precision hand tool used to apply a specific torque to fasteners such as nuts, bolts, and set screws during AGL fixture installation and maintenance. Proper torque ensures that connections are secure without being over-tightened, preventing damage to fixtures, stripped threads, and electrical failures due to loose connections.

Overview

What Is It?

A Torque Wrench is a precision measuring instrument that applies a controlled amount of rotational force (torque) to a fastener. In AGL maintenance, torque wrenches are indispensable for ensuring that all mechanical and electrical connections are tightened to the manufacturer's specified values. The tool typically consists of:

  • A calibrated spring or beam mechanism that measures the applied torque
  • A micrometer or scale adjustment to set the desired torque value
  • A ratchet or fixed head that accepts standard sockets or bits
  • A visual or audible indicator (click, dial, or digital display) that signals when the preset torque is reached

Torque wrenches are available in various types — click-type, beam-type, dial-type, and digital — each suited for different applications and accuracy requirements. In AGL systems, they are used for securing fixture mounting bolts, terminal connections, cable glands, and frangible couplings.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The torque wrench exists to remove guesswork from a step that has safety consequences at both extremes. Under-torquing a terminal or mounting bolt leaves a connection that can work loose under vibration and thermal cycling, producing intermittent circuit faults or arcing that are difficult to trace once the fixture is reinstalled in the field. Over-torquing is equally damaging: it can strip threads, crack fixture housings, or — critically on elevated fixtures — over-stress a frangible coupling so that it no longer breaks away cleanly at the load it was designed for, turning a safety feature meant to protect an aircraft on impact into a rigid obstruction. By applying and confirming an exact, manufacturer-specified value every time, the tool ensures every mechanical and electrical connection in the AGL system performs consistently and predictably, regardless of which technician tightened it.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Torque wrenches are used across all AGL maintenance and installation activities where threaded fasteners are involved:

  • Fixture Mounting: Securing inset and elevated fixtures to base cans and mounting plates with correct torque to prevent loosening from vibration.
  • Electrical Connections: Tightening terminal block screws, bus bar connections, and cable lugs to ensure low-resistance, reliable electrical joints.
  • Cable Gland Installation: Applying correct torque to cable glands to ensure watertight sealing without damaging the cable or the gland.
  • Frangible Coupling Assembly: Tightening frangible coupling bolts to the specified torque to ensure proper break-away characteristics.
  • Isolating Transformer Terminals: Securing primary and secondary connections to transformer studs.
  • Junction Box and Distribution Box Covers: Ensuring cover fasteners are evenly torqued to maintain IP ratings.

The tool is used by maintenance crews during installation, scheduled preventive maintenance, and unscheduled repairs.

Care

Storage & Handling

Torque wrenches are stored in a dedicated case or tool crib rack, released to zero (or their lowest setting) after use to avoid keeping the internal spring under tension, which can drift the wrench out of calibration over time. They are kept away from drops, impacts, and use as a breaker bar or hammer, since abuse of this kind is a common cause of accuracy loss between calibration cycles. Each wrench is labelled with its next calibration due date and taken out of service if that date passes or if it fails a spot-check, and crews carry the wrench to the fixture or panel being worked on, selecting the correct range and drive size for the fastener rather than forcing an out-of-range wrench to save a trip back to the tool store.

Function

How Does It Work?

A torque wrench operates by measuring the rotational force applied to a fastener. The user sets the desired torque value on the wrench's scale or digital display. When the wrench is used to tighten a fastener, an internal mechanism (such as a calibrated spring, a beam deflection, or a strain gauge) measures the applied torque. In a click-type torque wrench, when the preset torque is reached, the mechanism releases with an audible "click" and a slight break in the handle, signaling the user to stop applying force. This ensures that the fastener is tightened to the exact specification, without over-torquing (which can strip threads or damage components) or under-torquing (which can lead to loose connections and electrical faults).

Digital torque wrenches provide a real-time readout of the applied torque and can store data for quality assurance records. Beam-type wrenches use a simple deflection scale for visual indication. Regardless of the type, all torque wrenches must be regularly calibrated to maintain accuracy.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A mechanical torque wrench has no control logic at all — the click or beam deflection is a purely mechanical threshold with no sensing, memory, or reporting. A digital torque wrench, by contrast, contains a small internal control loop: a strain gauge in the wrench head produces a signal proportional to applied torque, an onboard microcontroller continuously compares that signal against the preset target value, and once the reading reaches the threshold the controller triggers an audible tone, a light, or a display alert rather than a mechanical release. Some digital models extend this into a data-logging architecture, timestamping and storing each fastening event (and in networked versions, transmitting it to a quality-management system) so that torque compliance on safety-critical connections — an isolating transformer terminal, for example — can be verified after the fact rather than relying solely on the technician's word that the click was reached.

Construction

Physical Construction

A click-type wrench is built around a chrome-vanadium or chrome-molybdenum steel drive head and internal spring-and-cam mechanism, housed in a chrome-plated steel, aluminium, or fibreglass handle that gives an audible and tactile click when the set torque is reached. The torque scale is set by rotating a calibrated handle collar against an internal micrometer scale, which is locked with a knurled ring to prevent drift during use. Digital models replace the mechanical cam with a strain-gauge sensor and electronic display, adding a battery compartment and buttons for unit selection and target-torque alerts, but keep the same square-drive head sized to accept standard sockets. The square drive itself is hardened and typically detented or through-hole to retain the socket, and the whole tool ships in a fitted case with a calibration certificate, since accuracy depends on periodic recalibration against a reference torque standard.

Pre-Use

Setup Requirements

Calibration Currency Check
Pre-Use

Confirm the wrench's calibration due date has not passed and its certificate is current before use, taking it out of service if either check fails.

Torque Range & Drive Selection
Pre-Use

Select a wrench whose calibrated range covers the target torque value and a drive size matching the fastener's socket or bit, since operating near the extremes of a wrench's range reduces accuracy.

Target Value Setting
Pre-Use

Set the wrench to the fastener's specified torque value from the manufacturer's documentation before starting work, double-checking the units (Nm, ft-lb, in-lb) match the specification.

Physical Condition Check
Pre-Use

Inspect the wrench body, drive, and any sockets for cracks, bent shafts, or worn drive squares before use.

Click/Release Function Check
Pre-Use

Confirm the click or release mechanism engages cleanly at the set value on a test fastener before relying on it for a torque-critical connection.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Loss of Calibration

Torque wrenches lose calibration over time due to wear, misuse, or mechanical shock. Using an uncalibrated wrench can result in under-torqued or over-torqued fasteners, leading to loose connections or damaged threads. Regular calibration (every 5000 cycles or 12 months) with traceable standards is mandatory.

Incorrect Setting

Operators sometimes set the torque wrench to the wrong value, especially when switching between different fasteners. Double-checking the set value and using a checklist can prevent errors. Marking commonly used torque values on the tool can also help.

Using Torque Wrench as a Breaker Bar

Using a torque wrench to loosen seized fasteners or as a general-purpose breaker bar can damage the internal mechanism and cause loss of calibration. Always use a separate breaker bar for loosening, and reserve the torque wrench strictly for tightening to specified values.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fasteners Coming Loose Despite Using the Wrench
Likely Cause
The wrench has lost calibration due to wear, mechanical shock, or being used past its recalibration interval, so the click point no longer corresponds to the actual applied torque, or an incorrect value was set for that fastener.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the wrench's calibration due date and cycle count against the 5,000-cycle/12-month schedule; verify the set value was correct for the fastener in question against the checklist; test the wrench against a traceable torque reference if calibration is in question.
Corrective Action
Send the wrench for calibration against a traceable standard if overdue or found out of tolerance; retrain on double-checking the set value before each use; re-torque the affected fasteners to the correct specification with a verified wrench.
Click/Break Point Feels Inconsistent or Mushy
Likely Cause
Internal mechanism damage, often from the wrench having been used as a breaker bar to loosen seized fasteners rather than reserved strictly for tightening.
Diagnostic Steps
Ask whether the tool has recently been used to loosen a seized fastener; compare the click sensation against a known-good wrench of the same model if available; check for visible play or damage at the drive head.
Corrective Action
Withdraw the wrench from service and send for calibration/inspection before further use; reinforce that a separate breaker bar must be used for loosening seized fasteners going forward.
Wrong Torque Value Applied to a Fastener
Likely Cause
Operator set the wrench to the wrong value, especially likely when switching between different fastener types or specifications during a single work session.
Diagnostic Steps
Identify which fasteners may have been affected by cross-checking the work session against the fastener torque specification sheet; inspect affected fasteners for visible signs of over- or under-torquing.
Corrective Action
Re-torque affected fasteners to the correct specification; mark commonly used torque values on the tool or work checklist to reduce recurrence of setting errors.
Calibration Due Date Overdue or Uncertain
Likely Cause
The tool has been in service past its scheduled 12-month or 5,000-cycle recalibration interval, or its calibration record/sticker is missing or unclear.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the tool's calibration record or asset tag for the last calibration date and cycle count; cross-reference against the tool inventory log if the record on the tool itself is unclear.
Corrective Action
Withdraw the wrench from active use until it has been sent for calibration against traceable standards; update the calibration sticker and inventory log once returned to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Calibration Drift
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal spring or clutch mechanism wear from repeated use, or a drop or impact that shifted the wrench's internal calibration.
Effect
The wrong torque value is applied to a fastener even though the wrench appears to click normally, risking an under- or over-torqued connection.
Detection Method
Calibration certificate verification against the due date, and a torque value cross-check against a calibrated reference.
Mechanical Spring / Clutch Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cumulative fatigue in the internal spring or clutch mechanism after extensive use over the wrench's service life.
Effect
The click or break point feels inconsistent or mushy, making it harder for the technician to judge exactly when the set torque has been reached.
Detection Method
Click/release function test performed against a known reference before use on a critical connection.
Internal Mechanism Contamination
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Dust, grit, or moisture entering the wrench's internal mechanism through a worn seal or during outdoor use without a protective case.
Effect
Reduced accuracy or a sticking mechanism that doesn't release cleanly at the set torque value.
Detection Method
Storage and handling check, and a pre-use setting verification before each task.
Overload Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
The wrench used beyond its rated torque range, or used as a breaker bar to free a seized fastener instead of a dedicated tool.
Effect
Fasteners come loose despite using the wrench, because the overload has permanently damaged the internal mechanism's accuracy.
Detection Method
Torque value cross-check against a calibrated reference, performed whenever misuse is suspected.
Scale / Dial Misreading from Wear
Low Severity
Root Cause
Worn or faded markings on the wrench's scale or dial making the set value harder to read accurately, particularly on older units.
Effect
An incorrect setting applied to a fastener because the technician misread a worn scale rather than the wrench itself being out of calibration.
Detection Method
Zero/setting verification performed before use, checking the visible scale against the intended torque value.
Storage Case Damage Affecting Calibration
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A cracked or ill-fitting storage case failing to protect the wrench from drops or impact during transport.
Effect
The calibration due date becomes uncertain sooner than expected, since an undocumented impact may have shifted the wrench's setting.
Detection Method
Storage case and accessory check as part of scheduled maintenance, confirming the case still protects the wrench in transit.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Use Only Within Calibrated Range
Operational

Do not use the wrench beyond its calibrated torque range or past its current calibration due date; an out-of-calibration or overloaded wrench can under- or over-torque a connection without any obvious indication to the user.

Frangibility Not to Be Overtightened
Mechanical

Set the correct torque value before working on a frangible coupling; over-torquing can defeat the coupling's designed break-away characteristic just as effectively as substituting a rigid fitting, so always confirm the specified value rather than tightening by feel.

Electrical Isolation Before Torquing Live-Adjacent Hardware
Electrical Safety

De-energise and verify zero energy on the circuit before torquing cable gland, connector, or transformer terminal hardware, since the wrench itself provides no insulation from an energised connection.

Slip and Reaction-Force Hazard
Personnel Safety

Position hands and body to control the wrench's reaction force as the set torque is reached, particularly on click-type wrenches, to avoid a sudden slip if a fastener is seized or the socket disengages.

Storage and Handling
Operational

Store the wrench in its case at the low end of its scale (or as the manufacturer specifies) when not in use, and avoid dropping it, since mechanical shock is a common cause of calibration drift between scheduled checks.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify the fastener has reached and held the specified torque value before closing out the work, and log the wrench's calibration status alongside the maintenance record so the applied torque remains traceable.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Treat the Calibration Due Date as Strictly as Any Fixture Test
Calibration

A torque wrench drifts out of calibration with normal use or a single drop, and using an overdue wrench on a frangible coupling or high-voltage connection risks under- or over-torquing without any visible symptom; track calibration due dates with the same discipline applied to fixture testing intervals.

Back Off Click-Type Wrenches Before Storage
Maintenance

Storing a click-type torque wrench at its last-used or maximum setting keeps the internal spring under tension between uses, gradually weakening it and shifting calibration; back the setting off to its lowest value before returning the tool to storage.

Select a Wrench Whose Range Puts the Target Torque Mid-Scale
Technique

A wrench operating near the bottom or top of its working range gives less accurate readings than one sized so the target torque falls comfortably mid-scale; choose the tool to the fastener, not just whichever wrench is closest to hand.

Account for Temperature Effects on Precision Terminations
Technique

Beam- and spring-type torque wrenches can read differently in cold outdoor conditions than they were calibrated at room temperature; factor this into winter work on precision electrical terminations rather than assuming indoor calibration carries over unchanged.

Dedicate a Verified Wrench to Frangible-Critical Work
Quality Control

Frangible coupling torque values are safety-critical in a way that tolerates less ambiguity than general fastener work; if there's any doubt about a shared tool's calibration status, dedicate and label a specific wrench for frangible-critical connections.

Log the Actual Torque Achieved, Not Just "Torqued to Spec"
Documentation

A logged numeric reading is far more defensible in a later failure investigation than a simple checkbox confirming the fastener was torqued; record the actual value achieved on safety-critical connections rather than only noting that the procedure was followed.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about torque wrenches in AGL maintenance: types, accuracy, calibration, standards and common mistakes.

Q1Why are torque wrenches used in AGL maintenance?

They apply a controlled, measured torque to fasteners so mechanical and electrical joints are neither loose nor over-tightened. Uses include fixture mounting, terminal and bus bar connections, cable glands and frangible coupling bolts.

Q2What types of torque wrench are used?

Click-type, beam-type, dial-type and digital. Digital wrenches show a real-time readout and can store data for quality assurance records.

Q3What accuracy and range does a torque wrench have?

Typically 1 to 500 Nm depending on the model, with accuracy of ±3% to ±5% of reading for click-type and about ±1% for digital.

Q4How often should a torque wrench be calibrated?

Typically every 12 months or 5,000 cycles, whichever comes first, or per the manufacturer's schedule. Recalibrate if it may have been dropped or misused.

Q5How should a torque wrench be stored?

At its lowest or relaxed setting, and it should never be used as a breaker bar or to loosen seized fasteners.

Q6Which standards apply to torque wrenches?

The main references are ISO 6789 (also adopted as DIN EN ISO 6789), ASME B107.14 in the USA and ISO 17025 for calibration laboratory competence.

Q7What should be checked before each use?

Confirm the set torque value matches the task specification before applying it to a fastener, especially when switching between jobs.

Q8What are the common mistakes with torque wrenches?

Loss of calibration through wear or mechanical shock, setting the wrong value, and using the wrench as a breaker bar, which damages the mechanism.
