Approach Light Barrette

A short row of closely‑spaced lights installed perpendicular to the approach centreline as part of a precision or non‑precision approach lighting system. Approach light barrettes provide pilots with critical distance and lateral guidance cues during final approach, helping maintain accurate alignment and glide‑slope awareness in low‑visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

An Approach Light Barrette is a short row of high‑intensity lights installed perpendicular to the approach centreline, typically consisting of 3 to 5 lights spaced 1–2 metres apart, forming a distinctive "bar" across the approach path. These barrettes are placed at fixed distances from the runway threshold (e.g., 300 ft, 600 ft, 900 ft, 1,200 ft, etc.) as part of the approach lighting system (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSF, and other configurations). They provide pilots with a series of bright visual reference points that help maintain precise lateral alignment and judge distance‑to‑go during the final approach segment.

Approach light barrettes are a fundamental component of both precision and medium‑intensity approach lighting systems. They work in conjunction with centreline barrettes, crossbar lights, side row bars, and sequenced flashing lights (SFL) to create a comprehensive visual reference for the pilot, particularly in low visibility (CAT I/II/III) operations where accurate glide‑path and centreline tracking are critical.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The approach light barrette's purpose is essentially the same distance-and-alignment guidance the approach crossbar provides, applied more densely and closer to the runway to give a pilot a finer-grained series of visual checkpoints as the aircraft gets nearer to the threshold. Because barrettes are spaced more tightly than crossbars, their cumulative effect is a continuous, closely-spaced sequence of lateral reference bars that lets a pilot track alignment and closing distance with more precision exactly where the margin for error is smallest — in the final few hundred metres before touchdown.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Approach light barrettes are deployed on precision and non‑precision approach runways and are used in:

  • CAT I precision approach runways (ALSF‑1) – providing essential distance and lateral guidance for approaches with 200 ft decision height.
  • CAT II and III precision approach runways (ALSF‑2) – critical for low‑visibility operations with decision heights as low as 100 ft or less.
  • Medium‑intensity approach lighting systems (MALSF, MALSR) – providing enhanced visual cues for non‑precision runways with higher traffic volumes.
  • Runways with low‑visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) – ensuring pilots have continuous distance and alignment cues.
  • Major international airports – where high traffic volumes and adverse weather demand reliable approach guidance.
  • Military airfields – requiring all‑weather precision approach capability.
Placement

Installation & Location

Barrettes are mounted on the approach lighting structures at the stations set out along the extended runway centreline, spaced at the intervals specified for the ALS configuration in use, with each barrette's row of light units running perpendicular to the approach path. The barrette sits on a frangible support at the design height, individually levelled and aimed during commissioning so the row reads as a single bar of light from the approach. Secondary cabling from the nearest approach light junction box is run up the structure to the barrette, and its distance from the threshold is set out from the approach lighting survey so the full string of barrettes provides a continuous, evenly spaced line of guidance.

Function

How Does It Work?

Approach light barrettes function as a series of visual reference points along the approach path:

  • Configuration: Each barrette is a short row of 3‑5 lights, typically spaced 1–2 m apart, installed perpendicular to the approach centreline. Multiple barrettes are placed at specific distances from the threshold (e.g., 300 ft, 600 ft, 900 ft, 1,200 ft, 1,500 ft, etc.) to provide a regular visual pattern along the approach path.
  • Lighting: The lights are high‑intensity white, steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by the CCR (5‑step). In some systems, barrettes may flash in sequence to create a "running" effect that guides the pilot toward the runway.
  • Distance Reference: As the aircraft descends, the pilot sees the barrettes as short horizontal bars. The spacing between barrettes provides distance‑to‑go information, helping the pilot judge approach progress and descent rate.
  • Lateral Guidance: If the aircraft is off‑centreline, the barrettes will appear asymmetrical, indicating the need for lateral correction. The perpendicular orientation of the barrettes provides a clear reference for maintaining centreline alignment.
  • Integration: Approach light barrettes are integrated with centreline lights, crossbar lights, side row bars, and sequenced flashing lights (SFL) to form a comprehensive visual approach system. They are typically positioned at regular intervals along the approach lighting structure.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Barrettes are controlled at the loop level, exactly like the crossbars, side rows, and sequenced flashers they share the approach lighting circuit with — none of these fixture types can be switched or dimmed independently of the others once they sit on the same loop. An ALCMS, acting on RVR data, ATC instruction, or pilot-controlled lighting, selects the required intensity step and hands it to the approach lighting CCR, which drives the whole loop to that fixed current. Because barrette stations are more numerous and more closely spaced than crossbars (typically every 30 m, extending out to 900 m from the threshold per ICAO CAT II/III, or up to 3,000 ft/~915 m in FAA ALSF configurations), a single loop often carries a longer run of isolating transformers, which is one reason approach circuits are frequently split into inner and outer loop sections so a fault or maintenance isolation on one section doesn't blank the entire approach light array. Fault reporting stays loop-based regardless: the CCR flags an open circuit as a voltage-rise event on its loop, and pinpointing which barrette station failed still requires a field check rather than a discrete signal from any single unit.

ALCMS / ATC / PCL → Approach Lighting CCR → Series Circuit Loop (Inner / Outer Section) → Barrette Station → loop-level feedback

Construction

Physical Construction

Each barrette light is a self-contained high-intensity fixture — an LED array or 45W/65W/100W halogen lamp behind a precision optical prism, housed in a die-cast aluminium body that is anodised or powder-coated against jet fuel, de-icing fluid, and UV exposure. Elevated barrettes mount each fixture on a frangible coupling and pole seated in an L-867 base can, so an individual light shears cleanly off on impact without disturbing its neighbours; flush-mounted barrettes instead seat each fixture in an L-868 base can rated for aircraft wheel loading. What makes a barrette a single component rather than several unrelated lights is the shared civil layout: 3–5 fixtures are set 1–2 metres apart in a fixed row, wired back to individual isolating transformers of matched wattage, and aligned so their beams present as one continuous bar to an approaching aircraft. Gasket-sealed top caps and O-ring seals on each fixture give the assembly its IP67/IP68 rating, and sealed L-823 splice connectors carry the series circuit between fixtures and back to the primary cable.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Frangible Support Height & Station Spacing
Civil

Set each barrette's frangible support to the design height at its station along the extended centreline, spaced per the ALS configuration in use.

Fixture Row Assembly
Mechanical

Fix the 3–5 lamp housings to the frangible coupling and pole in a fixed row 1–2 metres apart, seating each in its L-867 or L-868 base can as the elevated or flush-mounted design calls for.

Secondary Cable Run to Barrette
Electrical

Run secondary cabling up the structure from the nearest approach light junction box to the barrette, leaving enough slack near the coupling to allow a frangible break without pulling the conductor apart.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal L-823 splice connectors between fixtures and back to the primary cable, keeping every splice fully submersible-rated before the structure is closed up.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm each fixture's isolating transformer wattage is matched across the barrette's row before connection, so all lights in the bar present the same intensity.

Levelling & Aiming
Mechanical

Level and aim each fixture during commissioning so the row reads as a single, continuous bar of light from the approach.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record barrette station, fixture positions, cable route, and connector seals as-built before backfill or final fixing.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the series loop feeding the barrette before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Light Count Check
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity through every light in the barrette and confirm the correct light count for the specific approach lighting configuration.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each fixture's connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm all lights across the barrette respond together, ruling out marginal transformers within the row.

Barrette Position & Spacing Check
Final Acceptance

Confirm the barrette sits at its design distance from the runway threshold and that light-to-light spacing matches the design interval.

Alignment & Aim Verification
Final Acceptance

Check elevation and azimuth of every light across the barrette against the design aiming diagram, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity and spread across the barrette with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record loop resistance, voltage, alignment, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Precise Spacing and Alignment

Approach light barrettes must be installed with exact spacing both within the barrette and between successive barrettes. Any misalignment or deviation in spacing can create confusing visual cues for pilots. Regular surveys using theodolites or laser alignment tools are essential, especially after pavement resurfacing or maintenance.

Lens Contamination and Light Output

Barrette lights are exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits, and de‑icing fluids. Contamination can reduce light output and affect the clarity of the visual pattern. Routine cleaning and photometric checks are critical, especially before low‑visibility seasons.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated barrette lights are subject to wind loading and thermal cycling, which can fatigue the frangible couplings. Regular inspection and replacement per manufacturer schedules ensure the lights remain safe and properly positioned.

Flashing Synchronisation Failure

In systems where barrettes flash in sequence, timing errors can occur due to control circuit issues. This can disrupt the "running" effect and confuse pilots. Periodic testing of the flashing sequence and synchronisation is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Visual Pattern Appears Skewed or Confusing to Pilots
Likely Cause
Barrette spacing within a barrette or between successive barrettes has drifted from design specification — often after pavement resurfacing or maintenance nearby.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct a survey using a theodolite or laser alignment tool, checking barrette spacing and alignment against the design drawing.
Corrective Action
Re-align and re-position barrettes found out of tolerance, and re-survey after any nearby pavement work going forward.
Reduced Light Output or Unclear Visual Pattern
Likely Cause
Jet blast, rubber deposits, or de-icing fluids have contaminated the barrette lenses.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect lenses for film or contamination; take a photometric reading and compare against specification, especially ahead of low-visibility season.
Corrective Action
Clean lenses per the routine maintenance schedule, prioritising this before low-visibility periods, and re-verify photometric output.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Wind loading and thermal cycling have fatigued the frangible coupling on an elevated barrette light.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically inspect the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against tolerance.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling per the manufacturer's schedule, and re-level and re-aim the fixture.
"Running" Flash Effect Disrupted or Out of Sequence
Likely Cause
A control circuit issue has introduced timing errors into the barrette flashing sequence.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the flashing sequence and synchronisation across all barrettes in the system, checking each unit's timing against the control specification.
Corrective Action
Repair or recalibrate the faulted control circuit component, then re-verify the full sequence before returning the system to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Individual Light Unit Failure Within the Barrette
Medium Severity
Root Cause
LED array or transformer failure at a single unit in the barrette, from thermal cycling or moisture-driven insulation breakdown.
Effect
One unit in the barrette goes dark, reducing the distance and lateral guidance cue at that station along the approach.
Detection Method
Test activation of the barrette with a visual count of illuminated units, and loop-resistance testing to isolate the failed unit.
Barrette Aim / Level Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
One or more units in the barrette shifting out of the level and aim set during commissioning, from structure settlement or handling during maintenance.
Effect
The barrette no longer presents the consistent visual cue expected at that station, weakening the distance and alignment guidance it's meant to provide in low-visibility CAT I/II/III operations.
Detection Method
Level and aim check of each unit in the barrette against the commissioning survey during scheduled inspection.
Frangible Support Fatigue / Impact Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading fatiguing the barrette's frangible support, or a strike given its location directly in the approach path.
Effect
Barrette leans, misaligns, or partially separates from its structure, degrading the station cue it provides.
Detection Method
Post-strike inspection procedure, and coupling condition check during scheduled maintenance.
Feed Cable / Junction Box Failure to the Whole Barrette
High Severity
Root Cause
Fault in the cable run out from the approach light junction box feeding the entire barrette, or a junction box seal failure allowing moisture ingress at the shared feed point.
Effect
Loss of the entire barrette rather than a single unit, since all units share this feed, removing the station cue completely.
Detection Method
Continuity and insulation testing of the barrette's dedicated feed run back to its junction box.
Series Circuit Insulation Breakdown Along the Structure Run
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cable insulation degradation from age, moisture, or handling damage along the run from the junction box up the approach light structure to the barrette.
Effect
Intermittent operation or a partial ground fault on the circuit segment serving the barrette.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit segment during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Barrette circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR, then verify zero energy at the barrette before opening any fixture or connector.

Off-Runway Access
Personnel Safety

Barrettes are positioned at intervals along the approach area beyond the threshold, often on unpaved or uneven ground; assess ground conditions before positioning vehicles or personnel near a barrette.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible coupling or mount with a rigid fitting, and do not over-torque connections during installation or repair; the mount must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

A dark or degraded barrette can affect published CAT I/II/III minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any barrette light is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

FOD Control Along the Approach
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every barrette worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work on a barrette circuit, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Uniformity Across the Barrette
Compliance

A barrette's guidance value depends on every light in the row reading as a single uniform reference point; verify aim and intensity are matched across all lights in the barrette, not just the unit that was serviced.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a barrette light out of aim tolerance or at reduced intensity; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Barrette Uniformity Depends on Matched Aim, Not Just Matched Fixtures
Verification

Individual lamps within a barrette can each test within photometric tolerance while small differences in aim between them create a visibly uneven barrette from the approach. Check aim consistency across all lamps in the barrette as part of routine verification, not just each lamp's individual output.

Lamp Spacing Within a Barrette Affects Perceived Brightness at Distance
Design

At the distances pilots view approach lighting from, closely spaced lamps within a barrette visually merge into a single perceived light source; uneven spacing changes that merged appearance even when individual lamp output is correct. Maintain design spacing tolerance during any barrette repair or replacement.

Heat Cycling Fatigues Barrette Wiring Harnesses Over Time
Electrical

The shared wiring harness feeding multiple lamps in a barrette experiences repeated heat cycling from step changes; connector fatigue in the harness can cause intermittent single-lamp dropout that's easy to mistake for an individual lamp fault. Check harness connections, not just the lamp, when troubleshooting intermittent barrette faults.

Barrette Support Structure Sees Combined Wind Load Across Multiple Lamps
Structural

A barrette's mounting structure carries the combined wind load of several lamp housings rather than one, and that combined load should be assessed for the specific barrette configuration rather than assumed proportional to a single-fixture rating.

Moisture Ingress in One Connector Can Affect the Whole Barrette
Troubleshooting

Because barrette lamps often share a common feed point, a single degraded connector at that junction can present as multiple lamps dimming or failing together, which is easy to misread as several independent lamp faults. Check the shared connection first when more than one lamp in a barrette shows the same symptom.

Trend Insulation Readings by Barrette, Not Just by Loop
Troubleshooting

On a loop feeding several barrettes, insulation resistance trending at the loop level can mask which specific barrette connector is degrading. Where test points allow it, trend readings at the barrette level to localise a developing fault before it takes the whole loop out.
