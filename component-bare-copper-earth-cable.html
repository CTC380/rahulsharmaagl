Bare Copper Earth Cable

An uninsulated, high‑purity copper conductor used as the backbone of the earthing system in AGL installations. It provides a low‑resistance, corrosion‑resistant path for fault currents, lightning strikes, and electrostatic discharge to safely dissipate into the earth, protecting personnel and equipment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Bare Copper Earth Cable (also known as a bare copper ground conductor, earth lead, or grounding conductor) is a solid or stranded copper conductor without any insulation or sheath, used exclusively for earthing (grounding) applications in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems. Its high conductivity and resistance to corrosion make it the preferred choice for connecting equipment, earthing pits, and lightning protection systems to the main earth grid.

Key characteristics of bare copper earth cables include:

  • Bare Conductor: No insulation or sheath, allowing direct contact with earth, soil, or concrete, which reduces the overall resistance to earth.
  • High Conductivity: Typically made of high‑purity (EC grade) copper with a conductivity of ≥ 99.9% IACS, ensuring low resistance for fault current dissipation.
  • Corrosion Resistance: Copper naturally forms a protective oxide layer that resists corrosion in most soils, though tinned copper may be used in aggressive environments.
  • Flexibility: Available in solid or stranded constructions, with stranded being more flexible and easier to route in trenches and around obstacles.

The cable is used for:

  • Main Earthing Grid: Connecting earth electrodes (rods, plates, or grids) in a network to achieve a low overall earth resistance (typically ≤ 10 Ω).
  • Equipment Bonding: Connecting AGL equipment such as CCRs, distribution panels, junction boxes, and light fixtures to the earthing system.
  • Lightning Protection: Providing a low‑impedance path for lightning currents from air terminals and down‑conductors to the earth.
  • Screen Termination: Connecting the screens of shielded cables to earth via earthing connectors.

Sizes typically range from 16mm² to 95mm² (AWG 6 to 3/0), depending on the fault current rating and the resistance requirements. The cable is installed in trenches, direct‑buried, or in concrete foundations, and is terminated using exothermic welding, compression lugs, or mechanical clamps.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The bare copper earth cable's purpose is to give fault current, lightning strikes, and static discharge a path of least resistance into the ground, so that current takes that route instead of through equipment casings, structural steel, or a person standing nearby. Leaving the conductor bare, rather than insulated, is itself part of the function: uninsulated copper makes direct contact with soil or concrete along its length, which lowers the overall earthing resistance in a way an insulated cable buried the same way could not achieve. Because every earthed component on the airfield, from CCRs to individual fixtures, ultimately routes back through this network, the reliability of the whole earthing system depends on this cable maintaining a genuinely low-resistance path rather than merely providing a nominal connection.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Bare copper earth cables are used throughout the airfield in all earthing and bonding applications, including:

  • Earth Pit Interconnections: Connecting multiple earth pits and electrodes to form a low‑resistance earthing grid.
  • CCR and Equipment Earthing: Bonding the chassis of CCRs, distribution panels, and control cabinets to the earthing system for safety and EMI reduction.
  • Lightning Protection Systems: Connecting lightning down‑conductors to the earthing grid, and bonding air terminals to the main earth.
  • Junction Box and Fixture Earthing: Providing an earth connection for metal enclosures, junction boxes, and light fixture housings.
  • Shielded Cable Screen Termination: Connecting the screen connectors of shielded cables to a dedicated earth bar.
  • Structural Steel Bonding: Bonding airfield structures (masts, poles, buildings) to the earthing system to prevent potential differences.
  • Counterpoise and Ring Earth Electrodes: Forming ring earth electrodes around sensitive equipment or buildings.

The cable is installed in trenches at a depth of at least 0.5m, often in a bed of backfill material to improve contact with the earth. It is terminated using exothermic welding (for permanent, low‑resistance connections) or compression lugs bolted to earth bars. The selection of conductor size is based on the site's prospective fault current for 1 second (roughly 2.8kA to 17kA across the 16–95mm² sizes commonly used, using k=176) and the required earth resistance.

Placement

Installation & Location

The cable is laid in trenches at a minimum depth of around 0.5 m, generally run alongside or beneath the primary and secondary cable routes so every piece of AGL equipment along the way has a short, direct connection back to the earthing grid. Where it interconnects earth pits, rods, or plates, the conductor is bedded in backfill or an earthing-enhancement compound that improves soil contact and lowers the local resistance around each electrode. Above-ground risers, at CCR cabinets, junction boxes, and mast bases, are protected from mechanical damage and UV exposure with a sleeve or conduit section, since the conductor itself is left bare to maximise soil contact below grade. Connections to electrodes and equipment are made by exothermic weld where a permanent, maintenance-free joint is required, or by bolted compression lug at points such as test wells and equipment bonding bars where periodic access or testing is needed.

Function

How Does It Work?

The bare copper earth cable functions as a low‑impedance conductor that provides a safe path for electrical faults and surges to dissipate into the ground. Its effectiveness relies on several principles:

  • Low Resistance: Copper's high conductivity ensures that fault currents can flow with minimal voltage drop, preventing dangerous potential rises on equipment enclosures.
  • Corrosion Resistance: The natural oxide layer on copper protects it from corrosion, maintaining a low‑resistance connection over decades.
  • Large Surface Area: The bare conductor is in direct contact with the surrounding soil or concrete, increasing the effective earth contact area and reducing the overall earth resistance.
  • Low Inductance: A straight, short run minimizes inductive reactance, which is important for lightning currents (high‑frequency transients).

In the event of a fault (e.g., a phase‑to‑earth short circuit), the fault current flows through the equipment's earthing connection, along the bare copper earth cable, and into the earth electrode (or grid). The low resistance of the cable ensures that the voltage rise on the equipment is limited to a safe level, preventing electric shock hazards and equipment damage. For lightning strikes, the cable provides a direct path for the high‑current surge, dissipating it into the earth without causing arcing or flashover.

The cable is typically connected to an earth electrode (rod, plate, or grid) at one end and to the equipment earthing bar at the other. Multiple cables may be connected in a star or ring configuration to achieve the required earth resistance. The entire system is designed to keep the earth resistance below the specified limit (typically ≤ 10 Ω for AGL systems).

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The bare copper earth cable sits entirely outside the constant current series circuit and its ALCMS/CCR control loop — it carries no lighting current in normal operation and is not part of the loop that the CCR regulates or monitors. Its role only becomes active under fault conditions: when insulation breaks down or a lightning strike occurs, the earth cable provides the low-impedance path that carries the resulting current safely to the electrode system rather than through equipment or personnel. Because this path is passive, there is no real-time signal the ALCMS can use to confirm it is intact — a corroded or severed earth cable produces no voltage anomaly on the series loop and will not trip a loop fault on the CCR. Its condition is instead verified offline, through periodic earth resistance testing (fall-of-potential or clamp-on methods) carried out as part of the scheduled maintenance programme rather than through the lighting control system itself.

Construction

Physical Construction

Unlike an insulated power or signal cable, the earth conductor is a single, uniform material end to end: high‑purity (EC grade) copper, drawn either as a solid round wire or as a bundle of fine strands twisted together for flexibility, with no insulation, sheath, or jacket of any kind. Bare construction is deliberate, since direct metal‑to‑soil or metal‑to‑concrete contact is what lowers the overall resistance to earth; any covering would insulate the conductor from the very medium it needs to bond to. Where aggressive soils are expected, the copper may be tin‑plated to slow corrosion without meaningfully raising resistance. At each end, the conductor is joined to an electrode, equipment frame, or another conductor by exothermic welding (a molten copper‑alloy reaction that fuses the two metals into a single continuous joint), a compression lug crimped under high pressure, or a mechanical clamp, with the joint method chosen for permanence and the lowest achievable contact resistance.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion in Aggressive Soils

In soils with high acidity, alkalinity, or salinity, bare copper can corrode over time, increasing resistance. Use tinned copper cable or apply protective coatings. Consider using copper‑clad steel or stainless steel earth electrodes in such environments.

Mechanical Damage During Installation

Bare copper cable can be nicked, cut, or crushed during trenching and backfilling, reducing its current‑carrying capacity. Lay the cable on a bed of sand or fine soil, and protect it with a marker tape or cover tiles.

Excessive Joint Resistance

Poorly executed terminations (e.g., loose clamps, insufficient weld material) can introduce high resistance, defeating the purpose of the earthing system. Use exothermic welding for permanent connections and follow the manufacturer's procedures for compression lugs.

Inadequate Earth Resistance

If the earth resistance is too high (e.g., > 10 Ω), fault currents may not be safely dissipated. This can be caused by a small conductor size, poor soil conductivity, or insufficient electrode depth. Use larger conductor sizes, add more earth rods, or treat the soil with bentonite or earth‑enhancement compounds.

Theft and Vandalism

Bare copper cables are a target for theft due to the high scrap value of copper. Secure cables with concrete encasement, use welded connections that are difficult to remove, and consider installing cable monitoring systems.

Galvanic Corrosion with Dissimilar Metals

Connecting bare copper to aluminium or steel can cause galvanic corrosion. Use bi‑metallic connectors or apply a protective coating and anti‑oxidant compound at the interface.

Inadequate Cross‑Sectional Area

Using a cable that is too small for the fault current can lead to overheating and melting. Always size the cable according to the maximum prospective fault current and the duration of the fault (typically 1 second).

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Earth Resistance Reading Above Acceptable Limit (>10 Ω)
Likely Cause
Conductor undersized for soil conditions, insufficient electrode depth, or poor soil conductivity at the test location.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a 3-pole or 4-pole earth resistance test at the affected pit; check conductor size and electrode depth against design.
Corrective Action
Add supplementary earth rods, treat the soil with a bentonite or earth-enhancement compound, or upsize the conductor, then re-test.
Visible Corrosion or Green Deposits on Exposed Cable
Likely Cause
Aggressive soil chemistry (high acidity, alkalinity, or salinity) is attacking the bare copper conductor.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect exposed sections at pits and risers for pitting or green corrosion product; check soil pH/resistivity if data is available.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded sections with tinned copper or copper-clad steel cable suited to the soil condition, and apply a protective coating at exposed points.
High Resistance at a Termination or Joint
Likely Cause
A poorly executed clamp, loose bolted connection, or an underfilled exothermic weld is introducing resistance at the joint.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure contact resistance across the joint with a micro-ohmmeter if available; visually inspect for a loose clamp or a porous, underfilled weld.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with a properly executed exothermic weld or correctly torqued compression lug per the manufacturer's procedure, then re-test.
Cable Missing or Cut Section Found During Inspection
Likely Cause
Theft of exposed bare copper due to its scrap value, or accidental mechanical damage during nearby excavation.
Diagnostic Steps
Trace the run to locate the missing/cut section; check for signs of digging or tampering at accessible points.
Corrective Action
Replace the missing section, encase exposed runs in concrete or conduit where theft risk is high, and consider a welded connection that is harder to remove.
Accelerated Corrosion at a Dissimilar-Metal Connection
Likely Cause
Direct connection of bare copper to aluminium or steel without a bi-metallic connector has set up galvanic corrosion.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect connection points between copper and other metals for white/grey corrosion product characteristic of galvanic attack.
Corrective Action
Replace the direct connection with an approved bi-metallic connector and apply anti-oxidant compound at the interface.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Bonding Continuity Verification Before Disconnection
Electrical Safety

Confirm an alternative or temporary earth path is in place before disconnecting any section of the earth grid, since removing a bonding conductor can leave connected equipment without fault protection.

Never Disconnect Earth on Live Equipment
Electrical Safety

Never disconnect an earth conductor from equipment that is or could be energised; treat the earth cable as safety-critical even though it normally carries no current.

Cable Locating Before Excavation
Operational

Obtain and follow an up-to-date cable locate before any excavation near an earth grid route, and take particular care around earthing pits and ground rods that may not be visible at the surface.

Galvanic Corrosion Prevention at Joints
Mechanical

Use compatible connectors and anti-corrosion compound at any joint between bare copper and a dissimilar metal, since a galvanic joint left untreated raises the earth path's resistance over time.

Theft Awareness & Reporting
Operational

Report any sign of cut, missing, or disturbed bare copper earth cable immediately, since exposed copper grounding conductors are a known theft target and a missing section can silently degrade the site's earthing system.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Healthy-Looking Grid Can Still Have a Weak Path
Troubleshooting

Overall earth resistance measured at a test pit can stay within tolerance even if a section of the grid between two points has developed high resistance, because current simply redistributes through the remaining healthy paths. Periodically test resistance between grid segments, not just at the boundary test points, to catch a degrading section before it becomes the only path left.

Theft Doesn't Always Announce Itself
Reliability

A partial cut or removal of buried earth conductor often goes unnoticed for a long time since the grid keeps functioning through the remaining intact paths until a fault actually occurs. Where copper theft is a known regional risk, periodic continuity testing between known grid points is a far more reliable early warning than visual inspection alone.

Dissimilar-Metal Joints Are the First Place Resistance Rises
Corrosion

Where bare copper meets a galvanized structure, rebar, or a different connector material without proper anti-corrosion treatment, that joint is almost always where measured grid resistance starts climbing first. Prioritise these transition points in any earth-grid inspection rather than treating the whole run as equally at risk.

Soil Resistivity Changes Seasonally, So Test Consistently
Design

Earth resistance readings taken in the dry season can look significantly worse than the same grid tested after monsoon rains, purely from soil moisture variation rather than any actual degradation. Take readings at a consistent time of year, or note seasonal conditions alongside the result, so trend comparisons aren't misread as grid deterioration.

As-Built Grid Drawings Rarely Survive Years of Repairs
Documentation

Emergency repairs and reroutes to the earth grid over time tend not to get documented as carefully as the original design drawing. Before excavating near an older grid, verify the actual route with a cable/grid locator rather than relying solely on the as-built record.

The Earth Grid Is Only as Good as Its Least-Tested Section
Maintenance

It's tempting to test earth resistance only at the main pits since that's where the instrument connects easily, but the sections in between carry the actual fault current path. Where practical, verify continuity across the full grid layout rather than sampling only the accessible test points.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the bare copper earth cable: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is bare copper earth cable?

Bare copper earth cable (also called bare copper ground conductor or earth lead) is a solid or stranded copper conductor with no insulation or sheath, used only for earthing in AGL systems. It connects equipment, earthing pits and lightning protection systems to the main earth grid.

Q2Why is the conductor bare?

The bare conductor can touch soil or concrete directly, which increases earth contact area and lowers overall resistance to earth. Copper also forms a natural oxide layer that resists corrosion for decades.

Q3Where is bare copper earth cable used?

For earth pit interconnections that form a low-resistance grid, for earthing CCRs, distribution panels and control cabinets, for connecting lightning down-conductors, and in other AGL earthing and bonding applications.

Q4What conductor sizes and materials are typical?

High-purity EC-grade copper of at least 99.9% IACS conductivity, in 16 mm², 25 mm², 35 mm², 50 mm², 70 mm² or 95 mm² (about AWG 6 to 3/0). Stranded construction is preferred for flexibility, and a tinned option gives extra corrosion protection in aggressive soils.

Q5What fault current can the cable carry?

About 2.8 kA for 16 mm² up to 17 kA for 95 mm² for 1 second, using k = 176 per IEC 60364-5-54. Verify this against the site's actual prospective fault current.

Q6How is the cable terminated?

Common methods are exothermic welding, compression lugs and mechanical clamps. Joint and termination resistance must stay low to keep the earth grid effective.

Q7Is bare copper earth cable part of the powered circuit?

No. It is an earth grid conductor, not part of the series or secondary circuit, and is kept physically and electrically separate from primary and secondary cabling except at designated bonding points.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are IEC 60228 (conductor sizes and resistance), BS 6360, ASTM B8 (concentric-lay-stranded copper conductors) and FAA AC 150/5340-30 (grounding and bonding practices). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should the earthing be tested and inspected?

Typical intervals are annual for earth resistance testing, joint and termination inspection, corrosion inspection and bi-metallic connection checks, and semi-annual for route and cover inspection and theft and tamper checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with bare copper earth cable?

Common problems are corrosion in aggressive soils, mechanical damage during installation, excessive joint resistance, inadequate earth resistance, theft and vandalism, galvanic corrosion with dissimilar metals and inadequate cross-section. An earth resistance reading above 10 Ω, green deposits on exposed cable or a missing section are typical warning signs.
