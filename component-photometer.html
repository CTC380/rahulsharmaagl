Photometer

A precision optical instrument used to measure luminous intensity, illuminance, luminance, and chromaticity of AGL fixtures. Essential for photometric testing, certification, and field verification of airfield ground lighting systems to ensure compliance with ICAO, FAA, and IEC standards.

Overview

What Is It?

A Photometer is a sophisticated optoelectronic instrument designed to quantify light characteristics as perceived by the human eye. In the context of airfield ground lighting (AGL), photometers are used to measure and verify the photometric performance of runway, taxiway, and approach lighting fixtures. Key parameters measured include:

  • Luminous intensity (candela, cd) — the amount of light emitted in a specific direction
  • Illuminance (lux, lx) — the amount of light falling on a surface
  • Luminance (candela per square metre, cd/m²) — brightness of a surface
  • Chromaticity coordinates — colour accuracy according to ICAO/FAA colour specifications
  • Beam pattern — spatial distribution of light output

Photometers are used both in the laboratory (for type testing and certification) and in the field (for commissioning, maintenance, and troubleshooting). They are a critical tool for ensuring that AGL systems provide the required visual guidance for safe aircraft operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The photometer's purpose is to turn the abstract requirement that a fixture be "bright enough" into an objective, measurable pass/fail result, because the human eye is a poor and inconsistent judge of absolute light output, especially across the wide range of conditions AGL fixtures must perform in. By quantifying intensity, illuminance, and beam pattern in physical units, the photometer lets a manufacturer prove a new fixture meets its certified specification before it is ever installed, lets an airport confirm a fixture is still performing to specification years into its service life, and lets a technician distinguish between a light that only looks dim to the eye and one that has genuinely degraded below the level a pilot needs for safe guidance.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Photometers are deployed in various scenarios across the AGL lifecycle:

  • Manufacturing and Type Testing: Used by AGL fixture manufacturers to certify that new products meet ICAO, FAA, and IEC photometric specifications before they are released to the market.
  • Airport Commissioning: During new AGL system installation or major upgrades, photometers are used to verify that every fixture meets the required intensity and beam pattern specifications.
  • Periodic Maintenance Inspection: Airports perform routine photometric checks (e.g., annually) to detect degradation in light output due to ageing, contamination, or optical damage.
  • Troubleshooting: When pilots report poor visibility of certain lights, photometers are used to pinpoint the cause — whether it’s low intensity, wrong colour, or misalignment.
  • Calibration of Field Test Equipment: Portable photometers are often used to calibrate simpler lux meters and alignment tools used by maintenance crews.
Placement

Installation & Location

As a portable measuring instrument rather than a fixed fixture, the photometer has no permanent airfield location — it is stored in a calibration case in the maintenance workshop and carried to the test point for each job. For fixture testing, it is set up on a tripod or goniophotometer bench at the specified test distance and angle from the light source, aligned to the fixture's optical axis per the relevant ICAO or FAA test geometry. In the workshop, a fixed test bench with a dark enclosure is often used for repeatable bench measurements of removed fixtures, while field measurements are taken directly at the installed light with the unit levelled and referenced against the fixture's aiming point. The instrument itself is returned periodically to an accredited calibration laboratory to maintain its traceability to national standards.

Function

How Does It Work?

A photometer typically consists of:

  • Optical System: A collection lens or aperture that captures light from the fixture under test. For directional measurements (intensity), the photometer is placed at a specific distance (e.g., 30 m) and aligned with the fixture’s optical axis. For illuminance measurements, the sensor is placed on the surface.
  • Photodetector: A silicon photodiode or photomultiplier tube with a spectral response that mimics the human eye’s photopic response (V(λ) curve). This is achieved using a precision colour correction filter.
  • Signal Conditioning and ADC: The photodetector output is amplified, filtered, and digitised for processing.
  • Microprocessor and Software: The instrument calculates photometric quantities based on the detector signal and calibration constants. Advanced photometers also measure colour (via tristimulus filters or spectroradiometric techniques) and can store data for analysis.
  • Display and Interface: Readings are shown on an LCD screen or transmitted to a PC or mobile device via Bluetooth, USB, or Wi-Fi.

For AGL measurements, the photometer is often mounted on a tripod at a specific height and distance from the fixture. The fixture is powered by a constant current regulator at the required step (e.g., 6.6 A for maximum intensity). The photometer captures the intensity over multiple angles to map the beam pattern, ensuring the fixture meets the required angular distribution.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A field photometer is a self-contained measurement instrument rather than a networked control system, but its accuracy depends on a defined calibration and data chain. The detector head converts received luminous intensity into an electrical signal, which is conditioned and digitized by onboard electronics and referenced against the instrument's stored calibration coefficients, traceable to a photometric standard lamp. The processed reading — intensity, and on angular photometers, the beam's spatial distribution — is logged locally and/or transmitted to a maintenance database, where it is compared against the fixture's certified isocandela requirement to generate a pass/fail result. Because the photometer measures absolute intensity, any drift in its own calibration directly corrupts every downstream pass/fail decision, which is why periodic recalibration against a certified reference lamp is built into the maintenance interval rather than left to instrument self-check alone.

Construction

Physical Construction

A field photometer is a hand-held, rugged instrument built around a precision-filtered silicon photodiode or photomultiplier detector, colour-corrected to the CIE photopic response so its readings match human eye sensitivity across the visible spectrum. The detector sits behind a lens or aperture assembly that defines the instrument's field of view, housed in a moulded, impact-resistant polymer body sized to be operated one-handed while sighting the fixture under test through an optical or electronic viewfinder. A digital display on the housing shows live intensity, illuminance, or chromaticity readings, with control buttons for range selection, data logging, and interface settings, and an internal rechargeable battery (or AC adapter for bench use) powers the unit for a full day of field measurements. Data ports — USB, Bluetooth, RS-232, or Wi-Fi — allow logged readings to be downloaded to a laptop or tablet for reporting, and the casing is sealed to a moderate ingress-protection rating suited to outdoor field use rather than the fully submersible ratings used on buried AGL fixtures.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Calibration Drift

Photometers are precision instruments that require regular calibration against a traceable reference standard (e.g., from a national metrology institute). Drift can occur due to ageing of the photodiode, changes in filter transmission, or mechanical shocks. Annual calibration is recommended, and field checks with a portable reference light source should be performed before each measurement session.

Stray Light and Ambient Light Interference

In outdoor measurements, ambient light (sunlight, other fixtures) can corrupt readings. Using a baffle or hood on the photometer, measuring at night, or using differential techniques can mitigate this. Some instruments have a built-in ambient light compensation feature.

Alignment Errors

Incorrect alignment of the photometer’s optical axis with the fixture’s beam centre can lead to low intensity readings. Using a laser pointer or the fixture’s own alignment marks helps. Many modern photometers include a built-in camera or viewfinder for precise aiming.

Temperature Effects

Photodetectors and electronics are temperature-sensitive. For accurate results, the photometer should be allowed to stabilise to ambient temperature before measurements. Some instruments have temperature compensation circuitry.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Readings Drift from a Reference Standard
Likely Cause
Ageing of the photodiode, changes in filter transmission, or mechanical shock have shifted the instrument's calibration.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a field check with a portable reference light source before the measurement session; review calibration history for drift.
Corrective Action
Send the photometer for calibration against a traceable reference standard, or replace it if it cannot be brought back into tolerance.
Unexpectedly High or Noisy Readings Outdoors
Likely Cause
Ambient light from sunlight or other fixtures is corrupting the measurement.
Diagnostic Steps
Check whether the built-in ambient light compensation is enabled; confirm a baffle or hood is fitted and correctly oriented.
Corrective Action
Fit or reposition the baffle/hood, measure at night where possible, or use differential measurement techniques to remove the ambient contribution.
Low Intensity Reading on a Known-Good Fixture
Likely Cause
The photometer's optical axis is not properly aligned with the fixture's beam centre.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-align using a laser pointer or the fixture's own alignment marks, or the photometer's built-in camera/viewfinder if fitted.
Corrective Action
Re-align the photometer to the beam centre and re-take the measurement, confirming the reading returns to the expected value.
Readings Vary Between the Start and End of a Session
Likely Cause
The photodetector and electronics have not yet stabilised to ambient temperature.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor a fixed reference reading over time after power-on to check for a stabilising trend; check whether temperature compensation is active.
Corrective Action
Allow the photometer to stabilise to ambient temperature before starting measurements, and enable temperature compensation if available.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Calibration Currency Verification
Operational

Confirm the photometer's calibration certificate is current and traceable before using it for compliance testing, and do not accept readings from an out-of-calibration instrument as evidence of conformance.

Safe Positioning During Runway/Taxiway Readings
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations before taking readings on or near an active runway or taxiway, and keep personnel and equipment clear of the path of taxiing or landing aircraft.

Ambient Light Control During Testing
Operational

Shield the sensor from extraneous light sources such as vehicle headlights, ambient sky glow, or nearby fixtures not under test, since stray light biases photometric readings.

Battery/Power Check Before Field Use
Operational

Verify battery condition before starting a photometric survey; a failing battery can cause the instrument to drift or shut down partway through a scheduled set of readings.

Night Working Visibility
Personnel Safety

Wear high-visibility clothing and use a suitable work light when conducting photometric surveys at night, since most fixture testing is done after dark on an active movement area.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Cross-Check Against a Reference Fixture, Not Just the Calibration Sticker
Calibration

A valid calibration certificate confirms the instrument was accurate on the bench on a given date; it doesn't confirm it's still tracking correctly in the field months later. Carrying a known-output reference fixture for a quick spot-check before a survey catches drift between formal calibration cycles.

Cable Length Can Bias Low-Signal Readings
Electrical

On the lowest brightness steps, the signal from the detector head to the readout unit is very small, and a long or poorly shielded cable run can introduce enough noise to shift the reading. If low-step readings look inconsistent between two otherwise identical setups, check cable routing before suspecting the fixture.

Angular Alignment Matters More Than Distance
Measurement

Small errors in detector aim relative to the fixture's optical axis change the reading far more than small errors in measurement distance do, particularly close to the beam edge. Verify aim with a sighting aid rather than relying on eye judgment alone, especially on tightly collimated PAPI or approach lighting units.

Track Detector Aging Separately From the Calibration Date
Reliability

Photodiode responsivity degrades gradually with UV and thermal exposure over years of service, which a single-point calibration check doesn't always catch early. Logging the reading against a fixed reference source at every use builds a trend that flags a detector nearing the end of its useful accuracy before it fails a compliance test.

Correlate Field Readings With Lab Goniophotometer Data Periodically
Verification

Field photometers measure a single point or narrow angular slice, while a lab goniophotometer captures the full distribution; running the two against a sample fixture on the same batch periodically confirms the field instrument's angular response still matches its factory characterisation.

Account for Pavement Reflectance in Ambient Checks
Field Technique

Wet or newly resurfaced pavement reflects markedly more stray light back into the detector than dry, aged pavement, which can skew ambient-light baseline checks taken on different nights. Note pavement condition alongside the reading when trending ambient levels over a maintenance season.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL photometers: what they measure, accuracy, use cases, standards, calibration and common faults.

Q1What is a photometer?

A photometer is an optoelectronic instrument that quantifies light as perceived by the human eye. In AGL it measures and verifies the photometric performance of runway, taxiway and approach lighting fixtures.

Q2What does a photometer measure?

Luminous intensity (cd), illuminance (lux), luminance (cd/m²), chromaticity coordinates against ICAO/FAA colour specifications, and beam pattern. Typical ranges are 0.001–500,000 cd for intensity and 0.01–200,000 lx for illuminance.

Q3Why can't the eye judge whether a fixture is bright enough?

The human eye is a poor and inconsistent judge of absolute light output across the wide range of conditions AGL fixtures operate in. A photometer turns "bright enough" into an objective, measurable pass/fail result in physical units.

Q4Where are photometers used in the AGL lifecycle?

Manufacturing and type testing, airport commissioning of new or upgraded systems, and periodic maintenance inspections (for example annually) to detect degradation from ageing or contamination.

Q5How does a photometer work?

A lens or aperture collects light onto a silicon photodiode or photomultiplier with a colour-correction filter that mimics the eye's photopic V(λ) response. A signal conditioning stage and ADC then produce the reading. For intensity it is placed at a set distance, such as 10 m, 30 m or 100 m depending on the standard, and aligned with the fixture's optical axis.

Q6How accurate is a photometer?

Typically ±2% of reading for intensity, with a spectral response error f₁' of 3% or less, measuring chromaticity in CIE 1931 (x,y) or 1976 (u',v'). The field of view is typically 1° to 5°.

Q7Is a photometer part of the airfield lighting circuit?

No. It is a portable, battery- or AC-adapter-powered test instrument stored in a calibration case in the workshop and carried to each test point, set up on a tripod or goniophotometer bench at the specified distance and angle.

Q8Which standards apply to photometry of AGL fixtures?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program), IEC/TS 61827 and CIE standards for photometry and colour measurement. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a photometer be calibrated and checked?

Typical intervals are before each session for a field check with a portable reference source, quarterly for optics and baffle cleaning and temperature stabilisation checks, semi-annual for alignment and viewfinder checks, and annual for traceable reference calibration and battery and electronics checks. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why are my photometer readings drifting from a reference standard?

Ageing of the photodiode, changes in filter transmission or mechanical shock have shifted the calibration. Do a field check with a portable reference source, review calibration history and send the instrument for traceable calibration, or replace it if it cannot be brought into tolerance.

Q11Why are outdoor readings high or noisy?

Sunlight or other fixtures are corrupting the measurement. Check that ambient light compensation is enabled and that the baffle or hood is fitted and correctly oriented, and measure at night where possible or use differential measurement.
