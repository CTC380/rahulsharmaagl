LED & Light Source Comparison

SINGLE LED VS MULTIPLE LED

One Primary Source vs Combined Sources — single-LED architecture uses one primary LED light source to produce the required optical output, whereas multiple-LED architecture uses several LEDs working together to produce the required output. The number of LEDs alone does not determine the quality or compliance of the fixture; what matters is whether the complete fixture meets the required photometric performance.

02 · Basic Architecture

How Each Signal Chain Is Built

Single LED

Electrical input → driver → one LED → optics → AGL light beam. One LED, or one primary LED package, provides the source for the required beam.

Multiple LED

Electrical input → driver/control → several LEDs (e.g. LED 1–4) → optics → AGL light beam. Several LEDs contribute to the final optical output.

04 · Light Output

Scaling the Optical Power

A single LED has a practical limit to the optical power it can produce while maintaining suitable thermal performance, reliability, electrical operation, and optical performance. Pushing a single LED harder means higher drive current and higher thermal stress. Multiple LEDs can distribute the required output across several sources instead.

Multiple LEDs can achieve higher total fixture output without requiring all optical power from one source.

05 · Thermal Management

Concentrated Heat vs Distributed Heat

Single LED

High power at one source concentrates the thermal load, which must be managed by the heat sink around that single point.

Multiple LEDs

Heat is generated at several points and can be distributed physically across the fixture rather than concentrated at one spot.

Multiple LEDs still produce the same total thermal load associated with the system's total power — good thermal design remains essential regardless of architecture.

06 · Reliability & Redundancy

What Happens When a Source Fails

ScenarioSingle LEDMultiple LEDs
Critical source failsLight output can be lost completelyRemaining LEDs may continue operating (design-dependent)
Degradation patternAbrupt lossPotentially graceful degradation

Multiple LEDs can provide graceful degradation — but only if the fixture is specifically designed to tolerate individual LED failures. Simply having multiple LEDs does not automatically guarantee redundancy.

07 · Optical Design

Alignment vs Mixing

Single LED

With one source position, optical alignment (LED → lens → controlled beam) can be comparatively straightforward.

Multiple LEDs

Multiple sources may require careful optical mixing, lens design, source positioning, and intensity balancing — otherwise the fixture can produce uneven intensity, multiple visible source images, or a non-uniform beam pattern.

08 · Beam Quality

Single Source vs Combined Beam

The single-LED beam is shaped from one primary source. The multiple-LED beam's final photometric distribution depends on how effectively the individual outputs are combined.

More LEDs do not automatically mean better photometric performance. The complete fixture must still meet the required intensity and angular distribution.

09 · Electrical Architecture

Simple Chain vs Multiple Arrangements

A single-LED fixture's electrical architecture (supply → driver → LED) may be comparatively simple. Multiple LEDs can be connected in different arrangements, each with different implications:

ArrangementDescription
SeriesLEDs connected LED → LED → LED → LED in one string
ParallelLEDs connected across the supply in parallel branches
Multiple controlled channelsSeparate drivers feeding separate LED groups

The actual configuration affects current sharing, failure behaviour, redundancy, monitoring, and electrical complexity.

13 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: Multiple LEDs are always more reliable
Fact

Not necessarily. A poorly designed multi-LED circuit can have a common failure point, such as one driver or one electrical connection, which can defeat any redundancy benefit.

Myth: One high-power LED is always simpler and better
Fact

Not necessarily. A single high-power LED can create concentrated thermal and reliability challenges that a distributed multi-LED design avoids.

Myth: More LEDs automatically mean more candela
Fact

Not necessarily. Candela depends on the combined source output and optical distribution, not LED quantity alone.

15 · Engineering Verdict

Single LED vs Multiple LED Architecture

Engineering Verdict

Single LED architecture uses one primary light source with a simpler source and optical arrangement, but less inherent tolerance to single-source failure.

Multiple LED architecture uses combined light sources with greater output scalability and potential graceful degradation, but with more complex optical, thermal, and electrical design. The number of LEDs alone never determines the quality or compliance of the fixture — only the complete fixture's photometric performance does.

Single LEDOne source + simpler optics/electrical + concentrated thermal/failure risk.
Multiple LEDCombined sources + scalable output + potential redundancy, if designed for it.

For AGL fixtures, the preferred architecture depends on the required photometric output, beam geometry, thermal design, electrical reliability, and failure-performance requirements — not on LED count alone.

16 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the simplest way to remember the difference?

Single LED architecture = CONCENTRATION, one source. Multiple LED architecture = COMBINATION, several sources.

Are multiple LEDs always more reliable than a single LED?

Not automatically. A poorly designed multi-LED circuit can share a common failure point, such as one driver or connection, that removes the redundancy benefit multiple sources would otherwise offer.

Does having multiple LEDs guarantee graceful degradation?

No. Graceful degradation only occurs if the fixture's electrical and optical design is specifically engineered to tolerate individual LED failures.

Do more LEDs always mean higher candela output?

No. Candela output depends on the combined source output and how effectively the optical system distributes it, not simply on how many LEDs are present.

Which architecture is thermally easier to manage?

Multiple LEDs can distribute heat physically across the fixture, but the total thermal load tied to the system's total power stays the same — good thermal design is essential either way.

How should an engineer choose between the two?

Based on required intensity, optical geometry, thermal limits, reliability requirements, failure tolerance, and physical fixture size — not on LED count as a standalone criterion.

17 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

FAA AC 150/5345-46

Specification for Runway, Taxiway, Heliport, and Vertiport Light Fixtures — the equipment spec against which the complete fixture's photometric output is qualified, regardless of internal LED source architecture.

FAA AC 150/5340-30

Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids — sets the photometric requirements that any single- or multiple-LED fixture design must meet as a complete unit.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — photometric characteristics of aeronautical ground lights, applicable regardless of internal LED source architecture.

Fixture-Level Photometric Testing

Compliance is assessed on the complete fixture's combined photometric output, not on the specification of individual LED components.

Final Engineering Note

Do not select an architecture based on LED count alone. Confirm thermal design, failure behaviour, and combined photometric performance against the governing standard.

Single LED architecture is not multiple LED architecture — one concentrates output in a single source, the other combines several. For AGL, the fixture's complete photometric, thermal and reliability performance is what matters, not the LED count.
