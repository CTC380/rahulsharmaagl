Calvert System

A British‑developed approach lighting system featuring a distinctive "barrette" pattern of lights that provides both horizontal and vertical guidance to pilots during final approach. Originally designed for the Royal Air Force, the Calvert system uses a series of crossbars and centreline lights to create a unique visual signature that enhances depth perception and glide‑slope awareness, particularly in low visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

The Calvert System is a distinctive approach lighting configuration originally developed in the United Kingdom for the Royal Air Force, and later adopted at many civil airports. It is characterised by a pattern of barrettes (short rows of lights perpendicular to the runway centreline) and centreline lights that extend from the runway threshold into the approach area. The system's unique arrangement provides pilots with both horizontal and vertical guidance cues, helping them maintain the correct glide path and lateral alignment during the final stages of an approach.

There are two main variants: Calvert I (the original configuration) and Calvert II (an enhanced version with additional lights and a longer configuration). The system is particularly known for its barrette pattern, which creates a "ladder" effect that gives pilots a strong sense of depth and position relative to the runway, improving situational awareness and approach stability.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The Calvert System's barrette-and-centreline "ladder" pattern exists to give pilots a strong, immediate sense of depth and closure rate on approach, not just lateral alignment, by presenting a repeating structure that visually compresses as the aircraft closes on the threshold in a way a simple row of points does not communicate as clearly. This depth cue matters most in the final stages of approach, where judging remaining distance and rate of closure accurately is as important to a stable landing as staying laterally aligned, and where a purely two-dimensional (left-right) reference leaves a gap in the pilot's overall situational picture. Its purpose, and the reason it was adopted well beyond its original military origin, is this combined horizontal-and-depth guidance in a single coherent visual pattern, which is why later civil precision lighting standards inherited many of its structural principles.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The Calvert System is installed at airports worldwide, particularly in the UK and Commonwealth countries. It is typically used on:

  • Military airbases (original Royal Air Force installations)
  • Major international airports in the UK, Australia, and other Commonwealth nations
  • Precision approach runways requiring enhanced visual guidance for low‑visibility operations
  • Runways where terrain or obstacles require a distinctive visual cue for approach path monitoring
  • Airports seeking a proven, reliable approach lighting design with a long history of operational success
Placement

Installation & Location

The centreline row is set out on frangible stands along the extended runway centreline at 30 m spacing, running out to the site's configuration length. The distinctive crossbars are built as separate lateral structures at the fixed 150 m, 300 m, 450 m, and 600 m points (with further crossbars added for Calvert II), each carrying 3 to 5 lights and requiring a wider, more heavily braced frangible mount than the single centreline stands. Where the approach crosses roads, water, or uneven ground, both centreline and crossbar structures are elevated on piers to keep the array on a consistent plane. Cabling runs in duct back to the regulator vault, with the centreline and crossbar circuits typically kept separate. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline extended and threshold survey.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Calvert System provides guidance through a distinctive arrangement of lights:

  • Barrettes (Crossbars): Short rows of lights (typically 3‑5 lights) perpendicular to the runway centreline, placed at regular 150m intervals (150m, 300m, 450m, 600m, 750m from the threshold — 5 crossbars) — crossbar length increases with distance from the threshold. These provide lateral guidance and a strong sense of distance and depth.
  • Centreline Lights: A row of lights along the approach centreline, extending from the threshold to the outer end of the system, providing lateral alignment reference.
  • Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL): In some configurations, a line of flashing lights (often in the centreline) creates a "rolling" effect toward the threshold, indicating approach direction and helping pilots maintain the correct descent path.
  • Visual Ladder Effect: The combination of barrettes and centreline lights creates a "ladder" effect that gives pilots a clear sense of height and distance, improving glide‑slope monitoring and reducing the risk of unstable approaches.
  • Intensity Control: The entire system is controlled by CCRs with multiple intensity steps (typically 5‑step), allowing ATC to adjust brightness for visibility conditions.
  • Variants: both Calvert I and Calvert II extend 900 metres from the threshold; Calvert II adds two rows of red side-row barrettes extending 270m from the threshold, plus sequenced flashing lights, rather than adding extra length.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

In its base form the Calvert System is controlled entirely at the loop level like a standard AGL circuit: an ALCMS selects the required brightness step from RVR data or ATC instruction, the CCR regulates the whole series loop to that fixed current, and every barrette and centreline light on the loop, fed through its own isolating transformer, burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no independent control of a single crossbar or a single centreline segment. Where a configuration adds sequenced flashing lights to the centreline for a stronger rolling effect, that SFL element runs on a separate flasher/sequencer controller that fires each lamp in outer-to-threshold order via capacitor discharge, commanded in step with the steady loop's brightness but electrically independent of it — a fault on one subsystem is detected and reported without affecting the other. Fault detection on the steady loop remains loop-based: the CCR monitors loop voltage against commanded current, and a voltage rise flags an open circuit or failed isolating transformer as a loop fault rather than a specific fixture.

Construction

Physical Construction

The centreline row uses frangible stands carrying a single die-cast, gasket-sealed lamp housing each, spaced at 30 m intervals along the approach. The crossbars are built as wider, more heavily braced frangible structures, each carrying three to five lamp housings on a rigid bar strong enough to support the additional mass and wind loading of a multi-light array at the fixed 150 m, 300 m, 450 m, and 600 m points (with further crossbars added for Calvert II). All lamp housings share the same toughened, gasket-sealed lens construction rated IP67/IP68, and where Calvert II's optional sequenced flashing lights are fitted, they use the same xenon strobe and capacitor-bank construction found on other SFL-equipped approach systems. Where the approach crosses roads, water, or uneven ground, both centreline and crossbar structures are elevated on piers engineered to keep the whole array on a consistent plane. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 connectors feeding separate isolating transformers for the centreline and crossbar circuits, with stainless-steel fasteners and corrosion-resistant finishes used throughout.

Installation Requirements

Centreline Stand Footing
Civil

Set each centreline stand footing at the 30 m spacing along the extended runway centreline, holding elevation tolerance out to the site's configuration length.

Crossbar Foundation & Bracing
Civil

Build the wider, more heavily braced foundations needed at the fixed 150 m, 300 m, 450 m, and 600 m crossbar points, sized for the added mass and wind loading of a multi-light bar.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay separate duct runs for the centreline and crossbar circuits back to the regulator vault, keeping the two systems on independent feeders.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal primary connectors on both centreline and crossbar circuits per manufacturer procedure, keeping every splice fully submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm each fixture's isolating transformer wattage matches its circuit's rated current step, with centreline and crossbar transformers kept on their respective loops.

Crossbar Assembly & Frangible Mounting
Mechanical

Fix the 3 to 5 lamp housings to each crossbar and torque the frangible mounts to spec, confirming the heavier bar still shears cleanly under lateral load.

Setting-Out to Extended Centreline
Mechanical

Tie centreline and crossbar positions to the extended runway centreline and threshold survey so the distinctive ladder pattern reads correctly on approach.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record centreline and crossbar stand positions, cable routes, connector seals, and torque values as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the centreline circuit and each barrette-bar circuit separately before backfill, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum on every branch.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity and record total loop resistance along the full centreline run and at each crossbar (150m, 300m, 450m, 600m, 750m) before first energisation.

Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each centreline and barrette-bar transformer matches the rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

Sequenced Flashing Response (Calvert II)
Energisation

Where sequenced flashing lights are fitted, confirm correct outer-to-threshold firing order and flash rate before proceeding to alignment checks.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm the centreline row and all barrette bars respond correctly, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify barrette and centreline beam intensity against ICAO / CAP 168 thresholds using a calibrated photometer across the full configuration length.

Barrette & Centreline Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Clear dust, insect residue, and weathering film from every barrette and continuous centreline lens so beam output stays within spec along the approach.

Barrette Spacing & Configuration Audit
Annual

Walk the array to confirm the 150m/300m/450m/600m barrette points and 30m centreline spacing still match the Calvert I/II design layout, since ground settlement can shift fixtures over such a long run.

Sequenced Flasher Function Check
Semi-annual

Where sequenced flashers are fitted (typically on Calvert II), confirm they fire in the correct outer-to-threshold order and at the specified rate.

Seal & Gasket Inspection
Annual

Check top-cap O-rings and gaskets across elevated and inset fixtures for cracking or compression set, and re-torque fasteners to keep each unit watertight.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test the isolation transformers and series circuit feeding the configuration to confirm resistance stays above minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a circuit-wide fault.

Frangible Coupling Check
Annual

Inspect couplings on elevated fixtures for brittleness, corrosion, or shear fatigue and replace on schedule, since the approach lighting structure sits in the direct overrun path.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Examine primary and secondary cable connections across the array for corrosion or loose crimps, a common source of intermittent series-circuit faults over a run this long.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Barrette Alignment Drift

Barrette lights, especially elevated ones, can be knocked out of alignment by wind, wildlife, or maintenance vehicles. Since the barrette pattern relies on precise perpendicular alignment to the runway, regular alignment checks using a theodolite or laser system are essential to maintain the visual ladder effect.

Lens Contamination

All Calvert system components are exposed to rubber deposits, bird droppings, de‑icing chemicals, and environmental pollutants. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is essential to maintain light output and colour fidelity, especially for the barrettes which are critical for depth perception.

SFL Synchronisation Drift (Calvert II)

If the system includes sequenced flashing lights, timing drift can disrupt the rolling effect, confusing pilots. Regular calibration and testing of the SFL controllers are critical for Calvert II installations.

Power Supply and CCR Issues

Calvert systems draw significant power, especially if they include SFL. Voltage fluctuations, CCR faults, or cable damage can cause inconsistent brightness or total failure. Regular power quality monitoring, cable testing, and CCR maintenance are recommended.

Vegetation and Obstruction Growth

At some airfields, vegetation or structures can grow up around the barrette and centreline lights, obstructing the light output. Regular site inspections and vegetation management are essential to maintain clear lines of sight.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Barrette Light Dark, Row Otherwise Normal
Likely Cause
Failed lamp/LED in one barrette light, or an open isolating transformer at that fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp/LED if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Barrette Row or Centreline Dark
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop feeding that row (severed cable, corroded splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
Barrette Ladder Effect Distorted / Rungs Out of Alignment
Likely Cause
One or more barrette bars have drifted out of perpendicular alignment to the runway, breaking the visual ladder and depth-perception cue the Calvert pattern relies on.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-check barrette alignment with a theodolite or laser system against the runway centreline; inspect mounting stakes for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Re-align the affected barrette(s) and correct any base settlement; re-verify the ladder pattern is consistent along the full approach.
SFL Rolling Effect Disrupted (Calvert II)
Likely Cause
Timing drift or a failed flasher unit in the sequenced flashing light controller has broken the rolling sequence.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify controller timing against the specified sequence rate; check each flasher head in turn for correct firing order.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the SFL controller, or replace the faulty flasher head; confirm the rolling effect is restored end to end.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Reduced Light Output at Barrettes
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from rubber deposits, de-icing residue, or bird droppings is degrading the depth-perception cue the barrettes provide.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the affected lenses.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lenses; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Barrette LED / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised barrette housing seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single light within a barrette or crossbar goes dark or drops below minimum intensity, leaving a gap in the barrette row.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol along the centreline, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken a single barrette light or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down an entire barrette row.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance along the barrette or crossbar circuit.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each barrette during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or wildlife activity, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run beneath the barrette rows.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop feeding a barrette row or crossbar.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Barrette / Crossbar Support Frangible Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading, mower or maintenance-vehicle contact, or ordinary material fatigue over the service interval acting on the low-profile barrette housing or crossbar mount.
Effect
Barrette or crossbar shifts out of level or alignment, distorting the visual pattern the flush-mounted array is meant to present.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking, play, or settlement at the barrette housing or crossbar mount, and periodic level checks against the design layout.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on an otherwise healthy circuit.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Barrette Alignment Drift / Ladder Effect Distortion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ground settlement, frost heave, or turf/pavement movement gradually shifting one or more barrette housings out of their designed row-and-column position.
Effect
The characteristic ladder effect of aligned barrette rows becomes distorted, with rungs appearing out of step and weakening the roll-guidance cue to pilots.
Detection Method
Periodic survey of barrette position and level against the as-built layout, and visual patrol to spot rungs that no longer line up.
SFL Flasher Failure / Rolling Effect Disrupted (Calvert II)
High Severity
Root Cause
Ageing capacitor bank in the condenser discharge unit, a failed trigger transformer, or timing drift in the sequenced flashing light controller used on Calvert II installations.
Effect
Individual flasher fails to fire or fires out of sequence, breaking the rolling visual effect that reinforces the barrette centreline on Calvert II systems.
Detection Method
Verify controller timing against the specified sequence rate, check each flasher head in firing order, and inspect the condenser discharge unit for capacitor swelling or leakage.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Centreline and barrette circuits run on constant-current series loops that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR, then verify zero energy at the barrette or crossbar before opening any enclosure.

Sequenced Flasher Check Where Fitted
Electrical Safety

Some Calvert I/II installations add sequenced flashing lights along the centreline; where fitted, discharge each condenser discharge unit through its bleed resistor and confirm zero voltage before servicing a flasher head, since it stores a high-energy charge after the controller is switched off.

Off-Runway Ladder Pattern Access
Personnel Safety

The barrette "ladder" pattern extends across the approach area beyond the threshold, often over uneven or environmentally sensitive ground; assess terrain and drainage before positioning vehicles or personnel at outlying crossbars.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible coupling or mounting with a rigid fitting, and do not over-torque connections during installation or repair; barrette and centreline supports must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Work on approach lighting affects an active runway's usable minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any barrette or centreline light is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

FOD Control Along the Approach
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every barrette worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work on a barrette or centreline circuit, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a barrette out of aim tolerance or at reduced intensity; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Confirm Which Variant Before Ordering Parts
Configuration

Calvert I and Calvert II differ enough in barrette count and configuration length that spares, mounting hardware, and even foundation spacing aren't always interchangeable. Verify the as-built variant against records rather than assuming from the airport's category alone.

The Ladder Effect Depends on Relative, Not Just Absolute, Alignment
Structural

A single barrette can be individually within tolerance and still visibly break the ladder pattern if it's slightly rotated relative to its neighbours. Assess alignment as a set against adjacent barrettes, not only against an absolute reference line.

Legacy Installations May Predate Current Loop Standards
Electrical

Calvert systems installed decades ago sometimes run on wiring topologies or CCR configurations that predate current series-circuit practice. Document the actual as-installed circuit before assuming it matches a modern reference design when troubleshooting or planning upgrades.

SFL Retrofit Timing Rarely Matches Original Barrette Spacing
Photometric

Where sequenced flashing lights have been added to an original Calvert I installation to create a Calvert II-like configuration, the retrofit timing controller needs recalibration against the actual as-built barrette spacing, not a generic default sequence rate.

Barrette Colour Fidelity Fades Before Total Failure
Troubleshooting

Ageing lenses and coatings can shift perceived barrette colour subtly before any individual light drops below its intensity minimum, degrading the depth cue the ladder pattern relies on. Include colour assessment in the same inspection cycle as intensity, not as a separate concern.

Treat Non-Standard Geometry as Documented Design, Not a Defect
Heritage

Because Calvert layouts predate some current ICAO/FAA barrette spacing conventions, a technician unfamiliar with the system's history can mistake original geometry for installation error. Keep as-built drawings on hand at the site to avoid unnecessary and costly 'corrective' work.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the Calvert approach lighting system: layout, variants, power, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is the Calvert approach lighting system?

The Calvert System is an approach lighting configuration developed in the United Kingdom for the Royal Air Force and later adopted at many civil airports. It uses barrettes (short rows of lights across the runway centreline) plus a row of centreline lights extending from the threshold into the approach area, giving both lateral and vertical guidance.

Q2What is the difference between Calvert I and Calvert II?

Calvert I is the original configuration. Calvert II is an enhanced version that adds two rows of red side-row barrettes extending 270 m from the threshold, plus sequenced flashing lights. Both variants extend 900 m from the threshold, so Calvert II adds lights rather than length.

Q3Why does the Calvert system use a barrette 'ladder' pattern?

The repeating barrette and centreline pattern gives pilots a strong sense of depth and closure rate. The structure visually compresses as the aircraft nears the threshold, which helps judge remaining distance and approach stability, not just left-right alignment.

Q4How is a Calvert system laid out?

Centreline lights run along the extended runway centreline at 30 m spacing. Crossbars (barrettes of typically 3 to 5 lights, generally 5 in Calvert II) are placed at 150 m intervals at 150, 300, 450, 600 and 750 m from the threshold, with crossbar length increasing with distance from the threshold.

Q5What are sequenced flashing lights in the Calvert system?

In some configurations, and often in Calvert II, a line of white flashing lights along the centreline produces a rolling effect toward the threshold, showing approach direction and helping pilots maintain the correct descent path. Where fitted they run from a separate capacitor-discharge trigger circuit synchronised by a flasher controller.

Q6Where is the Calvert system used?

It is installed at airports worldwide, particularly in the UK and Commonwealth countries: military airbases (the original RAF installations), major international airports in the UK, Australia and other Commonwealth nations, precision approach runways needing enhanced visual guidance, and runways where terrain or obstacles call for a distinctive approach path cue.

Q7What are typical Calvert system specifications?

The light source is an LED array or 100 to 200 W halogen, white steady and flashing. It is 5-step CCR controlled (2.8 A to 6.6 A) on 6.6 A series circuits with 100 to 200 W isolating transformers, uni-directional toward the approach, elevated or inset mounted, and rated IP67/IP68.

Q8How is a Calvert system powered?

The centreline row and crossbar rows are fed from independent CCR series circuits, with a dedicated isolating transformer per fixture and a separate discharge circuit for the sequenced flashers where fitted. Cable is L-824 with L-823 connectors, and insulation resistance should be at least 50 MΩ.

Q9Which standards apply to the Calvert system?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (recognised approach lighting configuration and photometric requirements), UK CAA CAP 168 (Calvert specification for UK airfields), IEC 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief #67 (LED Calvert component performance). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q10How often should a Calvert system be maintained?

Typical practice is quarterly lens cleaning, semi-annual sequenced flasher checks where fitted, and annual photometric intensity checks, configuration and spacing audits, seal and gasket inspection, insulation resistance tests, frangible coupling checks and cable and connector inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11What are the common Calvert system problems?

Barrette alignment drift (elevated lights knocked by wind, wildlife or vehicles), lens contamination from rubber deposits, bird droppings and de-icing chemicals, sequenced flasher timing drift in Calvert II, power supply and CCR issues affecting brightness, and vegetation or structures growing up around the lights.
