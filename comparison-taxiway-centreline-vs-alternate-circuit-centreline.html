Taxiway Lighting Comparison

Taxiway Centreline VS Alternate-Circuit Centreline

Guidance vs Redundancy — taxiway centreline lighting provides the normal visual guidance an aircraft follows along the taxi route, while an alternate-circuit centreline arrangement is the same centreline lighting wired across separate electrical circuits to maintain that guidance if the primary circuit fails. Same green light, different electrical architecture.

02 · Definitions

What Is a Taxiway Centreline & Alternate-Circuit Centreline?

Taxiway Centreline

Normal taxiway centreline lighting provides visual guidance along the aircraft's intended taxi path. The pilot uses the centreline as the visual route to follow.

Provides

  • Continuous directional guidance
  • A single line of green lights along the taxi route
  • The pilot's primary "where to steer" reference

Alternate-Circuit Centreline

An alternate-circuit arrangement does not create a different centreline function — the same centreline lights are instead arranged across separate electrical circuits.

Purpose

  • Improve continuity of centreline lighting
  • Preserve availability if one circuit becomes unavailable
  • Add electrical redundancy, not a new guidance function
03 · Biggest Difference

Function vs Electrical Redundancy

Taxiway centreline asks "what does it do?" — the answer is taxi guidance. Alternate-circuit centreline asks "how is it powered?" — the answer is a separate/alternate circuit.

Therefore: Taxiway Centreline = function. Alternate-Circuit Centreline = electrical redundancy. The two describe different layers of the same system — what the light communicates to the pilot, and how reliably it stays powered.

04 · Simplified Circuit Concept

How the Alternate Circuit Works

Imagine the centreline lights split across two independent circuits, A and B, alternating along the row.

Normal — Both Circuits Energised
ABABA

Full centreline coverage — every light along the route is available.

Circuit A Lost
ABABA

Circuit B can preserve some centreline lighting coverage, subject to the actual system design.

05 · Why Use an Alternate Circuit?

Reducing the Effect of a Single Circuit Failure

Normal

Circuit A ✓ and Circuit B ✓ — full centreline lighting is available across the route.

One Circuit Fails

Circuit A ✗ and Circuit B ✓ — some centreline lighting remains available, providing greater system resilience than relying entirely on one circuit.

06 · Pilot Perspective

What the Pilot Actually Sees

From the cockpit, the pilot is primarily concerned with following the green centreline. The pilot normally does not need to know which electrical circuit is operating — the circuit arrangement is an engineering/system-reliability feature, not a piece of information conveyed to the flight crew.

07 · Colour Does Not Change

Same Green, Same Guidance

Both remain part of the taxiway centreline lighting function: green centreline light means guidance, regardless of which circuit is currently supplying it. An alternate circuit does not mean a different colour or a different pilot instruction.

08 · Important Distinction

Not the Same as an Alternate Taxi Route

This distinction is important. Alternate-circuit centreline means the same taxiway, the same centreline, but a different electrical circuit. It does not mean an alternate taxiway or an alternate aircraft route. "Alternate-circuit" refers to the lighting electrical system, not a different path for the aircraft to follow.

10 · Myth vs Fact

Common Centreline Circuiting Confusion

Myth: Alternate-circuit centreline is a different type of light
Fact

It is the same green taxiway centreline light and function. The difference is purely electrical — how the fixtures are wired and supplied, not what colour or guidance message they provide.

Myth: Alternate-circuit means an alternate taxi route
Fact

Alternate-circuit refers strictly to the lighting electrical system. It does not signal a different taxiway or a different path for the aircraft to follow — the taxiway and centreline stay the same.

Myth: The pilot needs to know which circuit is active
Fact

The pilot's task is simply to follow the green centreline. Circuit arrangement and redundancy are engineering/system-reliability considerations handled behind the scenes.

11 · Engineering Verdict

Taxiway Centreline vs Alternate-Circuit Centreline

Engineering Verdict

Taxiway centreline and alternate-circuit centreline describe two different layers of the same system, not two competing lighting types. One defines what the light tells the pilot; the other defines how reliably that light stays powered.

Together, normal centreline lighting gives the aircraft its route to follow, while an alternate-circuit arrangement improves the continuity of that guidance if a single circuit fails — the guidance message never changes.

CentrelineGreen + normal circuit + taxi route guidance.
Alternate-CircuitGreen + separate/alternate circuit + continuity after circuit failure.

Easy memory: Centreline → where to taxi. Alternate circuit → how the lighting stays available.

12 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest difference between taxiway centreline and alternate-circuit centreline?

Taxiway centreline describes the lighting function — taxi guidance. Alternate-circuit centreline describes the electrical arrangement — the same lights supplied across separate circuits for redundancy.

Does an alternate circuit change the light colour or pilot instruction?

No. The lights remain green taxiway centreline lights with the same guidance message, regardless of which circuit is currently supplying them.

Does alternate-circuit centreline mean an alternate taxi route?

No. It refers only to the lighting electrical system — the same taxiway and the same centreline, just wired across separate circuits. It does not indicate a different aircraft route.

What happens to centreline lighting if one circuit fails?

Depending on the system design, the alternate circuit can maintain some centreline lighting coverage, providing greater resilience than relying entirely on a single circuit.

Does the pilot need to know which circuit is operating?

No. The pilot's concern is following the green centreline. Circuit arrangement and redundancy are engineering/system-reliability features handled independently of flight-crew guidance.

Why would an alternate-circuit arrangement be used at all?

To reduce the effect of a single electrical circuit failure and improve the continuity and availability of centreline lighting, particularly on busy or complex taxi routes.
