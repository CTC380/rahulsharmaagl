Flasher Power Supply

A dedicated power supply unit that provides the required electrical energy to Sequenced Flashing Light (SFL) units and their associated control circuits. The Flasher Power Supply ensures reliable, high‑intensity flashes by delivering regulated voltage and current to the SFL system, enabling the precise "rolling" effect that guides pilots during final approach in low‑visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Flasher Power Supply (also referred to as an SFL Power Supply or Flash Power Supply) is an electrical unit that provides the necessary power to Sequenced Flashing Light (SFL) units within an approach lighting system. Unlike standard constant‑current regulators (CCRs) that supply steady power to continuous‑burning lights, the Flasher Power Supply is specifically designed to deliver short, high‑energy pulses of power that produce the brilliant flashes required for SFL systems. These units often incorporate high‑voltage capacitors that discharge rapidly to generate the intense light output needed for visibility in fog, rain, and snow. The Flasher Power Supply is a critical component of precision (ALSF‑1/2) and medium‑intensity (MALSF/MALSR) approach lighting systems, ensuring the SFL units flash with consistent intensity and timing.

Modern Flasher Power Supplies use advanced switched‑mode technology, offering high efficiency, compact size, and precise output control. They are designed to interface with the Flasher Control Unit, receiving trigger signals to synchronise the power pulses with the desired flash sequence. Many units include built‑in diagnostics, remote monitoring, and redundant power stages for critical applications.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The flasher power supply's purpose is to deliver the brief, intense burst of energy an SFL unit needs to produce a flash bright enough to cut through fog, rain, or snow — a fundamentally different job from the steady, continuous current a CCR supplies to conventional runway and approach lights. Because that flash has to be both intense and precisely timed to align with the rest of the sequence, the unit's capacitor-based energy storage exists specifically to build up power between flashes and then release it almost instantaneously, achieving a peak brightness that a continuously-drawn current could never produce from the same average power budget. Its role is therefore purpose-built around a single job: convert steady input power into a burst that is bright enough, at exactly the right moment, to remain visible in the low-visibility conditions where SFL guidance matters most.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Flasher Power Supplies are deployed as part of approach lighting systems that require sequenced flashing guidance. They are used in:

  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems – providing power for SFL units in CAT I, II, and III precision approaches.
  • MALSF and MALSR systems – powering SFL for medium‑intensity approach lighting.
  • Calvert II systems – supplying power for SFL in British approach lighting configurations.
  • Runways with low‑visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) – where reliable high‑intensity flashing is essential for pilot guidance.
  • Major international airports – with CAT II/III capability requiring robust SFL power systems.
  • Military airbases – requiring all‑weather approach capability with SFL guidance.
  • Airfields with remote monitoring – where the Flasher Power Supply integrates with ALCMS for centralised control and diagnostics.
Placement

Installation & Location

The flasher power supply is installed in the same equipment shelter or CCR room as the flasher control unit, mounted on a rack or panel with adequate ventilation for the heat generated during its charge cycle. It is fed from the shelter's distribution board through its own protective device, and its high-energy discharge output is wired out to the sequenced flashing lights on the approach lighting structures using cabling rated for the peak discharge current. Because it stores a significant charge between flashes, its mounting position keeps the unit's discharge terminals and capacitor bank away from routine foot traffic in the equipment room, and access is arranged so a technician can safely isolate and discharge it before any maintenance.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Flasher Power Supply operates through a combination of energy storage, pulse generation, and precise control:

  • AC to DC Conversion: The unit converts incoming AC mains power (typically 120V or 230V) to a regulated DC voltage using a high‑efficiency switched‑mode power supply (SMPS) or linear regulator.
  • Energy Storage: The DC power charges a bank of high‑voltage capacitors (or a pulse‑forming network) that store energy for the flash. These capacitors are designed to discharge rapidly, delivering a high‑current pulse to the SFL units.
  • Pulse Generation: When the Flasher Control Unit sends a trigger signal, the power supply discharges the stored energy through a switching circuit (IGBT or thyristor) into the SFL unit, generating a high‑intensity flash. The pulse duration and peak current are precisely controlled to achieve the required luminous intensity.
  • Intensity Control: The Flasher Power Supply interfaces with the CCR or intensity control system to adjust the flash intensity (brightness) based on ATC commands or automatic RVR‑based dimming. This is achieved by varying the capacitor charging voltage or pulse width.
  • Multiple Outputs: Some Flasher Power Supplies have multiple independent output channels, each feeding a separate SFL unit or group of units. This allows for individual control and redundancy.
  • Monitoring and Diagnostics: The unit continuously monitors its own operation, including capacitor voltage, output current, and temperature. It can detect faults such as capacitor failure, overcurrent, or overheating, and report them to the ALCMS or Flasher Control Unit.
  • Redundancy: Critical installations often use dual Flasher Power Supplies with automatic failover, ensuring continuous SFL operation in the event of a primary unit failure.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Flasher Power Supply is the energy stage of the SFL control chain — it holds no sequencing logic of its own and acts only on commands issued above it. The ALCMS or ATC sets the intensity step, which the Flasher Control Unit converts into a timed trigger for each SFL position in the sequence. On receipt of its trigger, the power supply discharges its capacitor bank (or pulse-forming network) through the switching circuit into the SFL, at a charging voltage or pulse width set by the current intensity step — this is where the commanded brightness step actually becomes flash energy. Where a site uses multiple independent output channels or dual power supplies for redundancy, each channel discharges only on its own trigger, so a fault or overload on one channel does not interrupt the others. Capacitor voltage, output current, and temperature are monitored continuously and reported to the Flasher Control Unit or ALCMS; on a fault such as capacitor failure or overcurrent, a standby power supply takes over automatically in redundant installations.

ALCMS → Flasher Control Unit (timing) → Flasher Power Supply (energy) → SFL (with fault feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The Flasher Power Supply is housed in a floor-standing or wall-mounted steel cabinet, similar in outward form to a CCR cabinet, sized to accommodate its high-voltage capacitor bank alongside the switched-mode charging and discharge circuitry. A bank of high-voltage capacitors, mounted in a dedicated compartment with adequate clearance and venting, stores the energy for each flash pulse, while a separate power section houses the charging circuit (rectifier and switched-mode converter) that replenishes the capacitors between flashes. A discharge/trigger circuit, typically a thyristor or IGBT switch, connects the capacitor bank to the output terminals and is fired by the trigger signal from the Flasher Control Unit. The control and monitoring electronics occupy a separate, isolated section of the cabinet to protect them from the high-voltage switching transients in the power section, and heavy-duty output terminals, an input isolator/breaker, and surge protection are provided consistent with other AGL power equipment.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Capacitor Ageing and Failure

The energy‑storage capacitors in the Flasher Power Supply have a limited operational life, typically 5–10 years, depending on temperature and usage. Capacitor ageing reduces the energy stored per flash, leading to diminished flash intensity. Regular capacitance and ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance) testing is recommended, with proactive replacement of aged capacitors.

Power Supply Instability and Voltage Dips

Flasher Power Supplies draw high peak currents during the charging cycle, which can cause voltage dips in the mains supply if the input wiring is undersized. This can affect the performance of other AGL equipment. Proper sizing of input cables and use of dedicated supply feeders are essential.

Communication Failures with Control Unit

The Flasher Power Supply relies on communication with the Flasher Control Unit for trigger and intensity commands. Cable damage, connector corrosion, or signal interference can cause missed triggers or incorrect intensity. Regular inspection and testing of communication links are essential.

Overheating and Thermal Shutdown

Flasher Power Supplies generate significant heat during operation. Inadequate cooling or blocked ventilation can cause the unit to overheat, leading to reduced output or thermal shutdown. Regular cleaning of cooling fans and filters is recommended, with temperature monitoring for critical installations.

Output Short Circuits and Overloads

A short circuit in the SFL cabling or unit can cause the Flasher Power Supply to go into protection mode, resulting in no flash. Regular insulation testing of SFL cables and connectors is essential to prevent such faults.

Transient Voltage Surges

Lightning strikes or switching transients can damage the Flasher Power Supply. Surge protection devices (SPDs) should be installed at the input and output of the unit, and the earthing system must be verified regularly.

Likely Cause
Ageing energy-storage capacitors have reduced the energy stored per flash cycle.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform capacitance and ESR testing on the storage capacitors and compare against the manufacturer's acceptable range.
Corrective Action
Proactively replace aged capacitors per the 5-10 year expected service life, and re-verify flash intensity.
Voltage Dips Affecting Other AGL Equipment
Likely Cause
High peak charging currents drawn by the unit are causing mains voltage dips due to undersized input wiring.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure voltage dip at the input during a charging cycle and compare against acceptable tolerance for connected equipment.
Corrective Action
Upsize input cabling or move the unit to a dedicated supply feeder sized for its peak current draw.
Missed Triggers or Incorrect Flash Intensity
Likely Cause
A communication fault with the Flasher Control Unit from cable damage, connector corrosion, or signal interference.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect communication cabling and connectors; test the trigger/intensity signal path from the control unit.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable/connector and re-verify correct triggering and intensity.
Reduced Output or Thermal Shutdown
Likely Cause
Inadequate cooling or blocked ventilation is causing the unit to overheat under normal operation.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect cooling fans and filters for blockage; monitor unit temperature if instrumentation is available.
Corrective Action
Clean fans and filters, confirm airflow is restored, and add temperature monitoring for critical installations.
No Flash / Unit in Protection Mode
Likely Cause
A short circuit in the SFL cabling or the unit itself has tripped the unit's protection circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform insulation testing on SFL cables and connectors to locate the short.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted cable/connector, then reset the unit and confirm normal flash operation resumes.
Unit Damaged Following a Storm
Likely Cause
A transient voltage surge from a lightning strike or switching event has damaged internal components.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect installed SPDs for failure indication; check the earthing system resistance; test the unit for damage.
Corrective Action
Replace failed SPDs and any damaged components, verify earthing meets spec, and re-test before re-energising.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Mains and Load Before Opening
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out both the incoming mains supply and the SFL output circuit before opening the unit's enclosure, and verify zero energy before handling internal connections.

Discharge High-Voltage Capacitors Before Touching Internals
Electrical Safety

Treat the internal high-voltage capacitors as charged even after the unit is switched off and isolated; use the manufacturer's discharge procedure (or a properly rated discharge tool) and verify zero voltage across the capacitor terminals before touching any internal component.

Allow the Manufacturer's Wait Time After Power-Off
Personnel Safety

Wait the minimum time specified in the manufacturer's manual after removing power before opening the enclosure, since capacitor bleed-down circuits take a defined interval to bring residual voltage to a safe level.

PPE for High-Energy Discharge Circuits
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE rated for the unit's capacitor voltage when working inside the enclosure, and keep one hand clear of grounded surfaces while probing to reduce the risk of a current path across the body.

Verify Output Before Reconnecting SFL Units
Electrical Safety

After any repair, verify the pulse output voltage, energy, and timing are within specification on a test load before reconnecting the live SFL units, since an out-of-tolerance pulse can damage the flash lamps or under-drive the flash intensity.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools and fasteners accounted for at all times when the unit is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, particularly given the high-voltage internals.

Confirm Trigger Signal Isolation
Electrical Safety

Verify the low-voltage trigger interface from the Flasher Control Unit is properly isolated from the high-voltage discharge circuit before working on control wiring, since the two sides operate at very different energy levels.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return the unit to service with a capacitor bank showing degraded capacitance, an out-of-tolerance pulse, or a fault indicator active; confirm correct flash intensity and timing at the SFL units before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Discharge Capacitors Before Any Hands-On Work, Every Time
Electrical Safety

The high-voltage capacitors that generate the flash pulse can retain a dangerous stored charge well after the unit is powered down and appears inert; treat capacitor discharge and verification as a mandatory step before opening the enclosure, not a precaution reserved for units that "look" like they might still be charged.

Capacitor Capacitance Fades Gradually, Then Fails Abruptly
Reliability

Electrolytic discharge capacitors lose capacitance slowly over years of charge/discharge cycling, producing a gradual dimming of the flash intensity that's easy to miss without measurement, followed by a comparatively sudden failure once capacitance drops below the threshold needed for a compliant flash. Trend measured flash intensity, not just pass/fail, to catch the gradual phase.

Heat Shortens Capacitor Life Disproportionately
Environmental

Discharge capacitor lifespan is strongly temperature-dependent, and a unit installed in a poorly ventilated enclosure or a hot climate can see meaningfully shorter service life than the same unit in a cooler, well-ventilated cabinet. Enclosure ventilation is a reliability decision for this component, not just a comfort consideration.

Verify Pulse Energy, Not Just Peak Voltage
Testing

A unit can show correct peak discharge voltage on a quick check while still delivering reduced total pulse energy to the flash tube, because energy also depends on discharge duration and internal resistance. Where test equipment allows, verify actual delivered energy per pulse rather than voltage alone.

Recharge Time Between Flashes Is a Design Constraint, Not Just a Spec Line
Design

The time needed to recharge the capacitor bank between pulses sets a hard ceiling on achievable flash rate; a modification that increases flash frequency without re-verifying recharge margin against the specific supply installed can leave the unit unable to reach full charge before the next trigger, silently reducing flash intensity.

A Dim Flash Across Multiple Units Points to the Supply, Not the Tubes
Troubleshooting

If several SFL units sharing a supply all show reduced flash intensity together, suspect the shared power supply's output before replacing multiple flash tubes individually; a coincidental simultaneous failure of several independent tubes is far less likely than a common supply issue.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about flasher power supplies for sequenced flashing lights: how they work, energy per flash, capacitor life, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a flasher power supply?

A flasher power supply, or SFL power supply, provides the short, high-energy pulses of power that produce the flashes in sequenced flashing light units. Unlike a CCR, which supplies steady current to continuous-burning lights, it uses capacitors that discharge rapidly to give intense light output for visibility in fog, rain and snow.

Q2How does a flasher power supply work?

It converts mains AC (120 or 230 V) to regulated DC through an SMPS or linear regulator, charges a high-voltage capacitor bank or pulse-forming network, and when the flasher control unit sends a trigger it discharges a high-current pulse to the SFL units.

Q3What are the typical output specifications?

Up to 400 V DC for high-intensity halogen or 48 V DC for LED SFL, with 10–50 A pulsed peak current, pulse duration of 50–200 ms and energy up to 200 J per flash (halogen) or 50 J (LED). Maximum flash rate is up to 180 per minute, system-controlled, with efficiency above 85%.

Q4Which approach lighting systems use flasher power supplies?

ALSF-1 and ALSF-2 (CAT I, II, III), MALSF and MALSR, Calvert II systems, runways with low-visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) and major CAT II/III airports.

Q5Where is a flasher power supply installed?

In the same equipment shelter or CCR room as the flasher control unit, on a rack or panel with adequate ventilation for heat from the charge cycle. It is fed from the shelter's distribution board through its own protective device, and its pulsed output uses cabling rated for the peak discharge.

Q6What safety features does it have?

A mains-isolated power stage, trigger outputs isolated from the AC input, chassis and capacitor case bonded to earth, discharge/bleed resistors on the capacitors, and overcurrent, overvoltage and capacitor-fault protection.

Q7Which standards apply to flasher power supplies?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-51 (discharge-type flashing light equipment), FAA Order 6850.2 and IEC 61000-6-2. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q8How often should a flasher power supply be maintained?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for thermal performance and communication link tests, and annual for capacitor condition checks, output voltage stability and surge protection checks. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9Why has flash intensity diminished?

Ageing energy-storage capacitors store less energy per flash. Capacitors typically last 5–10 years, so test capacitance and ESR and replace aged capacitors proactively, then re-verify intensity.

Q10Why does the unit cause voltage dips on other AGL equipment?

High peak charging currents on undersized input wiring dip the mains voltage. Measure the dip during a charging cycle, then upsize the input cabling or move the unit to a dedicated feeder sized for its peak current.
