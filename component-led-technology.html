LED Technology in AGL Systems

The transformative shift from halogen to solid‑state lighting — delivering longer life, lower power consumption, instant restrike, and precise photometric control, while challenging traditional series‑circuit compatibility and retrofit strategies.

Overview

What Is It?

LED (Light Emitting Diode) technology has revolutionised airfield ground lighting. Unlike halogen or incandescent lamps that produce light by heating a filament, LEDs are solid‑state devices that emit light when current passes through a semiconductor junction. The result is higher efficacy (lumens per watt), dramatically longer life (50,000+ hours vs. 1,000–2,000 for halogen), and the ability to produce precise colours without filters.

In AGL systems, LEDs are now the default choice for new installations and are increasingly being retrofitted into existing fixtures. However, their electrical characteristics (low forward voltage, need for constant current drive, and sensitivity to reverse polarity) require careful integration with the traditional series‑circuit architecture and CCRs. The transition involves not just swapping lamps, but often replacing the entire light engine and sometimes the isolating transformer or its secondary circuit.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

A pilot judging a runway's edges or a stop bar at night is trusting that every fixture in the circuit is still producing the intensity it was commissioned at — a halogen lamp's filament degrades gradually and largely invisibly between scheduled photometric checks, so a fixture can be running under-intensity for months before anyone notices. LED technology's purpose in AGL is not simply efficiency: its stable, near-constant light output over a very long service life closes that gap, keeping the fixture closer to its rated intensity for far longer, while also reducing how often technicians need to be out on live movement areas to replace lamps — itself a source of airside safety exposure. Realising that benefit is also why LEDs cannot simply be dropped into existing fixtures: because the series circuit and CCR are built to regulate constant current rather than constant voltage, the LED driver and light engine have to be engineered to work within that architecture, which is why an LED retrofit is frequently a light-engine and transformer change rather than a lamp swap.

Application

Where Is It Used?

LEDs have been adopted across the full range of AGL fixtures, with varying levels of maturity:

  • Runway Edge and Centreline: LED versions of elevated and inset fixtures are now standard. They offer better intensity control and longer intervals between maintenance.
  • Taxiway Edge and Centreline: Blue and green LED edge/centreline lights are widely used, with improved colour stability over lamp life.
  • Approach Lighting: LED approach light bars and crossbars are common, with some systems using pulsed LED for sequenced flashing.
  • PAPI / VASI: LED PAPI units are replacing halogen, providing sharper colour transition and lower energy consumption.
  • Stop Bars and Guard Lights: Red and yellow LED stop bars and guard lights are standard, with faster switching for dynamic control.
  • Airfield Signage: LED‑lit signs are more uniform and easier to maintain than fluorescent or lamp‑lit versions.
  • Obstruction Lighting: LED obstruction lights are the de facto choice for all intensity classes, often with solar options.

Retrofit kits are available for many legacy fixtures, allowing airports to upgrade without replacing the entire housing, though compatibility with the existing series circuit and transformer must be verified.

Placement

Installation & Location

LED light engines are fitted inside the existing fixture housing in the same position the halogen lamp and reflector previously occupied, but because LEDs run on regulated constant current rather than the higher voltage a halogen lamp tolerates, an isolating transformer swap or secondary-side adjustment is usually part of the same installation rather than a separate step. Retrofit installation is normally done fixture by fixture on a live circuit, with each unit taken out of service, the halogen lamp assembly and reflector removed, the new LED module and driver fitted and wired to the transformer secondary, and the fixture returned to service and intensity-checked before moving to the next; this staged approach keeps the runway or taxiway circuit largely operational throughout the changeover rather than requiring the whole circuit to go dark at once. Compatibility between the new LED load and the existing transformer's regulation characteristics is verified at each fixture during installation, since an LED module's low forward voltage and constant-current requirement do not always suit a transformer that was originally sized and wound for a halogen load.

Function

How Does It Work?

In an LED AGL fixture, the LED module is driven by a constant‑current driver circuit that accepts the AC secondary voltage from the isolating transformer and converts it to a regulated DC current suitable for the LEDs. The driver may also incorporate dimming or pulse‑width modulation (PWM) to achieve the required intensity steps, which are typically controlled by the primary current level (the same 2.8A / 3.4A / 4.1A / 5.2A / 6.6A CCR steps used for halogen fixtures).

Key functional aspects include:

  • Colour Control: LEDs produce light in specific wavelengths; red, green, blue, and white LEDs are available without filters, leading to more accurate and stable colours over life.
  • Dimming and Intensity Steps: LED drivers can be designed to respond to the primary current level by reducing LED current or using PWM to emulate lower intensities. This ensures compatibility with existing CCR step settings.
  • Instant Restrike: LEDs reach full brightness immediately, unlike halogen lamps that require a warm‑up time. This is advantageous for stop bars and other rapidly switched applications.
  • Thermal Management: LEDs generate heat at the junction, which must be conducted away via heat sinks or active cooling. Proper thermal design is critical to maintain life and colour stability.
  • Optical Design: LEDs are small point sources, allowing advanced optical systems (lenses, reflectors) to shape the beam precisely to meet photometric requirements without the bulk of halogen reflectors.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Inside an LED AGL fixture, the driver circuit is itself a small closed-loop controller: it accepts the AC secondary voltage from the isolating transformer, rectifies and regulates it, and continuously senses the current actually flowing through the LED array, adjusting its output to hold that current at the level the LEDs require regardless of supply variation or thermal drift. The primary-side intensity step set by the CCR is read by the driver as an input signal, which the driver translates into the corresponding LED current or PWM duty cycle, so the fixture's visible brightness tracks the same step commands used for legacy halogen fixtures without any separate control channel. A thermal management loop runs alongside the current loop: temperature sensed at the LED junction or heat sink feeds back into the driver, which derates current if the junction runs hot, protecting lumen output and colour stability at the cost of a small, self-limiting intensity reduction rather than an outright failure.

Construction

Physical Construction

An AGL LED light engine is built around one or more semiconductor LED die mounted on a metal-core printed circuit board (MCPCB), chosen for its ability to conduct heat away from the junction efficiently, since LED lifetime and light output both degrade rapidly if junction temperature is allowed to rise excessively. The MCPCB is thermally bonded, typically with a thermal interface pad or paste, to a heat sink integrated into the fixture housing, which may be finned aluminium or simply the die-cast housing itself acting as a heat spreader. A driver circuit, converting the transformer secondary's AC or DC output into the regulated current the LED array requires, is housed in a separate potted or conformal-coated module within the fixture, protected from moisture and vibration to the same ingress protection standard as the rest of the luminaire. Optical elements, whether a moulded lens, reflector, or diffuser, are positioned directly over the LED array to shape the beam pattern, and the entire light engine assembly is gasket-sealed within the fixture housing so the LED array and its electronics remain protected while still allowing heat generated at the die to conduct outward to the ambient air.

Confirm the LED driver's input voltage and current requirements match the isolating transformer's secondary output before connection, since a retrofit often needs the transformer or secondary circuit replaced alongside the lamp.

Polarity Observance

Wire the LED array and driver to the correct polarity, since LEDs are sensitive to reverse polarity in a way conventional lamps are not.

Current Step Compatibility
Electrical

Confirm the driver's intensity control (current-sensing or PWM) responds correctly across the CCR's 6.6A step range before the fixture is returned to service.

Mechanical

Gasket Re-Sealing
Mechanical

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record the driver/transformer pairing and thermal bond confirmation as-built before the fixture is closed up and returned to service.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Driver Failure

The LED driver is the most failure‑prone component in an LED fixture. Voltage spikes, thermal stress, or component ageing can cause driver failure, often without visible damage to the LEDs themselves.

Colour Shift Over Time

White LEDs can exhibit colour temperature shift (usually towards yellow/blue) as they age. This is less of an issue for monochromatic (red, green) LEDs, but must be monitored for uniform appearance.

Thermal Management Failure

If heat sinks are clogged with dust or the fixture is poorly installed, the LED junction temperature can rise, accelerating lumen degradation and shortening life.

Compatibility with Existing Transformers

Some LED retrofits require a specific secondary voltage or current. Mismatched transformers can cause over‑ or under‑driving, reducing performance or damaging the driver.

Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)

Switching drivers can generate EMI that may interfere with other airfield systems (e.g., communications). Compliance with EMC standards is essential.

Intensity Step Mismatch

If the driver does not correctly interpret the primary current levels, the fixture may not dim uniformly or may not achieve the required step values, leading to non‑compliance with photometric testing.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fixture Dark, No Output at All
Likely Cause
Failed LED driver (most common single point of failure), an open connection between driver and LED array, or loss of secondary voltage from the isolating transformer.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm secondary voltage is present at the fixture with the CCR energised; check driver input and output terminals for continuity; inspect driver-to-array wiring for a loose or corroded connection.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED driver if input voltage is present but output is absent; re-terminate any faulted wiring; escalate to the transformer/circuit if secondary voltage itself is missing.
Visible Colour Shift Between Fixtures
Likely Cause
Uneven chromaticity ageing across white LED arrays, or a mix of LED batches/manufacturers installed on the same circuit or runway section.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare chromaticity coordinates of the affected fixture against ICAO/FAA colour limits using a calibrated colorimeter; check installation records for LED batch or supplier consistency along the row.
Corrective Action
Replace the outlying LED module with one from the same qualified batch, and standardise future replacements to a single approved LED source per circuit.
Progressive Lumen Loss / Early Dimming
Likely Cause
Thermal management failure — a clogged or damaged heat sink, poor thermal contact between LED PCB and housing, or a fixture installed without adequate ventilation clearance.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure LED junction/case temperature against manufacturer limits during steady-state operation; inspect heat sink fins for dust, debris, or corrosion; check thermal paste/pad condition if the fixture is opened.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the heat sink, restore the thermal interface, and confirm the fixture's mounting allows the design airflow path before returning it to service.
Fixture Over- or Under-Driven After Retrofit
Likely Cause
Mismatch between the LED driver's rated input and the existing isolating transformer's secondary voltage/current, common after retrofitting LED fixtures onto legacy series circuits.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure actual secondary current delivered to the fixture across all CCR intensity steps and compare against the LED driver's rated input range in its datasheet.
Corrective Action
Fit a driver rated for the circuit's actual regulated current, or replace the isolating transformer with one matched to the LED fixture's specified secondary rating.
EMI Interference Reported by Nearby Systems
Likely Cause
Switching noise from the LED driver's PWM/current-regulation stage radiating or conducting onto nearby communication or navigation equipment, often from a missing or degraded EMI filter.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the driver and any associated EMI filter meet the applicable EMC standard; check filter component condition and cable shielding/bonding at the fixture.
Corrective Action
Replace a degraded EMI filter, restore proper cable shielding/grounding, or substitute a driver model with verified EMC compliance for the affected zone.
Uneven Dimming Across Intensity Steps
Likely Cause
LED driver misinterpreting the CCR's primary current steps, producing non-linear or non-compliant output at one or more brightness settings.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric check at each CCR intensity step and compare measured output against the required step ratios; verify the driver's step-detection settings match the CCR's actual current values.
Corrective Action
Reconfigure or replace the driver so its step thresholds align with the CCR's actual output currents, then re-verify with a follow-up photometric test.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Complete LED Fixture Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised board seal, or a driver failure cutting output entirely.
Effect
Fixture dark with no output at all, removing that unit from the lighting pattern until replaced or repaired.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and driver output verification at the fixture.
Colour Consistency Drift Between Fixtures
Medium Severity
Root Cause
LED bin variation between manufacturing batches, or differential ageing between fixtures installed or replaced at different times.
Effect
Visible colour shift between fixtures on the same circuit, creating an inconsistent visual presentation along the lit surface.
Detection Method
Scheduled chromaticity testing across fixtures on the same circuit, and sourcing replacements from a matched bin where possible.
Progressive Lumen Depreciation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ordinary LED ageing over its rated service life, accelerated by sustained operation at elevated junction temperature.
Effect
Progressive lumen loss and early dimming relative to the fixture's rated life expectancy.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing against the ICAO/FAA threshold, tracked against the fixture's rated lumen maintenance curve.
Driver Mismatch After Retrofit
High Severity
Root Cause
An LED retrofit driver not correctly matched to the CCR's output characteristics or the fixture's original electrical design.
Effect
Fixture over- or under-driven after retrofit, risking premature LED failure or output below the required photometric threshold.
Detection Method
Output current and voltage verification at the retrofitted fixture against the driver's rated specification.
EMI Emission from LED Drivers
Low Severity
Root Cause
A driver's switching frequency or a degraded EMI filter allowing electrical noise to radiate or conduct onto nearby wiring.
Effect
EMI interference reported by nearby systems, such as communication or navigation equipment sharing the same infrastructure.
Detection Method
EMI measurement at the affected system, and driver inspection/replacement if the emission is traced back to a specific fixture.
PWM Dimming Instability
Low Severity
Root Cause
A degraded driver component, or PWM dimming frequency interacting poorly with a specific intensity step setting.
Effect
Uneven dimming across intensity steps, most noticeable as visible flicker or stepping inconsistency between brightness levels.
Detection Method
Visual and instrumented flicker check across all intensity steps during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Driver Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR or supply circuit before opening a fixture housing to inspect or replace an LED module or driver, and verify zero energy at the fixture before handling connections.

Verify Driver-to-CCR Compatibility Before Retrofit
Electrical Safety

Confirm a retrofit LED driver is correctly matched to the CCR's output characteristics before energising the fixture; a mismatch can over- or under-drive the LEDs, risking premature failure or output that falls below the required photometric threshold without an obvious fault indication.

Match LED Bin on Replacement
Operational

Source replacement fixtures or modules from a matched LED bin where possible, and check chromaticity against neighbouring fixtures on the same circuit after installation, since bin variation between batches can create a visible colour shift along a lit surface.

EMI Awareness Near Sensitive Equipment
Operational

If EMI interference is reported by nearby communication or navigation equipment, investigate the driver's switching frequency and EMI filter condition before assuming an unrelated cause, and replace a driver confirmed as the source rather than working around the symptom.

Thermal Management
Operational

Ensure heat sinking and enclosure seals are correctly reinstated after any driver or module work, since sustained operation at elevated junction temperature accelerates lumen depreciation and shortens LED service life.

Verify Output Across All Intensity Steps
Compliance

After any driver replacement or firmware change affecting dimming, check for visible flicker or stepping inconsistency across all intensity steps before closing out the work, since PWM dimming instability is often step-specific rather than uniform.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on driver or power connections, and confirm the fixture is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting supply.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture in service with unverified driver output, colour, or dimming behaviour after any retrofit or repair; confirm photometric and chromaticity conformance before closing out the work.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
L70 Lifetime Is a Statistical Point, Not a Guarantee
Reliability

L70 ratings describe the population median time to 70% lumen output under specified test conditions; individual modules in a hot, dusty apron environment can fall below threshold well before the rated hours. Treat L70 as a planning input for budget cycles, not a promise for any single fixture.

Constant-Current vs Constant-Voltage Topology Sets Retrofit Compatibility
Electrical

Series airfield circuits expect a constant-current load response; a driver designed around constant-voltage assumptions can behave unpredictably as CCR output current steps change. Confirm the driver topology matches the series circuit before specifying a retrofit, not just its input voltage range.

Thermal Derating Margins Shrink Fast on Hot Aprons
Thermal

Manufacturer lumen and lifetime figures are usually quoted at a moderate ambient; a fixture on a sun-facing apron in a hot climate can run well above that reference temperature for much of the day. Check the driver's derating curve against actual site ambient, not the datasheet's test condition.

Match Surge Protection to the Loop's Real Exposure
Electrical

A driver's built-in surge suppression is sized for typical conditions, not every site. Loops with long overhead runs or high lightning-strike density benefit from supplemental surge protection sized to the loop's actual exposure, evaluated separately from the fixture's stock rating.

Binning Tolerance Belongs in the Purchase Spec
Design

Chromaticity and flux binning vary batch to batch even within one manufacturer's product line. Specify an acceptable binning tolerance at the purchasing stage, rather than discovering colour or brightness mismatch after installation next to existing fixtures.

Dimming Step Response Time Affects Perceived Uniformity
Performance

Some LED drivers step to a new brightness level noticeably faster or slower than others when the CCR changes current. On a shared loop with mixed fixture ages, uneven step response time can make brightness transitions look staggered even when steady-state output matches.

Common symptoms covered are: Fixture Dark, No Output at All; Visible Colour Shift Between Fixtures; Progressive Lumen Loss / Early Dimming; Fixture Over- or Under-Driven After Retrofit; EMI Interference Reported by Nearby Systems; Uneven Dimming Across Intensity Steps. For example, for "Fixture Dark, No Output at All": Likely Cause: Failed LED driver (most common single point of failure), an open connection between driver and LED array, or loss of secondary voltage from the isolating transformer. Diagnostic Steps: Confirm secondary voltage is present at the fixture with the CCR energised; check driver input and output terminals for continuity; inspect driver-to-array wiring for a loose or corroded connection. Corrective Action: Replace the LED driver if input voltage is present but output is absent; re-terminate any faulted wiring; escalate to the transformer/circuit if secondary voltage itself is missing.
