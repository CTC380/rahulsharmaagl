Crossbar Lights

A series of lights arranged perpendicular to the runway centreline, forming distinct "bars" across the approach path. Crossbar lights provide pilots with critical distance and lateral guidance during final approach, serving as visual waypoints that enhance depth perception and glide‑slope monitoring in low visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

Crossbar Lights are a series of white lights arranged in a row perpendicular to the runway centreline, positioned at specific fixed distances from the runway threshold along the approach lighting system. A crossbar is distinct from a centreline barrette: barrettes are the narrow, closely‑spaced light rows repeated every 30 m (100 ft) along the full centreline, while a crossbar is a wider bar occurring at only one or two defined stations in the layout (for example the 150 m and 300 m crossbars of the ICAO/UK Calvert system, or the 500 ft and 1,000 ft "decision"/"roll" bars of the US ALSF‑2 system) — the terms are sometimes used loosely in the field, but they refer to different fixtures within the same approach lighting layout.

These lights are a defining feature of precision approach lighting systems such as ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, and the Calvert System, and are also used in medium‑intensity systems like MALS and MALSF. The crossbars provide critical distance and lateral guidance, helping pilots maintain the correct glide path and align with the runway centreline, particularly in low visibility conditions where external visual references are limited.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The crossbar exists to give a pilot a visual width reference at fixed points along the approach, since a single point of light conveys distance but not scale, while a short perpendicular row conveys both at once — the pilot can judge range partly from how the bar's apparent width changes as the aircraft closes on the threshold. Positioned at known, fixed distances from the threshold, the crossbars effectively function as visual milestones along the approach, letting the pilot cross-check descent progress and glide path against distance in a way a continuous centreline alone cannot provide as distinctly. This combination of distance and lateral cues is why crossbars are a defining, recurring feature across precision and medium-intensity systems alike, from full ALSF configurations down to simpler medium-intensity setups.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Crossbar lights are installed as part of approach lighting systems at airports that require enhanced visual guidance. They are typically used in:

  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems (precision approach runways with CAT I, II, III capability)
  • Calvert I and II systems (British approach lighting configuration)
  • MALS and MALSF systems (medium‑intensity approach lighting with crossbars for distance reference)
  • Precision approach runways requiring enhanced depth perception and glide‑slope monitoring
  • Major international airports with CAT II/III operations
  • Military airbases requiring all‑weather approach capability
Placement

Installation & Location

Crossbar assemblies are erected on a dedicated row of piers set perpendicular to the extended runway centreline, at the 300 m bar (CAT I) and/or the 150 m bar position within the approach lighting layout. Each crossbar spans several frangible poles set at the correct lateral spacing either side of the centreline, all piers tied to a common foundation line so the bar reads as a single continuous span to the approaching pilot. On approaches over water, marsh, or uneven terrain the crossbar piers are mounted on a raised platform or bridge structure matching the adjacent centreline piers. Cabling for the full crossbar is looped from pole to pole and back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with isolating transformers at each pole base; pier setout is surveyed off the runway centreline extension to keep the bar square to the approach path.

Function

How Does It Work?

Crossbar lights provide essential visual guidance through their distinctive arrangement and placement:

  • Distance Reference: Crossbars are placed at specific fixed distances from the threshold — 150 m and 300 m from threshold in the ICAO/UK Calvert system, or the 500 ft and 1,000 ft ("decision"/"roll") bars in the US ALSF‑2 system. As the pilot passes each crossbar, they gain a clear sense of their distance from the runway.
  • Lateral Guidance: The row of lights across the approach path helps the pilot maintain lateral alignment with the runway centreline — if the crossbar appears centred in the cockpit view, the aircraft is on the correct lateral path.
  • Depth Perception: The progression of crossbars at different distances creates a "ladder" or "ruler" effect that enhances depth perception and helps pilots judge their height and closure rate.
  • Glide‑Slope Monitoring: By observing the spacing and position of crossbars relative to the aircraft, pilots can assess whether they are on the correct glide slope — appearing too close together may indicate being too high or too low.
  • Steady‑Burning: Crossbar lights are typically steady‑burning (white) to provide a stable reference against the dynamic flashing lights (SFL) in the system.
  • Intensity Control: The lights are controlled by CCRs with multiple intensity steps (typically 5‑step), allowing ATC to adjust brightness for visibility conditions.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Crossbar lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the approach lighting series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR or ATC instruction. This command is passed to the approach lighting CCR, which regulates the entire crossbar loop to the corresponding fixed current (5-step). Every light across the crossbar span, via its own isolating transformer at the base of its pole, draws from the same current and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — the whole bar brightens or dims as one unit, and there is no way to intensify one end of the crossbar relative to the other. Because a crossbar spans several poles wired into a single continuous loop, the fixture's role as a lateral reference depends on every lamp along that span staying lit together; the loop-level design that keeps intensity uniform is the same mechanism that determines how a fault presents. Fault detection is loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault. A single lamp outage within a long crossbar span can quietly shorten the perceived bar without tripping an obvious visual gap, so field verification of full-span continuity is part of routine crossbar inspection.

Construction

Physical Construction

A crossbar spans several individual lamp heads mounted across multiple frangible poles, each head built the same way as a barrette unit: a die-cast aluminium housing with a toughened-glass or polycarbonate lens, gasket-sealed for an IP65/IP66 rating against the weather exposure typical of an open approach lighting site. Every pole in the bar shares a common foundation line and elevation setting so the assembly reads as one continuous span rather than a series of separate lights, and each pole incorporates a frangible coupling near its base to shear cleanly if struck. On approaches over water, marsh, or uneven terrain, the same lamp-head and pole hardware is instead carried on a raised platform or bridge structure tied to the adjacent centreline piers. Cabling loops from pole to pole along the bar and back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with an isolating transformer housed at each pole base, and stainless-steel fasteners and sealed junction boxes used throughout for long-term outdoor durability.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Common Foundation Line Setting
Civil

Set foundations for every pole in the crossbar to a common line and elevation, holding tolerance so the assembly reads as one continuous span rather than a series of separate lights.

Frangible Pole Erection
Mechanical

Erect each frangible pole across the bar at matching height, torquing the frangible coupling near the base of every pole so it shears cleanly if struck.

Pole-to-Pole Cable Looping
Electrical

Route series-circuit cable pole to pole along the bar back to the approach lighting control cabinet, avoiding sharp bends that could stress connectors at final termination.

Isolating Transformer Installation
Electrical

Install and confirm the isolating transformer housed at each pole base matches the lamp head wattage and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Lamp Head Wiring & Sealing
Electrical

Wire each lamp head across the crossbar and seal its junction box to the fixture's IP65/IP66 rating, keeping all splices weatherproof before energisation.

Light Count & Cross-Level Setout
Mechanical

Confirm the correct light count for the system type (5 for ALSF-2, 3 for Calvert I) and set every lamp head cross-level along the bar before final fixing.

Raised Platform / Bridge Structure Check
Civil

Where the approach crosses water, marsh, or uneven terrain, confirm the raised platform or bridge structure is structurally complete and tied to the adjacent centreline piers before crossbar installation.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record pole positions, cable routing, transformer ratings, and light count as-built before the crossbar circuit is energised.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the isolating transformer and series loop feeding each crossbar before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity through every light in the crossbar and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Light Count & Cross-Level Check
Pre-Energisation

Confirm each crossbar carries the correct light count for its system type (5 for ALSF-2, 3 for Calvert I) and that all lights sit cross-level along the bar before backfill.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each fixture's L-823 connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm all lights in the crossbar respond together, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections within the bar.

Crossbar Alignment & Aim Verification
Final Acceptance

Check elevation and azimuth of every light across the bar's width against the design aiming diagram, correcting any deviation so the beam presents uniformly toward the approach.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity and spread across the crossbar with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances prior to operational handover.

Crossbar Spacing & Sequencing Check
Final Acceptance

Verify each crossbar's distance from threshold and from adjacent crossbars matches the system's design spacing, and that its firing does not conflict with the sequenced flasher pattern.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Alignment Survey
Annual

Check crossbar fixture alignment with a theodolite or laser system to confirm the row still reads as a straight, symmetric line from the cockpit.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean rubber deposits, bird droppings, and de-icing chemicals off each lens to keep the crossbar clearly visible from a distance.

Individual Fixture Check
Monthly

Walk the crossbar to catch failed individual lights early, since a single outage breaks visual continuity of the distance reference.

Power Quality Check
Semi-annual

Monitor CCR output and supply voltage, since fluctuations or CCR faults can cause inconsistent brightness across the crossbar.

Vegetation & Sightline Clearance
Quarterly

Clear grass and vegetation growth around fixtures so nothing obstructs the light output or the pilot's line of sight to the crossbar.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test the series circuit and isolating transformers to confirm insulation resistance stays above minimum, catching moisture ingress before it causes a fault.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Misalignment of Crossbar Fixtures

Elevated crossbar lights can be knocked out of alignment by wind, wildlife, or maintenance vehicles, causing the row to appear uneven or misaligned from the cockpit. Regular alignment checks using a theodolite or laser system are essential to maintain the visual symmetry of the crossbar.

Individual Light Failures

A single failed light in a crossbar can break the visual continuity, reducing the effectiveness of the distance reference. Regular inspection and prompt replacement of faulty units are essential.

Lens Contamination

Crossbar lights are exposed to rubber deposits, bird droppings, and de‑icing chemicals. Regular cleaning with approved solvents is essential to maintain light output and visibility, especially for crossbars that need to be clearly seen from a distance.

Power Supply and CCR Issues

Crossbar systems draw significant power; voltage fluctuations or CCR faults can cause inconsistent brightness. Regular power quality monitoring is recommended.

Vegetation and Obstruction Growth

Vegetation or structures can grow up around crossbars, obstructing the light output. Regular site inspections and vegetation management are essential to maintain clear lines of sight.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Light Dark, Rest of Crossbar Normal
Likely Cause
Failed lamp/LED module, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding) at that fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp/LED module if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the connectors before backfill.
Entire Crossbar Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop feeding the crossbar (severed cable, corroded splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked lenses, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Crossbar Appears Uneven or Misaligned From the Cockpit
Likely Cause
One or more elevated crossbar fixtures have been knocked out of alignment by wind, wildlife, or contact from a maintenance vehicle.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture aim using a theodolite or laser alignment tool against the design pattern; inspect mounting hardware for damage or looseness.
Corrective Action
Re-aim and re-secure the affected fixture, repairing or replacing any damaged mounting hardware, then re-verify visual symmetry from the approach.
Reduced or Obscured Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from bird droppings, rubber deposits, or de-icing chemicals, or vegetation growth encroaching on the crossbar's line of sight.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against the required output; inspect and clean the lens; walk the approach to check for obstructing vegetation or structures.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lens, clear obstructing vegetation, and re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Power Supply Fluctuation or CCR Instability
Likely Cause
Voltage fluctuation on the supply feed or a CCR fault causing inconsistent brightness across the crossbar's significant power draw.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor incoming supply voltage and CCR output under load; check for loose or corroded connections along the feeder run.
Corrective Action
Correct the power quality issue at source, repair or replace faulted connections, or replace the CCR if it fails to hold regulation.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Lamp / LED Module Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
A single light within the crossbar goes dark, quietly shortening the perceived bar without an obvious visual gap, while the rest of the span continues to burn normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken a single lamp or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire crossbar span.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Series Cable / Splice Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or cable jacket damage from nearby excavation.
Effect
Intermittent operation, a partial ground fault degrading insulation resistance, or a full open circuit that darkens the entire crossbar loop.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each pole base during maintenance.
Fixture Misalignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wind loading, wildlife contact, or a maintenance vehicle striking an elevated fixture and knocking it out of its designed aim.
Effect
The bar appears uneven or asymmetric from the cockpit, degrading its value as a lateral and distance reference even though every lamp is lit.
Detection Method
Periodic alignment survey using a theodolite or laser tool, and visual inspection of mounting hardware for damage or looseness.
Lens Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits, bird droppings, and de-icing chemical residue accumulating on the outer lens surface.
Effect
Reduced or obscured light output that weakens the crossbar's visibility as a distance milestone, particularly at range.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of the lens during routine cleaning.
CCR Regulation Fault / Power Supply Instability
High Severity
Root Cause
Voltage fluctuation on the incoming supply, an internal CCR component failure, or a load mismatch given the crossbar's significant power draw.
Effect
Inconsistent brightness across the crossbar, failure to hold the regulated current, or nuisance tripping on an otherwise healthy loop.
Detection Method
Supply voltage and CCR output monitoring under load, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Vegetation / Obstruction Growth
Low Severity
Root Cause
Unmanaged vegetation growth or encroaching structures near the crossbar pier line over time.
Effect
Partial or full obstruction of the light output from the approach sightline, even though the fixture itself remains healthy.
Detection Method
Routine site inspection walking the approach sightline, and periodic vegetation management surveys.
Pier Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure on approaches over water, marsh, or uneven terrain.
Effect
One or more poles along the crossbar span tilt or shift, throwing the bar out of its square, continuous appearance to the approaching pilot.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of pier foundations during annual maintenance, and a full-span alignment survey against the design pattern.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Crossbar fixtures sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the pole base before climbing or handling connectors.

Elevated Multi-Pole Work
Personnel Safety

A crossbar spans several poles at once; confirm every pole feeding the bar is isolated, not just the one being worked on, and use fall protection appropriate to pole height, especially where the piers are carried on a raised platform or bridge over water.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible pole with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; each pole in the crossbar must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft on a low approach or go-around.

Approach Surface Coordination
Operational

The crossbar pier line sits within the approach protected surface; any work, including crane or lift use, requires ATC coordination, a NOTAM for the affected bar, and confirmation that temporary equipment stays within the height limit for that surface.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the entire bar.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave any light in the bar dark, out of alignment with its neighbours, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; a crossbar reads as a single span to the pilot, so verify the full bar's conformance before closing out.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Wind Loading on Elevated Crossbar Structures
Mechanical

Crossbars mounted on elevated approach lighting structures see continuous wind loading that flush fixtures never experience; check mounting hardware and frangible fittings for fatigue on the overhaul cycle, not only after a reported impact.

Foundation Settlement Skews Roll Guidance
Alignment

Because a crossbar's job is to give pilots a level roll reference, even a small amount of foundation settlement on one side of a structure — common on soft or reclaimed ground off the runway end — can introduce a barely perceptible tilt that undermines that cue; resurvey crossbar levelness periodically, not only after visible damage.

Keep Crossbar Timing Synchronised With the SFL
Sequencing

Where crossbars are used alongside sequenced flashing lights, controller drift between the two systems can desynchronise the rolling effect pilots rely on; verify crossbar and SFL timing together after any controller firmware or configuration change, not each system in isolation.

Match Intensity Steps Across the Bar
Retrofit

An LED retrofit that hits the correct intensity at each individual step but tracks a different brightness curve than its neighbours leaves the crossbar looking uneven between steps even though every lamp measures in spec on its own; verify the step curve across the whole bar, not lamp by lamp.

Off-Airport Foundations Corrode Faster
Durability

Approach lighting structures often sit on marshy or off-airport ground with limited drainage; specify corrosion-resistant hardware and inspect foundation bolts and structure bases more frequently here than for fixtures on the paved airfield.

Access Coordination for Off-Airport Structures
Operational

Crossbar structures beyond the airport perimeter often require landowner access coordination or specialised vehicles to reach; build that lead time into maintenance planning so a failed unit isn't left dark longer than a comparable on-airport fixture would be.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about crossbar lights: width reference in approach lighting, spacing, photometrics, standards and maintenance.

Q1What are crossbar lights?

A series of white lights arranged in a row perpendicular to the runway centreline at specific fixed distances from the threshold along the approach lighting system. A crossbar differs from a centreline barrette, which lies along the centreline.

Q2Why do approach lighting systems use crossbars?

A single point of light conveys distance but not scale. A perpendicular row conveys both, so the pilot can judge range partly from how wide the crossbar appears.

Q3Where are crossbar lights used?

In approach lighting systems where enhanced visual guidance is needed, including ALSF-1 and ALSF-2 (CAT I, II and III), Calvert I and II, MALS and MALSF systems.

Q4Where are crossbars positioned?

At fixed distances from the threshold. In the ICAO/UK Calvert system these are at 150, 300, 450, 600 and 750 m, while FAA ALSF-2 uses steady-burning rows at about 30 m (100 ft) longitudinal centres.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

An LED array or 100-200 W halogen source, white steady-burning light, 3-5 lights per crossbar (typically 5 in ALSF-2 and 3 in Calvert I), a width of 15-30 m, light spacing of 3-5 m, and elevated frangible or flush mounting rated IP67/IP68.

Q6How are crossbar lights powered?

From a CCR-fed 6.6 A constant current series circuit with a 5-step schedule, through an individual 100-200 W isolating transformer per lamp head, and they integrate with ALCMS.

Q7What photometric performance is expected?

At least 15,000 cd white at 100% intensity, a horizontal spread of ±4.5° either side of the approach centreline plane, an intensity tolerance of ±25% at each CCR step, and chromaticity within the ICAO Annex 14 white boundary.

Q8Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46, IEC TS 61827 and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program).

Q9How often should crossbar lights be maintained?

Monthly individual fixture checks; quarterly lens cleaning and vegetation clearance; semi-annual power quality checks; and annual alignment surveys and insulation resistance tests.

Q10What if a single crossbar light is dark?

A failed lamp or LED module, or an open isolating transformer at that fixture. Confirm series loop continuity through the transformer primary.
