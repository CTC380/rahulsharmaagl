LED Module

The solid-state light source that powers modern AGL fixtures. Combining high-efficiency LEDs, thermal management, and precise optics, LED modules offer superior energy savings, long life, instant restart, and excellent colour stability compared to traditional halogen lamps.

Overview

What Is It?

An LED Module is a complete solid-state light engine specifically designed for AGL applications. It consists of one or more high-power LEDs mounted on a metal-core printed circuit board (MCPCB) with integrated drive circuitry, thermal management, and optical alignment features. Unlike traditional halogen or incandescent lamps, LED modules produce light through electroluminescence, offering significantly higher efficacy (lumens per watt), longer operational life (up to 50,000 hours), and instant restart capability. LED modules are available in various colours (white, green, red, yellow, blue) and intensities, and are the standard light source for modern LED-based AGL fixtures.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The LED module's purpose is to be the actual light-emitting element that gives the pilot the visual signal the fixture exists to provide — the correct colour, correct intensity, and correct beam coordinates that ICAO Annex 14 and FAA specifications define for that fixture type. Its practical value over a halogen lamp is reliability at scale: because an airfield's safety case depends on hundreds of individual lights all working simultaneously, an LED module's longer life (up to 50,000 hours) and instant restart capability directly translate into fewer failed lights, fewer maintenance visits, and a lower chance of a gap appearing in a runway or approach lighting pattern that a pilot is using for guidance.

Application

Where Is It Used?

LED modules are used in all modern AGL fixtures that utilise LED technology, including:

  • Runway Edge & Centreline Lights: High-intensity white or yellow modules for precision guidance.
  • Threshold & End Lights: Green and red modules with precise colour coordinates.
  • Taxiway Lights: Blue and green modules for edge and centreline guidance.
  • Approach Lighting: High-flux white modules for barrettes and flashing lights.
  • PAPI & VASI: Specialised modules that produce the required red/white transition.
  • Obstruction Lights: Red and white modules for aviation obstruction marking.
  • Beacons & Wind Cones: LED modules for rotating beacons and illuminated windsocks.
Placement

Installation & Location

The module is bolted or clipped onto the heat sink at the fixture's optical focus, with thermal interface material applied at the mounting face so heat generated at the LED junction conducts away efficiently rather than building up behind the emitter. Its position and orientation are set to the manufacturer's optical alignment marks so the emitter sits precisely where the reflector or prism expects it; even a small offset here can shift or distort the projected beam pattern. Wiring is terminated to the module's connector before the housing is closed, with the leads kept clear of the optical path, and the module is seated so its face is protected behind the lens and gasket once the fixture is resealed.

Function

How Does It Work?

An LED module works by passing current through a semiconductor junction, which emits light through electroluminescence. The module includes:

  • LED Chips: High-power semiconductor die that emit light at specific wavelengths (colours).
  • MCPCB: Metal-core printed circuit board that provides electrical connections and thermal spreading.
  • Drive Circuitry: Constant-current driver that regulates current (typically 350mA to 1A per LED) for stable light output.
  • Thermal Management: Heat sink or thermal pad to dissipate heat and maintain junction temperature within limits.
  • Optical Alignment: Precision placement and lens/reflector integration to achieve the required beam pattern.

The module is powered by the series circuit through an isolating transformer, with intensity controlled by the CCR's current steps. LED modules are designed to meet the photometric requirements of ICAO Annex 14 and FAA specifications.

Construction

Physical Construction

The module is built around a metal-core printed circuit board (MCPCB), usually aluminium-clad, which carries the LED die array and spreads heat efficiently from the emitters toward the heat sink mounted directly behind it. High-power LED packages are surface-mounted and, in many AGL modules, individually binned for colour and output so the array delivers consistent chromaticity and intensity across the beam. A secondary optic — a moulded lens, dome, or array of individual micro-lenses over each LED — sits over the emitter array to begin shaping the beam before the light reaches the fixture's main prism or reflector, and locating pins or a keyed mounting frame fix the module's rotational and lateral position relative to that downstream optic. The MCPCB is bolted or clipped to the heat sink through a thermal interface layer, and wiring exits the module through a sealed, often locking, connector so the module can be swapped without disturbing the potted or soldered LED-to-board joints.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

LED Degradation (Lumen Depreciation)

Over time, LED modules experience gradual lumen depreciation due to thermal stress and chip aging. Regular photometric measurements are essential to identify modules that have fallen below acceptable intensity levels.

Thermal Management Failure

If the heat sink or thermal interface becomes compromised (e.g., due to dust accumulation or loose mounting), LED junction temperature rises, accelerating degradation and reducing life. Regular inspection and cleaning of cooling paths is recommended.

Driver Circuit Failure

The constant-current driver is a common failure point. Surges, moisture ingress, or component aging can cause driver failure, leading to flickering or complete loss of light. Replace the module or driver as required.

Colour Shift

LED colour can shift over time due to phosphor degradation (in white LEDs) or changes in the semiconductor junction. Colour shift can affect compliance with ICAO chromaticity coordinates. Replace modules that show significant colour shift.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Module Completely Dark
Likely Cause
Failed driver circuit, an open connection between the driver and module, or a catastrophic LED chip failure within the module.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm driver output voltage/current is present at the module's input; check the interconnect for continuity; substitute a known-good driver if available to isolate module versus driver as the fault.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed module or driver as identified, and repair any open interconnect wiring before returning to service.
Flickering or Intermittent Output
Likely Cause
Driver circuit degradation from surges, moisture ingress, or component ageing causing an unstable current supply to the module.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor driver output current/voltage for stability under load; check for intermittent connections at the module's interconnect.
Corrective Action
Replace the driver if output is unstable, or repair the interconnect if a loose connection is the cause; re-verify stable output before closing out.
Reduced Light Output
Likely Cause
Gradual lumen depreciation from thermal stress and chip ageing, often accelerated by a compromised thermal path (dust, loose mounting, or dried thermal interface material at the heat sink).
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against the rated output; inspect the cooling path (heat sink fins, thermal interface, mounting) for degradation contributing to elevated junction temperature.
Corrective Action
Replace the module if output has fallen below the acceptable threshold, and address any thermal management issue (cleaning, re-mounting, fresh TIM) found during inspection to slow future depreciation.
Colour Outside Chromaticity Tolerance
Likely Cause
Phosphor degradation in white LEDs or gradual changes in the semiconductor junction shifting the emitted colour over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour check against the applicable ICAO/FAA chromaticity coordinates; compare against the module's age and expected colour-shift curve.
Corrective Action
Replace the module once colour shift exceeds tolerance; there is no field correction for phosphor or junction colour shift.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the fixture supply and verify zero energy before disconnecting or replacing the LED module; because the module is instant-on, treat the circuit as live the moment power is present rather than waiting to see if it lights.

ESD Precautions
Component Handling

Use anti-static precautions when handling the module's MCPCB and integrated drive circuitry to avoid latent damage that may not show up until later in service.

Direct Viewing
Personnel Safety

Avoid staring directly into the LED array during functional testing, particularly on high-intensity modules.

Thermal Contact on Reinstallation
Mechanical

Ensure proper thermal contact with the heat sink when reseating a module; poor thermal coupling accelerates lumen depreciation and colour shift even if the module appears to work normally at first.

Correct Colour Replacement
Compliance

Confirm the replacement module is the correct colour and intensity variant for the fixture; modules across the white, green, red, yellow, and blue range can be physically similar but are not interchangeable.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for electrical work and clean gloves when handling the module to avoid contaminating the optical surface.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify colour, intensity, and beam pattern meet specification before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Lumen Depreciation Is Predictable, Not Random
Photometric

LED output declines on a fairly predictable curve driven by operating temperature and drive current, unlike a lamp filament's more abrupt end-of-life failure; use the manufacturer's L70/L80 depreciation data to plan proactive group replacement on high-value circuits rather than waiting for individual modules to fail a photometric spot check.

Thermal Coupling Failures Are Invisible at First Light
Diagnostics

A module reseated with poor thermal contact to its heat sink will still light correctly and pass an initial commissioning check; the accelerated depreciation and colour shift this causes only becomes apparent months later, so confirm proper thermal contact procedurally at reinstallation rather than relying on immediate visual performance.

Colour Binning Variation Between Batches Is Real
Procurement

LED modules from different production batches, even of the same nominal part number, can show a visible colour or intensity variation known as binning drift; when replacing a subset of modules on a shared circuit, source from a batch matched as closely as possible to the existing modules to avoid a visibly inconsistent installation.

ESD Damage Can Be Latent, Not Immediate
Component Handling

A module that suffers minor electrostatic discharge damage during handling can still function correctly at first power-on while carrying a weakened junction that fails prematurely months later; enforce anti-static handling discipline as a standing procedure, since the consequence of skipping it doesn't show up until well after the installation is complete.

Colour-Specific Modules Are a Common Stores Mix-Up
Compliance

Modules for different colours in the same physical form factor are easy to mix up in a stores environment, especially where labelling is small or packaging looks similar; verify colour on the module label at the point of installation, not just at the point of stores issue, since a colour error installed at a critical position (e.g., a stop bar or runway guard light) is a compliance issue, not just an aesthetic one.

Track Module Age Alongside the Fixture's Age
Maintenance

Where a fixture has had its module replaced partway through its service life, the module's own age clock is independent of the fixture's; keep a per-module install date in maintenance records so depreciation-based replacement planning uses the module's actual service time rather than the fixture's overall age.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL LED modules: construction, colours, power, thermal management, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is an LED module in an AGL fixture?

An LED module is a complete solid-state light engine for AGL applications. It consists of one or more high-power LEDs on a metal-core printed circuit board (MCPCB), with drive circuitry, thermal management and optical alignment features. It is the standard light source for modern LED-based AGL fixtures.

Q2How does an LED module compare with a halogen lamp?

LED modules offer higher efficacy (typically 100 to 150 lm/W for white and 40 to 80 lm/W for coloured), a lifetime of up to 50,000 hours (L70 at rated current), instant restart and good colour stability. That means fewer failed lights and fewer maintenance visits across an airfield.

Q3What colours are AGL LED modules available in?

White (4000 K to 6500 K), green, red, yellow and blue. White or yellow modules are used for runway edge and centreline lights, green and red for threshold and end lights, and blue and green for taxiway lights. Specialised modules produce the red/white transition needed for PAPI and VASI.

Q4How is an LED module powered and dimmed?

It is powered from the series circuit through an isolating transformer and LED driver, with intensity controlled by the CCR's current steps. The module typically runs at a forward current of 350 mA to 1 A, with a forward voltage of 3 to 4 V per LED for white and 2 to 3 V for coloured.

Q5Where does the LED module sit electrically?

It is a low-voltage DC load downstream of the LED driver, isolated from the primary series loop through the driver and transformer. An LED string failure is detected through the driver's output monitoring rather than as a series-loop fault.

Q6Why is thermal management so important for an LED module?

Heat at the LED junction accelerates degradation. The module needs adequate heat sinking to keep junction temperature below 85°C, and typical junction-to-case thermal resistance is 2 to 5°C/W. A degraded thermal interface, dust build-up or loose mounting raises junction temperature and speeds up lumen depreciation.

Q7Which standards apply to AGL LED modules?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (photometric and colour requirements), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (LED AGL luminaire specification), IEC 61827 and IES LM-79/LM-80 (photometric measurement and long-term lumen maintenance). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should an LED module be checked?

Typical practice is an annual lumen depreciation check against baseline, thermal pathway inspection, colour consistency check and driver circuit continuity test. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common LED module problems?

The common issues are gradual lumen depreciation, thermal management failure, driver circuit failure (a common failure point, caused by surges, moisture or component ageing) and colour shift from phosphor degradation or junction changes, which can affect compliance with ICAO chromaticity requirements.

Q10What should I check when replacing an LED module?

Lock out the supply and verify zero energy, use anti-static precautions, and confirm the replacement is the correct colour and intensity variant for the fixture. Make sure thermal contact with the heat sink is good, and verify colour, intensity and beam pattern meet specification before returning the fixture to service.
