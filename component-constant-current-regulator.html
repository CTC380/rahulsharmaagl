Constant Current Regulator

The heart of the AGL series circuit. A precision power supply that maintains a fixed current (typically 6.6A) regardless of load variations, ensuring consistent light intensity across all connected fixtures. Available in various technologies including thyristor, IGBT, and ferroresonant designs.

Overview

What Is It?

A Constant Current Regulator (CCR) is a specialised power supply used in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems. It converts the mains supply into a constant current output, typically 6.6 amperes, which is fed into a series circuit that powers all the lights on a particular runway, taxiway, or approach lighting system. The CCR ensures that every fixture in the loop receives the same current, so that all lights burn at the same intensity regardless of how many lights are connected or changes in the circuit impedance. This is essential for maintaining uniform brightness and safe visual guidance. Modern CCRs are available in different technologies, including thyristor (SCR), IGBT, and ferroresonant types, each offering specific advantages in efficiency, reliability, and control.

For the full engineering guide, see our constant current regulator (CCR) deep dive.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The CCR exists because the series-circuit topology used across AGL systems requires every fixture on a loop to receive exactly the same current regardless of how many fixtures are connected or how circuit impedance varies over the loop's length, a requirement that ordinary constant-voltage power distribution cannot satisfy. Without the CCR forcing a fixed current through the whole loop, some fixtures would burn brighter or dimmer than others depending on their position in the circuit, undermining the uniform-intensity visual reference that runway and taxiway lighting exists to provide in the first place. Its purpose is therefore foundational to the entire AGL power architecture: every other power-side component described elsewhere in this library — UPS, generator interface, distribution panel, monitoring systems — exists to keep the CCR itself powered, protected, and controllable, because the CCR is what actually converts raw electrical supply into the uniform current the lighting system depends on.

Application

Where Is It Used?

CCRs are installed in airfield electrical rooms or equipment shelters, typically near the runway or taxiway they serve. They are used in all AGL applications that require a series circuit, including:

  • Runway Lighting: Edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, and touchdown zone lights.
  • Taxiway Lighting: Edge lights, centreline lights, and stop bars.
  • Approach Lighting: All approach lighting systems (ALSF-1, ALSF-2, MALS, etc.) that use series circuits.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Some obstruction lighting systems may also use CCRs.
  • Heliport Lighting: For helipad lighting circuits.

A typical airfield may have multiple CCRs, each dedicated to a specific lighting circuit (e.g., one for runway edge lights, one for centreline, one for taxiways).

Placement

Installation & Location

CCRs are floor-mounted on a concrete plinth or wall-mounted within the electrical equipment room or shelter that serves the circuit, positioned close to the runway or taxiway to minimise the length of the high-voltage series loop. Incoming mains and control/communication cabling enter from below through a cable trench or duct, while the high-voltage output feeds the series circuit and isolating transformers via its own dedicated cable route, kept separate from mains wiring. The regulator frame is bonded to the room's earthing system, and clearance is left on the ventilated sides and front access panel per the manufacturer's cooling and maintenance requirements. A typical equipment room houses several regulators side by side, each dedicated to one lighting circuit (edge, centreline, taxiway, and so on).

Function

How Does It Work?

The CCR takes the incoming AC mains (typically 230V or 400V) and regulates it to provide a constant current output, usually 6.6A RMS, with a high open-circuit voltage (up to several thousand volts) to drive the series circuit. The CCR uses a control loop that continuously monitors the output current and adjusts the voltage to maintain the set current despite changes in the load (e.g., due to lamp failures or temperature variations). The output current is adjustable in steps (typically 5 brightness levels: 2.8A, 3.4A, 4.1A, 5.2A, 6.6A) to provide different light intensities for various weather conditions (clear, mist, fog). The CCR includes protection features such as overcurrent, overvoltage, short-circuit, and earth fault detection. Modern CCRs often have digital control and communication interfaces for remote monitoring and integration with Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring Systems (ALCMS).

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Output voltage rises automatically to maintain 6.6A, regardless of how many fixtures are in the loop or lamp failures along it.
Construction

Physical Construction

A CCR is generally housed in a floor-standing or wall-mounted steel cabinet, powder-coated for corrosion resistance and fitted with lockable doors, ventilation louvres, and a cable gland plate at the base for incoming mains and outgoing series-circuit cabling. Internally, the cabinet is arranged in distinct sections: an input section with isolator, circuit breaker, and input filtering; a power conversion section (transformer, thyristor bridge, IGBT module, or ferroresonant core, depending on technology); an output section with current transducers and output isolation switching; and a control section housing the regulation electronics, HMI, and terminal blocks, usually on a separate sub-panel to isolate it from heat and electrical noise in the power section. Forced-air cooling with filtered fan intakes is common on higher-power units, while smaller regulators may rely on natural convection through the cabinet's louvred sides. Earthing bars, surge arrestors, and clearly labelled terminal strips are provided for the incoming supply and outgoing series circuit, and the cabinet is rated for its intended installation environment, whether an equipment room or an outdoor enclosure.

Position the cabinet on a level plinth or floor fixing within the equipment room, keeping the manufacturer's clearance around louvres and doors for airflow and access.

Mechanical

Move and set the cabinet using rated lifting equipment, securing it to the floor or plinth to prevent movement under vibration or maintenance loading.

Electrical

Terminate the incoming supply at the input isolator through the cable gland plate, confirming the breaker rating and cable size match the regulator's input current.

Series Circuit Output Termination
Electrical

Earthing & Surge Protection Bonding

Ventilation Clearance
Mechanical

Confirm louvres and fan intakes are unobstructed and filters are fitted, since restricted airflow shortens the life of the power conversion section.

Communication Cabling
Systems

Run and terminate the RS-485, Modbus, or Ethernet cabling to the CCR controller or monitoring system, keeping it separated from power wiring.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the CCR's input, output, and series loop windings before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Series Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity of the complete series loop and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance joints before first energisation.

No-Load / Open-Circuit Protection Test
Energisation

Open the series loop with the CCR running and confirm the open-circuit protection trips within its rated response time rather than allowing output voltage to climb unchecked.

Output Current Step Verification
Energisation

Cycle through every brightness step and confirm actual output current matches the rated value (e.g. 2.8A–6.6A) at each step within tolerance.

Load Resistance Sweep Test
Energisation

Vary the connected load resistance across its expected range and confirm the CCR holds constant output current rather than drifting with loop impedance.

Earth Fault & Ground Isolation Check
Final Acceptance

Introduce a controlled earth fault on the output side and confirm the CCR's protection detects and reports it correctly.

Harmonic Distortion (THD) Test
Final Acceptance

Measure input current harmonic distortion at full load and confirm it stays within the levels assumed by the site's power quality design.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, loop resistance, step-current, and protection test results in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Capacitor Aging

Filter capacitors in the DC link of IGBT and thyristor CCRs degrade over time, leading to increased ripple, reduced efficiency, and eventual failure. Regular capacitor condition monitoring and replacement (typically every 5–7 years) is essential.

Cooling Fan Failure

Forced-air cooled CCRs rely on cooling fans that can fail due to dust, bearing wear, or overheating. Fan failure leads to overtemperature shutdown. Regular cleaning and fan replacement schedules are recommended.

Transient Voltage Spikes

Lightning strikes or switching transients can damage the input protection circuits or control electronics. Installation of surge protection devices (SPDs) and proper earthing are critical to ensure long-term reliability.

Communication Failures

Remote monitoring and control rely on communication links (RS-485, Ethernet). Cable damage, connector corrosion, or protocol mismatches can cause loss of communication. Regular testing and proper termination are necessary.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Increased Output Ripple or Reduced Regulation Accuracy
Likely Cause
Filter capacitors in the DC link have aged, a common failure mode after 5-7 years of service on IGBT and thyristor CCRs.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure DC link ripple and compare against commissioning baseline; check capacitor ESR/capacitance against manufacturer tolerance.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded capacitor bank on the recommended service interval, and re-verify output regulation with a load test.
Overtemperature Shutdown
Likely Cause
A cooling fan has failed due to dust accumulation, bearing wear, or motor fault on a forced-air cooled unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fan operation and airflow at the heatsink; inspect for dust buildup blocking ventilation paths; review the CCR's temperature log for the shutdown event.
Corrective Action
Clean the cooling path and replace the failed fan; confirm temperatures stay within limits under a full-load test before returning to service.
Control Electronics Damaged After a Storm
Likely Cause
A lightning strike or switching transient has bypassed inadequate surge protection and damaged the input protection or control circuits.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect surge protection devices (SPDs) for tripped or burnt-out indicators; test control electronics for correct operation; verify site earthing resistance.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged SPDs and any affected control electronics, and improve earthing or SPD coordination if the fault recurs.
Loss of Communication with the Monitoring System
Likely Cause
Damaged or poorly terminated RS-485/Ethernet cabling, connector corrosion, or a network configuration mismatch (baud rate, parity, IP addressing) between the two devices.
Diagnostic Steps
Check cable continuity and shielding/grounding along the run; verify termination resistors are correctly placed; confirm both ends are configured with matching communication settings.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable section, correct the termination or configuration mismatch, and confirm stable communication over a sustained test period before returning to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Output Filter Capacitor Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Electrolytic capacitor ageing under sustained thermal and ripple current stress, causing capacitance to fall and ESR to rise over years of service.
Effect
Increased output ripple and reduced regulation accuracy, which can shorten the life of downstream lamps or LED fixtures.
Detection Method
Output ripple measurement against the rated tolerance, and periodic capacitor ESR/capacitance testing during major maintenance.
Overtemperature Shutdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Blocked or fouled cooling fins, a failed cooling fan, or operation in an enclosure with inadequate ventilation for the ambient conditions.
Effect
The CCR trips on overtemperature protection, interrupting the runway lighting circuit until it cools and is reset.
Detection Method
Internal temperature monitoring against the trip threshold, and inspection of cooling fans/fins during scheduled maintenance.
Surge/Lightning Damage to Control Electronics
High Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced transient or switching surge on the input, output, or control wiring that exceeds the unit's surge protection rating.
Effect
Damaged control board, erratic regulation, or complete failure to energise the series circuit.
Detection Method
Post-storm functional and self-test check, and inspection of onboard surge protection devices and control board for visible damage.
Series Reactor / Output Transformer Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Insulation breakdown from age or moisture, or a winding short caused by a sustained overload condition.
Effect
Loss of regulation on the affected output, or complete failure to energise the series loop if the fault is severe.
Detection Method
Winding resistance and insulation (megger) testing, and thermal imaging of the reactor/transformer during load.
Loss of Communication with Monitoring System
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
Remote monitoring and control of the CCR is lost, though the regulator continues local operation on its last commanded step.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the monitoring system, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Input Rectifier / Bridge Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Diode fatigue under repeated surge current, or a single diode failure that unbalances the rectifier bridge.
Effect
Unstable or absent DC bus voltage feeding the regulation stage, leading to loss of output or a protective shutdown.
Detection Method
Input bridge voltage and ripple measurement, and thermal imaging to identify an overheating diode under load.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

High Open-Circuit Series Voltage
Electrical Safety

The output loop can present several kV open-circuit even at modest input voltage, since the CCR forces current through whatever impedance the series circuit presents; de-energise and lock out the CCR at its supply, and verify zero energy at the output terminals with a suitably rated meter before touching them.

Residual Stored Charge
Electrical Safety

Internal filter and snubber capacitors can retain a dangerous charge after the unit is switched off; allow the manufacturer's specified discharge time, or confirm discharge with a rated meter, before opening the cabinet.

Hot Surfaces
Personnel Safety

Allow the output transformer, heatsinks, and any load resistors to cool before handling after active operation; these components run continuously hot under normal load.

PPE and Arc-Flash Precautions
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working in or near the cabinet's live sections, and follow the site's arc-flash procedure for any work on the incoming mains connection.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Taking a CCR out of service darkens an entire series circuit; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before isolation, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the runway/taxiway strip per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the series loop before restoring power after any cable, connector, or fixture work downstream, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm output current matches the commanded brightness step and the loop is within its impedance tolerance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Load Resistance Sweep Catches What a Single-Point Check Misses
Testing

A CCR that regulates current correctly at the loop's present impedance can still be marginal at the extremes of its rated load range, a condition that only a resistance-sweep test reveals rather than a single spot-check at the loop's current, as-installed configuration. Run the full load resistance sweep at commissioning and major service intervals, not only a check at the loop's present fixture count.

Insulation Resistance Trends Predict Failures Before They Trip the CCR
Reliability

A single megger reading that passes the acceptance threshold says less than a trend of readings across successive services, since a slowly failing splice or cable shows as gradual insulation-resistance decline long before it produces an actual fault trip. Log and trend insulation resistance results rather than treating each test as a pass/fail with no memory of prior readings.

Cooling Fan and Filter Condition Drives DC Link Capacitor Life
Maintenance

Reduced airflow from a clogged filter or slowing fan raises internal operating temperature and shortens the service life of the DC link capacitors well before the fan itself fails outright or trips a thermal alarm. Treat filter and fan condition as a preventive item tied to capacitor longevity, not just as its own standalone check.

Harmonic Distortion Worth Watching Even Within Spec
Power Quality

A THD reading that passes commissioning acceptance can still be trending upward across service visits as internal components age, giving early warning of a developing fault before it produces a hard failure or an actual compliance breach. Trend THD readings over successive services rather than treating a single pass as closing the item permanently.

Confirm Output Current Calibration Against a Calibrated Reference
Calibration

A CCR's own internal display can drift from the actual delivered current over time without any fault indication, since the unit is reporting what its own sensing circuit believes rather than an independently verified value. Periodically confirm output current against a calibrated external reference meter rather than relying solely on the CCR's self-reported reading.

Earth Fault Checks Matter More on a Series Loop Than on Parallel Wiring
Electrical

On a series circuit, a single earth fault anywhere in the loop can produce a symptom far from the actual fault location, unlike a parallel-wired system where the fault is usually localised to the affected branch. Prioritise systematic earth fault and ground isolation checks on this fixture type rather than assuming a fault reading points directly at the nearest suspect equipment.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about constant current regulators: what they do, output current, technologies, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a constant current regulator (CCR)?

A CCR is a specialised power supply used in airfield ground lighting that converts mains into a constant current output, typically 6.6 A, feeding a series circuit. Every fixture in the loop receives the same current, so all lights burn at the same intensity regardless of how many are connected.

Q2Why do airfield lighting circuits use constant current instead of constant voltage?

AGL uses a series-circuit topology, which needs exactly the same current through every fixture regardless of the number of fixtures or circuit impedance. Ordinary constant-voltage distribution cannot do this, and without a CCR some fixtures would burn brighter or dimmer than others.

Q3What brightness steps does a CCR provide?

Five output steps of 2.8 A, 3.4 A, 4.1 A, 5.2 A and 6.6 A RMS, selected locally or from the ALCMS to suit weather and visibility. The regulator holds the set current within about ±1%.

Q4What types of CCR are there?

The main technologies are thyristor (SCR), IGBT and ferroresonant. Typical efficiency is above 90% for IGBT types, about 85% for thyristor and about 80% for ferroresonant.

Q5What size are CCRs?

Output power is typically 5 kVA to 30 kVA depending on the load, with 230 V or 400 V AC input at 50/60 Hz. The output has a high open-circuit voltage, up to several thousand volts, to drive the series loop.

Q6Where is a CCR installed?

In an airfield electrical room or equipment shelter near the runway or taxiway it serves, floor-mounted on a plinth or wall-mounted. Being close keeps the length of the high-voltage series loop short.

Q7What lighting circuits use CCRs?

Runway edge, centreline, threshold and touchdown zone lights, taxiway edge and centreline lights and stop bars, approach lighting systems that use series circuits, some obstruction lighting and heliport lighting.

Q8What protection does a CCR have?

Overcurrent, overvoltage, short-circuit, earth fault and overtemperature protection, plus ground fault and open-circuit detection on the output loop. Communication via RS-485, Modbus or Ethernet connects it to the airfield lighting control and monitoring system.

Q9Which standards apply to CCRs?

The main references are IEC 61822 and EN 61822, FAA AC 150/5345-10 (constant current regulators and regulator monitors) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q10How often should a CCR be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cooling fan and filter cleaning, and annual for DC link capacitor checks, surge protection and earthing checks, communication link tests, output current calibration and terminal and enclosure checks. DC link capacitors are typically replaced every 5–7 years. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11Why does a CCR shut down on overtemperature or show increased ripple?

Overtemperature is usually a failed cooling fan or a clogged air path on a forced-air unit, fixed by cleaning the path and replacing the fan. Increased ripple or reduced regulation accuracy usually means aged DC link filter capacitors, a common failure after 5–7 years on IGBT and thyristor CCRs.
