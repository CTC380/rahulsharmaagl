Distribution Box

A robust enclosure that distributes electrical power from the main supply to multiple AGL circuits. Distribution boxes typically house circuit breakers, fuses, terminal blocks, and bus bars, providing overcurrent protection and individual circuit isolation for reliable airfield lighting power distribution.

Overview

What Is It?

A Distribution Box (also known as a feeder distribution unit or sub-distribution panel) is an electrical enclosure that accepts incoming power from a main switchgear or bus bar and distributes it to multiple outgoing AGL circuits. It typically contains:

  • Incoming terminals or bus bars for the main supply
  • Circuit breakers or MCCBs for each outgoing circuit, providing overcurrent and short-circuit protection
  • Terminal blocks for neat and serviceable wiring
  • Neutral and earth bars for complete system earthing

Distribution boxes are a key component in AGL power networks, enabling safe and flexible distribution of electrical energy to CCRs, lighting circuits, control systems, and other airfield equipment.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The distribution box's purpose is to take a single incoming power feed and split it safely into multiple independently protected and switchable circuits, so that a fault or maintenance need on one branch of the AGL power network doesn't have to bring down every circuit fed from the same source. By giving each outgoing circuit — a CCR, a control system, an auxiliary load — its own dedicated overcurrent protection and isolation point, it confines the effect of a short circuit or overload to the single circuit where it occurred, rather than letting it cascade back into the main supply and take out unrelated lighting sections. This containment function is what allows a large, multi-circuit airfield lighting system to be maintained and repaired incrementally rather than as an all-or-nothing block.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Distribution boxes are deployed throughout the AGL power infrastructure at various voltage levels:

  • Main Distribution Boards: Located in equipment shelters or substations, receiving power from the utility supply or generator and distributing to multiple CCR panels.
  • Sub-distribution Panels: Placed near CCRs or remote equipment rooms to supply individual regulators and auxiliary loads.
  • Field Distribution Points: Installed in underground vaults or outdoor enclosures to distribute power to lighting circuits and transformers.
  • Lighting Control Centers: Housing distribution for ALCMS and monitoring equipment.
  • Emergency Power Systems: Integrating with UPS and generator changeover to ensure uninterrupted supply.
Placement

Installation & Location

Distribution boxes are sited according to their role in the network: main boards inside equipment shelters or substations close to the incoming utility or generator supply, sub-distribution panels near the CCRs or remote equipment rooms they feed, and field distribution points in underground vaults or weatherproof outdoor enclosures close to the lighting circuits they serve. Indoor units are wall- or frame-mounted with clear access for opening doors and racking out breakers, while outdoor and buried enclosures are rated for water and dust ingress and set on a raised plinth or in a chamber to prevent standing water reaching the terminals. Cable entries use glands sized and sealed for the number and gauge of incoming and outgoing circuits, and the box location is tied into the site's cable route so runs to and from it stay within design length limits.

Function

How Does It Work?

A distribution box acts as a central hub for power distribution. Incoming power (typically 415V AC, 3-phase) is connected to a main bus bar or terminal block inside the box. From this bus, individual circuit breakers or MCCBs feed each outgoing circuit. Each circuit is protected by its own overcurrent device, allowing individual isolation for maintenance or fault clearance. The box also provides:

  • Protection: Against overloads, short circuits, and earth faults via MCBs, MCCBs, or fuses.
  • Isolation: For safe maintenance and troubleshooting.
  • Monitoring: Some boxes include metering or status indication for each circuit.
  • Earthing: Internal earth bars ensure all metallic parts and circuits are properly earthed.

Distribution boxes are typically wall-mounted or floor-standing, with hinged doors for easy access. They are designed to meet IP ratings appropriate for their environment, from indoor IP31 to outdoor IP66.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The distribution box is a passive branching node in the power architecture, positioned between the main bus bar or incoming switchgear and the individual downstream circuits it feeds — CCRs, control panels, or auxiliary loads. It has no control logic of its own: each outgoing way is protected by its own MCB or MCCB, and a fault on one way is cleared by that way's breaker without the distribution box itself sensing or reporting anything. On installations with remote monitoring, individual breaker or metering modules within the box can be fitted with communication interfaces (Modbus, or simple volt-free alarm contacts) that report status upstream to a SCADA panel or the ALCMS workstation, turning what is otherwise a purely mechanical distribution point into a monitored node — but this reporting capability sits in the breakers and meters mounted inside the box, not in the enclosure or bus bar itself.

Construction

Physical Construction

The distribution box is built around a welded or folded steel, stainless steel, or GRP enclosure, powder-coated or gel-coated for corrosion resistance and sized to house the incoming supply termination, main protective device, and a row of outgoing MCB, MCCB, RCBO, or fuse-switch ways mounted on copper or tin-plated aluminium bus bars. The enclosure door is gasketed and fitted with quarter-turn or lockable latches to achieve its rated ingress protection, from IP31 for sheltered indoor panels up to IP66 for pole- or outdoor wall-mounted units exposed to weather. Internally, bus bars are shrouded with insulating barriers to prevent accidental contact and phase-to-phase flashover, and a dedicated earth bar with multiple termination points is bonded to the enclosure body. Cable entry is via sealed glands or a gland plate at the base or side of the enclosure, and a clearly labelled circuit directory is fitted to the inside of the door to identify each outgoing way.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Overheating Due to Loose Connections

Loose bus bar or terminal connections cause increased resistance, leading to localised heating and potential fire risk. Regular thermal imaging inspections and torque checks are recommended.

Water Ingress Through Cable Entries

Improperly sealed cable glands or conduit entries allow moisture ingress, causing corrosion and insulation degradation. Regular inspection and resealing is essential, especially in outdoor installations.

Circuit Breaker Nuisance Tripping

Harmonics, inrush currents, or faulty protective devices can cause unwanted tripping. Using correctly rated and type-selected breakers (e.g., C-curve or D-curve) and performing coordination studies can mitigate this.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Localised Heating at a Terminal or Bus Bar
Likely Cause
Loose or under-torqued connections increasing contact resistance under load.
Diagnostic Steps
Thermal-image the box under normal load to locate hot spots; check torque on all bus bar and terminal connections against the manufacturer's specification.
Corrective Action
De-energise, re-torque or replace the affected connection, and re-check with thermal imaging after re-energising to confirm the hot spot has cleared.
Moisture or Corrosion Found Inside the Enclosure
Likely Cause
Failed or improperly sealed cable glands or conduit entries allowing water ingress over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect all cable glands, conduit entries, and door seals for damage or improper sealing; check for visible corrosion on bus bars, terminals, and breaker frames.
Corrective Action
Reseal or replace failed glands and gaskets, clean and treat corroded contacts or replace affected components, and schedule more frequent inspection for outdoor installations.
Circuit Breaker Within the Box Trips Repeatedly
Likely Cause
Harmonic or inrush currents from downstream loads, a faulty breaker, or an uncoordinated protection scheme between the box and upstream/downstream devices.
Diagnostic Steps
Log load current during trip events to check for inrush/harmonic content; verify the breaker's trip curve and rating against the connected load; review protection coordination with adjacent devices.
Corrective Action
Fit a correctly type-selected and rated breaker (e.g., C-curve or D-curve), and revise the coordination study if trips continue with a correctly rated device.
Box Energised but No Output on One Circuit
Likely Cause
A tripped or failed breaker/fuse for that circuit, a loose downstream connection, or an open-circuit fault in the outgoing cable.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the status of the relevant breaker/fuse and reset if safe to do so; verify voltage at the outgoing terminal; megger the outgoing cable if voltage is present at the terminal but absent downstream.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the breaker/fuse, re-terminate loose connections, or repair the outgoing cable fault before re-energising the circuit.
Enclosure Door or Seal Failure
Likely Cause
Degraded gasket material, corroded hinges/latches, or physical damage from impact or over-tightened hardware.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the door gasket for compression set, cracking, or gaps; check hinge and latch operation and alignment.
Corrective Action
Replace the door gasket and any corroded hardware, and realign the door to restore a full weatherproof seal.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Loose or Under-Torqued Terminal Connection
High Severity
Root Cause
A bus bar or terminal connection left under-torqued at installation, or loosened over time by thermal cycling and vibration.
Effect
Localised heating that can progress to a hot-spot failure or fire risk if left undetected.
Detection Method
Thermal imaging of the box under normal load, and a torque check on bus bar and terminal connections against specification.
Cable Gland / Conduit Seal Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Failed or improperly sealed cable glands or conduit entries allowing water ingress into the enclosure over time.
Effect
Moisture or corrosion found inside the enclosure, risking damage to bus bars, terminals, and breaker frames.
Detection Method
Inspection of all cable glands, conduit entries, and door seals for damage, and a check for visible corrosion on internal components.
Breaker/Load Coordination Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Harmonic or inrush currents from downstream loads, a faulty breaker, or an uncoordinated protection scheme between the box and adjacent devices.
Effect
A circuit breaker within the box trips repeatedly even though the connected load is not genuinely faulted.
Detection Method
Load current logging during trip events, a check of the breaker's trip curve and rating against the load, and a review of protection coordination.
Downstream Open Circuit or Failed Protection Device
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A tripped or failed breaker/fuse for a circuit, a loose downstream connection, or an open-circuit fault in the outgoing cable.
Effect
The box remains energised but one circuit shows no output.
Detection Method
Breaker/fuse status check, voltage verification at the outgoing terminal, and a megger test of the outgoing cable if voltage is present but absent downstream.
Enclosure Door / Seal Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Degraded gasket material, corroded hinges/latches, or physical damage from impact or over-tightened hardware.
Effect
A compromised weatherproof seal at the enclosure door, allowing progressive moisture ingress.
Detection Method
Inspection of the door gasket for compression set or cracking, and a check of hinge and latch operation and alignment.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Upstream Isolation Before Opening
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the incoming supply before opening a distribution box, and verify zero energy at the incoming terminals or busbar before working inside it, even where individual outgoing breakers are already off.

Confirm Outgoing Circuit Before Isolating a Breaker
Operational

Positively identify which outgoing breaker feeds which downstream circuit before switching it, since operating the wrong breaker can leave the intended work area live while de-energising an unrelated circuit.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any work inside the box, verify insulation resistance and correct terminal connections before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip an upstream device or damage connected equipment.

Arc-Flash Awareness
Personnel Safety

Follow the site's arc-flash procedure, including appropriate PPE and safe working distance, when working on or near live busbars in the box, given the fault current available at a main distribution point.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and breaker blanks accounted for at all times when the box is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it.

Check for Thermal Damage at Connections
Personnel Safety

Inspect busbar joints and terminal connections for discolouration or heat damage during maintenance, since a loose or high-resistance connection at a distribution point can generate significant localised heat under load.

Lock Out Before Working on Any Outgoing Circuit
Electrical Safety

Lock out and tag the specific outgoing breaker for the duration of work on its downstream circuit, so it cannot be closed unintentionally by another party while that circuit is being worked on.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a distribution box with an incorrectly rated breaker, an unlabelled circuit, or a loose termination after maintenance; verify correct labelling and torque before closing out the work order.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Re-Verify Discrimination Whenever a Circuit Is Added
Coordination

Adding an outgoing circuit to a distribution box that was originally coordinated as a complete set can quietly break the discrimination between the incoming protection and the other existing circuits; treat any change to the box's circuit list as a trigger to re-check coordination, not just confirm the new circuit trips correctly on its own.

Enclosure Loading Affects Internal Ambient Temperature
Design

A distribution box populated close to its physical capacity runs hotter inside than a lightly loaded one with the same total current, which can push thermally-rated devices into nuisance tripping even when each circuit is individually within its rated current; leave margin in enclosure sizing rather than filling every available way.

Neutral and Earth Bar Terminations Loosen Silently
Maintenance

Neutral and earth bar connections in a distribution box see less routine attention than the breakers themselves, but a loosened termination here can cause intermittent faults across multiple circuits that look unrelated; include torque-checking these bars in the periodic maintenance routine, not just the breaker terminals.

IP Rating Is Only as Good as Gland and Seal Maintenance
Durability

An enclosure's published ingress protection rating assumes glands and door seals remain intact; a gland that wasn't correctly re-fitted after the last cable change, or a door seal that's gone brittle with age, can undermine the box's rating without any visible external sign until moisture actually gets in.

Keep the Circuit Directory Current, Not Just Accurate at Handover
Documentation

A distribution box's circuit directory is often correct at initial commissioning and then drifts out of date as circuits are added, moved, or relabelled over the years; a directory that no longer matches reality turns a routine isolation into a hazard during future maintenance, so update it at the time of any change, not retrospectively.

Keep Spare Ways Genuinely Spare
Field Practice

Spare ways left with live bus bar exposed, rather than blanked or properly isolated, are a common point of confusion and hazard during later work by someone unfamiliar with the box's history; blank unused ways and confirm their isolation status rather than leaving them as an ambiguous "maybe live" space.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about distribution boxes: function, IP rating, protection devices, testing, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a distribution box used for in an AGL system?

A distribution box takes a single incoming power feed from a main switchgear or bus bar and splits it into multiple individually protected and switchable outgoing circuits, such as CCRs, control systems and auxiliary loads. This confines a fault or overload on one circuit to that circuit alone, so the rest of the AGL power network stays live.

Q2What is the difference between a distribution box and a distribution board?

The terms are largely interchangeable in AGL power distribution; "distribution box" and "distribution board" (or sub-distribution panel) both describe an enclosure that receives an incoming supply and feeds multiple individually protected outgoing circuits. Naming often reflects scale and location rather than a functional difference, with larger installations sometimes reserving "board" for main boards and "box" for smaller field or sub-distribution enclosures.

Q3What IP rating should a distribution box have?

The required ingress protection depends on installation environment: sheltered indoor panels are typically rated around IP31, while pole- or wall-mounted units exposed to weather, or those installed in outdoor and buried enclosures, need up to IP66. The rating depends on door gaskets, latches and cable gland seals remaining intact, not just the enclosure's published spec.

Q4What protective devices are used inside a distribution box?

Each outgoing way is protected by its own MCB, MCCB, RCBO or fuse switch, individually rated to the connected load and to the prospective fault current at the point of installation. This lets a single circuit be isolated for maintenance or fault clearance without de-energising the other ways fed from the same bus bar.

Q5How is a distribution box tested and commissioned?

Commissioning includes megger-testing the incoming supply, bus bars and each outgoing way before energisation, torquing bus bar and termination bolts to specification, verifying phase sequence and earth bar continuity, then energising each outgoing way individually to confirm correct protective device operation before all ways are brought into service together. Enclosure IP rating, door seals and the circuit directory are checked at final acceptance.

Q6How often should a distribution box be inspected?

Typical intervals are annual for bus bar and terminal torque checks and cable gland/seal inspection, and semi-annual for thermal imaging of internal connections to catch developing hot spots before they become a fire risk. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence over these general intervals.

Q7Why does a distribution box run hot or show a thermal hot spot?

The most common cause is a loose or under-torqued bus bar or terminal connection, left under-torqued at installation or loosened over time by thermal cycling and vibration. Thermal-image the box under normal load to locate the hot spot, then de-energise and re-torque or replace the affected connection before re-checking with thermal imaging.

Q8Why does a circuit breaker inside a distribution box trip repeatedly?

Repeated tripping is usually caused by harmonic or inrush currents from downstream loads, a faulty breaker, or an uncoordinated protection scheme between the box and adjacent devices. Log load current during trip events, verify the breaker's trip curve and rating against the connected load, and review protection coordination before fitting a correctly type-selected and rated breaker.

Q9Why is there no output on one circuit even though the distribution box is energised?

The likely cause is a tripped or failed breaker/fuse for that circuit, a loose downstream connection, or an open-circuit fault in the outgoing cable. Check the breaker/fuse status and reset if safe, verify voltage at the outgoing terminal, and megger the outgoing cable if voltage is present at the terminal but absent downstream.

Q10What standards apply to distribution boxes used in AGL systems?

The main references are IEC 61439 and BS EN 61439 for low-voltage switchgear and controlgear assemblies (distribution boards), UL 508A for industrial control panels in the USA, and IEC 60529 for the enclosure's IP ingress protection rating. Always verify the current edition and your project's approved specification.
