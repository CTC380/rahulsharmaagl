Runway Light Directionality Comparison

UnidirectionalVSBidirectional

Unidirectional runway lights produce their required light output in one specified direction. Bidirectional runway lights produce their required light output in two opposite directions. The main difference is the number of operational viewing directions served by the fixture — this is not a colour classification and is not the same as omnidirectional.

02 · Unidirectional

Unidirectional Runway Light

A unidirectional light is designed to be seen primarily from one specified direction. From the opposite direction, the fixture does not provide the same intended signal. Its purpose is to provide visual guidance for a particular direction of operation.

Light output is projected toward one required direction only.

  • Single optical system aimed at one required direction
  • Correct aiming is essential — incorrect orientation can leave the beam unseen by approaching aircraft
  • Generally simpler fixture design
  • Used where visual guidance applies only to aircraft moving or approaching from one direction, for example ICAO runway threshold lights (green) and runway end lights (red)
03 · Bidirectional

Bidirectional Runway Light

A bidirectional light provides light output in two opposite directions. Pilots approaching from either operational direction can receive the required indication from the same fixture location.

A single fixture produces compliant output toward two opposite directions.

  • Two optical arrangements within one fixture, aimed at opposite directions
  • Both directional outputs must be correctly aligned during installation
  • Generally more complex than a unidirectional fixture
  • Used where a runway or lighting position must provide information to aircraft operating in opposite directions, from one location, instead of installing two separate fixtures
04 · Main Difference

The Core Distinction

Unidirectional = one viewing direction. Bidirectional = two opposite viewing directions. The distinction is purely about the number of intended operational directions the fixture serves — not brightness, not colour, and not fixture family.

05 · Example: Threshold/End Combination Light

A Clear Bidirectional Example

The runway threshold/end combination light is one of the clearest examples of a bidirectional runway light: green faces aircraft on approach and marks the threshold, while red faces the runway and marks the runway end for aircraft rolling toward that end. The same physical fixture therefore serves two directional functions.

Standards note: ICAO Annex 14 specifies threshold lights (green) and runway end lights (red) as separate unidirectional functions. A combined fitting is a bidirectional fixture that delivers both from one position, which is only possible where the threshold and the runway end coincide.

Bidirectional does not automatically mean two different colours. Depending on the application, both directions may show the same colour (e.g. runway edge lights: white ← light → white) — it simply means the fixture provides specified output in two opposite directions.

06 · In Practice

Where Direction Requirements Come From

The direction and colour required at each position are set by the light's function in the governing standard, not by the fixture family. The table shows how this plays out on a runway in ICAO Annex 14 terms.

Runway light Direction / colour requirement (ICAO Annex 14) Typical fixture arrangement
Runway edgeWhite; visible at the angles in azimuth needed to guide a pilot landing or taking off in either direction, and at all angles where circling guidance is intendedBidirectional (e.g. white/white) or omnidirectional, depending on intensity class and specification
Edge – caution zoneLast 600 m or one-third of the runway length, whichever is less, at the remote end from the take-off start, may show yellowBidirectional white/yellow: the yellow face is seen by aircraft rolling toward that end; the opposite face stays white
Edge – displaced thresholdLights between the runway start and the displaced threshold show red in the approach directionBidirectional red/white
Threshold / wing barFixed unidirectional, green, in the direction of approachUnidirectional green, or the green face of a combined threshold/end fitting
Runway endFixed unidirectional, red, in the direction of the runwayUnidirectional red, or the red face of a combined threshold/end fitting
Runway centrelineWhite; alternating red and white from 900 m to 300 m before the runway end (on runways under 1,800 m, from the mid-point); red in the last 300 mTypically bidirectional in the red/white sections near each end, where the two directions need different colours

FAA equivalent: AC 150/5345-46 lists L-861 runway edge fixtures as omnidirectional or bidirectional, L-862 as bidirectional, and L-861E/L-862E threshold/end fixtures as bidirectional red/green or red/red (or unidirectional green). Check the current edition of each document before specifying.

08 · Typical Applications

Where Each Is Used

Unidirectional

Used where lighting is intended for one operational direction, such as direction-specific visual guidance.

  • Runway threshold and wing bar lights (green)
  • Runway end lights (red)

Bidirectional

Used where aircraft operating in opposite directions require visual guidance from the same fixture location.

  • Threshold/end combination lights (green/red)
  • Runway edge lights (white/white, white/yellow, red/white)
  • Runway centreline lights near the runway ends (different colour each way)

The exact application depends on the lighting system design and governing standard.

09 · Important Clarification

Bidirectional Does Not Mean Omnidirectional

These terms should not be confused with each other.

Unidirectional

One direction only.

Bidirectional

Two opposite directions.

Omnidirectional

All horizontal directions (360° in azimuth).

Omnidirectional is a real fixture category, not just a term to avoid: some medium-intensity runway edge lights and blue taxiway edge lights are omnidirectional. The three terms simply describe how many directions a fixture is designed to serve.

Bidirectional ≠ omnidirectional. A bidirectional fixture serves exactly two opposite directions, not all directions.

10 · Common Misunderstandings

Myths vs Facts

Myth: Bidirectional lights are visible from every angle
Fact

No. Bidirectional refers specifically to two opposite intended directions, not all-around (omnidirectional) visibility.

Myth: Bidirectional always means two different colours
Fact

Not necessarily. Both directions may show the same colour depending on the application — the defining feature is the two directions, not the colours used.

Myth: A unidirectional light emits no light at all behind the fixture
Fact

The specified beam and colour apply only in the intended direction. Some stray light outside that beam can exist, but it is not part of the specified signal and is not what pilots should rely on for guidance.

Myth: Runway edge lights are always bidirectional
Fact

Not always. Depending on intensity class and specification, edge fixtures may be bidirectional or omnidirectional. The requirement is guidance for pilots landing or taking off in either direction.

11 · Engineering Verdict

Unidirectional vs Bidirectional

Engineering Verdict

The choice depends on whether the runway lighting system must provide guidance in one operational direction or in two opposite directions. In practice the governing standard fixes the direction and colour required at each position; the designer then specifies a certified fixture type that delivers it.

UnidirectionalOne intended viewing direction. Simpler fixture, single optical system, correct aiming is essential.
BidirectionalTwo opposite intended viewing directions. More complex fixture, two optical outputs that must both be correctly aligned.

Easy memory: UNI = ONE. BI = TWO. Unidirectional → one way. Bidirectional → two ways (not all ways).

13 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents. This page is an educational reference and does not replace the governing standard or the project specification.

FAA AC 150/5345-46 and AC 150/5340-30

AC 150/5345-46 (Specification for Runway, Taxiway, Heliport and Vertiport Light Fixtures) defines fixture types, light direction (omni-, uni- or bidirectional), colours and photometric requirements. AC 150/5340-30 (Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids) covers layout and installation.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations. Chapter 5 (Visual Aids for Navigation) sets direction, colour and intensity requirements for edge, threshold, runway end and centreline lights; Appendices 1 and 2 give colours and light characteristics. Guidance: Doc 9157, Aerodrome Design Manual, Part 4 (Visual Aids).

DGCA / AAI Requirements

For Indian airport projects, also verify DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I (Aerodrome Design and Operations), AAI requirements, airport-specific technical specifications, project drawings and contract specifications.

Final Engineering Note

Do not assume directionality from colour or fixture family alone. Verify the specified photometric output, intended direction(s), colour requirements and installation orientation against the applicable current governing standard.

Unidirectional → light output in one required direction. Bidirectional → light output in two opposite required directions, not all directions.
