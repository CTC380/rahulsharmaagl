Stop Bar Sensor

A precision detection device that monitors aircraft and vehicle presence at runway holding positions. Stop Bar Sensors are a critical component of Runway Status Light (RWSL) systems, providing real‑time occupancy data to prevent runway incursions and enhance surface safety.

Overview

What Is It?

A Stop Bar Sensor is a compact, high‑reliability detection device installed at runway holding positions (stop bars) to detect the presence of aircraft or vehicles that are approaching, crossing, or occupying the stop bar area. The sensor is a key component of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system, providing the real‑time occupancy data needed to activate runway entrance lights (RELs), take‑off hold lights (THLs), and runway guard lights. Stop Bar Sensors typically use radar, infrared, or inductive loop technology to detect metallic targets with high accuracy and reliability, even in adverse weather conditions. They are designed for harsh airfield environments, with rugged housings, wide temperature tolerance, and lightning protection. The sensor's output (typically a dry contact or digital signal) is fed to the RWSL processing unit, which uses it to determine runway status and control the associated warning lights. By providing precise occupancy detection, Stop Bar Sensors help prevent runway incursions and support safe, efficient ground operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Where a runway occupancy sensor watches an entire runway segment, the stop bar sensor's purpose is narrower and more precise: to detect the exact moment an aircraft or vehicle crosses a specific holding position, the one line on the airfield where a runway incursion actually begins. That point-detection role is what makes it suitable for triggering RELs and THLs at the specific location where entry onto the runway occurs, rather than reporting on runway status more broadly. Its rugged construction and lightning protection reflect the fact that it sits at one of the highest-consequence locations on the airfield, where a missed or false detection has a direct bearing on runway incursion risk rather than being a general monitoring inconvenience.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Stop Bar Sensors are deployed at all runway holding positions where stop bars are installed, and in other critical areas where occupancy detection is required:

  • Runway Holding Positions: At taxiway/runway intersections, sensors detect aircraft waiting to enter the runway, triggering RELs and warning lights.
  • Runway Entrance Lights (REL) Systems: Sensors provide the detection input that activates the RELs when an aircraft crosses the stop bar.
  • Take‑Off Hold Lights (THL) Systems: Sensors detect aircraft at the take‑off position on the runway, activating the THLs to warn other traffic.
  • Runway Guard Light Systems: Sensors trigger flashing amber warning lights when a vehicle or aircraft approaches a runway holding position.
  • RWSL (Runway Status Light) Systems: As a primary input sensor, providing accurate occupancy data to the RWSL logic processor.
  • A‑SMGCS (Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control Systems): Integrating with A‑SMGCS for enhanced surface surveillance and conflict detection.
  • Apron and Gate Areas: Detecting aircraft or vehicles at gate positions for ground handling coordination.
  • Vehicle Access Points: Monitoring vehicle crossings of taxiways or runways to ensure safety.
Placement

Installation & Location

Sensors are set into or beside the pavement at the runway holding position, either as an inductive loop cut into a saw-cut slot in the taxiway surface or as a surface-mounted microwave/infrared head on a short frangible post clear of wingtip clearance. Loop sensors are sealed into their slot with a bitumen or epoxy compound and home-run in duct back to the local field cabinet, while surface-mounted heads are aligned so their detection zone covers the full stop bar width without triggering on adjacent lanes. Detector output cabling is kept on its own screened pair back to the REL/THL controller or Remote I/O rather than sharing conduit with lighting series-circuit cable, since a nuisance trip on this input directly drives a runway safety light.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Stop Bar Sensor uses a combination of detection technologies to provide reliable target presence indication. Key functional aspects include:

  • Detection Technologies: Sensors typically use one or a combination of:
    • Radar (FMCW or pulsed): Detects moving targets and provides range and velocity information, immune to weather conditions.
    • Infrared (active or passive): Uses thermal or reflected IR to detect targets, effective in darkness and fog.
    • Inductive Loop: Buried wire loops detect changes in inductance when a metallic target passes over; simple and reliable.
  • Target Classification: Advanced sensors can differentiate between aircraft and vehicles, or ignore small objects (birds, debris) to reduce false alarms.
  • Coverage Zone: The sensor is positioned to cover the stop bar area, typically a zone 5‑10 metres wide and 1‑3 metres deep, ensuring that any aircraft or vehicle crossing the stop bar is detected.
  • Output Signal: The sensor provides a clean, isolated output—typically a dry contact (relay) or transistor output—that changes state when a target is detected. The output is connected to the RWSL logic processor or PLC.
  • Self‑Diagnostics: The sensor monitors its own health (e.g., power supply, signal quality, internal faults) and provides a diagnostic output (or fault indicator) to alert maintenance staff.
  • Environmental Protection: The sensor is housed in a rugged, IP65/67 enclosure, with weatherproof connectors and surge protection for lightning‑prone airfield environments.

The sensor is typically mounted on a small pole (0.5‑1.5 m high) adjacent to the stop bar, or embedded in the pavement (inductive loops). Its position and orientation are carefully calibrated to ensure it only detects targets that are actually within the stop bar zone, avoiding false triggers from adjacent taxiways or apron areas.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Stop Bar Sensor is a detection input at the edge of the control system rather than a controller itself — it reports a target's presence to the RWSL logic or ALCMS server, typically via a Remote I/O unit or directly wired into the local PLC, and takes no action on the stop bar or runway status lights on its own. That upstream logic combines the sensor's detection with the current runway/taxiway occupancy state and traffic clearances to decide whether the stop bar should be illuminated, and issues the corresponding command back down to the CCR controlling that circuit. Because the sensor's output is only ever an input to that decision, a sensor fault is treated as a loss of detection at that specific location — the system typically fails toward the conservative state (stop bar lit) rather than assuming clear, and the fault is reported to the ALCMS as a sensor-level alarm distinct from a lighting-circuit fault.

Construction

Physical Construction

The sensor is built into a compact, sealed housing — die-cast aluminium or glass-reinforced polymer — rated IP65/IP67, small enough to mount on a short pole beside the stop bar or flush into the pavement where inductive loop technology is used instead. A radome or optical window covers the radar or IR aperture, transparent to the sensing wavelength while shedding rain, dust, and de-icing fluid, and pole-mounted units include an adjustable bracket for setting detection angle during commissioning. Internal electronics are conformally coated and connected through sealed M12 or similar circular connectors rather than open terminals, keeping moisture out of the detection and output circuitry. Built-in transient voltage suppression is mounted near the cable entry to protect against lightning-induced surges, and the compact housing dimensions keep the unit unobtrusive at the holding position while still meeting the IP and temperature ratings needed for continuous outdoor exposure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

False Detections from Adjacent Traffic

Radar and IR sensors may detect aircraft or vehicles on adjacent taxiways if the field of view is too wide. Careful alignment and, where possible, use of narrow beamwidth and range‑gating can mitigate this. Regular calibration is essential.

Sensor Obscuration (Radar/IR)

Snow, ice, heavy rain, or dirt accumulation on the sensor window can reduce detection performance. Regular cleaning and the use of heated housings or wipers can prevent obscuration.

Inductive Loop Damage

Buried inductive loops can be damaged by pavement resurfacing, ground movement, or heavy aircraft loads. Periodic loop testing (using a loop tester) is required to detect open or short circuits.

Interference from RF Sources

Radar sensors operating at 24 GHz can experience interference from other nearby radar systems (e.g., SMR, weather radar) or communication transmitters. Frequency coordination and filtering are necessary.

Power Supply Instability

Voltage fluctuations or spikes can cause sensor resets or damage. Use a regulated power supply and surge protection devices to ensure reliable operation.

Alignment Drift

Over time, sensor mounts can shift due to vibration or thermal expansion, altering the detection zone. Regular site surveys and alignment checks are recommended.

Loop Detector Not Registering / Stuck Output
Likely Cause
Buried inductive loop damaged by resurfacing, ground movement, or heavy aircraft loads.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the loop with a loop tester for an open or short circuit and inspect accessible loop wiring and sealant at the saw-cut.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged loop, reseal the saw-cuts, and retest before returning to service.
Erratic Readings Near Other Radar Systems
Likely Cause
RF interference from other 24 GHz radar systems (SMR, weather radar) or nearby transmitters.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for correlation between erratic readings and nearby radar/transmitter activity, and review frequency coordination records.
Corrective Action
Apply filtering, coordinate the frequency assignment, and relocate or shield the sensor if interference persists.
Detection Zone Drifted from Design Boundary
Likely Cause
Alignment drift from vibration or thermal expansion of the sensor mount.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a site survey using calibration targets and compare the current alignment to commissioning records.
Corrective Action
Re-align the sensor to the design boundary, secure the mounting hardware, and schedule periodic re-verification.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise the sensor's 24VDC (or 12VDC) supply from the local field distribution/control cabinet and verify zero energy before opening the housing or disconnecting cable.

Safety-Critical Function — Coordinate Before Isolating
Operational

The sensor feeds real-time occupancy data to the RWSL system that activates runway entrance and take-off hold lights; taking it out of service removes that protection, so coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before isolation, and confirm an interim procedure is in place for the affected stop bar.

Pavement/Embedment Work Coordination
Operational

Excavating or re-setting an embedded or inductive-loop sensor requires ATC/airside coordination, a NOTAM, and pavement closure, with the surrounding surface reinstated to the required load rating before reopening.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and excavated material accounted for at all times when the sensor or its housing is open, and clear the area of debris before reinstating the surface.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Test the signal and power cabling for insulation integrity after any cable or connector work before restoring power, since a residual fault can produce a false occupancy indication rather than an obvious failure.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify correct detection and a clean occupancy signal to the RWSL logic before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A False-Clear Is More Dangerous Than a False-Occupied
Design

A sensor that fails to a "no occupancy" state on fault is a much worse outcome for RWSL logic than one that fails to "occupied," since the latter simply keeps hold lights on unnecessarily while the former can suppress a genuine warning; confirm the fail-safe direction of a replacement sensor matches the original before commissioning it.

Nuisance Trips Often Trace to Ground Loop Interference
Troubleshooting

Intermittent false detections on an inductive-loop sensor are frequently caused by electrical noise coupling from adjacent series circuits rather than a fault in the sensor itself; check loop screening and separation from AGL power cabling before replacing hardware on a recurring nuisance-trip complaint.

Pavement Overlay Work Changes Loop Depth
Maintenance

A resurfacing or overlay project that adds pavement thickness above an embedded inductive loop can change its detection sensitivity even though the loop itself wasn't touched; re-verify detection performance after any pavement work in the vicinity, not just after work that directly disturbs the sensor.

Verify Against Real Traffic, Not Just a Bench Test
Verification

A sensor can pass a static bench or simulated-target test while still under-detecting a specific aircraft type's gear geometry or approach speed in service; where practical, confirm detection against actual traffic during commissioning rather than relying solely on a synthetic test signal.

Response Latency Matters as Much as Detection Accuracy
Verification

A sensor that correctly detects occupancy but reports it to the RWSL logic with excessive latency can still leave a hold light illuminating too late relative to the actual hazard; verify end-to-end response time during commissioning, not just detection accuracy in isolation.

Log Every Interim Procedure Used During Isolation
Operational

When a sensor is taken out of service and an interim manual procedure covers the affected stop bar, document exactly what that interim procedure was and confirm it was formally stood down when the sensor was restored; an interim procedure that quietly persists after restoration is a latent operational risk.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about stop bar sensors: detection technology, placement, electrical requirements, standards and troubleshooting.

Q1What is a stop bar sensor?

A stop bar sensor is a compact detection device installed at runway holding positions to detect aircraft or vehicles approaching, crossing or occupying the stop bar area. It feeds the runway safety and stop bar control chain.

Q2How is it different from a runway occupancy sensor?

A runway occupancy sensor watches an entire runway segment. A stop bar sensor is narrower and more precise: it detects the exact moment a target crosses one specific holding position.

Q3Where are stop bar sensors installed?

At runway holding positions where stop bars are installed, such as taxiway and runway intersections, and in other critical areas where occupancy detection is required.

Q4What detection technologies do they use?

FMCW radar (typically 24 GHz), active or passive infrared, or inductive loops, alone or in combination.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

Radar or IR range of 2-15 m (1-3 m for inductive loops), detection speed 0.5-50 km/h, response time of 100 ms or less, dry-contact or opto-isolated 24 VDC output, IP65/IP67 protection and -30°C to +70°C operating temperature.

Q6How is a stop bar sensor powered and protected?

It runs on 24 VDC (18-32 VDC) or 12 VDC from the local cabinet, drawing 2-10 W for radar or IR. Screened cable, built-in transient suppression up to 6 kV, an external SPD on long runs, and earth bonding of the housing and cable screen are typical.

Q7Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14 (runway holding position and safety system requirements), FAA AC 150/5345-56 (L-890 ALCMS) and EUROCAE ED-87 (MASPS for A-SMGCS).

Q8How often should a stop bar sensor be maintained?

Quarterly lens or radome cleaning; semi-annual detection accuracy and alignment checks; and annual power supply stability checks and, for inductive loops, loop continuity and resistance tests.

Q9Why is a stop bar sensor giving false detections?

A common cause is a field of view too wide for the stop bar zone, picking up adjacent taxiway or vehicle traffic. Review alarm logs against known movements and check the beamwidth and range-gate settings.
