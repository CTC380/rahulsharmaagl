Runway Edge Lighting Requirements as per ICAO Annex 14: A Practical Guide for AGL Engineers

Every aircraft landing appears smooth to passengers. What they don't see is the carefully engineered lighting system guiding the aircraft from touchdown to rollout — and runway edge lights are one of the most fundamental parts of it.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

Published July 6, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

Among all Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) systems, Runway Edge Lights are one of the most fundamental visual aids. They define the lateral limits of the runway, provide alignment cues during take-off and landing, and become especially critical during night operations and reduced visibility.

See also our complete guide to runway lighting systems.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I (section 5.3.9, with Appendices 1 and 2 for colour and photometry) specifies detailed engineering requirements for these lights. Annex 14 contains Standards (“shall”) and Recommended Practices (“should”), and each State applies them through its own regulation — in India, through DGCA aerodrome requirements — so always confirm the national rule and any filed differences. Let's understand these requirements from an engineering perspective.

Key Takeaway

Runway edge lights are fixed, variable-white lights defining the lateral limits of the runway — red between the runway start and a displaced threshold (as seen on approach) and yellow over the final caution zone towards the runway end. ICAO Annex 14 governs their spacing, colour transitions, and photometric performance, and every one of those parameters exists to preserve a pilot's judgment of runway width and remaining distance.

01

What Are Runway Edge Lights?

Runway Edge Lights are white lights installed along both sides of a runway. Their primary purpose is to:

  • Define runway boundaries
  • Assist pilots in maintaining runway alignment
  • Improve depth perception during landing
  • Support aircraft rollout after touchdown
  • Provide guidance during take-off

Without these lights, pilots would struggle to judge runway width and position during night operations.

02

When Are Runway Edge Lights Required?

According to ICAO Annex 14, runway edge lights shall be provided for:

  • Runways intended for night operations
  • Precision approach runways intended for day or night use

Annex 14 also provides for runway edge lights on runways intended for take-off in low visibility (operating minima of the order of RVR 800 m or less by day). Check whether your Annex 14 edition and national regulation treat this as a Standard or a Recommended Practice.

03

Location Requirements

One of the most important installation requirements is the location. ICAO specifies that runway edge lights shall:

  • Be fixed (steady-burning) lights, shown at all azimuth angles needed by a pilot landing or taking off in either direction
  • Extend along the full runway length
  • Be installed in two parallel rows
  • Be equally spaced from the runway centreline
  • Be located along the edges of the area declared for use as the runway, or outside those edges by not more than 3 metres

Incorrect positioning can distort the pilot's perception of runway width — for AGL engineers, accurate physical installation is just as important as selecting the correct lighting equipment.

04

Spacing Requirements

Uniform spacing is essential for continuous visual guidance. ICAO specifies the following maximum spacing:

Instrument RunwaysMaximum spacing: 60 metres
Non-Instrument RunwaysMaximum spacing: 100 metres

Lights are uniformly spaced in each row, and lights on opposite sides sit on lines at right angles to the runway axis. At runway intersections, lights may be spaced irregularly or omitted, provided adequate guidance remains available to the pilot. These are maximum spacings — a closer spacing is a design choice and is common on precision runways.

05

Colour Requirements

Although pilots generally see white runway edge lights, there are important exceptions along the runway length:

Runway ZoneColourPurpose
Normal SectionVariable White (fixed)Standard lateral edge guidance; intensity adjustable by brightness step
Runway Start to Displaced Threshold (where a displaced threshold exists)Red, as seen by an aircraft approachingSignals that this pavement is not available for landing
Last 600 m or one-third of runway length, whichever is less, at the far end in the direction of take-off or landingYellowCaution zone: the runway end is approaching

The yellow zone is defined relative to the direction of operation, so on a bi-directional runway each end has its own caution zone. Edge fittings are therefore commonly dual-coloured or bi-directional: the same position can show red or white one way and white or yellow the other. This transition gives valuable situational awareness during landing and rejected take-off.

06

Photometric Requirements

A runway light is much more than a bulb. Each fitting must meet strict photometric requirements:

Beam SpreadCorrect cone of light
Light IntensityCandela output for the category
ChromaticityColour within the Annex 14 Appendix 1 limits
Vertical DistributionIntensity profile by elevation angle
Horizontal DistributionIntensity profile across azimuth
UniformityConsistency fitting to fitting

For precision approach runways, Annex 14 Appendix 2 specifies dedicated isocandela distributions, with different diagrams depending on runway width, so pilots receive the correct visual cues on approach. Measured performance is judged against the isocandela diagram, not by a single candela figure.

07

Intensity Requirements

Intensity must be adequate for the conditions of visibility and ambient light in which the runway is intended to be used. For runways without precision-approach requirements, Annex 14 gives a minimum main-beam intensity of 50 candela, which may be reduced to not less than 25 candela at an aerodrome without extraneous lighting, to avoid dazzling pilots. For precision approach runways, the Appendix 2 isocandela diagrams (Figures A2-9 and A2-10, depending on runway width) require a much higher main-beam intensity, of the order of 10,000 candela average for white light (confirm the figure in your edition). FAA edge-light classes (L-860, L-861, L-862) carry their own intensity requirements, which should not be mixed with the ICAO values.

Modern LED systems typically support multiple brightness steps controlled by the Constant Current Regulator (CCR), allowing air traffic control to adjust intensity according to visibility — making the interaction between the field lighting system and the airport electrical/control infrastructure an important design and commissioning consideration.

08

Engineering Considerations During Installation

From field experience, compliance involves much more than installing fittings. Engineers should verify:

Correct spacing
Proper elevation
Fixture orientation
Beam alignment
Frangibility
Circuit continuity
Isolating transformer connections
Connector integrity (e.g. FAA L-823 type)
Earthing
CCR compatibility
Photometric performance
Correct colour and facing in red and yellow zones

Even small installation errors can significantly reduce lighting effectiveness. A correctly selected fixture can still perform poorly if it is incorrectly positioned, tilted, misaligned, or connected improperly.

09

Common Field Issues

During inspections and maintenance, the following issues are frequently encountered:

Incorrect spacing after runway rehabilitation
Tilted fittings causing beam misalignment
Reduced light output from dirty optical lenses
Incorrect colour filters
Red or yellow zones not updated after a displaced-threshold or runway-length change
Damaged frangible couplings
Water ingress
Cable insulation failures
Isolating transformer faults
Photometric non-compliance

Routine preventive maintenance helps identify these issues before they affect operations — for safety-critical AGL systems, identifying a problem during inspection is far better than discovering it during an operational requirement.

10

Key Takeaways

  • Runway edge lights are fixed variable-white lights in two parallel rows, on the declared runway edge or not more than 3 m outside it
  • Maximum spacing is 60m for instrument runways and 100m for non-instrument runways
  • Red between the runway start and a displaced threshold (approach direction) and yellow over the last 600 m or one-third of the runway, whichever is less, give pilots additional situational cues
  • Minimum main-beam intensity is 50 candela on runways without precision-approach requirements (reducible to not less than 25 candela at aerodromes without extraneous lighting); precision approach runways need far higher values from the Appendix 2 diagrams
  • CCR brightness steps let ATC adjust intensity to visibility — the field system and airport electrical infrastructure must be designed together
  • Installation accuracy — spacing, elevation, orientation, beam alignment, earthing, connector integrity — determines whether a compliant fixture actually performs as designed

Final Thoughts

Runway Edge Lighting is one of the simplest AGL systems in appearance, yet one of the most safety-critical in operation. Behind every light lies careful engineering involving electrical design, photometric compliance, precise installation, maintenance planning, and adherence to ICAO Annex 14 as applied through your State’s regulations.

For pilots, runway edge lights provide continuous information about alignment, runway width, distance remaining, and safe landing path. The effectiveness of these cues depends on the complete system — not simply the light fitting itself. Electrical performance, installation accuracy, photometry, maintenance, and control all contribute to the final visual guidance available to the pilot.

As airports continue adopting LED technology, advanced monitoring systems, and smart airfield solutions, the fundamental objective remains unchanged: provide pilots with clear, reliable, and unmistakable visual guidance for every take-off and landing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are runway edge lights and what are they used for?

Runway edge lights are lights installed along both sides of a runway to define its lateral limits. They help pilots maintain runway alignment, improve depth perception during landing, support aircraft rollout after touchdown and provide guidance during take-off, especially at night and in reduced visibility.

When does ICAO Annex 14 require runway edge lights?

Annex 14 requires runway edge lights on runways intended for night operations and on precision approach runways intended for day or night use. It also provides for them on runways intended for take-off in low visibility, with operating minima of the order of RVR 800 m or less by day. Check whether your Annex 14 edition and national regulation treat this as a Standard or a Recommended Practice.

Where must runway edge lights be located?

They must be fixed (steady-burning) lights extending along the full runway length in two parallel rows, equally spaced from the runway centreline. They are located along the edges of the area declared for use as the runway, or outside those edges by not more than 3 metres.

What is the maximum spacing for runway edge lights?

The maximum spacing is 60 metres on instrument runways and 100 metres on non-instrument runways, with lights uniformly spaced in each row. These are maximum values, so closer spacing is a design choice and is common on precision runways.

What colours do runway edge lights show?

Normally they show variable white. They show red between the runway start and a displaced threshold, as seen by an approaching aircraft, and yellow over the last 600 m or one-third of the runway length, whichever is less, in the direction of operation. Edge fittings are therefore commonly dual-coloured or bi-directional.

What intensity do runway edge lights need?

For runways without precision-approach requirements, Annex 14 gives a minimum main-beam intensity of 50 candela, reducible to not less than 25 candela at an aerodrome without extraneous lighting. Precision approach runways need much higher values from the Appendix 2 isocandela diagrams, of the order of 10,000 candela average for white light, so confirm the figure in your edition. FAA edge-light classes carry their own requirements and should not be mixed with ICAO values.

What should engineers verify when installing runway edge lights?

Compliance involves more than installing fittings. Engineers should verify spacing, elevation, fixture orientation, beam alignment, frangibility, circuit continuity, isolating transformer connections, connector integrity, earthing, CCR compatibility, photometric performance, and correct colour and facing in the red and yellow zones. Frequent field issues include incorrect spacing after runway rehabilitation, tilted fittings that misalign the beam, isolating transformer faults and photometric non-compliance.

Does ICAO Annex 14 apply directly at an aerodrome in India?

Annex 14 contains Standards ("shall") and Recommended Practices ("should"), and each State applies them through its own regulation. In India this is done through DGCA aerodrome requirements, so always confirm the national rule and any filed differences.
