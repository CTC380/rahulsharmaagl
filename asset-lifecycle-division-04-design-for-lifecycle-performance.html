DFL-01 Design-for-Lifecycle Principles
Purpose

Design-for-lifecycle means the design team considers the entire expected life of the AGL asset, rather than focusing only on construction and commissioning.

The Design Should Consider
  • Initial performance
  • Expected service life
  • Reliability
  • Maintainability
  • Accessibility
  • Replacement
  • Spare parts
  • Environmental exposure
  • Energy consumption
  • Future expansion
  • Obsolescence
  • Lifecycle cost
  • Safety
  • Operational risk
Design Lifecycle Sequence
DESIGN REQUIREMENTASSET SELECTIONINSTALLATIONCOMMISSIONINGOPERATIONMAINTENANCECONDITION MONITORINGREPAIR/REFURBISH/UPGRADEREPLACEMENTDECOMMISSIONING
Basic Principle

Design the AGL system not only to work on Day 1, but to remain supportable and reliable throughout its intended lifecycle.

DFL-02 Design Service Life
Purpose

Design service life is the intended period during which an asset is expected to provide its required function when properly operated and maintained.

Design Documentation Should Establish
  • Intended service life
  • Design assumptions
  • Operating conditions
  • Environmental conditions
  • Expected duty cycle
  • Maintenance assumptions
  • Replacement assumptions
  • Critical components
  • Expected degradation mechanisms
Examples of Lifecycle Considerations

AGL fixture: Optical performance, electrical components, housing, sealing, mechanical components, environmental exposure.

CCR: Power electronics, control system, cooling, switching components, internal components, software/controls.

Register Link

The expected service life should subsequently become an asset-register field. Expected Service Life is a required field in the Standard Asset Record.

DFL-03 Maintainability in Design
Purpose

Maintainability means designing equipment so that required inspection, maintenance, testing and repair can be performed effectively.

Design Should Consider
  • Ease of access
  • Safe working space
  • Component accessibility
  • Test-point accessibility
  • Cable access
  • Connector access
  • Fastener accessibility
  • Component replacement
  • Diagnostic capability
  • Isolation arrangements
  • Maintenance clearances
  • Documentation availability
AGL Examples

CCR: Design should allow technicians to safely access relevant internal components, testing points and controls.

Base Can: The design should consider access to the fixture, transformer, connectors and associated wiring.

ALCMS: The design should consider access to servers, network equipment, interfaces and diagnostic information.

DFL-04 Reliability in Design
Purpose

Reliability in design means selecting and configuring assets so that they can perform their required function with an appropriate level of reliability.

Reliability Considerations
  • Proven technology
  • Appropriate component ratings
  • Environmental suitability
  • Thermal management
  • Electrical protection
  • Component quality
  • Redundancy where justified
  • Failure-mode considerations
  • Maintainability
  • Vendor support
  • Spare availability
  • Obsolescence risk
Design Objective

The design should reduce: failure frequency, unplanned downtime, repeat failures, difficult repairs and single points of failure.

Downstream Link

Reliability engineering later becomes a lifecycle function including MTBF, MTTR, failure-rate analysis and reliability improvement.

DFL-05 Accessibility
Purpose

Accessibility concerns how easily personnel can safely reach an asset for inspection, testing, maintenance and replacement.

Design Should Consider
  • Physical access
  • Working clearances
  • Equipment-room access
  • Base-can access
  • Cable-route access
  • Handhole access
  • Test-point access
  • Lighting
  • Safe isolation
  • Removal path
  • Maintenance equipment access
Poor Accessibility Can Result In
  • Longer maintenance time
  • Increased safety risk
  • Higher maintenance cost
  • Delayed restoration
  • Increased downtime
  • Difficulty performing inspections
Principle

An asset that cannot be safely accessed cannot be effectively maintained.

DFL-06 Replaceability
Purpose

Replaceability means designing assets so that failed or obsolete components can be replaced without unnecessary disruption.

Design Should Consider
  • Component removal
  • Equipment lifting
  • Fasteners
  • Connectors
  • Cable termination
  • Physical clearances
  • Isolation
  • Replacement access
  • Compatibility of replacement components
  • Future product availability
Example

A fixture may have excellent initial performance but poor lifecycle performance if replacing the internal component requires complete infrastructure reconstruction. Replaceability should therefore be considered during design rather than discovered during maintenance.

DFL-07 Standardization
Purpose

Standardization reduces unnecessary equipment variety across the airport.

Standardization Can Cover
  • Equipment types
  • Models
  • Components
  • Connectors
  • Cables
  • Transformers
  • CCR configurations
  • Control interfaces
  • Spare parts
  • Testing arrangements
  • Documentation
Benefits

Easier maintenance, reduced training requirements, fewer spare types, easier procurement, better interchangeability, lower lifecycle cost, and simplified troubleshooting.

Limit

Standardization should not override safety, technical suitability or applicable requirements.

DFL-08 Interchangeability
Purpose

Interchangeability means that an approved replacement or equivalent component can be used without unnecessary redesign.

Design Should Consider
  • Mechanical compatibility
  • Electrical compatibility
  • Optical compatibility
  • Control compatibility
  • Connector compatibility
  • Physical dimensions
  • Performance requirements
  • Approved alternatives
Example

If multiple runway fixtures use standardized components, the airport may be able to maintain a smaller and more effective spare-parts inventory. Interchangeability also supports lifecycle resilience when an original manufacturer stops supplying a product.

DFL-09 Spare-Parts Considerations
Purpose

Spare-parts requirements should be considered during design.

Design-Stage Spare Assessment Should Identify
  • Critical components
  • Long-lead items
  • Manufacturer-specific components
  • High-failure components
  • Strategic spares
  • Emergency spares
  • Consumable parts
  • Obsolescence-sensitive parts
  • Interchangeable alternatives
Questions
  • Can the component be procured locally?
  • What is the lead time?
  • Is a manufacturer-specific part required?
  • Is an alternative approved?
  • Is the part likely to become obsolete?
  • What quantity should be held?
Downstream Link

This connects directly to Division 13 — Spare Parts & Inventory Lifecycle, which covers critical spares, stock levels, long-lead items, strategic spares and inventory optimization.

DFL-10 Environmental Considerations
Purpose

AGL assets operate in outdoor and electrically demanding environments. Design should consider the environmental conditions relevant to the airport and installation.

Potential Factors
  • Temperature
  • Humidity
  • Rain
  • Moisture ingress
  • Dust
  • Corrosion
  • UV exposure
  • Drainage
  • Flooding
  • Mechanical loading
  • Chemical exposure
  • Coastal/environmental exposure where applicable
Design Implications

May influence material selection, enclosures, sealing, corrosion protection, cable selection, equipment location, drainage, foundation design and maintenance requirements.

DFL-11 Energy Efficiency
Purpose

Lifecycle design should consider energy consumption throughout the asset's operating life.

Consider
  • Equipment efficiency
  • Lighting technology
  • CCR efficiency
  • Control strategies
  • Operating hours
  • Dimming/brightness control
  • Standby consumption
  • Losses
  • System configuration
  • Future technology upgrades
Downstream Link

Energy should be considered as a lifecycle cost, not simply as an installation consideration. Energy consumption and performance trends later appear under Operations & Asset Performance, and energy cost under Lifecycle Cost Management.

DFL-12 Future Expansion
Purpose

AGL designs should consider foreseeable airport expansion or modification.

Design Should Consider
  • Additional runway requirements
  • Taxiway expansion
  • Additional circuits
  • Additional fixtures
  • Future signs
  • Additional CCR capacity
  • Cable-route capacity
  • Duct capacity
  • Spare ways
  • Control-system expansion
  • Network capacity
  • Equipment-room capacity
Objective

Avoid designing an installation that meets today's requirement but makes tomorrow's expansion unnecessarily expensive.

DFL-13 Obsolescence Considerations
Purpose

Obsolescence risk should be assessed during design.

Potential Obsolescence Risks
  • Manufacturer discontinuation
  • Product discontinuation
  • Component discontinuation
  • Electronic component obsolescence
  • Software obsolescence
  • Communication-protocol obsolescence
  • Legacy control systems
  • Vendor dependency
  • Lack of spare parts
Design Questions
  • Is the technology still actively supported?
  • How long will spare parts be available?
  • Is there a documented upgrade path?
  • Are alternative products available?
  • Can the system be migrated later?
  • Does the manufacturer provide lifecycle support?
Downstream Link

This links Division 04 to Division 18 — Obsolescence Management, which includes product discontinuation, spare-part obsolescence, technology/software obsolescence, alternative-product evaluation and replacement planning.

DFL-14 Lifecycle-Cost Considerations
Purpose

Design decisions should consider total lifecycle cost, not only initial procurement price.

Lifecycle Cost Can Include
DESIGNPROCUREMENTINSTALLATIONCOMMISSIONINGENERGYINSPECTIONTESTINGMAINTENANCESPARE PARTSFAILUREDOWNTIMEREHABILITATIONUPGRADEREPLACEMENTDISPOSAL

= Lifecycle Cost. Lifecycle-cost management is established later in the guide and includes each of these cost categories.

Design Decision Example

A cheaper product should not automatically be selected if it has: shorter service life, higher failure rate, higher energy consumption, higher maintenance cost, poor spare availability, or higher replacement cost.

DFL-15 Maintainability Review
Purpose

A formal maintainability review should be conducted during design.

Review Questions
  • Can the asset be safely accessed?
  • Can routine inspection be performed?
  • Can testing be performed?
  • Can failed components be replaced?
  • Are test points available?
  • Are isolation arrangements adequate?
  • Are maintenance clearances sufficient?
  • Are spare parts available?
  • Are maintenance tools available?
  • Is technical documentation adequate?
Review Output — Design Team Should Record
  • Finding
  • Risk
  • Required action
  • Responsible person
  • Due date
  • Closure status
DFL-16 Reliability Review
Purpose

The reliability review evaluates whether the design provides an appropriate level of reliability.

Review Areas
  • Failure modes
  • Critical components
  • Single points of failure
  • Redundancy
  • Component quality
  • Environmental suitability
  • Protection
  • Cooling/thermal considerations
  • Vendor reliability
  • Historical failure information
  • Maintainability
  • Spare availability
Reliability Review Should Ask

What are the most credible ways this system can fail, and what has the design done to reduce the probability or consequence of those failures?

Downstream Link

This creates an important connection with the AGL Failure Database and Reliability Engineering functions.

DFL-17 Design Lifecycle Assessment
Purpose

The design lifecycle assessment evaluates the proposed design against its expected lifecycle.

Assessment Should Consider

Technical: Performance, reliability, maintainability, service life.

Operational: Availability, accessibility, operational continuity.

Financial: Acquisition cost, maintenance cost, energy cost, replacement cost, lifecycle cost.

Strategic: Standardization, interchangeability, expansion, technology, obsolescence.

Risk: Safety, operational, regulatory, supply-chain risk.

Output

A design should receive an overall lifecycle assessment before final approval.

DFL-19 Design-Stage Asset Strategy
Purpose

This is the final and most important topic of Division 04. Before the design is finalized, every major asset should have a preliminary lifecycle strategy.

Strategy Should Establish
  • Asset type
  • Expected service life
  • Criticality
  • Reliability expectations
  • Maintenance philosophy
  • Inspection requirements
  • Testing requirements
  • Spare-parts strategy
  • Replacement philosophy
  • Upgrade potential
  • Obsolescence risk
  • Lifecycle-cost expectation
  • Future expansion requirements
  • Documentation requirements
Example — CCR

Define required capacity; establish expected service life; assess criticality; evaluate redundancy; specify maintainability requirements; establish spare-module requirements; assess manufacturer support; assess technology lifecycle; define testing requirements; define replacement strategy; establish lifecycle-cost assumptions.

What Happens Next

The resulting strategy should then follow the asset through procurement, installation, commissioning and operation.

Division 04 — Core Principle

AGL assets should be designed for their entire intended lifecycle — not merely for initial installation and commissioning.

The design decision should consider: Performance + Service Life + Reliability + Maintainability + Accessibility + Replaceability + Standardization + Spares + Environment + Energy + Expansion + Obsolescence + Lifecycle Cost + Risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Design for Lifecycle Performance in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 04 — Design for Lifecycle Performance in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 04 is the design-stage bridge between the Asset Lifecycle Guide and the AGL Design Manual. It covers 19 topics on design-for-lifecycle principles, service life, maintainability, reliability, accessibility, replaceability, standardization, interchangeability, spares, environment, energy, expansion, obsolescence, lifecycle cost, reviews, assessments and design-stage asset strategy.

Q2What does design-for-lifecycle mean for AGL systems?

It means the design team considers the entire expected life of the AGL asset rather than focusing only on construction and commissioning. It covers initial performance, expected service life, reliability, maintainability, accessibility and replacement.

Q3What is design service life?

Design service life is the intended period during which an asset is expected to provide its required function when properly operated and maintained. It rests on stated design assumptions, operating conditions, environmental conditions and expected duty cycle.

Q4How do maintainability and accessibility affect AGL design?

Maintainability means designing equipment so inspection, maintenance, testing and repair can be performed effectively, with ease of access, safe working space and accessible components and test points. Accessibility concerns how easily personnel can safely reach the asset, including equipment rooms, base cans, cable routes and handholes.

Q5What is the difference between standardization and interchangeability?

Standardization reduces unnecessary variety in equipment types, models, components, connectors, cables and transformers across the airport. Interchangeability means an approved replacement or equivalent component can be used without unnecessary redesign, covering mechanical, electrical, optical, control and connector compatibility.

Q6Which environmental, energy, expansion and obsolescence factors should design consider?

AGL assets operate in outdoor and electrically demanding conditions, so design considers temperature, humidity, rain, moisture ingress, dust and corrosion. It also considers energy consumption over the operating life, foreseeable expansion, and obsolescence risks such as manufacturer, product and component discontinuation.

Q7What design reviews does Division 04 require?

It calls for a maintainability review (can the asset be safely accessed, inspected, tested and repaired), a reliability review (failure modes, critical components, single points of failure, redundancy), a design lifecycle assessment and a design risk assessment covering safety, operational, technical, reliability, maintainability and supply-chain risk.

Q8What is a design-stage asset strategy?

It is described as the final and most important topic of Division 04. Before the design is finalized, every major asset should have a preliminary lifecycle strategy covering items such as asset type, expected service life, criticality, reliability expectations, maintenance philosophy and inspection requirements.
