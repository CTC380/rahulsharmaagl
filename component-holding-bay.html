Holding Bay

A designated area on the taxiway where aircraft can hold and wait for clearance before entering an active runway or crossing another taxiway. Equipped with holding position markings, lights (stop bars or runway guard lights), and signs to ensure safe and orderly ground traffic flow, particularly in low‑visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Holding Bay (also known as a holding point or holding position) is a defined area on an airfield taxiway where aircraft are required to stop and hold pending clearance from Air Traffic Control (ATC) to proceed. Holding bays are typically located adjacent to runway entry points, at runway intersections, or at points where taxiways cross other active taxiways. They are critical for managing the flow of ground traffic and preventing runway incursions.

Key characteristics of a holding bay include:

  • Holding Position Markings: A yellow painted pattern — under both FAA and ICAO convention — across the taxiway that indicates the exact point where aircraft must stop (FAA: four yellow lines, two solid and two dashed, with the solid lines on the holding side; ICAO uses a similar dual-line or "ladder" pattern, also in yellow). The red/white colour scheme belongs to the adjacent mandatory instruction sign, not the pavement marking itself.
  • Stop Bars: A row of red lights embedded in the pavement across the taxiway at the holding position, which are illuminated to indicate that aircraft must hold.
  • Runway Guard Lights: Flashing amber lights installed on the side of the taxiway to warn pilots that they are approaching a runway holding position.
  • Signage: Mandatory instruction signs — white inscription on a red background — showing the runway designation (e.g. "09-27") at a runway-holding position, or the CAT I/II/III inscription at a precision approach holding position. Signs are installed on both sides of the taxiway at the holding position and are frangible.
  • Location: Positioned at a safe distance from the runway edge to ensure that holding aircraft do not interfere with the ILS critical area or the runway obstacle limitation surfaces.
Purpose

Purpose & Function

The holding bay's purpose is to be the single physical location where several independent layers of runway incursion protection, markings, stop bar lights, runway guard lights, and mandatory instruction signage, converge on the same point, so that if a pilot or vehicle operator misses one cue there is still another one to catch. This layering matters because no single indication is treated as sufficient on its own for a decision as consequential as entering an active runway; a painted marking can fade, a sign can be missed in poor visibility, but a stop bar light or an amber flashing guard light provides an independent confirmation that reinforces the others. Positioning the bay at a defined distance from the runway edge also ensures that simply holding there, before any clearance is given, does not itself create a hazard by encroaching on the ILS critical area or the runway's obstacle limitation surfaces.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Holding bays are found at all airports where aircraft need to queue for runway access or crossing:

  • Runway Holding Positions: At the entrance to each runway, where aircraft stop to await take‑off clearance or to wait for landing traffic to clear.
  • Intermediate Holding Positions: On taxiways leading to runways, where aircraft may be held before approaching the runway holding point (especially at busy airports).
  • CAT II/III Holding Positions: Located further back from the runway to protect the ILS critical area, ensuring that holding aircraft do not interfere with the instrument landing system signals.
  • Taxiway Intersections: Where one taxiway crosses another, holding points may be designated to prevent conflict between aircraft on different taxi routes.
  • Apron Exit Points: At the boundary between the apron and the taxiway system, to control the flow of aircraft leaving the apron.

Holding bays serve several operational functions:

  • Traffic Sequencing: Allowing ATC to order aircraft for take‑off or to manage the flow of arrivals to the apron.
  • Runway Incursion Prevention: Clearly marking the boundary beyond which aircraft must not enter without clearance, reducing the risk of unauthorised runway entry.
  • Protecting ILS Signals: Ensuring that aircraft holding for take‑off do not block or distort the ILS localizer and glide slope signals for landing aircraft.
  • Improving Safety: Providing a defined stopping point for aircraft, especially in low visibility where visual references are limited.
Placement

Installation & Location

The holding position is set out at the distance from the runway centreline mandated by the runway's approach category, with CAT II/III positions surveyed further back to clear the ILS critical and sensitive areas, since a holding aircraft too close to the runway can reflect or block the localizer and glide slope signal. The pavement marking, holding position lights (where fitted), and any associated signage are laid out together during construction so all three read as one consistent boundary to an approaching aircraft, and the bay's paved area is sized to accommodate the largest aircraft type expected to queue there without any part of the airframe encroaching back into the taxiway's own safety margins. Surface construction matches the adjoining taxiway pavement so aircraft transition without a structural step or grade change while stopped.

Function

How Does It Work?

A holding bay integrates several visual and electronic aids to guide pilots and ATC:

  • Markings: The holding position is marked on the taxiway surface with a pattern that is distinct from other taxiway markings. Both FAA and ICAO use yellow paint for the holding position marking itself — the FAA pattern is four parallel lines (two solid, two dashed), while ICAO uses a comparable dual-line or ladder-style marking. (The red background/white "RUNWAY" text belongs to the adjacent mandatory instruction sign, covered separately below — it is not the pavement marking colour.) The markings provide a clear visual boundary that pilots can see from the cockpit.
  • Stop Bars: A row of red lights (usually inset) is installed across the taxiway at the holding position. These lights are controlled by ATC and are illuminated when aircraft must hold. When the stop bar is on, pilots are required to stop before the bar. When it is off, aircraft may proceed (subject to ATC clearance). Stop bars are often interlocked with the runway lighting system to prevent conflicting movements.
  • Runway Guard Lights: These are flashing amber lights installed on the side of the taxiway, typically in pairs, to warn pilots that they are approaching a runway holding position. They provide an additional visual cue, especially in low visibility.
  • Signage: A mandatory instruction sign (red background with white text) is placed adjacent to the holding position, indicating the runway designator (e.g., "09") and, for precision runways, the holding category (e.g., "CAT II" or "CAT III"). This sign ensures that pilots are aware of which runway they are about to enter.
  • Control and Monitoring: ATC manages the holding bay operations through ground radar, visual observation, and radio communication. At busy airports, the stop bars are integrated into the ALCMS, allowing automated control based on runway occupancy and traffic flow.
  • Low‑Visibility Procedures: Stop bars are required at runway-holding positions used in RVR conditions below 550 m, and the operating rule is absolute — an aircraft must never cross an illuminated stop bar, and clearance is given by ATC extinguishing it, not by any separate signal. There is no data link that reports stop bar state to the flight deck; the light itself is the instruction. Where a stop bar is selectively switchable, the taxiway centreline lights beyond it are interlocked so they only lead on once the bar is out.
Control

System Architecture & Control Logic

The pavement and markings are passive, but the stop bar sits in an active interlock chain: at ALCMS-controlled installations it is commanded ON by default and only permitted OFF when runway occupancy and traffic conditions allow, gated by an ATC "select" combined with an automatic runway-occupancy check — so a single ATC action cannot clear a stop bar against a runway-occupied state. Runway guard lights typically run on their own independent flasher circuit rather than sharing the stop bar's loop, so a stop bar power or loop event does not also disable the guard-light warning function they are meant to back up.

System Integration

Circuit & System Overview

The stop bar and ATC clearance form an interlocked control loop: an aircraft's status at the holding position is continuously re‑evaluated against runway occupancy, so the same holding bay can hold one aircraft while releasing another moments later once the runway is confirmed clear.

Construction

Physical Construction

A holding bay isn't a separate pavement structure of its own — it's a defined point on the existing taxiway pavement where several independent physical elements converge: the holding-position marking (a yellow paint pattern under both FAA and ICAO convention) applied directly to the taxiway surface, a row of red stop-bar lights inset flush into the pavement across the full taxiway width, flashing amber runway guard lights mounted on frangible posts to either side, and a red-background mandatory instruction sign on its own frangible coupling nearby. Each element is installed using its own standard construction (inset light base cans for the stop bar, frangible mounts for the sign and guard lights) but positioned together at a location set back far enough from the runway edge — further still for CAT II/III operations — to keep holding aircraft clear of the ILS critical area.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Worn Holding Position Markings

Holding position markings can fade or become obscured by rubber deposits, especially at busy airports. Faded markings reduce visibility, increasing the risk of pilots overshooting the hold point. Regular repainting (every 1–2 years) and cleaning are essential to maintain clear visual cues.

Stop Bar Light Failures

Stop bar lights (red inset lights) are critical for low‑visibility operations. Lamp burnout, cable damage, or controller faults can lead to stop bar failures. Regular testing (nightly visual checks or ALCMS monitoring) and prompt repair are necessary to ensure safety. Redundant power supplies and spare lamps should be available.

Runway Guard Light Malfunctions

Flashing amber guard lights can fail due to lamp burnout, power supply issues, or controller faults. They are essential for alerting pilots to holding positions, especially in poor visibility. Regular inspection and testing of the flash pattern and intensity are required.

Signage Obstruction or Damage

Mandatory instruction signs can be obscured by snow, vegetation, or ground equipment, or damaged by vehicles or aircraft. Obscured or damaged signs can lead to pilot confusion and runway incursions. Regular sign audits, cleaning, and repairs are essential.

Stop Bar Interlock Failures

In automated systems, the stop bar is interlocked with the runway lighting and other airfield systems to prevent conflicting movements. If the interlock fails (e.g., stop bar remains off when runway is occupied), it poses a serious safety risk. Regular testing of the interlock logic and fail‑safe mechanisms is critical.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Holding Position Markings Hard to See
Likely Cause
UV fading or rubber deposit buildup has reduced the visibility of the holding position markings.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect marking contrast and retroreflectivity by day and at night; check for rubber deposit buildup, especially at busy holding positions; note time since last repaint (typically 1–2 years).
Corrective Action
Clean rubber deposits from the marking area, and repaint with high-visibility paint if fading is confirmed; flag any pilot reports of overshooting the hold point in the interim.
Stop Bar Light Dark, Flickering, or Failed to Illuminate on Command
Likely Cause
Lamp burnout, cable damage, or a controller fault in the stop bar circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the ALCMS or controller for a fault indication on the affected stop bar; visually or remotely test the fixture; inspect cabling for damage if voltage is present but the light stays dark.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp/LED module, repair damaged cabling, or address the controller fault; confirm redundant power supply and spare lamp stock are available, and re-test before returning to low-visibility service.
Runway Guard Light Not Flashing Correctly
Likely Cause
Lamp burnout, a power supply issue, or a controller fault affecting the flash pattern or intensity.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the flash pattern and intensity against specification; check supply voltage at the fixture; test the controller output for the guard light circuit.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp or LED module, repair the power supply, or correct the controller fault; re-verify flash pattern and intensity before signing off.
Mandatory Instruction Sign Obscured or Damaged at the Holding Bay
Likely Cause
Snow, vegetation, or ground equipment is blocking the sign, or it has been struck and damaged.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the sign for physical damage and check the immediate surroundings for obstructions; confirm the sign is still legible from the approach to the hold point.
Corrective Action
Clear obstructions, repair or replace a damaged sign, and note the finding in the next sign audit so recurring obstruction sources can be addressed.
Stop Bar Fails to Activate When Runway Is Occupied
Likely Cause
A fault in the interlock logic between the stop bar and the runway lighting/airfield systems.
Diagnostic Steps
Treat as an urgent safety finding; test the interlock logic against the runway occupancy status per the maintenance procedure; review controller logs for the failure event.
Corrective Action
Take the stop bar and associated hold point out of low-visibility service until the interlock fault is identified and corrected, and re-verify the fail-safe behaviour before returning to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Do Not Bypass Interlock Logic
Operational

Never bypass or defeat the holding bay's interlock logic between stop bars, runway guard lights, and ATC switching to force a state locally; a fault in the interlock must be reported and reflected in ATC procedures until repaired.

Circuit Isolation for Stop Bar/RGL Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out stop bar and runway guard light circuits at the CCR before opening a base can or handling cable connectors within the holding bay.

ATC Coordination Before Work
Operational

Coordinate with ATC before any work in a holding bay, since it sits directly on the taxi route to an active runway and any obstruction or outage affects the incursion-prevention function it exists to serve.

Runway Incursion Awareness
Operational

Keep personnel and vehicles positioned clear of the holding position markings during work, and never allow equipment to encroach on the runway side of the holding bay without explicit ATC clearance.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed materials accounted for at all times when work is underway, and sweep the area before reopening it to taxiing traffic.

Frangibility of Associated Signage Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the mandatory instruction sign's frangible coupling with a rigid fitting; it must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify Stop Bar and Guard Light Coordination as a System
Integration

A stop bar and its associated runway guard lights can each individually pass a functional test while their combined timing or state logic has drifted out of sync; verify the full holding-position light picture together, not each subsystem in isolation.

Faded Markings Are a Gradual Hazard That's Easy to Under-Prioritise
Markings

Because holding position marking degradation happens slowly, it's tempting to defer repainting past the scheduled interval when budgets are tight; treat marking visibility as a safety-critical item on its own schedule, not one that flexes with other paving work priorities.

Isolate Stop Bar Circuit Faults From the Runway Guard Light Circuit
Electrical

Verify the wiring topology allows a stop bar fault to be diagnosed independently of the runway guard light circuit; a shared fault path between the two slows troubleshooting exactly when a clear, fast diagnosis matters most for restoring the holding position to service.

Coordinate De-Icing and Snow Removal Around Marking and Light Visibility
Operational

De-icing fluid residue and snow removal operations at busy holding bays can obscure both markings and inset stop bar lights faster than the general taxiway surface; prioritise clearing and inspection at holding positions specifically during winter operations rather than treating them the same as general taxiway clearing.

Weight Inspection Frequency by Actual Traffic Volume, Not a Uniform Schedule
Maintenance Planning

A holding bay at a busy runway intersection accumulates rubber deposits and light-cycling wear far faster than one at a lower-traffic taxiway; align inspection and repainting frequency with actual observed traffic rather than applying the same interval airfield-wide.

Document Marking and Light Condition Together, Not as Separate Reports
Compliance

Since pilots rely on markings, stop bars, and guard lights together as a single holding-position cue, keeping their inspection records in one combined report — rather than scattered across separate paving and electrical maintenance logs — makes it far easier to spot when the whole picture is degrading, not just one component.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about airfield holding bays and holding positions: markings, stop bars, guard lights, signage, holding distances, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a holding bay?

A holding bay, also called a holding point or holding position, is a defined area on a taxiway where aircraft must stop and hold until Air Traffic Control clears them to proceed. They are typically at runway entry points, runway intersections and points where taxiways cross other active taxiways.

Q2Why is a holding position marked in so many ways?

It is the one location where several independent layers of runway incursion protection converge: pavement markings, stop bar lights, runway guard lights and mandatory instruction signage. If a pilot or vehicle operator misses one cue, another is there to catch it.

Q3What does the holding position marking look like?

It is yellow under both FAA and ICAO convention. The FAA pattern is four parallel lines (two solid, two dashed), and ICAO uses a comparable dual-line or ladder-style marking. The red background with white "RUNWAY" text belongs to the adjacent mandatory instruction sign, not the pavement marking.

Q4How do stop bars work at a holding bay?

Unidirectional red lights are spaced at 3 m intervals across the full taxiway width, showing red toward approaching traffic and switched by ATC. Selectively switchable stop bars are interlocked with the taxiway centreline lights beyond them.

Q5What are runway guard lights?

Flashing yellow lights at 30–60 flashes per minute, alternating so one of each pair is always lit. Configuration A is a pair of alternately flashing lights either side of the taxiway, and Configuration B is a row of lights across the taxiway.

Q6What does the mandatory instruction sign say?

White text on a red background, such as "RUNWAY 09" or "CAT II", identifying the runway or protected area the pilot is about to enter.

Q7How far is a holding position from the runway?

Under ICAO Annex 14 Table 3-2, 90 m from the runway centreline for CAT I (code 3 or 4) and 107.5 m for CAT II/III (code 4), with an extra 5 m per metre of elevation difference where the holding position is higher than the threshold. Smaller code numbers use the table figures, and FAA hold line distances are set separately in AC 150/5300-13.

Q8Why are CAT II/III holding positions further back?

They must protect the ILS critical and sensitive areas. A holding aircraft too close to the runway can reflect or block the localizer and glide slope signal.

Q9Which standards apply to holding bays?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (holding position markings, stop bars and signage), FAA AC 150/5340-30, FAA AC 150/5345-46, ICAO Annex 10 Vol I Attachment C and ICAO Doc 9476 (surface movement guidance and control). Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q10How often should a holding bay be maintained?

Typical intervals are monthly for stop bar light tests and runway guard light tests, quarterly for stop bar interlock tests, semi-annual for signage audits, and repainting of holding position markings every 1–2 years. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11Why are the holding position markings hard to see?

UV fading or rubber deposit build-up has reduced visibility, especially at busy holding positions. Check contrast and retroreflectivity by day and night, clean off rubber deposits and repaint if fading is confirmed.

Q12Why is a stop bar dark, flickering or not lighting on command?

Lamp burnout, cable damage or a controller fault in the stop bar circuit. Check the ALCMS or controller for a fault indication, test the fixture and inspect the cabling if voltage is present but the light does not respond.
