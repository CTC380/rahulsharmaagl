Cable Tester

A portable diagnostic instrument used to verify the integrity, continuity, and insulation quality of AGL primary and secondary cables. Cable testers detect opens, shorts, ground faults, and insulation breakdowns, enabling rapid fault location and reducing downtime in airfield lighting systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Cable Tester is a portable electronic test instrument used to assess the condition of electrical cables in AGL systems. It performs a range of tests to verify cable integrity, including:

  • Continuity testing – checks for broken conductors or open circuits
  • Insulation resistance testing – measures the resistance between conductors and ground
  • Short circuit detection – identifies unintentional connections between conductors
  • Earth fault location – pinpoints leakage to ground
  • Distance-to-fault measurement – uses time-domain reflectometry (TDR) to locate faults

Advanced cable testers can store test results, generate reports, and interface with asset management systems. They are essential tools for commissioning new AGL installations, troubleshooting lighting outages, and performing preventive maintenance.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The cable tester exists to answer the specific question that matters most when an AGL fixture or circuit goes dark: is the problem in the cable itself, and if so, exactly where. Given that AGL cabling often runs buried across large distances of airfield, finding a fault by physical inspection alone would mean digging up long cable runs speculatively, which is slow, expensive, and disruptive to airfield operations.

Its distance-to-fault capability, using time-domain reflectometry, is what turns that search from guesswork into a precise location, letting maintenance crews excavate only where the fault actually is rather than along the entire run. This directly shortens both repair time and the disruption to lighting circuits, which matters for restoring full airfield lighting coverage as quickly as possible after an outage.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Cable testers are used throughout the AGL lifecycle – from installation to maintenance and fault diagnosis:

  • Installation Commissioning: Verifying that newly installed primary and secondary cables meet specifications before energizing circuits.
  • Routine Preventive Maintenance: Periodic testing to detect early degradation of cable insulation (e.g., due to moisture ingress or mechanical damage).
  • Fault Diagnosis: Rapidly locating faults in series lighting circuits to minimize runway or taxiway downtime.
  • Repair Verification: Confirming that cable repairs or splices have been performed correctly and restore original electrical characteristics.
  • Asset Management: Trending test results over time to predict cable failures and plan replacements.

Testing is performed at various points – from the CCR room to field junction boxes and isolating transformer connections – using portable testers with suitable adapters.

Care

Storage & Handling

Between uses, the tester is returned to its padded transit case with test leads, adapters, and probes coiled loosely and stored in their designated compartments rather than wound tightly, since a tight coil accelerates wear at the point where the lead exits the instrument body. Batteries are removed or the instrument is left on charge if it will sit unused for an extended period, avoiding the corrosion and leakage that a discharged cell can cause inside the housing. The unit is kept within its rated temperature and humidity range in storage, and is never packed away wet, since AGL testing often takes place in pits, handholes, or trenches where the case and leads can pick up moisture that needs to dry before the tester is sealed back into its case. Calibration due dates are checked before each significant test campaign, with the instrument sent for recalibration on the interval set by the manufacturer rather than only when a reading looks questionable.

Function

How Does It Work?

A cable tester operates by applying known electrical signals to the cable under test and measuring the response. For continuity and resistance measurements, the tester applies a low DC voltage and measures the resulting current. For insulation resistance, a higher DC voltage (typically 250V, 500V, or 1000V) is applied and the leakage current is measured to calculate resistance. For fault location, time-domain reflectometry (TDR) sends a pulse down the cable and measures the time for reflections to return, calculating the distance to the fault based on the velocity of propagation. The tester displays results on a digital screen, often with graphical representations of the cable's impedance profile. Many modern testers also include data logging and Bluetooth connectivity for reporting.

In AGL applications, cable testers are used with specialized adapters to interface with L-823 connectors, isolating transformer terminals, and other AGL-specific interfaces, making the testing process quick and safe.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Selecting a test mode does not just change what the display shows — it reconfigures which internal generator is connected to the cable. For continuity and resistance, the MCU enables a low-voltage constant-current source and routes the return signal to a precision ADC. For insulation testing, it instead energises a DC-DC boost converter to raise the internal supply to the selected test voltage (250 V, 500 V, or 1000 V) and switches in a high-impedance leakage-current sense path, since the currents involved are microamps rather than milliamps. For TDR, the MCU triggers a fast pulse generator and starts a high-speed timer, then correlates the returned reflection against the cable's configured velocity of propagation to compute distance. In every mode the digitised result is passed to the same firmware layer for scaling, limit-checking, and display, and — on models with data logging — timestamped and written to memory or streamed over Bluetooth, so the mode-select switch is effectively choosing which front-end circuit feeds one common measurement and reporting pipeline.

Mode select switches the active front-end generator, but every mode converges on the same ADC, firmware scaling, display, and logging chain.
Operating Principle

Three Test Modes, One Instrument

A single instrument switches between three distinct test circuits — continuity, insulation resistance, and time-domain reflectometry — but reports through one shared display and logging path, so a technician can run all three checks on the same connection without re-wiring.
Construction

Physical Construction

The tester is built into a rugged, impact-resistant polymer case with an over-moulded rubber boot, sized for one-handed field use and shaped to survive drops on hard pavement during runway or taxiway cable testing. Internally, the insulation-test and continuity-test circuitry, TDR pulse generator, and battery pack are mounted on a shock-isolated PCB assembly, with high-voltage test leads brought out through recessed, shrouded terminals to guard against accidental contact. A backlit LCD sits behind a scratch-resistant window, and a sealed membrane keypad provides splash-proof control input consistent with the unit's IP54 rating. The rechargeable Li-ion pack is housed in a separate compartment accessible without exposing the measurement electronics, and test lead storage is integrated into the case or a supplied pouch to protect probes and clips in transit between test points.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Test Voltage Selection

Selecting too high a test voltage for low-voltage cables can damage insulation or cause breakdown. Always use the voltage recommended by the cable manufacturer or standard (e.g., 500V for 600V rated cables).

Poor Test Lead Connection

Loose or corroded test lead connections can produce false readings, especially for insulation resistance. Ensure clean, secure connections and use proper adapters for AGL connectors.

Interference from Other Circuits

Nearby energized cables can induce voltages that affect measurement accuracy. Isolate the cable under test from all power sources and avoid parallel runs with active circuits during testing.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Insulation Resistance Reading Unstable or Low
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress in the cable or connectors, contamination on the cable ends, or a genuine insulation breakdown in the cable under test.
Diagnostic Steps
Repeat the test after cleaning and drying cable ends; check the reading trend over the test duration (a rising trend suggests surface moisture); segment the cable to isolate the faulted length.
Corrective Action
Dry and reseal terminations if moisture is the cause, or replace the faulted cable section if insulation resistance remains below the acceptable minimum after cleaning.
Continuity Test Shows Unexpected Open Circuit
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded test lead connection, a genuine break in the conductor, or the wrong test lead adapter for the AGL connector type.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-seat and inspect test lead connections for corrosion; confirm the correct adapter is fitted for the connector under test; retest at both ends of the run to localise the open.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace corroded test leads/adapters, or excavate and repair the cable at the localised break point.
Tester Displays Erroneous or Implausible High Reading
Likely Cause
Interference from a nearby energised circuit, an uncleared residual charge on the cable, or the tester's internal calibration has drifted.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise and isolate all adjacent circuits before testing; fully discharge the cable under test before connecting the meter; verify the tester against a known resistance/voltage reference.
Corrective Action
Re-test with adjacent circuits isolated and the cable properly discharged; send the tester for recalibration if it fails the reference check.
Tester Will Not Power On
Likely Cause
Depleted battery, a blown internal fuse, or a failed charging circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Check battery voltage or charge state; inspect the fuse per the manufacturer's manual; try an alternate known-good battery or charger.
Corrective Action
Replace batteries or the fuse as applicable, or return the unit for repair if it still fails to power on with a known-good supply.
Result Inconsistent With Previous Test Records
Likely Cause
Selected test voltage differs from the standard used previously, ambient temperature/humidity differences, or genuine cable degradation over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the test voltage matches the cable's rated standard and previous test conditions; note ambient temperature and humidity; compare trend across multiple historical readings rather than a single data point.
Corrective Action
Standardise test voltage and conditions for future comparisons, and schedule closer monitoring or replacement if a genuine downward trend in insulation resistance is confirmed.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Test Lead Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated flexing and connection cycling wearing the test lead insulation or connector contacts over years of field use.
Effect
Insulation resistance reading unstable or low, which can be mistaken for a genuine cable fault when the tester's own leads are at fault.
Detection Method
Test lead continuity and insulation check using a known-good reference cable before relying on a field reading.
Internal Connector Contact Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Contact wear on the tester's internal input connectors from repeated mating cycles over its service life.
Effect
Continuity test shows an unexpected open circuit, even when the cable under test is actually intact.
Detection Method
Cross-check against a second tester or a known-good reference cable when an unexpected open circuit is reported.
Calibration Drift
High Severity
Root Cause
Ageing of the internal measurement reference, or accumulated thermal stress shifting the tester's calibration away from factory settings.
Effect
Tester displays an erroneous or implausible high reading, undermining confidence in every test result until recalibrated.
Detection Method
Scheduled calibration verification against a certified reference standard, at the interval specified by the manufacturer.
Battery / Power Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Battery ageing beyond its service life, or a loose battery contact that causes an intermittent power interruption.
Effect
Tester will not power on, preventing any field testing until the power issue is resolved.
Detection Method
Battery voltage check before each field use, and inspection of battery contacts during scheduled maintenance.
Test Methodology Inconsistency
Low Severity
Root Cause
A different test voltage, duration, or lead configuration used than in a prior test, or a change in ambient temperature/humidity between tests.
Effect
Result inconsistent with previous test records, even though neither the cable nor the tester has an actual fault.
Detection Method
Verification that the current test uses the same voltage, duration, and configuration as the historical baseline before flagging a discrepancy.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Confirm the Circuit Under Test Is De-Energised
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the cable or circuit before connecting test leads; the tester is designed to test a de-energised cable, not to be connected across a live series circuit or fixture.

High Test Voltage During Insulation Testing
Electrical Safety

Insulation resistance tests apply a test voltage (typically 250-1000V DC) between conductors and earth; keep hands clear of exposed test leads and terminals for the duration of the test, and warn anyone else working nearby before starting one.

Residual Charge After Test
Electrical Safety

A cable under test, particularly a longer run, can retain a capacitive charge after an insulation resistance test; allow the tester's automatic discharge cycle to complete, or manually discharge the cable, before disconnecting leads or touching conductors.

Battery Charging Safety
Personnel Safety

Charge the tester's internal Li-ion battery pack only with the supplied USB or AC charger, and avoid charging or storing it in extreme heat.

Inspect Leads and Probes Before Use
Operational

Check test leads, probes, and clips for damaged insulation or worn contacts before each use; a compromised lead can give a false-safe reading or expose the operator to the test voltage.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Testing cable that forms part of an active series circuit requires the circuit to be isolated first; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM for the affected circuit before starting the test.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the tested cable meets the required insulation resistance and continuity values before returning the circuit to service, and log the readings for traceability.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Wet Cable Reads Like a Failing Cable
Diagnostics

A cable that has recently sat in standing water in a duct can show a low insulation resistance reading that recovers once it dries out, without any actual damage to the conductor insulation. Where practical, allow drying time and retest before condemning a cable based on a single low reading.

Fault Distance Estimates Assume the Right Cable Constant
Technique

A time-domain reflectometer's distance-to-fault reading is only as good as the velocity of propagation setting entered for that specific cable type. Using a generic default constant instead of the correct one for the installed cable can put the estimated fault location tens of metres off.

Test Lead Contact Resistance Skews Continuity Readings
Technique

Corroded or worn test lead clips add resistance that can make a genuinely good cable appear marginal on a continuity or loop resistance test. Null out test lead resistance, or inspect and clean the clips, before trusting a borderline reading.

Tester Calibration Currency Matters More Than It Seems
Compliance

An insulation resistance tester with lapsed calibration can drift enough to produce a false pass on a cable that is actually degrading. Confirm calibration currency before using the tester for acceptance testing, not only when a result already looks suspicious.

Trend Loop Resistance Over Successive Maintenance Cycles
Troubleshooting

A single insulation resistance reading that clears the acceptance threshold can still be on a slow downward trend from a splice degrading underground. Logging readings by cable or loop ID across maintenance cycles catches this well before it becomes a field fault.

Tie Results to the Cable Run, Not Just the Test Date
Process

Recording test results against a specific cable run identifier, rather than only a date and general location, makes it possible to spot that the same run keeps showing marginal readings across multiple maintenance visits, which is a stronger signal than any one reading alone.
