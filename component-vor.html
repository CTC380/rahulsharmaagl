VHF Omnidirectional Range (VOR)

A ground‑based radio navigation system that provides azimuth (bearing) information to aircraft equipped with VOR receivers. VOR is the primary en‑route navigation aid, enabling pilots to determine their position relative to a fixed ground station and to navigate along defined airways.

Overview

What Is It?

The VHF Omnidirectional Range (VOR) is a ground‑based radio navigation aid that transmits a signal from which an aircraft can determine its bearing (radial) relative to the station. The system operates in the VHF band (108.0 MHz to 117.975 MHz) and provides 360 distinct radials (each 1° apart) that extend outward from the station like spokes on a wheel. By tuning to a VOR frequency and interpreting the received signal, a pilot can determine which radial they are on, and by combining this with distance information (from DME), can fix their position. VOR is the cornerstone of the airway system, used for en‑route navigation, terminal area procedures, and non‑precision approaches.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The VOR's purpose is to give every aircraft within range a continuous, real-time bearing to a known fixed point, and it is that continuously available radial structure, rather than any single station, that forms the skeleton of the airway system pilots have navigated by for decades. Because the 360 radials radiate outward like spokes, a network of VOR stations lets routes be defined as sequences of radials and intersections rather than requiring point-to-point dead reckoning, which is what made structured, repeatable airway navigation possible before satellite navigation became widespread. Combined with DME for distance, a single VOR/DME station gives a pilot a full position fix, which is why the two are so often co-located rather than deployed as separate services.

Application

Where Is It Used?

VOR is used across all phases of flight and is installed at thousands of locations worldwide:

  • En‑route Navigation: VORs define the airway structure (Victor Airways in the US, Blue and Red Airways elsewhere). Aircraft navigate from one VOR to the next, following designated radials.
  • Terminal Area: VORs are used for arrival and departure procedures, providing bearing information to airports and waypoints.
  • Non‑precision Approaches: VOR approaches provide lateral guidance to runways where ILS is not available (VOR/DME and VOR approaches).
  • Holding Patterns: VORs are used as holding fixes for traffic sequencing.
  • Military Operations: Tactical navigation and training.
Placement

Installation & Location

The VOR ground station sits on its own counterpoise (a circular ground-plane structure) at a site chosen for a clear, obstruction-free horizon in all directions, since buildings, terrain, or even large aircraft parked nearby can distort the radiated pattern and cause course errors. The antenna array and equipment shelter are set inside a fenced, restricted-access compound with its own earthing and lightning protection, positioned clear of the runway/taxiway obstacle limitation surfaces despite typically sitting well away from the movement area itself. Power and monitoring cabling runs underground from the compound back to the airport electrical supply and to the ATC facility for remote status monitoring; site selection is verified by a flight-check survey before the station is commissioned, and re-checked if nearby construction changes the surrounding terrain.

Function

How Does It Work?

The VOR system operates on the principle of phase‑comparison between two signals:

  • Reference Signal: A 30 Hz signal frequency‑modulated onto a 9960 Hz subcarrier (which in turn amplitude‑modulates the main carrier) is transmitted omnidirectionally. This signal is constant in phase regardless of bearing and serves as the phase reference.
  • Variable Signal: A second, 30 Hz amplitude‑modulated signal is produced by a rotating or electronically scanned antenna pattern. This signal's phase varies with the bearing relative to the station. At the moment the antenna pattern points north, the variable signal is in phase with the reference; at other bearings, the phase difference equals the bearing angle.
  • Bearing Determination: The aircraft's VOR receiver compares the phase of the variable (AM) signal with the reference (FM subcarrier) signal. The phase difference (in degrees) directly indicates the radial from the station.
  • Identification: The station also transmits a Morse‑coded identifier (three‑letter code) at regular intervals, allowing the pilot to confirm correct station selection.
  • Coverage: VOR signals are line‑of‑sight, with published service volumes ranging from about 25 nautical miles (Terminal) up to roughly 40–130 nautical miles depending on altitude, for Low and High Altitude station classes.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The VOR ground station sits outside the AGL lighting control hierarchy entirely — it is a navigation aid, not a lighting circuit, and has no connection to the ALCMS. Its own architecture centres on the transmitter and its continuous self-monitoring: the reference and variable phase signals are generated and radiated from the antenna array, while a co-located monitor system independently receives the broadcast and checks the phase relationship, modulation, and radiated power against tolerance. If the monitor detects an out-of-tolerance condition, it does not simply raise an alarm — it can automatically switch the transmitter to a standby unit or shut the station down and flag it as unusable, because a VOR broadcasting bad navigation data is more dangerous than one that is off the air. Station status (operating, standby active, or off-air) is reported to ATC and maintenance over a separate remote-control/monitoring link, distinct from any airfield lighting network.

Construction

Physical Construction

The station's radiating element is an Alford loop or similar antenna array mounted atop a ground-plane counterpoise, typically a raised metal disc or mesh structure a few metres in diameter that shapes the radiation pattern needed to produce accurate radials. The antenna and counterpoise assembly sits on a low, obstruction-free mound at the station centre, chosen so surrounding terrain and structures do not distort the radiated signal, with the transmitter, monitor, and standby equipment housed in an equipment shelter beside or beneath the antenna structure. The shelter is a weatherproof, temperature-controlled building housing the dual (hot-standby) transmitters, monitoring receivers, and battery/UPS backup, connected to the antenna via a short, low-loss coaxial feed to minimise transmission loss between the equipment and the radiating element. Both the antenna structure and the equipment shelter are rated for continuous outdoor exposure (IP65/IP67 on external components), with lightning protection and earthing designed into the counterpoise and shelter grounding system given the station's isolated, elevated siting.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Site Interference (Multipath)

Reflections from terrain, buildings, or large metallic structures can distort the VOR signal, causing bearing errors (scalloping). Proper site selection and the use of Doppler VOR (DVOR) mitigate this issue.

Antenna Pattern Degradation

Damage to the counterpoise or antenna elements can alter the radiation pattern, affecting accuracy. Regular inspection and maintenance of the antenna system are essential.

Phase Modulation Drift

The phase relationship between the reference and variable signals can drift with temperature or component ageing, leading to bearing errors. Periodic calibration using flight inspection is required.

Power Amplifier Failure

The transmitter amplifiers are subject to thermal stress and ageing. Regular maintenance and the use of hot‑standby systems are critical to ensure service continuity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Bearing Errors / Scalloping in Certain Sectors
Likely Cause
Multipath from terrain, buildings, or large metallic structures reflecting the VOR signal.
Diagnostic Steps
Correlate reported bearing errors with aircraft position/sector and review site survey and flight inspection history for known problem radials.
Corrective Action
Address site obstructions where possible, or upgrade to Doppler VOR (DVOR) for improved multipath immunity.
Reduced Accuracy or Coverage
Likely Cause
Damage to the counterpoise or antenna elements altering the radiation pattern.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the antenna system and counterpoise for physical damage and check the monitor system for radiation pattern alarms.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace damaged antenna elements/counterpoise and re-verify with flight inspection.
Gradual Bearing Drift Detected During Flight Inspection
Likely Cause
Phase modulation drift between the reference and variable signals from temperature or component ageing.
Diagnostic Steps
Review flight inspection trend data, check internal monitor phase readings, and inspect for component ageing indicators.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the phase relationship, replace ageing components as needed, and schedule a follow-up flight inspection.
Loss of Service or Reduced Range
Likely Cause
Power amplifier failure or degradation from thermal stress and ageing.
Diagnostic Steps
Check transmitter output power against specification, review amplifier alarm/monitor status, and confirm the hot-standby changeover function.
Corrective Action
Replace or service the faulted amplifier, verify the hot-standby system engages correctly, and restore normal power output.
Monitor System Trips VOR Off-Air
Likely Cause
The integral monitor has detected an out-of-tolerance parameter (bearing, modulation, or power) and shut down or transferred to standby per its safety function.
Diagnostic Steps
Review monitor alarm logs to identify which parameter tripped and check the corresponding subsystem for the underlying fault.
Corrective Action
Rectify the underlying fault identified by the monitor and verify parameters are within tolerance before restoring to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Antenna Pattern Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Physical damage, icing, or corrosion changing the counterpoise or antenna elements' electrical characteristics from their calibrated pattern.
Effect
Bearing errors or scalloping appear in certain sectors, degrading navigation accuracy for aircraft on those radials.
Detection Method
Antenna pattern inspection and a ground radial accuracy test compared against the commissioning baseline.
Phase Modulation Drift
High Severity
Root Cause
Component ageing in the modulation circuitry gradually shifting the phase relationship the VOR signal depends on for bearing accuracy.
Effect
Gradual bearing drift detected during flight inspection, potentially not caught by ground checks alone if the drift is subtle.
Detection Method
Modulation and phase verification against the certified reference, backed by scheduled flight check.
Power Amplifier Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Component ageing, thermal stress, or a fault in the RF power amplifier stage feeding the antenna system.
Effect
Loss of service or reduced range as transmitted signal strength falls below the level needed for the published service volume.
Detection Method
Power amplifier performance check against rated output, and monitor system fault indication review.
Site Interference from Multipath
Medium Severity
Root Cause
New construction, vegetation growth, or terrain changes near the site introducing reflections that weren't present at commissioning.
Effect
Bearing errors or scalloping in specific sectors correlated with the direction of the new obstruction rather than an internal fault.
Detection Method
Ground radial accuracy test focused on the affected sector, and a site survey comparing current conditions to the original siting study.
Monitor System False Trip
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A monitor system that has drifted out of calibration, or a genuine marginal signal parameter sitting right at the trip threshold.
Effect
The monitor system trips the VOR off-air unnecessarily, removing a serviceable facility from use until investigated.
Detection Method
Monitor system trip test to confirm calibration, cross-checked against the actual transmitted signal parameters at the time of the trip.
Standby Transmitter Switchover Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
A fault in the automatic switching relay or logic that's meant to bring the standby transmitter online when the primary fails or trips.
Effect
A primary transmitter fault results in complete loss of service instead of an automatic, low-disruption changeover to standby.
Detection Method
Standby transmitter switchover test performed on a scheduled basis to confirm automatic changeover actually works.
Precautions

Personnel Safety

Confirm the transmitter is powered down or reduced to a safe level before working near the antenna array; VHF transmission at close range can exceed RF exposure limits, and standby/monitor equipment should never be assumed inactive without verification.

Electrical Safety

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Siting Errors Show Up as Scalloping, Not Obvious Failure
Design

A VOR sited too close to reflective terrain, buildings, or metal structures can produce radial errors — often called scalloping or bends — that vary with aircraft position rather than a clean pass/fail signal. Siting studies need to account for multipath reflection sources across the full service volume, not just line-of-sight to the antenna.

Counterpoise and Ground Plane Condition Affects Signal Quality Long Before Visible Damage
Maintenance

The counterpoise or ground plane beneath a VOR antenna maintains the radiation pattern the system depends on; corrosion, vegetation encroachment, or ground settlement beneath it can degrade signal accuracy gradually well before any visible structural damage appears. Inspect ground plane condition as a distinct maintenance item, not just the antenna itself.

Flight Inspection Findings Should Be Cross-Checked Against Ground Monitor Trends
Verification

A flight inspection captures a snapshot of system performance at the time of the check; trending the ground monitor's continuous parameters between flight inspections can catch a slow drift developing well before the next scheduled flight check flags it as out of tolerance.

Nearby Construction Can Degrade a Previously Compliant Site
Operational

A VOR site that passed its original siting survey can be affected by later construction, new buildings, or even seasonal vegetation growth within its protected area; changes near the site should trigger a re-evaluation of siting criteria rather than being assumed clear because the original survey passed.

Standby Transmitter Switchover Should Be Verified as Seamless, Not Just Functional
Reliability

Confirming the standby transmitter comes on when tested is different from confirming the switchover happens without a service-affecting glitch that could disrupt navigation during the transition; verify switchover behaviour under representative conditions, not just that the standby unit itself works.

RF Exposure Zones Change With Any Power or Antenna Modification
Safety

Any change to transmitter power output or antenna configuration shifts the calculated RF exposure boundary around the site; re-verify and, if needed, re-mark the exposure zone after such changes rather than relying on boundaries established for a previous configuration.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about VOR: how it works, specifications, power, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a VOR?

A VHF Omnidirectional Range is a ground-based radio navigation aid that transmits a signal from which an aircraft can determine its bearing (radial) from the station. It operates in the 108.0-117.975 MHz VHF band.

Q2How does a VOR work?

By phase comparison of two signals: a 30 Hz reference signal frequency-modulated onto a 9960 Hz subcarrier and transmitted omnidirectionally, and a 30 Hz variable signal, amplitude-modulated, whose phase depends on bearing.

Q3Where is VOR used?

Across all phases of flight, especially en route, where VORs define the airway structure (Victor Airways in the US), and in approach navigation.

Q4What are the typical VOR specifications?

Channel spacing of 50 kHz, bearing accuracy of ±1 to ±2 degrees, transmitter power of 10-200 W, and coverage classes Terminal (25 NM), Low (40 NM) and High (up to 130 NM). A three-letter Morse identifier is transmitted regularly.

Q5How is VOR kept reliable?

Critical installations use dual redundant hot-standby transmitters, continuous self-monitoring with alarms, and mains power backed by UPS and generator.

Q6Which standards apply to VOR?

ICAO Annex 10 (frequency, modulation and performance), FAA AC 00-31A (US National Aviation Standard for VOR/DME/TACAN), ICAO Doc 8071 Volume I (ground and flight testing) and RTCA DO-196 (VOR airborne receivers).

Q7How often is a VOR checked?

Annually for flight-check radial accuracy, antenna pattern inspection and power amplifier performance, and semi-annually for modulation depth and phase relationships.

Q8Why do some sectors show bearing errors or scalloping?

Multipath from terrain, buildings or large metallic structures reflecting the VOR signal. Correlate reported errors with aircraft position and sector, and review the surrounding site for reflectors.
