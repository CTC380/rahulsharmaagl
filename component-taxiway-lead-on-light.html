Taxiway Lead-on Light

A colour-coded taxiway centreline light that guides pilots and vehicle drivers from a runway holding position onto the runway. Lead-on lights follow the taxiway centreline itself as a single in-line row, alternating green and yellow, and are switched on with the ATC clearance to enter or cross the runway as the stop bar extinguishes.

Overview

What Is It?

A Taxiway Lead-on Light is a taxiway centreline light, colour-coded and independently switched, that guides pilots and vehicle drivers from a runway holding position onto the runway. Lead-on lights are installed in a single row following the taxiway centreline itself — the same line the aircraft already tracks — rather than as a separate paired fixture either side of it. They carry the same alternating green/yellow colour pattern as lead-off lights, extending from the stop bar toward the runway, so that a pilot who has just been cleared to enter or cross the active runway sees a continuous, unambiguous path forward the moment the stop bar goes dark.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The lead-on light's purpose is to resolve exactly the moment where a taxiing aircraft stops being at a holding position and becomes cleared to enter or cross an active runway — a transition where the consequence of confusion is a runway incursion. A controlled stop bar confirms ATC's clearance by going dark, but a dark stop bar on its own tells the pilot nothing about where to go next. The lead-on lights answer that: as the stop bar extinguishes, the taxiway centreline lights immediately ahead illuminate in the same alternating green/yellow pattern used for lead-off, marking that this stretch of centreline lies within the runway or ILS critical area boundary. The colour coding, not a change in fixture layout, is what carries the safety information — it tells the pilot they are now inside the runway environment, rather than relying on the pilot to judge that boundary from signage or memory under workload.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Taxiway lead-on lights are installed on the taxiway centreline stretch between a runway-holding position and the runway itself:

  • Runway holding positions served by a controlled stop bar, extending from the stop bar toward the runway
  • ILS critical area boundaries where the alternating colour pattern marks the sensitive area
  • Low visibility operations (typically below 1,200 ft RVR) where visual confirmation of the runway boundary is essential
  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) with controlled stop bar installations
  • Complex or high-movement airfields using an Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control System (A-SMGCS) for selective route switching
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out in a single row directly along the existing taxiway centreline, using the same inset base can spacing as ordinary taxiway centreline lights, tightened where the centreline curves toward the runway. The lead-on stretch begins at the stop bar and extends toward the runway centreline (commonly around 45–50 m for the first segment on a controlled stop bar installation), with individual fixtures alternating green and yellow lenses along that stretch. Because this segment must be switched independently of the general taxiway circuit — illuminating only once ATC clears the aircraft to enter or cross, in step with the stop bar going dark — it is wired on its own controlled segment rather than the parent taxiway centreline series loop. Cabling runs in duct beneath the pavement back to the isolating transformers and the stop bar/lead-on control cubicle, with setting-out tied to the runway holding position's surveyed location.

Function

How Does It Work?

Taxiway lead-on lights operate as part of the taxiway and runway guidance system:

  • Installation: Lead-on lights are installed in a single row along the taxiway centreline itself, spaced as for ordinary taxiway centreline lights (tighter through curves), extending from the stop bar toward the runway.
  • Lighting: Each fixture is steady-burning and fixed to either a green or yellow lens, alternating along the row, beginning with green nearest the stop bar — the same colour-coding used for lead-off lights.
  • Guidance: The alternating colour pattern tells the pilot the centreline ahead lies within the runway or ILS critical area boundary, confirming the route is clear to proceed once the stop bar extinguishes.
  • Integration: Lead-on lights are switched together with the controlled stop bar they serve, and are coordinated with taxiway centreline lights and, where fitted, runway guard lights and A-SMGCS routing logic.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Lead-on lights share the same constant-current series circuit and CCR intensity step as the taxiway centreline lights they extend, so brightness always follows the loop-level step — there is no independent per-fixture dimming. What is distinct is the switching logic layered on top: the lead-on segment is controlled together with its stop bar, either by a dedicated selector relay or, at airports with selective switching, by the ALCMS/A-SMGCS route logic, so the segment stays dark while the stop bar is illuminated and lights only on the specific route ATC has cleared. Fault reporting stays loop-based — a voltage rise at the CCR flags an open circuit or failed transformer somewhere on the loop, but pinpointing it to a specific lead-on fixture still requires sectionalised field testing.

Construction

Physical Construction

Taxiway lead-on light fixtures follow standard flush taxiway centreline light construction: a die-cast aluminium housing set into an L-852 base can, with a hardened glass or polycarbonate lens gasket-sealed to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating against fuel spillage and standing water. Each fixture is fitted with a fixed-colour lens — green or yellow depending on its position in the alternating sequence — rather than a colour-changing element, since the pattern is set once by fixture position and does not need to vary. Internal wiring terminates at sealed L-823 connectors feeding the fixture's isolating transformer within the same base can, and the fixture body is kept low-profile and load-rated to withstand aircraft wheel loading, since the row sits directly on the taxiway centreline through a high-traffic transition zone into the runway. Stainless-steel fasteners and corrosion-resistant coatings are used throughout given this siting.

Installation Requirements

Civil

Set out fixtures in a single row along the existing taxiway centreline from the stop bar to the runway, using standard centreline light spacing tightened through any curve.

L-852 Base Can Installation
Civil

Install the standard inset base can recessed into the pavement at each centreline light position, tied to the surveyed holding position and runway centreline.

Independent Segment Wiring
Electrical

Wire the lead-on stretch as its own switched segment tied to the stop bar it serves, so it energises only once the stop bar is cleared, independent of the general taxiway centreline circuit.

Cable Route to Transformers
Electrical

Run cabling in duct beneath the pavement back to the isolating transformers and the stop bar/lead-on control cubicle.

Colour Sequence Verification
Mechanical

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the isolating transformer and series loop before the circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity end to end and record total loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at the connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle the CCR through every intensity step and confirm the fixture responds correctly at each.

Stop Bar / Lead-On Interlock Test
Energisation

Confirm the lead-on segment stays dark while its stop bar is illuminated, and energises correctly when the stop bar is cleared; at airports with selective switching, confirm ALCMS/A-SMGCS activates only the cleared route.

Position & Colour Sequence Verification
Final Acceptance

Check each fixture's position against the design setting-out and confirm the alternating green/yellow sequence is correct along the full stretch before sign-off.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity, colour, and beam spread with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances prior to operational handover.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, stop bar/lead-on interlock logic, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

Lead-on lights are exposed to rubber deposits from aircraft tyres and de-icing fluids. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and visibility.

Fixture Settlement or Misalignment

Inset lead-on lights can shift or tilt due to pavement settlement or impact damage, reducing conspicuity along the centreline. Regular level and position checks using a digital level are recommended.

LED Driver Failure

LED drivers in lead-on lights can fail due to thermal stress or power surges. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers is essential to maintain system reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fixture Dim or Discoloured Output
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits or de-icing fluid residue have contaminated the lens, scattering and reducing the effective light output.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for film or discolouration; take a photometric intensity reading and compare against the expected output for the fixture type.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush per the manufacturer's cleaning procedure; re-verify photometric output after cleaning.
Fixture Out of Position or Level
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement or impact damage has shifted or tilted the inset fixture, so it no longer sits flush and level in the centreline path leading to the runway.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture level and position with a digital level; compare against the design setting-out and inspect the base can for settlement.
Corrective Action
Re-set and re-level the fixture to the design tolerance, and correct any base or subgrade settlement causing the fixture to shift.
Fixture Flickers or Cycles On and Off
Likely Cause
A failing LED driver is unable to maintain a stable output under thermal or electrical stress.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor the fixture through a full duty cycle for repeated flicker; check driver temperature and output voltage against specification.
Corrective Action
Replace the faulty LED driver and confirm stable operation through at least one full on/off cycle before closing out the fault.
Fixture Fails to Illuminate
Likely Cause
Complete LED driver failure, or an open-circuit fault in the fixture's supply cable or connector.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for supply voltage at the fixture base; isolate and megger the feeder cable and inspect the connector for corrosion if voltage is present but the fixture stays dark.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed driver or LED module, or repair the cable/connector fault, then re-seal and re-test before backfilling.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED / Lamp Module Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Fixture dim or discoloured output, weakening the colour cue that confirms to pilots they are cleared into the runway/ILS critical area.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Yellow Filter Fade / Chromaticity Shift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure and thermal stress fading or shifting the yellow filter over successive operating seasons.
Effect
The alternating green/yellow sequence becomes harder to read at a distance, weakening the colour cue pilots rely on to confirm the runway/critical area boundary.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric chromaticity testing and visual inspection of the filter for fading or discolouration.
Loose or Intermittent Connector
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the connector pins over time.
Effect
Fixture flickers or cycles on and off, an intermittent fault that is often harder to diagnose than a fully dark unit.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors during scheduled maintenance.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Fixture fails to illuminate at all, an open-circuit point in the series loop that can also take down the rest of the circuit if it extends further.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic loading from mower or snowplough contact on elevated units, material fatigue over the service interval, or corrosion at the coupling threads.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or separates entirely from its base on elevated installations, taking the unit out of aim tolerance or out of service.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim/level checks against tolerance.
Lens Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits from ground traffic, dust, and de-icing fluid residue building up on the lens over successive operating seasons on flush-mounted units.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds even while the fixture otherwise operates normally.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of the lens during routine cleaning.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or ground traffic, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire lead-on series loop.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the taxiway series circuit, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling cable connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations before working near an active taxiway-to-runway junction, since lead-on lights guide aircraft alignment onto the runway and any outage affects entry procedures.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the mounting must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for whenever the base can is open, and secure loose material before reinstating the fixture to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power to avoid tripping the CCR or damaging healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools when working on series-circuit connections and confirm the fixture and base can are bonded/grounded before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify correct aim, colour, and photometric output before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify the Transition From the Cockpit Line-of-Sight
Design

The green-to-yellow handoff at the runway holding position reads differently from a taxiing aircraft's eye height than it does from ground level; where possible, check the transition from a vehicle at approximate cockpit height rather than relying on a walking inspection alone.

Match Intensity Steps to the Runway Edge Circuit
Electrical

Lead-on lights are the pilot's last colour cue before crossing onto the runway edge series; if the lead-on fixtures step on a different CCR channel than the runway edge lights, a step mismatch during a brightness change can make the runway look like it's stepping unevenly right at the entry point.

Layout Follows the Entry Geometry, Not a Standard Table
Layout

Skewed or non-perpendicular taxiway-to-runway junctions change the effective spacing an approaching pilot perceives; verify fixture positions against the as-built junction geometry, especially at older aerodromes where the taxiway centreline doesn't meet the runway edge at a clean right angle.

Frangible Couplings Age Even Without Impact
Mechanical

UV and thermal cycling can shift the shear characteristics of a composite or aluminium frangible coupling over years of service; a coupling that looks visually fine can no longer shear at its rated load, so build torque/shear verification into the overhaul cycle rather than waiting for a post-impact inspection.

Trend Loop Readings at High-Traffic Junctions
Troubleshooting

Runway entry points see more base-can flexing and connector wear from repeated heavy-aircraft turning loads than a straight taxiway run; logging megger readings over successive cycles at these positions catches a developing splice fault well before it takes the loop down.

De-Icing Fluid Concentrates Near Holding Points
Durability

Aircraft often pause briefly at the runway holding position, so glycol-based de-icing fluid tends to pool around lead-on fixtures more than along an open taxiway run; specify glycol-rated seals here and check gasket condition at overhaul rather than only after a reported ingress fault.
