Goniophotometer

A specialised electro‑optical instrument used to measure the spatial distribution of luminous intensity from AGL fixtures. By rotating the fixture or the photometer head through multiple angles, the goniophotometer generates a complete far‑field beam pattern, providing critical data for verifying that runway, taxiway, and approach lights meet the required angular intensity specifications defined by ICAO and FAA.

Overview

What Is It?

A Goniophotometer is a precision measurement system that combines a photometer (or spectroradiometer) with a mechanical rotation stage. It measures the luminous intensity of a light source as a function of angle (azimuth and elevation), producing a comprehensive spatial distribution map – the beam pattern. In AGL applications, every fixture has a defined beam pattern that must meet strict angular intensity requirements to ensure pilots receive adequate visual guidance from the correct angles.

Key beam pattern characteristics measured by a goniophotometer include:

  • Beam width (spread) — the angular extent of the main beam (e.g., 4° vertical × 6° horizontal for centreline lights)
  • Peak intensity direction (aiming point) — the angle at which the light is most intense
  • Intensity uniformity — how smoothly the intensity varies across the beam
  • Off‑axis performance — intensity at critical viewing angles (e.g., side‑lobe levels)
  • Colour uniformity — chromaticity variation across the beam (when combined with a colorimeter/spectrometer)

Goniophotometers are primarily used in laboratory settings for type testing, certification, and R&D. They are essential tools for AGL manufacturers and accredited testing laboratories.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The goniophotometer's purpose is to verify not just how bright a fixture is, but whether that brightness is being delivered at exactly the angles a pilot will actually be looking from during approach, landing, and rollout — since a fixture with the right peak intensity but a beam that is too narrow, too wide, or aimed slightly off-axis can still fail to provide adequate guidance at the angles that matter operationally. By mapping intensity across the full range of azimuth and elevation angles, it produces the complete beam-pattern data that ICAO and FAA specifications require for certification, and it gives engineers the angular detail needed to diagnose problems — such as a misaligned reflector or an incorrect optical component — that a single-point intensity reading would miss entirely.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Goniophotometers are deployed in specialised scenarios where full spatial intensity data is required:

  • Type Testing and Certification: AGL fixtures must be tested in an accredited laboratory using a goniophotometer to verify compliance with ICAO Annex 14 and the relevant FAA L‑type fixture specification's beam pattern requirements (e.g. AC 150/5345‑46). The test results are used for certification and to generate photometric data sheets.
  • Product Development and R&D: When designing new AGL fixtures (especially LED‑based designs), engineers use goniophotometers to optimise the optical system (reflectors, lenses, prisms) to achieve the required beam shape and intensity.
  • Quality Assurance in Manufacturing: High‑end manufacturers may use goniophotometers to perform sample testing from each production batch to ensure consistent optical performance.
  • Forensic Troubleshooting: When an AGL system exhibits unexpected performance issues (e.g., poor visibility from certain angles), a goniophotometer can be used to measure the actual beam pattern of a suspect fixture and compare it with the design specification, revealing misalignment, optical damage, or incorrect component sourcing.
  • Calibration of Field Measurement Tools: Portable alignment tools and photometers used in the field can be calibrated using the accurate intensity and angle data from a reference goniophotometer.
Care

Storage & Handling

As a large, precision optical instrument, the goniophotometer is a fixed installation within an accredited testing laboratory or manufacturer's QC facility rather than field-portable equipment, housed in a dark room isolated from vibration and stray light that would otherwise corrupt intensity measurements. The fixture-under-test is brought to the goniophotometer and mounted on its rotating stage, rather than the instrument being moved, and the room's environmental controls (temperature, humidity, blackout) are maintained continuously to protect both the instrument's optics and the validity of ongoing test series. Access is restricted to trained operators, and the unit undergoes its own periodic calibration against reference standards to keep its angular and intensity measurements traceable.

Function

How Does It Work?

A typical goniophotometer system consists of the following components:

  • Mechanical Rotation Stage: A precision motorised goniometer that rotates the fixture (or the photometer) around one or two axes. Common configurations include:
    • Mirror‑type goniophotometer: The fixture remains fixed while a mirror rotates, directing the beam to a stationary photometer. This is suitable for heavy fixtures.
    • Rotating arm goniophotometer: The fixture rotates around a vertical axis, and the photometer moves along a horizontal arc to cover elevation angles.
    • Double‑axis goniophotometer: The fixture rotates around both azimuth and elevation axes, providing full spherical coverage.
  • Photodetector (Photometer or Spectroradiometer): A calibrated light sensor that measures intensity at each angular position. The detector is placed at a fixed distance (typically 10 m, 30 m, or 100 m) from the fixture, following the inverse square law for intensity calculations.
  • Control and Data Acquisition System: Software that synchronises the rotation, captures the detector signal, and records the intensity as a function of angles. The system also corrects for background light, detector dark current, and distance variations.
  • Analysis Software: Post‑processing tools that generate polar plots, isocandela diagrams, and tabulated intensity values at specified angular increments. The software can compare the measured data against the required standard and produce pass/fail reports.

For AGL measurements, the fixture is powered at the required current step (e.g., 6.6 A for max intensity). The goniophotometer scans the beam in both horizontal (azimuth) and vertical (elevation) planes, typically in steps of 0.5° to 2°. The measured intensities are then used to calculate the beam pattern, which must fall within the tolerance zones defined in the relevant standard.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The goniophotometer's control architecture is a closed motion-and-measurement loop rather than a power-distribution one: a control computer commands the rotation stage to step through a sequence of azimuth and elevation angles, waits for the mechanism to settle at each position, and triggers the photodetector to take a reading before advancing to the next angle. Feedback in this loop comes from position encoders on the rotation stage, which confirm the actual angle reached matches the commanded angle before a measurement is logged, and from repeat or reference readings that catch drift in the fixture's output during a long scan. The fixture under test itself is usually powered from a separate, fixed-current supply set to the required CCR step rather than the goniophotometer's own control system, so the goniophotometer's logic governs only the measurement geometry and timing, while the resulting angle-by-angle intensity table is what downstream analysis software checks against the beam-pattern tolerance envelope in the applicable standard.

Construction

Physical Construction

The system is built around a precision motorised rotation stage — or a moving-mirror/goniometer arm in some designs — that sweeps a calibrated photometer or spectroradiometer detector through the full azimuth and elevation range around a fixed fixture-under-test, with mechanical positioning accurate to a fraction of a degree. The fixture holder is an adjustable clamp and mounting plate designed to accept AGL luminaires of different shapes and sizes, from small inset units to larger elevated fixtures, and includes an integrated CCR or variable supply so the fixture can be powered at its rated series-circuit current during the test. The whole assembly is installed in a darkened photometric laboratory or dark tunnel, walled and baffled with matte black, non-reflective surfaces to prevent stray light from reaching the detector, and held at a controlled temperature since lamp output and detector response both drift with ambient conditions. Data cabling runs from the detector and positioning stage back to a control computer over USB or Ethernet, where the intensity readings at each angle are logged and assembled into the fixture's photometric distribution.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Fixturing and Alignment Errors

Proper alignment of the fixture on the goniometer is critical. The fixture's optical axis must be precisely aligned with the rotation centre. Even small angular errors (0.1°) can shift the measured beam pattern significantly, causing false failures. Use laser alignment tools and precision datum points on the fixture.

Stray Light and Ambient Reflections

Goniophotometers are sensitive to stray light from the fixture's own reflections off walls, floor, or the rotation stage. The measurement room must be painted matte black, and baffles should be used to block direct reflections. Regular background subtraction and dark‑room measurements are essential.

Detector Non‑linearity and Saturation

Photodetectors can saturate when measuring high‑intensity beams (e.g., approach lights at 6.6 A). Using neutral density filters or adjusting the integration time helps. The detector's linearity must be verified using a set of calibrated neutral density filters.

Thermal Effects on Fixture Output

As the fixture heats up during measurement (especially halogen lamps), its intensity and beam pattern can change. Allow the fixture to stabilise at its normal operating temperature (typically 10–15 minutes) before starting the scan. For LEDs, the thermal equilibrium is usually faster but still should be monitored.

Data Interpretation and Standard Tolerances

Interpreting goniophotometric data requires careful comparison with the standard's tolerance zones. ICAO and FAA define minimum intensities at specific angles and also impose limits on intensity gradients. Software tools that automatically check against these criteria are indispensable.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Measured Beam Pattern Doesn't Match Expected Shape
Likely Cause
The fixture's optical axis is misaligned with the rotation centre, even by a fraction of a degree.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-check alignment using laser alignment tools and the fixture's precision datum points; compare against a known-good reference fixture measured on the same rig.
Corrective Action
Re-align the fixture on its mount and re-run the scan, confirming the pattern now matches the expected profile.
Elevated Background / Noisy Baseline Readings
Likely Cause
Stray light from reflections off walls, floor, or the rotation stage is contaminating the measurement.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a dark-room background measurement with the fixture off; inspect the room's matte-black coating and baffles for wear or gaps.
Corrective Action
Restore or add baffling to block direct reflections, and subtract the updated background measurement from subsequent scans.
Readings Flatten Out at High Intensity
Likely Cause
The photodetector is saturating on a high-intensity beam, such as an approach light at full current.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the raw detector output against its rated linear range; verify detector linearity using calibrated neutral density filters.
Corrective Action
Add a neutral density filter or reduce the integration time, and re-verify linearity before re-running the affected measurement.
Intensity Readings Change Over the Course of a Scan
Likely Cause
The fixture under test is still thermally stabilising, so its output is drifting as it heats up.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor intensity at a fixed angle over time after power-on; compare the drift curve against the expected stabilisation time for the lamp/LED type.
Corrective Action
Allow the fixture to reach thermal equilibrium (typically 10-15 minutes for halogen, faster for LED) before starting the scan.
Result Fails Standard Tolerance Check Unexpectedly
Likely Cause
Misapplied tolerance zones or a misconfigured comparison in the analysis software, rather than an actual fixture fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Cross-check the flagged angle and value manually against the applicable ICAO/FAA minimum-intensity and gradient tables.
Corrective Action
Correct the software's tolerance configuration if it was in error, or confirm the fixture genuinely fails and flag it for replacement.

Low Severity
Root Cause
The fixture under test still thermally stabilising, so its output drifts as it heats up during the scan.

Monitoring intensity at a fixed angle over time after power-on, compared against the expected stabilisation curve for the lamp/LED type.

Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wear or backlash developing in the rotation stage bearings or drive mechanism over years of repeated scans.
Effect
Position repeatability degrades, introducing angular error into the recorded beam pattern.
Detection Method
Periodic mechanical inspection of the rotation stage, and a repeatability check by re-scanning a stable reference fixture.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate the Fixture Under Test When Not Measuring
Electrical Safety

De-energise the fixture under test between measurement runs and when adjusting mounting or alignment hardware, since fixtures on the rig are often powered from mains or CCR-equivalent supplies.

Mechanical Pinch Points on the Rotation Stage
Personnel Safety

Keep hands and loose clothing clear of the rotation stage while it is moving, and confirm the stage is stopped and, where possible, locked out before reaching into the test volume.

Eye Protection Near Bright Sources
Personnel Safety

Avoid looking directly into the beam of high-intensity fixtures under test, particularly at close range or on-axis, and use appropriate eye protection where the light source warrants it.

Allow Fixture Cool-Down
Personnel Safety

Allow the fixture under test to cool before handling it for repositioning, since sustained operation during a measurement sequence can heat the housing and lens significantly.

Verify Rig Calibration and Alignment
Compliance

Check optical axis alignment and detector calibration against a reference source before a measurement campaign, since even small axis or wear-related drift can invalidate the beam-pattern result.

Confirm Results Before Sign-off
Compliance

Compare the measured beam pattern against the applicable photometric standard before issuing a pass/fail result, and flag any reading that falls near the tolerance boundary for a repeat measurement.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Mechanical Axis Alignment Errors Compound Across the Whole Beam Map
Calibration

A goniophotometer's rotation stage axes must be aligned to the photometric bench's reference axis with real precision; a small consistent offset doesn't just shift one reading, it subtly distorts the entire beam pattern map in a way that can be hard to spot without comparing against a known reference fixture. Verify axis alignment against a certified reference source, not just the mechanical calibration of the stage itself.

Angular Resolution Trades Off Against Test Time — Choose It Deliberately
Testing

A coarse angular step size finishes a scan quickly but can miss a narrow dip or peak in the beam pattern that falls between sampled points, particularly near a sharp transition edge on PAPI-type optics. Match the angular resolution to how tightly collimated the fixture under test actually is, rather than using one default resolution for every fixture type.

Allow the Fixture Under Test to Fully Stabilise Before Scanning
Field Technique

Both LED and traditional lamp sources shift output somewhat as they warm from a cold start, and beginning a full angular scan before the source has stabilised produces a beam map that's internally inconsistent, since early-scanned angles were measured at a different output level than later ones. Confirm output stability before starting the scan, not just before taking a single reading.

Mounting Repeatability Matters as Much as Mounting Accuracy
Testing

If a fixture is remounted partway through a test series, for example after a modification, slight differences in how it sits in the fixture even within nominal tolerance can shift the beam pattern enough to confuse a before/after comparison. Use a repeatable mounting jig and note any remounting between scans that are meant to be directly compared.

Stray Light Control Is a Whole-Room Discipline, Not Just a Baffle Around the Detector
Environmental

Reflections off walls, ceiling, or equipment in the test chamber can contribute enough stray light to skew low-intensity off-axis readings, even with proper baffling immediately around the detector head. Periodically verify the chamber's stray-light floor with the source off, not just when initially commissioning the facility.

Cross-Check a Sample of Lab Results Against Field Photometer Readings
Verification

A goniophotometer characterises a fixture in controlled lab conditions, but the field photometer is what actually verifies installed performance; periodically correlating results from the two on the same fixture batch confirms the lab data is still a reliable predictor of what gets measured once fixtures are in service.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the goniophotometer: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a goniophotometer?

A goniophotometer is a precision measurement system that combines a photometer or spectroradiometer with a mechanical rotation stage. It measures the luminous intensity of a light source as a function of angle, azimuth and elevation, producing a full spatial distribution map of the beam pattern.

Q2Why do AGL fixtures need goniophotometer testing?

Each fixture has a defined beam pattern that must meet strict angular intensity requirements so pilots receive adequate visual guidance from the correct angles. Goniophotometry verifies that, and produces the results used for certification and photometric data sheets.

Q3What does a goniophotometer measure?

Beam width (for example 4° vertical by 6° horizontal for centreline lights), peak intensity direction (aiming point), intensity uniformity across the beam and off-axis performance.

Q4What types of goniophotometer are there?

A mirror type keeps the fixture fixed while a mirror directs the beam to a stationary photometer, which suits heavy fixtures. A rotating arm type rotates the fixture about a vertical axis while the photometer moves along an arc. A double-axis type rotates the fixture in both azimuth and elevation for full spherical coverage.

Q5Where are goniophotometers used?

In specialised scenarios needing full spatial intensity data: type testing and certification in accredited laboratories against ICAO Annex 14 and the relevant FAA L-type fixture specification (for example AC 150/5345-46), and product development.

Q6What are the typical specifications?

Azimuth 0° to 360°, elevation typically -90° to +90°, angular resolution 0.1° to 1°, and positioning accuracy ±0.1°. Measurement distance is typically 10 m, 30 m or 100 m, the intensity range about 0.1 cd to 1,000,000 cd and intensity accuracy typically ±2% of reading.

Q7How is the fixture under test powered?

The instrument is mains-powered (230 V or 110 V) with an integrated CCR matched to the fixture's rated series current, for example 6.6 A. It is a laboratory instrument and not part of an airfield series circuit.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (photometric test methods within each L-type fixture specification), IEC 61827 and CIE 121 (photometry and goniophotometry of luminaires). Always verify the current edition and your accreditation requirements.

Q9How is a goniophotometer maintained?

Typical intervals are before each test series for alignment verification, per test session for thermal stabilisation, semi-annual for a stray light baseline check and annual for detector calibration. Your laboratory's quality procedures take precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with goniophotometer measurements?

Common problems are optical axis misalignment, stray light and ambient reflections, detector non-linearity and saturation, thermal drift of the fixture under test and rig mechanical wear. A measured beam pattern that does not match the expected shape, or readings that flatten at high intensity, point to these causes.
