Why Runway Lights Change Colour: Runway Lighting Colours Explained

Different colours on the runway are not random. They are used deliberately to show where the runway begins, how much of it remains and where it ends — defined by international aviation standards and vital to safe aircraft operations.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

Published March 16, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) systems are designed not only to illuminate the runway but also to communicate critical information to pilots through visual cues. One of the most important elements of this visual communication is colour change in runway lighting.

These colour transitions are defined by international aviation standards and play a vital role in safe aircraft operations.

Key Takeaway

Runway lighting colours are not decorative. White, amber, green, and red lights form a standardised visual language, defined by ICAO Annex 14 and FAA specifications, that helps pilots judge where they are on the runway and how much of it remains.

01

Clear Visual Guidance for Pilots

During landing and takeoff, pilots depend heavily on runway lighting for spatial awareness. Colour changes provide immediate visual feedback about the aircraft's position on the runway:

White LightsNormal usable portion of the runway
Amber (Yellow) LightsWarn that the far end of the runway is approaching
Red LightsMark the runway end (and the last part of the centreline)

These colour transitions give pilots a quick visual cue about the remaining runway length — the lighting becomes a visual communication system between the runway and the pilot. The cue supplements, and never replaces, published declared distances, runway markings and aircraft performance calculations.

02

Distance Awareness During Landing

One of the most critical moments in flight is the landing roll, when an aircraft decelerates after touchdown. Runway edge lights are white along most of the runway, then change to amber (yellow) near the far end, signalling to the pilot that the runway is nearing its end. Under ICAO Annex 14 this caution zone is the last 600 m of the runway or one-third of the runway length, whichever is less. The FAA uses a longer zone on instrument runways: the last 2,000 ft (about 610 m) or half the runway length, whichever is less.

This simple visual cue helps flight crews stay aware of the remaining runway during the landing roll and rollout, particularly in:

  • Wet runway conditions
  • Heavy aircraft operations
  • High-speed landing conditions

The change in colour gives an immediate indication that the available runway distance is becoming limited.

03

Threshold, Runway End & Centreline Colours

The beginning of the landing area and the end of the runway must be unmistakable, especially at night or in low visibility:

Threshold Lights – Green
Appear green to an aircraft on approach, marking the beginning of the runway available for landing.
Runway End Lights – Red
Appear red when viewed from the runway, marking the extremity of the runway. Where the threshold is at the runway end, threshold and end lights are often combined in one fitting (green toward the approach, red toward the runway).
Displaced Threshold – Red Edge Lights
Where a threshold is displaced, the runway edge lights before it show red in the approach direction (ICAO), warning that the pavement ahead is not available for landing.
Runway Centreline Lights – White, then Red
Where fitted, centreline lights are white, then alternate red and white, then turn fully red. ICAO: alternating from 900 m to 300 m before the runway end, red for the last 300 m. FAA: alternating over the last 3,000 ft, red for the last 1,000 ft.

Together, these colours help pilots distinguish the start of the landing area from the physical end of the runway, helping prevent misjudgment during landing or take-off.

04

ICAO and FAA Standardisation

The use of colour in runway lighting is not arbitrary. It is strictly defined by international aviation standards such as ICAO Annex 14 (Volume I, Chapter 5 and Appendices 1 and 2) and, in the United States, FAA documents such as AC 150/5340-30 and AC 150/5345-46, which specify:

  • Approved colours for each type of airfield light
  • Exact locations where colour transitions must occur
  • Chromaticity limits (CIE colour boundaries) to ensure correct colour perception from the cockpit

Standardisation gives pilots broadly consistent visual cues worldwide. ICAO and FAA arrangements are similar in principle but differ in detail — for example, the length of the amber caution zone and the centreline red/white zones — so always apply the rules that govern the aerodrome concerned (in India, ICAO as implemented through DGCA requirements).

05

Enhanced Situational Awareness in Low Visibility

In fog, rain, or night conditions, runway markings may not be clearly visible. Lighting colours then become a primary visual reference, helping pilots:

  • Identify the runway threshold quickly
  • Maintain alignment with the runway centreline
  • Recognise when they are approaching the runway end

This is especially important during CAT I, CAT II, and CAT III instrument approaches, where reliable visual cues can play an important role in helping pilots interpret the runway environment.

06

Supporting Safe Takeoff Operations

Runway lighting colours also help during the take-off roll. As the aircraft accelerates, pilots maintain directional control and monitor the runway ahead. When the edge lights turn amber (and, where fitted, the centreline lights begin to alternate red and white), the aircraft is within the final section of the runway, supporting:

  • Awareness of remaining runway, including during a rejected take-off
  • Runway awareness
  • Directional awareness

Runway lighting colour is useful not only during landing but also during departure. Take-off decisions remain based on performance calculations, decision speeds and operating procedures — the lights are a situational-awareness aid, not a performance tool.

07

The Engineering Behind Colour-Coded Runway Lighting

Behind every colour transition is a carefully defined AGL design. The lighting system must deliver the required:

ColourChromaticity inside the specified limits for each light type
LocationPrecise position of each transition
IntensityRequired candela output at each brightness step
Directional VisibilityCorrect colour and intensity within the specified beam coverage
Photometric PerformanceVerified, compliant output

The objective is not simply to make a light appear coloured, but to ensure the intended colour is produced and perceived correctly from the operational viewing position — which is why chromaticity is an important part of AGL photometric performance.

08

Key Takeaways

  • Runway light colours form a standardised visual language: white for the normal runway, amber for the final caution zone (up to 600 m under ICAO), red at the end
  • Green threshold lights and red end lights let pilots distinguish the start of the landing area from the physical runway end
  • Colours, transition locations, and chromaticity are strictly defined by ICAO Annex 14 and FAA lighting specifications
  • Colour cues become the primary visual reference in fog, rain, or night conditions, especially during CAT I/II/III approaches
  • The amber transition also supports directional and distance awareness during takeoff roll, not only landing
  • Correct colour perception depends on precise chromaticity, intensity, and directional photometric performance — not just a coloured lens
  • ICAO and FAA differ in detail (caution-zone length, centreline red/white zones), so always apply the standard that governs the aerodrome

Conclusion

Runway lights change colour for a simple but critical reason: to communicate vital information to pilots instantly and clearly. Through carefully designed colour transitions, AGL systems provide distance awareness, runway boundary identification, improved situational awareness, and compliance with aviation standards.

These colour cues ensure that even in darkness or poor visibility, pilots receive reliable visual guidance during the most critical phases of flight.

Runway lighting is therefore much more than a collection of lights along the pavement. Runway lighting colours transform simple lights into an essential language for pilots.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do runway lights change colour?

Colour changes communicate critical information to pilots instantly. White marks the normal usable portion of the runway, amber (yellow) warns that the far end is approaching and red marks the runway end and the last part of the centreline, giving a quick cue about remaining runway length.

How long is the amber caution zone on runway edge lights?

Under ICAO Annex 14 the caution zone is the last 600 m of the runway or one-third of the runway length, whichever is less. The FAA uses a longer zone on instrument runways, the last 2,000 ft (about 610 m) or half the runway length, whichever is less.

What colours are the threshold, runway end and displaced threshold lights?

Threshold lights are green toward approach and runway end lights are red when viewed from the runway, and where the threshold is at the runway end they are often combined in one fitting. Where a threshold is displaced, the runway edge lights before it show red in the approach direction under ICAO, warning that the pavement ahead is not available for landing.

How do centreline light colours change toward the runway end?

Where fitted, centreline lights are white, then alternate red and white, then turn fully red. Under ICAO the alternating section runs from 900 m to 300 m before the runway end with red for the last 300 m, and under FAA rules it is alternating over the last 3,000 ft with red for the last 1,000 ft.

Do ICAO and FAA use the same runway light colour rules?

They are similar in principle but differ in detail, for example the length of the amber caution zone and the centreline red/white zones. Always apply the rules that govern the aerodrome concerned, which in India is ICAO as implemented through DGCA requirements.

Why do colour cues matter in low visibility and on take-off?

In fog, rain or at night, markings may not be clearly visible, so lighting colours become a primary visual reference for identifying the threshold, maintaining alignment and recognising the approaching runway end, especially in CAT I, II and III approaches. During take-off, amber edge lights and alternating red and white centreline lights show the aircraft is within the final section of the runway, but decisions remain based on performance calculations and procedures.

What engineering ensures that runway light colours are correct?

The system must deliver chromaticity inside the specified limits for each light type, precise location of each transition, the required candela output at each brightness step, correct colour and intensity within the specified beam coverage and verified photometric performance. Correct colour perception depends on chromaticity, intensity and directional performance, not just a coloured lens.
