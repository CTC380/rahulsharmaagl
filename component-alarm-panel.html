Alarm Panel

A dedicated visual and audible annunciator that provides immediate notification of abnormal conditions in the Airfield Ground Lighting system. The Alarm Panel consolidates critical alarms, prioritises events, and ensures operators are alerted to faults requiring urgent attention, enhancing safety and response times.

Overview

What Is It?

An Alarm Panel (also known as an Annunciator Panel or Alarm Annunciator) is a dedicated hardware and software interface that provides visual and audible indication of abnormal conditions within the AGL system. It is typically installed in CCR control rooms, ATC towers, or maintenance offices, where operators need immediate awareness of faults. The panel consolidates alarms from all AGL subsystems—CCRs, lighting circuits, power supplies, communication networks, and field devices—and presents them in a clear, prioritised manner. Alarms are typically categorised by severity (critical, major, minor, warning) and displayed with a flashing LED, alphanumeric message, and audible buzzer/siren. The panel also provides acknowledgment, silence, and reset functions, and often includes a historical log of alarm events. The Alarm Panel is a vital safety component, ensuring that operators are alerted to conditions such as circuit failures, overcurrent, lamp outages, communication loss, or power supply issues that could compromise airfield safety.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The alarm panel exists because a fault in the AGL system — a failed circuit, a lamp outage, a lost communication link — is only useful information if a human operator actually notices it in time to act. Without a consolidated, prioritised alarm presentation, a fault buried in one of dozens of subsystems could go unnoticed until it affects a live approach or departure, at which point the response is reactive rather than preventive.

By flashing, sounding, and prioritising alarms by severity, the panel makes sure a critical fault — one with immediate safety implications — can't be lost among routine warnings, giving operators a clear, unambiguous cue for when a lighting circuit needs urgent attention versus when an issue can be scheduled for later maintenance. This triage function is what turns raw fault data into an actionable operational picture.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Alarm Panels are deployed in critical locations where immediate notification of AGL faults is essential:

  • CCR Control Rooms: Primary alarm annunciation for all lighting circuits and power supplies, with audible and visual alerts to operators.
  • ATC Towers: Providing tower controllers with immediate notification of lighting failures that could affect aircraft operations.
  • Maintenance Shops: Alerting maintenance staff to faults requiring field intervention, with details of the affected circuit or equipment.
  • Remote Substations: Local panels at substations to indicate faults specific to that location, facilitating rapid local response.
  • Airport Operations Centers: Integration with central operations for overall situational awareness of airfield status.
  • Fire & Rescue Stations: Providing alarm status for emergency lighting circuits and runway status lights.
  • Approach Lighting Control Shelters: Dedicated panels for approach lighting sequencer and strobe power supply alarms.
Placement

Installation & Location

Panels are wall- or rack-mounted inside the CCR control room, substation, or ATC tower equipment space they serve, positioned within easy sightline and reach of the operator position rather than tucked behind other equipment. Input wiring from field alarm contacts and monitoring points is home-run to the panel's terminal rack, kept on screened cable where it runs alongside power cabling to avoid induced nuisance alarms; panels intended for 24-hour-manned locations are sited so the audible alarm is clearly heard over ambient room noise. Where a panel duplicates alarms already available on an ALCMS workstation, its cabling is still independent of the network connection so it continues to function during a network or server outage.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Alarm Panel operates as a dedicated fault annunciation system, performing several key functions to ensure operators are promptly and accurately informed:

  • Alarm Acquisition: The panel receives alarm signals from the ALCMS server, PLCs, or Remote I/O units via communication links (Ethernet, serial, or hardwired contacts). Each alarm is associated with a specific point (circuit, CCR, sensor) and has a predefined severity level.
  • Visual Indication: Alarms are displayed using high-visibility LEDs or a graphical LCD screen. Typically, a flashing red LED indicates a critical alarm, flashing amber for major, steady amber for minor, and flashing green for warnings or acknowledgments. The message area displays the alarm description, location, and timestamp.
  • Audible Alerts: A built-in buzzer or external siren sounds when a new alarm occurs. The audible tone may vary by severity (e.g., continuous for critical, intermittent for minor).
  • Acknowledgment & Silence: Operators can press an "Acknowledge" button to stop the audible sound and change the visual state from flashing to steady (indicating the alarm has been seen). A "Silence" button may allow temporary muting while the alarm remains active.
  • Alarm Logging: The panel stores a history of alarms with time-stamps, status changes (active, acknowledged, cleared), and operator actions for audit and troubleshooting.
  • Self-Supervision: The panel monitors its own health (communication, power, internal diagnostic) and provides a "panel healthy" indication to ensure operators know the panel itself is functioning.
  • Remote Reporting: Some panels can forward alarms to a central SCADA system or send SMS/email notifications to maintenance personnel.

Alarm Panels are typically configured using dedicated engineering software, where each alarm point is defined with a message, severity, and priority class. They are designed for high reliability and low maintenance, often with redundant power supplies and isolated inputs.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Inside the alarm panel, incoming signals from field devices — lighting circuit fault contacts, CCR status contacts, SPD status contacts, and communication-loss signals — are terminated on input modules that convert each contact or analog/digital signal into a normalized alarm state. A central logic controller polls or receives these states, applies prioritization rules (higher-priority alarms pre-empt lower-priority faults) and any programmed zone or cause-and-effect logic, then drives the output stage: audible/visual annunciators locally, and relay or network outputs to the ALCMS, a remote monitoring centre, or (where the panel also carries emergency lighting circuit status) a fire and rescue station. Every state change is timestamped and written to the event log, and the panel continuously self-monitors its own power supply, battery backup and field wiring, reporting a system trouble condition distinct from a genuine field alarm if its own health check fails.

Construction

Physical Construction

The alarm panel is built into a steel or composite enclosure sized for panel-mount or field-mount installation, with the front bezel housing the LED indicator matrix or graphical LCD, audible buzzer, and any local acknowledge/silence controls behind a sealed, gasketed front face rated IP65/NEMA 4X. The rear of the enclosure carries terminal blocks and connector strips for the hardwired dry-contact inputs, serial and Ethernet communication ports, and the power supply connection, typically protected to a lower IP20 rating appropriate for a controlled cabinet or back-of-panel environment, or upgraded to IP65 for field-mount applications exposed to the elements. Internally, the logic board, communication interfaces, and power supply are mounted on standoffs with clear separation between low-voltage signal wiring and mains or 24VDC power distribution to limit electromagnetic interference. A non-volatile event log memory retains alarm history through power loss, and the enclosure's mounting flanges or DIN-rail clips allow secure installation in equipment racks, control rooms, or outdoor cabinets.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Alarm Fatigue Due to Nuisance Alarms

Too many low‑priority alarms or improperly configured thresholds can lead to operators ignoring or silencing alarms. Proper alarm management (classification, deadband, delay) is essential to filter out noise and present only actionable information.

Communication Loss with ALCMS

If the panel loses communication with the ALCMS server or PLC, it may fail to update alarm status. Implement redundant communication paths and ensure the panel has a "heartbeat" supervision alarm.

LED Burnout or Dimming

LED indicators can fail or become dim over time, reducing visibility. Regular panel inspection and LED replacement (if modular) are recommended.

Insufficient Audible Volume

In noisy control rooms, the built‑in buzzer may not be loud enough to attract attention. Use an external horn or siren with adjustable volume to overcome ambient noise.

Configuration Errors

Incorrect alarm point assignment, message text, or severity levels can cause confusion during fault response. Ensure rigorous testing and documentation of the alarm configuration.

Power Supply Failure

If the panel loses power, operators lose all alarm indication. Use dual redundant power supplies and connect to a UPS to ensure uninterrupted operation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

No Alarm Indication Despite Confirmed Fault
Likely Cause
Failed LED/annunciator module, a stuck or mis-mapped alarm point in the configuration, or loss of the signal input from the ALCMS/PLC.
Diagnostic Steps
Force the field input to fault and confirm the point changes state at the controller; check the panel's point-mapping table against the as-built alarm schedule; test the LED module by substitution.
Corrective Action
Correct the point mapping or re-flash the configuration, replace the failed LED/annunciator module, and re-verify with an end-to-end forced-fault test.
Audible Alarm Not Sounding
Likely Cause
Buzzer/horn failure, the audible has been muted or silenced and not reset, or ambient noise in the control room exceeds the built-in buzzer's output.
Diagnostic Steps
Trigger a test alarm and listen at the panel; check the mute/acknowledge status and silence timer; measure ambient noise level against the buzzer's rated output.
Corrective Action
Replace a failed buzzer, reset the silence/mute function after each shift change, or fit an external horn/siren with adjustable volume where ambient noise is high.
False or Nuisance Alarms Flooding the Panel
Likely Cause
Poorly configured thresholds, missing deadband/delay timers, or a noisy field input toggling near its trip point.
Diagnostic Steps
Review alarm history for repeat offenders; check configured deadband and time-delay settings against the alarm management standard; inspect the field sensor/wiring for the offending point.
Corrective Action
Re-tune deadband and delay settings, reclassify low-priority points, and repair or replace the noisy field input to restore signal stability.
Loss of Communication with ALCMS or PLC
Likely Cause
Failed communication link (cable, switch, or port), a hung PLC/panel processor, or a configuration mismatch after a firmware update.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the heartbeat/supervision alarm status; verify physical link integrity and port activity lights; power-cycle the panel processor if the link is otherwise healthy.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the failed communication link, restore from a known-good configuration backup, and confirm the heartbeat alarm clears and stays clear.
Panel Dead / No Power
Likely Cause
Loss of primary supply, a failed power supply module, or an exhausted/disconnected UPS battery on a dual-redundant feed.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming supply voltage at the panel terminals; verify both redundant power supply outputs; test UPS battery condition and transfer function.
Corrective Action
Restore the incoming supply, replace the failed power supply module, and replace or recharge the UPS battery; confirm automatic transfer between redundant feeds.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Alarm Relay/Output Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Relay contact wear from repeated cycling, or a control board component failure on the affected alarm output.
Effect
No alarm indication despite a confirmed fault condition, leaving operators unaware of a genuine circuit problem.
Detection Method
Periodic forced-alarm test to confirm each output fires correctly, and inspection of relay contacts during scheduled maintenance.
Audible Alarm Device Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Speaker or buzzer element ageing, a blown fuse in the audible circuit, or a loose connection at the device.
Effect
Audible alarm not sounding on a genuine fault, even though the visual/logged indication is correct.
Detection Method
Scheduled functional test of the audible alarm device, and continuity check of its supply circuit.
Alarm Threshold Miscalibration
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A threshold set too tight for normal operating variation, or a sensor reference that has drifted since commissioning.
Effect
False or nuisance alarms flooding the panel, eroding operator confidence and risking a genuine alarm being overlooked.
Detection Method
Review of alarm history for a nuisance pattern, and threshold verification against the manufacturer's recommended settings.
Communication Loss with ALCMS or PLC
High Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
Loss of communication with the ALCMS or PLC, leaving the panel unable to report status or receive commands from the central system.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the panel, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Power Supply Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
A failed internal power supply component, a blown input fuse, or loss of the incoming supply feeding the panel.
Effect
Panel dead with no power, going fully dark and unable to report any alarm condition until restored.
Detection Method
Input voltage verification at the panel, and inspection of the internal power supply and fuses during maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the panel's power supply before working inside on wiring, relays, or the annunciator board, and verify zero energy before touching any terminal.

Never Silence Without Investigating
Operational

Never acknowledge or silence an alarm without first confirming the underlying condition; the panel is the operator's primary warning of faults across the AGL system, and a reflexive silence can mask a real fault in progress.

Verify a Fallback Alerting Path Before Maintenance
Operational

Confirm operators have an alternate way to detect AGL faults, such as direct ALCMS access, before taking the alarm panel offline for servicing, since its outage removes the primary alerting function for the duration of the work.

Threshold & Configuration Change Control
Operational

Apply alarm threshold or priority changes through a documented change-control process, since a miscalibrated threshold can cause either nuisance alarms that get ignored or a genuine fault that goes unreported.

Backup Power Verification
Electrical Safety

Test the panel's battery or UPS backup regularly, since a power interruption at the panel itself would silence the very warning system operators depend on during an outage.

Function Test After Servicing
Operational

Trigger a test alarm to confirm the visual indicator, audible device, and relay outputs all respond correctly before returning the panel to service after any repair or configuration change.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
An Aging Backup Battery Fails Silently
Maintenance

A backup battery that shows a healthy float voltage under no load can still be too degraded to carry the panel through even a short outage. Load-test the battery periodically rather than relying on voltage alone, since voltage under no load is a poor predictor of actual backup runtime.

Nuisance Alarms Get Silenced, Then Ignored
Process

Repeated nuisance alarms on a poorly tuned threshold train operators to acknowledge and dismiss quickly, which can delay response to a genuine event on the same point. Fix the root cause of a recurring nuisance alarm rather than letting operators normalise it.

Relay Output Wiring Errors Hide Until a Real Event
Diagnostics

A relay output miswired to the wrong downstream system can go unnoticed indefinitely if the panel's own test function only confirms the relay clicks, not that it reaches the correct destination. Trace the full signal path end-to-end during commissioning and after any panel configuration change.

Alarm Timestamps Depend on a Synced Clock
Troubleshooting

A panel with a drifted internal clock can produce alarm timestamps that don't line up with other systems' logs during an incident investigation, making it harder to reconstruct the actual event sequence. Verify time sync as part of routine maintenance, not only when a discrepancy is already suspected.

Alarm Priority Levels Need Periodic Review
Design

Alarm priorities set at commissioning can become stale as the site evolves, leaving a now-critical point at a low priority or vice versa. Review priority assignments against current operational risk periodically, not only when a new point is added to the panel.

Log Every Configuration Change, Not Just Faults
Process

An undocumented threshold or wiring change made during troubleshooting is a common source of confusion for the next technician who encounters unexpected panel behaviour. Keep a change log tied to the panel itself, separate from the general site maintenance log.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the alarm panel: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an AGL alarm panel?

An alarm panel (also called an annunciator panel) is a dedicated hardware and software interface that gives visual and audible indication of abnormal conditions in the AGL system. It consolidates alarms from CCRs, lighting circuits, power supplies, communication networks and field devices and presents them in a prioritised manner.

Q2Where are alarm panels installed?

In CCR control rooms for primary annunciation of circuits and power supplies, in ATC towers to alert controllers to lighting failures that could affect aircraft operations, and in maintenance shops to alert staff to faults needing field intervention.

Q3How does an alarm panel work?

It receives alarm signals from the ALCMS server, PLCs or Remote I/O units over Ethernet, serial or hardwired contacts. Each alarm is tied to a specific point and a predefined severity, and is shown with a flashing LED or LCD message and an audible buzzer or siren. Operators can acknowledge, silence and reset alarms, and a historical log is often kept.

Q4How are alarms prioritised?

Typically 3 to 5 priority levels such as critical, major, minor and warning. A flashing red LED usually indicates a critical alarm, flashing amber a major alarm, steady amber a minor alarm, and flashing green a warning or acknowledgment.

Q5How many alarm points can a panel handle?

Typical panels have 16, 32, 64 or 128 alarm points, or a customisable and expandable count. Displays are multi-colour LED indicators or a graphical LCD of 2 to 8 lines.

Q6What communication interfaces are typical?

Ethernet (Modbus TCP, Ethernet/IP, Profinet), serial (RS-232 or RS-485, Modbus RTU) and hardwired dry contacts.

Q7How loud is the audible alarm?

A built-in buzzer is typically 80 dB to 100 dB, and an external horn or siren output is available.

Q8How is an alarm panel powered?

By 24 V DC (18-32 V) or 120/230 V AC through an internal supply, drawing 5 W to 30 W. Battery or UPS backup is recommended so alarms stay active through supply interruptions, with surge arrestors on power and field alarm wiring.

Q9Which standards apply?

Relevant references are ISA 18.2 (alarm system management), FAA AC 150/5345-56B (L-890 ALCMS, including alarm annunciation), IEC 61000-6-2 (EMC immunity) and ISO 9241 (ergonomics of display design). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q10How often should an alarm panel be checked?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for communication link tests, and annual for alarm configuration review, LED function check, audible alarm volume check and power supply check. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11What are the common problems with alarm panels?

Common problems are alarm fatigue from nuisance alarms, communication loss with the ALCMS, LED burnout or dimming, insufficient audible volume, configuration errors and power supply failure. A panel showing no indication despite a confirmed fault, or a silent audible alarm, needs investigating promptly.
