OBSTRUCTION LIGHTING & MARKING

Obstruction lighting and marking are designed to make objects that may present a hazard to aircraft clearly identifiable during the required operating conditions. The engineering objective is not simply to install a red light on a structure — it is to identify the obstacle, assess its aviation significance, mark it, light it, power it reliably, monitor it and keep it visible for as long as it remains a hazard.

Identify → Assess → Mark → Light → Monitor → Protect

01 · Definition

What Is an Obstruction?

An obstruction is an object or structure that may interfere with aircraft operations or penetrate a protected airspace surface. An obstacle may be a building, tower, mast, crane, chimney, antenna structure, transmission line, wind turbine, elevated equipment, temporary construction, natural terrain or other structure.

Not every tall object automatically requires the same obstruction lighting or marking arrangement. The engineer should use the terminology specified by the applicable aviation authority, and the important engineering question is always: does the object create an aviation safety hazard or require identification under the applicable obstacle-control requirements?

BuildingsCranesTowersMastsChimneysCommunication StructuresTransmission StructuresWind TurbinesConstruction EquipmentElevated Temporary Structures
02 · Engineering Objective

Why Obstruction Lighting & Marking Matter

Aircraft may encounter obstacles during approach, departure, circuit operations, near aerodromes, during night operations, poor visibility or abnormal flight paths. Lighting provides visual conspicuity where required; marking is a separate, complementary identification method that defines a structure's boundaries, shape, height, extent, location and profile.

Obstacle Identification → Aeronautical Assessment → Marking → Lighting → Visibility → Monitoring → Maintenance → Operational Protection.

The correct system depends on the object's location, height, characteristics, proximity to an aerodrome, surrounding terrain, aviation requirements and applicable regulations.

04 · Obstacle Assessment

Obstacle Assessment & the Aerodrome Environment

Before selecting equipment, the object's relationship to the runway, taxiway, approach and departure paths, circling area, aerodrome boundary and protected airspace surfaces must be assessed. An object far from a runway may have a different aviation significance from an object of the same height close to an approach path.

Assess Before Selecting Equipment

Geographic LocationElevationStructure HeightRelationship to AerodromeNearby RunwaysApproach/Departure PathsProtected SurfacesSurrounding ObstaclesTemporary or Permanent Status

Permanent vs Temporary Obstacles

Permanent — buildings, towers, chimneys, masts, wind turbines.

Temporary — cranes, construction equipment, temporary towers, temporary structures.

Temporary obstacles can require rapid coordination because their configuration may change during construction — a crane's height, location, boom position and working period can all change the obstacle environment.

Construction cranes deserve particular attention. The lighting/marking requirement should be assessed based on the approved aviation and construction control process — not assumed from the crane's presence alone.

Construction Coordination

Construction projects near airports should coordinate Civil Work → Structure Height → Crane → Obstacle Assessment → Marking → Lighting → Operational Approval — before the structure reaches its final or temporary height where required.

Obstacle Height vs the Approach/Departure Surface

The same structure height can be insignificant far from the aerodrome and highly significant close to an approach path — the object's height is judged against the applicable protected surface, not in isolation

05 · Obstruction Marking

Obstruction Marking, Colour & Contrast

Marking can involve approved visual patterns or colours intended to increase daytime conspicuity. The exact marking pattern depends on structure type, size, shape, location and applicable regulation — do not apply one marking pattern universally to every obstacle.

The Engineer Must Verify

Correct ColourCorrect ContrastCorrect PatternCorrect CoverageContinued Visibility

Contrast Depends on Background

Sky BackgroundTerrainBuildingsVegetationWeatherSunlightViewing Direction
A marking that appears highly visible from one direction may be less conspicuous from another. The approved standard should govern the final marking arrangement, and marking should be treated as complementary to obstruction lighting, not a substitute for it.
06 · Light Intensity Classes

Low, Medium & High-Intensity Obstacle Lights

Obstruction lights provide visual conspicuity during the conditions specified by the applicable requirements. They may differ by intensity, colour, flashing characteristic, steady operation, mounting position, coverage, control and monitoring.

Low-Intensity

May be appropriate for certain smaller or lower obstacles. Verify required colour, intensity, operating characteristic, mounting arrangement and visibility against the applicable standard.

Medium-Intensity

May be used where greater conspicuity is required — flashing or steady operation, day/night intensity changes, automatic control and monitoring. Exact configuration is requirement-dependent.

High-Intensity

May be required for very tall or aviation-significant structures, providing strong visual conspicuity over greater distances or against difficult backgrounds. Use must follow applicable aviation requirements.

Light Colour Is Not Just a Lamp Specification

Source → Optical System → Colour Performance → Environmental Condition → Visual Recognition. Colour performance should be verified according to the applicable test method.

08 · Mounting & Geometry

Obstacle Light Mounting & Structural Geometry

Light mounting position is critical — the light must be visible from the intended aviation viewing directions. Tall structures may require multiple levels of obstacle lighting so that the vertical extent of the structure is visually apparent; a single light may not adequately identify a large or irregular structure.

11 · Lightning & Surge Protection

Lightning Protection & Surge Coordination

Tall structures are naturally exposed to lightning. Lightning protection should not be treated as an isolated equipment item — it requires coordination across the whole electrical system.

LIGHTNING PROTECTION SYSTEM
EARTHING
BONDING
SURGE PROTECTION (SPD)
OBSTRUCTION LIGHTING
Obstacle-lighting electronics may be vulnerable to transient overvoltages. Protection should involve SPDs, proper earthing, bonding, coordinated protection and correct cable routing — not equipment-level protection alone.
Lightning Current Path — Structure to Earth

A strike current takes the down-conductor to the earthing system; the SPD gives any current that strays onto the lighting circuit a low-impedance path to the same earth instead of through the electronics

17 · Troubleshooting

Obstruction Lighting Troubleshooting Philosophy

Observe → Identify → Isolate → Measure → Correct → Retest → Record.

For a failed obstruction light: Power → Controller → Driver → Light Source → Cable → SPD → Earthing → Monitoring.

For a marking problem: Visual Condition → Colour → Contrast → Surface → Pattern → Geometry → Applicable Requirement.

When several lights fail together, investigate common causes first — feeder failure, control failure, power supply failure, lightning event, surge damage, backup system failure or a common communication fault. Do not immediately replace every individual light.

After lightning activity, inspect obstruction lights, control equipment, SPDs, earthing/bonding, power supply, backup system, communication and monitoring, then perform functional and, where applicable, photometric verification.

21 · Common Mistakes

Common Obstruction Engineering Mistakes

01

Selecting a light before completing the obstacle assessment

Aeronautical significance must be established first.

02

Using the same light configuration for every structure

The correct system depends on the object's own characteristics.

03

Ignoring temporary construction obstacles

Cranes and temporary structures require the same rigour as permanent ones.

04

Ignoring marking requirements

Marking complements lighting; it is not optional decoration.

05

Treating electrical ON status as proof of optical performance

Photometric verification is a separate activity.

06

Ignoring lightning protection

Tall structures are naturally exposed and need coordinated protection.

07

Failing to test backup power

An untested backup system is an unproven one.

08

Failing to verify flashing characteristics

Frequency, sequence and synchronization are safety information.

09

Ignoring changes to structure height

Any height change can change the obstacle assessment.

10

Failing to maintain obstacle records

Without records, future maintenance and reassessment becomes difficult.

The Obstruction Lighting Principle

Obstruction lighting is not simply a light mounted on a tall structure. It is a complete system: Obstacle Assessment + Marking + Lighting + Electrical Supply + Control + Monitoring + Visibility + Maintenance + Operational Coordination.

The objective is not merely to illuminate an obstacle — it is to make the obstacle sufficiently conspicuous for safe aviation operations under the conditions and requirements for which the system is designed.

Keyword Reference

Obstruction Lighting Near Airports & Obstacle Clearance

Obstruction lighting near airport

Obstruction lighting near an airport marks towers, masts, buildings, chimneys and similar structures that could be hazards to aircraft, especially at night and in poor visibility. The type and number of lights depend on the height and position of the object and on the rules of the aviation authority. The obstacle assessment section of this page explains how the need is decided.

Airfield lighting obstacle clearance

Airfield lighting obstacle clearance means keeping the airfield lights, their masts and nearby structures clear of the surfaces that protect aircraft movement. Elevated lights, PAPI units and signs must not become obstacles themselves, and new construction near the aerodrome should be checked against the obstacle limitation surfaces. Frangibility and mounting height are set by the applicable standard and the project design.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is an obstruction in aviation?

An obstruction is an object or structure that may interfere with aircraft operations or penetrate a protected airspace surface. Examples include buildings, towers, masts, cranes, chimneys, antenna structures, transmission lines, wind turbines, elevated equipment and temporary construction. Not every tall object automatically needs the same lighting or marking arrangement.

Q2Why do obstruction lighting and marking matter?

Aircraft may encounter obstacles during approach, departure, circuit operations, night operations, poor visibility or abnormal flight paths. Lighting provides visual conspicuity where required, while marking is a separate, complementary method that defines a structure's boundaries, shape, height, extent, location and profile.

Q3What is the engineering sequence for obstruction lighting?

The chain runs identify, assess, classify, mark, light, control, monitor, inspect, test and maintain. It prevents the common mistake of selecting an obstruction light before understanding the aviation requirement.

Q4What should be assessed before selecting obstruction lighting equipment?

The object's location, elevation, height, relationship to the aerodrome, nearby runways, approach and departure paths, protected surfaces, surrounding obstacles, and whether it is temporary or permanent. The same height can be insignificant far from the aerodrome and highly significant near an approach path.

Q5Is marking a substitute for obstruction lighting?

No. Marking should be treated as complementary to obstruction lighting, not a substitute. The exact pattern depends on structure type, size, shape, location and applicable regulation, and no single pattern should be applied universally.

Q6What should be verified for obstruction marking?

Correct colour, contrast, pattern, coverage and continued visibility. Contrast depends on background such as sky, terrain, buildings, vegetation, weather, sunlight and viewing direction, and a marking highly visible from one direction may be less conspicuous from another.

Q7What are low, medium and high-intensity obstacle lights?

Low-intensity lights may suit smaller or lower obstacles, medium-intensity lights may be used where greater conspicuity is required, and high-intensity lights may be required for very tall or aviation-significant structures. Colour, intensity, operating characteristic, mounting and visibility must be verified against the applicable standard.

Q8How should temporary obstacles like cranes be handled?

Temporary obstacles can require rapid coordination because their configuration may change. A crane's height, location, boom position and working period can all change the obstacle environment, so the requirement should be assessed through the approved aviation and construction control process rather than assumed from the crane's presence.

Q9What should be coordinated for construction near an airport?

The sequence is civil work, structure height, crane, obstacle assessment, marking, lighting and operational approval, ideally before the structure reaches its final or temporary height where required.

Q10What should be verified where flashing obstruction lights are required?

Verify flashing frequency, synchronization, sequence and day or night mode, among other characteristics. Operating characteristics depend on the applicable requirements, so the specific arrangement should follow the approved standard.
