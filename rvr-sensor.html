What is an RVR sensor?

The runway sensor and computer that work out how far a pilot could see the lights and markings — from the air’s extinction, the light intensity step and the background light — and why the lighting and the RVR system are one chain.

The Fact ICAO Annex 3 · Doc 9328

Runway visual range (RVR) is the distance over which a pilot on the runway centreline could see the surface markings or the lights that mark the runway or its centreline. The RVR sensor is a transmissometer or forward-scatter sensor beside the runway that measures how much the air weakens light, and the RVR computer calculates the distance; it is not measured directly.

The computer uses three inputs: the sensor’s extinction coefficient, the runway light intensity (the step set on the lighting control system) and the background luminance. In the same fog, brighter lights are seen further away, so a higher intensity step gives a longer RVR. Local reports use a one-minute average, in steps of 25 m, 50 m and 100 m up to the assessed range of about 2 000 m.

That makes the lighting and the RVR system one chain. If the lights emit less than the computer assumes, the reported RVR is longer than the pilot can really see, so lamp failures, dirty fixtures and step mismatches matter to RVR as well as to the lighting. Visibility sensing is entry 11; control of intensity from RVR is entry 14.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is an RVR sensor?

RVR is the distance over which a pilot on the runway centreline could see the runway surface markings or the lights that mark the runway or its centreline. The RVR sensor is the instrument at the runway, a transmissometer or a forward-scatter sensor, that measures how much the air weakens light (the extinction coefficient). An RVR computer then turns that reading into a distance.

Q2Is RVR measured or calculated?

Calculated. The sensor measures the air, not what a pilot sees. The RVR computer combines the extinction coefficient with the runway light intensity and the background luminance (day or night) and applies the visual-range laws set out in ICAO Doc 9328: Allard's law for lights and Koschmieder's law for markings in daylight. The result is a distance in metres for each sensor position.

Q3Why does the lighting intensity step matter?

In the same fog, brighter lights can be seen from further away, so the calculated RVR is longer at a higher intensity step than at a lower one. The RVR computer therefore needs the intensity of the runway lights as an input, normally the step selected on the lighting control system. If the lights are not emitting what the computer assumes, because of failed lamps, dirty fixtures or a step mismatch, the reported RVR does not match what the pilot can see.

Q4Where are the sensors placed?

Along the runway, with the touchdown zone first and the mid-point and stop end added where needed, as decided by the meteorological authority. ICAO Annex 3 guidance puts them within about 120 m of the runway centreline and about 2.5 m above the runway. The touchdown-zone sensor is typically about 300 m from the threshold; the other positions are set by the authority. Masts in protected areas must be frangible.

Q5How is RVR reported?

Local reports use a one-minute average. The Annex 3 reporting scale is in steps of 25 m up to 400 m, 50 m from 400 m to 800 m and 100 m above 800 m, and the assessed range is about 50 m to 2 000 m. Values outside the range are reported as below or above the limits. The values are used for approach minima. The ICAO category definitions use, for example, RVR not less than 550 m for CAT I, 300 m for CAT II and 175 m for CAT IIIA, each together with a decision-height limit.

Q6How does it connect to the AGL system?

The RVR system is a separate meteorological system and not part of the series circuit, but the two are linked in use. The RVR computer takes the runway light intensity step from the lighting control system, and its output goes to ATC and the AWOS. At some aerodromes RVR drives the lighting brightness automatically; that is the subject of entry 14. The sensors need power, earthing, surge protection and recorded cables like any other airfield equipment.

Q7What goes wrong, and is it safe to work on?

Typical faults are dirty or wet windows, dew, frost or snow on the optics, misalignment in a transmissometer, lamp ageing, calibration drift and a wrong intensity input. Work near the runway needs the permit-to-work and aircraft-movement controls. Because RVR sets approach minima, tell ATC and the meteorological authority before taking a sensor or the RVR computer out of service, and do not change the intensity feed without agreement.
