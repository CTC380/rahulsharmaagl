Introduction to Airfield Ground Lighting Standards in ICAO Annex 14

Every runway light, taxiway light, sign board, and approach lighting fixture installed at an airport follows internationally accepted standards. For AGL engineers, airport authorities, contractors, manufacturers, and operators, ICAO Annex 14 is the primary reference that makes this possible.

May 27, 2026 Standards Guide

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) is one of the most critical safety systems in aviation. Every runway light, taxiway light, sign board, and approach lighting fixture installed at an airport follows internationally accepted standards to ensure safe aircraft movement during night operations and low visibility conditions.

The primary international reference for these standards is the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Annex 14. For AGL engineers, airport authorities, contractors, manufacturers, and operators, understanding the requirements and principles it establishes is essential for designing, installing, testing, commissioning, and maintaining reliable airfield lighting systems.

Key Takeaway

ICAO Annex 14 is the framework that lets a pilot recognize the same lighting logic at airports worldwide — from runway edge lights to PAPI to signage — with only limited national differences.

01

What Is ICAO Annex 14?

ICAO Annex 14 (Volume I, Aerodrome Design and Operations) is a globally recognized document that defines Standards and Recommended Practices for airport design and operations. Standards are mandatory for States unless a difference is notified to ICAO, while Recommended Practices are advisory. In India, the requirements are implemented through DGCA CAR Section 4 (Series B, Part I). Edition and amendment numbers change, so always confirm the current edition. It covers several important areas, including:

  • Runway dimensions
  • Taxiway layouts
  • Obstacle limitation surfaces
  • Markings and signage
  • Rescue and firefighting
  • Airfield Ground Lighting systems

For AGL engineers and airport authorities, Annex 14 acts as the foundation for designing safe and compliant airfield lighting systems.

The standardization provided by ICAO helps ensure that pilots can interpret airport lighting and visual guidance consistently, at airports worldwide, with only limited, documented national differences.

02

Why AGL Standards Are Important

Aircraft pilots depend heavily on visual guidance during night operations, fog conditions, heavy rain, low visibility approaches, and taxiing operations. Incorrect lighting intensity, spacing, colour, or beam alignment can create serious operational risks. Standardization ensures:

  • Broadly uniform airport lighting worldwide
  • Pilot familiarity at international airports
  • Reduced runway incursion risk (for example through stop bars)
  • Safe landing and take-off operations
  • Reliable aircraft guidance

A pilot landing in Delhi, Dubai, London, or Singapore should readily recognize runway lighting patterns — this global consistency is only possible because of internationally established ICAO standards.

03

Runway Edge Lighting

Runway edge lights define the lateral boundaries of the runway, providing pilots with important visual references during landing and take-off and helping maintain correct runway alignment. Key ICAO requirements include:

  • White lights along most of the runway
  • Yellow caution section near the runway end (on instrument runways, the last 600 m or the last third of the runway, whichever is less)
  • Specific spacing requirements (uniform, and not more than 60 m on an instrument runway or 100 m on a non-instrument runway)
  • Prescribed photometric intensity levels

Correct installation and alignment of runway edge lights are essential because even small deviations in position or beam direction can affect the pilot's perception of the runway.

04

Runway Threshold and End Lights

Runway threshold lights mark the beginning of the runway available for landing. The standardized colour coding, internationally standardized under ICAO, includes:

Green LightsFacing approaching aircraft
Red LightsIndicating the runway end

Threshold and runway end lighting gives pilots an immediate visual indication of where the usable landing surface begins and where the runway terminates.

05

ICAO Annex 14 Taxiway Lighting

Taxiway lighting guides aircraft safely between runway and apron areas. ICAO specifies important taxiway lighting systems such as:

  • Blue taxiway edge lights
  • Green centreline lights (with alternating green and yellow sections on certain exit taxiways within ILS/MLS critical or sensitive areas)
  • Stop bar lighting at controlled intersections
  • Runway guard lights, intermediate holding position lights and clearance bars

Proper taxiway lighting reduces the risk of ground collisions and wrong-route taxiing — reliable guidance becomes particularly important in low visibility, when pilots have limited references outside the lighting system.

06

Approach Lighting Systems

Approach lights help pilots transition from instrument flight to visual landing references. ICAO Annex 14 defines requirements related to system configurations, light spacing, crossbars, sequenced flashing lights, and installation distances from the runway threshold.

Approach lighting systems become extremely important during CAT-I, CAT-II, and CAT-III operations, where reliable visual guidance is essential to support precision approaches and landing operations.

The approach lighting system provides a visual reference that connects the instrument approach environment with the runway environment.

07

Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI)

PAPI provides vertical guidance to pilots during approach. The system uses combinations of red and white lights to indicate whether the aircraft is too high, too low, or on the correct glide path.

A properly calibrated PAPI significantly improves landing safety by giving pilots a clear visual indication of their approach path.

Because PAPI performance depends on accurate optical and installation parameters, correct alignment and calibration are essential.

08

Airfield Signage

Illuminated sign boards are also standardized under ICAO Annex 14. The standards define requirements for:

  • Mandatory instruction signs
  • Information signs
  • Location signs
  • Colour combinations
  • Character dimensions
  • Illumination requirements

Together with runway and taxiway lighting, standardized signage creates a consistent visual guidance environment for pilots operating on the airport surface.

09

Photometric Requirements in ICAO Standards

One of the most technical sections of Annex 14 relates to photometry. ICAO defines requirements, mainly in Appendix 1 (colours) and Appendix 2 (characteristics of aeronautical ground lights), related to:

Beam SpreadHow the light disperses
Horizontal DistributionIntensity across azimuth
Vertical DistributionIntensity across elevation
Intensity RequirementsMinimum candela output
Chromaticity LimitsColour accuracy boundaries
Photometric TestingVerifies compliance with specified values

Photometric performance is not simply about whether a light is switched on. The fitting must provide the required optical output in the correct direction, with the required intensity and colour characteristics.

Even a small deviation in beam angle or intensity can affect pilot visibility during critical operations — photometry forms the connection between equipment design, installation accuracy, commissioning, and operational performance.

10

Importance of Electrical Standards in AGL

Annex 14 sets high-level electrical requirements (for example a secondary power supply with defined switch-over times, circuit design and monitoring), while the detailed equipment arrangements are covered by ICAO Doc 9157 Part 5, FAA specifications and IEC standards. Important components include:

  • Constant Current Regulator (CCR) systems
  • 6.6A series circuits
  • Isolating transformers
  • Secondary connectors
  • Earthing systems
  • Cable insulation requirements

AGL electrical systems are designed as integrated systems rather than independent fixtures. The performance of the complete circuit depends on the interaction between the CCR, primary series circuit, isolating transformers, secondary connections, and lighting fittings — proper installation and testing are essential for reliable performance.

11

ICAO vs FAA Standards

While ICAO standards are internationally adopted, some airports and projects also follow Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) standards. In many international projects, the two work together rather than in competition:

FrameworkPrimary Role
ICAO Annex 14International Standards and Recommended Practices for aerodrome design, operations and the characteristics of lights
FAA StandardsDetailed national advisory circulars covering design, installation, maintenance and equipment specifications (the L-series)

Manufacturers often design products to meet both ICAO and FAA requirements to support global compatibility.

For engineers working on international airport projects, knowledge of both frameworks is valuable when evaluating equipment, preparing technical specifications, or working with international consultants and manufacturers.

12

Role of Compliance Testing

Before commissioning, AGL systems undergo a range of inspections and tests to verify that the installed system meets the required performance standards. Typical compliance testing includes:

01PhotometricOptical output testing
02InsulationResistance testing
03CCRPerformance testing
04TransformerRatio, continuity and insulation checks
05CableContinuity testing
06FieldAlignment verification

These tests confirm that the installed AGL system performs as required and that critical electrical, optical, and installation parameters are within the applicable requirements — compliance testing is an important part of the transition from installation to operational service. Electrical tests are carried out on circuits that are isolated, locked off and proven dead, under permit-to-work.

Key Takeaways

  • ICAO Annex 14 is the primary international reference for airport design and operations, including AGL
  • Standardization lets pilots interpret airport lighting consistently at airports worldwide, with limited national differences
  • Major AGL systems — runway edge, threshold, taxiway, approach, PAPI, and signage — each have defined colour, spacing, and photometric requirements
  • Photometry connects equipment design, installation accuracy, commissioning, and operational performance
  • ICAO and FAA standards typically complement each other — international requirements alongside detailed national specifications
  • Compliance testing bridges installation and operational service, verifying electrical, optical, and alignment parameters

Final Thoughts

Airfield Ground Lighting is far more than airport illumination. It is a precision-engineered visual guidance system designed around strict international safety standards. ICAO Annex 14 provides the framework that allows aircraft to operate safely across airports worldwide under different visibility conditions.

From runway edge lights and threshold lighting to taxiway guidance, approach lighting, PAPI, signage, photometry, and electrical infrastructure, every element contributes to the overall safety and reliability of airport operations.

For engineers, contractors, manufacturers, and airport operators, understanding these standards is essential for designing reliable and compliant AGL systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is ICAO Annex 14?

ICAO Annex 14 (Volume I, Aerodrome Design and Operations) is a globally recognised document defining Standards and Recommended Practices for airport design and operations, including airfield ground lighting. Standards are mandatory for States unless a difference is notified to ICAO, while Recommended Practices are advisory. In India it is implemented through DGCA CAR Section 4 (Series B, Part I). Edition and amendment numbers change, so confirm the current edition.

Why are AGL standards important?

Pilots depend on visual guidance at night, in fog, heavy rain and low-visibility approaches. Incorrect intensity, spacing, colour or beam alignment can create serious operational risks. Standardisation gives broadly uniform lighting worldwide, pilot familiarity at international airports, reduced runway incursion risk and reliable aircraft guidance.

What does Annex 14 require for runway edge lighting?

Key requirements include white lights along most of the runway, a yellow caution section near the runway end (on instrument runways, the last 600 m or the last third of the runway, whichever is less), uniform spacing of not more than 60 m on an instrument runway or 100 m on a non-instrument runway, and prescribed photometric intensity levels.

What colours do threshold, end and taxiway lights use?

Threshold lights are green, facing approaching aircraft, and runway end lights are red. Taxiway edge lights are blue and taxiway centreline lights are green, with alternating green and yellow sections on certain exit taxiways within ILS/MLS critical or sensitive areas. Annex 14 also covers stop bars, runway guard lights, intermediate holding position lights and clearance bars.

What do approach lighting and PAPI do?

Approach lights help pilots transition from instrument flight to visual landing references, and are extremely important in CAT-I, CAT-II and CAT-III operations. PAPI provides vertical guidance using combinations of red and white lights to show whether the aircraft is too high, too low or on the correct glide path. Its performance depends on accurate optical and installation parameters, so alignment and calibration are essential.

What photometric requirements does Annex 14 set?

Requirements, mainly in Appendix 1 (colours) and Appendix 2 (characteristics of aeronautical ground lights), cover beam spread, horizontal and vertical distribution, minimum intensity and chromaticity limits, with photometric testing verifying compliance. A light being switched on is not enough: the fitting must give the required output in the correct direction, with the required intensity and colour.

What electrical standards apply to AGL?

Annex 14 sets high-level electrical requirements, such as a secondary power supply with defined switch-over times, circuit design and monitoring. Detailed equipment arrangements are covered by ICAO Doc 9157 Part 5, FAA specifications and IEC standards. Important components are CCRs, 6.6 A series circuits, isolating transformers, secondary connectors, earthing and cable insulation.

How do ICAO and FAA standards relate?

In many international projects they work together rather than compete. ICAO Annex 14 provides international Standards and Recommended Practices for aerodrome design, operations and the characteristics of lights, while FAA standards provide detailed national advisory circulars covering design, installation, maintenance and equipment specifications (the L-series). Manufacturers often design products to meet both.

What compliance tests are done before commissioning?

Typical tests are photometric output testing, insulation resistance testing, CCR performance testing, transformer ratio, continuity and insulation checks, cable continuity testing and field alignment verification. Electrical tests are carried out on circuits that are isolated, locked off and proven dead, under permit-to-work.
