Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS)

A backup power system that bridges the switch-over gap defined in ICAO Annex 14 Table 8-1 — the near-instant (essentially no-break) supply that keeps CAT II/III runway centreline and touchdown zone lighting alive within the required one second, while a standby generator takes the full 15 seconds it needs to start and stabilise for the rest of the system.

Overview

What Is It?

A UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply) is an electrical apparatus that provides emergency power to a load when the input power source or mains supply fails. In the context of AGL systems, a UPS protects critical lighting equipment — particularly Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), control systems, and communication interfaces — from power interruptions, voltage sags, surges, and frequency fluctuations.

The UPS achieves this by storing energy in batteries (or flywheels) and using an inverter to convert DC battery power back to clean AC power. In the event of a mains failure, an online double-conversion UPS presents no interruption at all — its inverter is already supplying the load continuously, so there is nothing to "switch to" — while a line-interactive or standby topology introduces a brief transfer of a few milliseconds. Which behaviour a given installation has depends entirely on topology, which is why ICAO's one-second figure is a system design target, not something guaranteed by fitting any UPS. Not every AGL circuit needs this protection, either: Annex 14 Table 8-1 sets the required switch-over time by facility and approach category — 15 seconds is sufficient for CAT I approach and runway lighting, while only the specific facilities essential to CAT II/III operations (runway centreline and touchdown zone lighting among them) carry the tighter one-second, effectively no-break requirement that a static UPS exists to meet.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The UPS exists to close the timing gap that a generator alone cannot. ICAO sets that gap explicitly in Annex 14 Table 8-1: an automatic standby generator (referred to in the Aerodrome Design Manual as an interruptible power unit, or IPU) is allowed up to 15 seconds to start and stabilise, while precision approach CAT II/III operations require certain critical facilities — runway centreline and touchdown zone lighting, principally — to be restored within one second, a figure that in practice means no perceptible break at all. A battery-fed unit meeting that tighter figure is what ICAO Doc 9157 Part 5 calls a static uninterruptible power unit (SUPU); the generic term "UPS" as used in industry covers the same equipment. The SUPU's purpose is therefore narrowly focused on that one-second class of facility, not on sustained backup — it carries the no-break load only until the generator (IPU) is up and has taken over the full system, at which point the battery bank is no longer doing the work.

Application

Where Is It Used?

UPS systems are installed in central electrical rooms, equipment shelters, or dedicated AGL power buildings. They are deployed in:

  • Main AGL Power Centers: Providing no-break backup specifically to the CCRs serving the facilities Annex 14 Table 8-1 designates as needing a one-second switch-over — typically CAT II/III runway centreline and touchdown zone lighting — rather than to every CCR in the building indiscriminately.
  • Remote Equipment Huts: Smaller UPS units for individual CCRs located near the airfield perimeter.
  • ALCMS and Control Systems: Protecting ALCMS servers, workstations, and communication equipment from power loss, ensuring uninterrupted monitoring and control.
  • Critical Navigation Aid Equipment: Supporting ILS, DME, and other navigation systems that require continuous power.
  • Integration with Generators: The UPS/SUPU rides through the standby generator's start-up — up to the 15 seconds ICAO allows for CAT I facilities — but only the specific circuits that carry a one-second (effectively no-break) requirement under Annex 14 Table 8-1 need this ride-through; other circuits are adequately served by the generator alone within its 15-second allowance.
Placement

Installation & Location

The UPS is floor-standing in the main AGL power centre or equipment shelter, typically with its battery cabinets installed alongside it or in an adjacent, ventilated battery room to manage heat and any off-gassing from VRLA cells. It is connected between the incoming supply (utility or the generator interface) and the load it protects — CCRs, ALCMS servers, or navigation aid equipment — with input and output cabling entered from below or the rear per the manufacturer's layout. Larger units are seismically braced or floor-fixed given their weight, and clearance is kept at the front for the control panel and at the rear or sides for cooling airflow. Where a UPS bridges the gap before a standby generator picks up load, it is wired through the generator interface so the ATS logic sequences correctly between the two sources.

Function

How Does It Work?

A UPS operates by continuously converting incoming AC mains power to DC to charge the battery bank, and then inverting that DC back to AC to supply the load. This double-conversion (online) topology isolates the load from all mains power anomalies and provides genuinely zero transfer time, because the inverter is always the source — there is no switch to make when mains fails. Other common topologies (line-interactive, standby/offline) introduce a real, if brief, transfer time measured in milliseconds because they only engage the inverter or battery on detecting a fault. Online double-conversion is the topology capable of meeting ICAO's one-second, effectively no-break requirement with margin, which is why it is specified for the critical circuits that carry that requirement.

Key functional stages include:

  • Rectifier/PFC: Converts incoming AC to DC, while also correcting the power factor to reduce harmonic distortion on the mains supply.
  • Battery Bank: Stores energy in valve-regulated lead-acid (VRLA) or lithium-ion batteries, sized to provide a specified autonomy (typically 15–60 minutes) at full load.
  • Inverter: Converts DC from the rectifier or battery into a clean, regulated AC output, with tight voltage and frequency regulation.
  • Static Bypass: An automatic transfer switch that bypasses the UPS to directly connect the load to mains power in case of UPS overload or internal fault, ensuring continuity.
  • Monitoring and Communication: Built-in microprocessors monitor input/output parameters, battery health, and load status, providing alarms and data to the ALCMS via dry contacts or serial/network protocols.

Construction

Physical Construction

An AGL UPS is typically housed in a floor-standing steel cabinet (or, for smaller ratings, a rack-mount chassis), with the battery bank occupying either a dedicated compartment within the same enclosure or a separate adjacent battery cabinet for larger installations. Internally, the rectifier/charger, inverter, and static bypass switch are built on their own power modules, generally forced-air cooled with filtered intakes and thermostatically controlled fans, and separated from the battery compartment to limit heat exposure to the cells. A control and monitoring board, with a front-panel display or touchscreen for status and alarms, sits in its own compartment isolated from the power electronics. Heavy-duty DC cabling connects the battery bank to the rectifier/inverter section via fused disconnects, while AC input, output, and bypass connections terminate at labelled terminal blocks or busbars sized for the UPS's rated load. Ventilation is sized to handle both the electronics' heat dissipation and any hydrogen off-gassing from the battery bank, particularly for enclosures housing vented lead-acid batteries.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Battery Aging and Capacity Loss

VRLA batteries have a typical service life of 3–5 years in warm environments. Capacity degradation can lead to shorter autonomy and potential failure during extended outages. Regular battery testing, impedance measurement, and timely replacement are essential.

Overheating and Ventilation

UPS systems generate significant heat, especially during battery charging. Insufficient ventilation or blocked air filters can cause premature component failure. Ensure proper clearance, clean filters regularly, and consider air conditioning for high-power units.

Static Bypass Transfer Failures

The static bypass switch may fail to transfer the load to mains during UPS overload or fault, causing a complete outage. Regular testing of the bypass functionality and verifying coordination with upstream breakers is critical.

Communication Loss with ALCMS

UPS units often integrate with the ALCMS via Modbus or SNMP. Network interruptions or protocol mismatches can lead to loss of monitoring, preventing operators from receiving early warnings of UPS faults. Redundant communication paths and periodic protocol testing are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced Autonomy or Failure to Support Load During an Outage
Likely Cause
VRLA battery capacity has degraded with age, typically within a 3-5 year service life in warm environments.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a battery impedance/capacity test and compare against baseline; review recent runtime performance during any real outage.
Corrective Action
Replace batteries showing significant capacity loss, and schedule regular impedance testing to catch further degradation early.
Overtemperature Shutdown or Premature Component Failure
Likely Cause
Insufficient ventilation or blocked air filters have allowed heat from battery charging and normal operation to build up.
Diagnostic Steps
Check internal temperature against rated limits; inspect air filters and clearance for obstruction.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace air filters, restore proper clearance, and add air conditioning if the site consistently runs hot under load.
Load Not Transferred to Mains on UPS Overload or Fault
Likely Cause
The static bypass switch has failed to transfer, or is not correctly coordinated with upstream breakers.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the static bypass functionality under a controlled fault/overload condition; verify coordination settings against upstream breaker curves.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the static bypass switch, and correct any coordination mismatch with upstream breakers before returning to service.
Loss of Communication with the ALCMS
Likely Cause
Damaged or poorly terminated RS-485/Ethernet cabling, connector corrosion, or a network configuration mismatch (baud rate, parity, IP addressing) between the two devices.
Diagnostic Steps
Check cable continuity and shielding/grounding along the run; verify termination resistors are correctly placed; confirm both ends are configured with matching communication settings.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable section, correct the termination or configuration mismatch, and confirm stable communication over a sustained test period before returning to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Battery Degradation / Reduced Autonomy
High Severity
Root Cause
Ordinary battery ageing beyond its service life, sustained operation at elevated ambient temperature, or repeated deep-discharge cycling.
Effect
Reduced autonomy or failure to support the load through the full duration of an outage.
Detection Method
Scheduled battery capacity/load testing, and trend review of runtime performance during actual or simulated outages.
Inverter Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling fatigue in the inverter's power semiconductor devices, an overcurrent event, or a latent manufacturing defect.
Effect
Loss of regulated output power to the connected AGL load, even while utility or generator input remains available.
Detection Method
Fault log review for the tripped protection, and diagnostic testing of the inverter stage to confirm the failed component.
Overtemperature Shutdown
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Blocked or fouled cooling fins, a failed cooling fan, or operation in an enclosure with inadequate ventilation for the ambient conditions.
Effect
The UPS trips on overtemperature protection, or component life is shortened by sustained operation near the thermal limit.
Detection Method
Internal temperature monitoring against the trip threshold, and inspection of cooling fans/fins during scheduled maintenance.
Bypass Transfer Switch Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Contact wear or welding from repeated transfer cycles, or a control logic fault that prevents a clean transfer.
Effect
Load is not transferred to mains when the UPS is overloaded or faulted, risking an unprotected interruption to the AGL circuit.
Detection Method
Scheduled functional transfer test under load, and physical inspection of the bypass switch during maintenance.
Charger Circuit Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A degraded charging component or a control fault that leaves the battery undercharged or overcharged between outages.
Effect
Battery arrives at the next outage in a degraded state of charge, directly reducing available autonomy.
Detection Method
Charging voltage and current verification against the battery manufacturer's profile during scheduled maintenance.
Loss of Communication with the ALCMS
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
Remote status and alarm reporting from the UPS is lost, though the unit continues to operate locally.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the ALCMS, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Output Can Stay Live After Input Isolation
Electrical Safety

Isolating the UPS's mains input does not de-energise its output; the battery-fed inverter can continue to supply the load. Isolate and lock out both the input and the output/bypass path, and verify zero energy at the point of work before proceeding.

Battery Hazards
Personnel Safety

Treat the battery bank as a high-current short-circuit hazard even when the UPS itself is off; remove rings, watches, and other conductive jewellery, use insulated tools, and follow the manufacturer's procedure for battery disconnect and reconnect sequence.

Battery Gas Ventilation
Personnel Safety

Where the UPS uses vented lead-acid or similar battery technology, ensure the battery compartment or room is adequately ventilated, since these batteries can vent hydrogen gas during charging.

Battery Module Weight
Personnel Safety

Use correct lifting technique or equipment when replacing battery modules or cabinets, which can be heavy, and avoid working alone on larger battery sets.

Confirm Bypass Mode Before Servicing
Operational

Before internal work, confirm whether the UPS is in static bypass, and be aware that a bypass path can re-energise the output directly from mains even while the inverter section is isolated; lock out the bypass path as well if work requires the output fully dead.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Taking a UPS out of service removes ride-through protection for the CCRs or control systems it backs up; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM if the protected load itself must also be interrupted during the work.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the UPS transfers to battery and back to mains correctly, and that battery state-of-charge and runtime are within specification, before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Battery Impedance Testing Catches Failure Before Capacity Testing Does
Diagnostics

A battery bank can measure full open-circuit voltage while individual cells have developed high internal impedance that prevents them from actually delivering current under load, a condition a simple voltage check won't reveal but an impedance test will. Prioritise battery health and impedance checks over voltage readings alone, since voltage is one of the least reliable indicators of true battery condition.

Autonomy Testing Should Reflect the Actual Connected Load
Testing

A battery autonomy figure calculated against the UPS's rated capacity can overstate real-world runtime if the actual connected CCR and control load has grown since commissioning without a corresponding review of battery sizing. Re-verify autonomy against the current, as-built load rather than relying on the original design calculation indefinitely.

Static Bypass Failure Is the Fault That Actually Causes an Outage
Reliability

Most UPS failures that reach the connected CCR as an actual power loss trace back to a bypass switchover that didn't work as designed, not to the battery itself, since a battery fault alone should be caught and handled well before it becomes a hard outage. Give the bypass switchover test genuine priority in the test regime rather than treating it as secondary to the battery capacity test.

Ventilation for Off-Gassing Is a Safety Item, Not Just a Cooling Item
Safety

Ventilation around a UPS battery compartment isn't only about managing heat for battery life; certain battery chemistries off-gas hydrogen during charging, and inadequate ventilation is a genuine explosion risk in a confined equipment room, not merely an efficiency concern. Treat ventilation checks in the battery compartment as a safety-critical item on its own, separate from general enclosure cooling.

Battery Aging Accelerates in High-Ambient Equipment Rooms
Reliability

Battery capacity loss from aging follows a roughly exponential relationship with ambient temperature for most chemistries used in AGL UPS systems, so a room running consistently a few degrees above the design ambient can measurably shorten actual service life compared with the manufacturer's rated figures. Factor the equipment room's real operating temperature into expected battery replacement intervals rather than relying on the nameplate life rating alone.

Remote Monitoring Alarms Are Only as Good as Their Last Verified Test
Testing

A UPS alarm and remote monitoring path that was correctly configured at commissioning can silently stop reporting to the ALCMS after an unrelated network change elsewhere in the system, leaving a genuine battery or bypass fault undetected until a physical inspection catches it. Periodically force a test alarm through to confirm the remote monitoring path is still actually reporting, rather than trusting its original configuration indefinitely.
