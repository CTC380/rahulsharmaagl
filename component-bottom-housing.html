Bottom Housing

The foundational structural component of an AGL fixture, housing the electrical connections, isolating transformer interface, and mounting hardware. Designed for durability, corrosion resistance, and secure installation in the airfield pavement or on frangible poles.

Overview

What Is It?

The Bottom Housing is the lower structural component of an AGL fixture that provides the foundation for the entire assembly. It houses the electrical termination points, cable entry glands, and—in many cases—the isolating transformer interface. The bottom housing is designed to be securely mounted to the base plate, base can, or frangible pole, and is sealed to prevent moisture ingress. It is typically made from die-cast aluminium, stainless steel, or high-strength engineering plastics, with corrosion-resistant coatings to withstand the harsh airfield environment. The bottom housing also serves as the primary mechanical interface between the fixture and its mounting infrastructure, ensuring stable positioning and accurate optical alignment.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The bottom housing's purpose is to give an AGL fixture a stable, watertight foundation so that everything mounted above it — the light source, optics, and top cover — stays correctly positioned and electrically safe over years of exposure to weather, vibration, and aircraft-generated loads. By combining structural support, sealed cable termination, and the mechanical interface to the base plate, base can, or frangible pole in a single component, it ensures the fixture's optical axis stays aligned to the beam pattern the airfield's photometric design calls for, and that moisture cannot reach the electrical connections that would otherwise create a safety hazard or an outage. Because it is the point where the fixture actually meets the airfield's fixed infrastructure, its integrity underpins both the reliability of the light and, in elevated installations, the frangible breakaway behaviour that protects aircraft on impact.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Bottom housings are used in every AGL fixture type, with design variations for specific mounting configurations:

  • Elevated Runway & Taxiway Lights: Housings designed for frangible pole mounting, with cable entry from below.
  • Inset Runway & Taxiway Lights: Housings designed for flush mounting in pavement, with cable entry through the base.
  • Threshold & End Lights: Housings for inset or elevated mounting with cable glands for secondary connections.
  • Approach Lighting: Housings for barrettes and flashing light units, often with multiple cable entries.
  • PAPI & VASI: Precision housings with integrated adjustment mechanisms for optical alignment.
  • Obstruction Lights: Housings for pole-mounted beacon systems.
  • Connector Enclosures: Housings for L-823 connectors and junction boxes.
Placement

Installation & Location

The housing is bolted to the base can or foundation using its integral flange, with a gasket fitted between the two mating faces to seal the joint against moisture ingress. Cable entry points are fitted with glands or grommets sized to the incoming armoured or unarmoured cable, and the housing is orientated so the entry points face the direction the duct or trench approaches from, keeping the run inside as short and straight as practical. Elevated versions incorporate (or bolt directly onto) the frangible coupling, so the housing's base flange must align precisely with the coupling's mating face; inset versions are set so the housing sits fully recessed with the top cover finishing flush with the surrounding pavement or shoulder once fitted.

Function

How Does It Work?

The bottom housing performs several critical functions in an AGL fixture:

  • Structural Support: Provides a rigid foundation for the entire fixture assembly, supporting the top cover, optics, and light source.
  • Electrical Termination: Houses the cable glands and terminal blocks for connecting the secondary cable from the isolating transformer to the light source (LED driver or lamp holder).
  • Sealing: Forms a watertight and dust-tight seal when mated with the top cover, using gaskets or O-rings to achieve IP67/IP68 ratings.
  • Mounting Interface: Includes mounting holes, flanges, or threaded inserts for securing the fixture to the base plate, base can, or frangible pole.
  • Thermal Management: Acts as a heat sink or provides thermal paths to dissipate heat from the internal components to the mounting structure.
  • Access for Maintenance: Often includes removable access panels or covers for servicing cable connections and components.

In inset fixtures, the bottom housing is designed to be embedded in the pavement with a load-bearing top cover that is flush with the surface. In elevated fixtures, the housing is mounted on a frangible pole or pedestal, with a breakaway coupling to protect aircraft in case of impact.

Construction

Physical Construction

The housing is die-cast or machined as a single lower shell with an integral mounting flange that bolts directly to the base plate, base can, or frangible pole base, so mechanical load and electrical termination share the same structural part. Cable entry points are formed as threaded glands sized for the primary and secondary leads, each fitted with a compression seal or grommet to maintain the assembly's IP rating at the point of highest water-ingress risk. The upper rim carries a machined groove or flat face for the top cover's gasket or O-ring, with threaded bosses or a bayonet/threaded-ring feature for a repeatable, tool-free or low-tool reseal during lamp changes. Internal standoffs or brackets position the isolating transformer interface, lamp holder, or LED driver at a fixed height relative to the mounting flange, so optical alignment is set by the housing geometry rather than by field adjustment.

Installation Requirements

Mounting Flange Fixing
Mechanical

Bolt the housing's integral mounting flange to the base plate, base can, or frangible pole base, torquing fasteners evenly so mechanical load and electrical termination share the same structural part correctly.

Cable Gland Fitting
Electrical

Fit compression seals or grommets sized to the primary and secondary lead diameters at each threaded gland, maintaining the assembly's IP rating at the highest water-ingress risk point.

Internal Component Positioning
Mechanical

Fit the isolating transformer interface, lamp holder, or LED driver onto the internal standoffs or brackets at the fixed height set by the housing geometry.

Top Cover Gasket Seating
Mechanical

Confirm the top cover's gasket or O-ring seats correctly in the machined groove or flat face on the upper rim before the cover is fitted.

Earthing & Bonding
Electrical

Bond the housing to earth per the drawing, confirming continuity resistance meets the design requirement before the site is closed up.

Bayonet/Threaded-Ring Function Check
Mechanical

Confirm the bayonet or threaded-ring feature operates smoothly for repeatable, tool-free or low-tool reseal, so future lamp changes don't compromise the seal.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record fastener torque, gland sealing, and bonding values as-built before the housing assembly is closed up and energised.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Cable Glands and Entries

Cable gland threads and entry points are common corrosion sites, especially in coastal environments. Use stainless steel glands and apply anti-seize compound to threads during installation. Regular inspection of cable glands is essential.

Moisture Ingress via Cable Entry

If the cable gland is not properly tightened or the seal is damaged, moisture can enter the housing through the cable entry. This can cause corrosion of electrical connections and short circuits. Ensure glands are tightened to the correct torque and seals are intact.

Loose Mounting Fasteners

Vibration from aircraft and ground vehicles can cause mounting bolts to loosen over time. This can affect optical alignment and lead to fixture damage. Regular torque checks and use of thread-locking compound are recommended.

Cracked Housing from Impact

In elevated fixtures, impact from snowploughs or debris can crack the housing. While frangible couplings are designed to break away, the housing itself can be damaged in side impacts. Inspect housings for cracks and replace if necessary.

Galvanic Corrosion

When different metals are in contact (e.g., aluminium housing with stainless steel bolts), galvanic corrosion can occur. Use insulating washers or compatible materials to prevent this.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Water or Corrosion Found Inside the Housing
Likely Cause
Loose or improperly torqued cable gland, a damaged gland seal, or a cracked housing wall allowing moisture ingress at the cable entry.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the cable gland torque and seal condition; check for visible cracks in the housing wall; look for staining or corrosion tracking back to a specific entry point.
Corrective Action
Retorque or replace the cable gland to the correct spec, replace damaged seals, and replace the housing if cracked; clean and treat any internal corrosion before reassembly.
Fixture Loose or Optical Alignment Drifting
Likely Cause
Mounting fasteners loosened by continuous vibration from aircraft and ground vehicle operations.
Diagnostic Steps
Check mounting bolt torque against the specified value; inspect for missing or degraded thread-locking compound; re-check beam alignment against the reference orientation.
Corrective Action
Retorque fasteners to spec, apply thread-locking compound, and re-align the beam; add the bolt torque check to the periodic maintenance schedule if not already included.
Visible Crack in the Housing
Likely Cause
Side impact from a snowplough, mower, or debris strike; frangible couplings only protect against a designed breakaway load direction, not all impact angles.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the full housing surface for cracks, especially near mounting points and cable entries; check whether the crack compromises the seal or structural mounting.
Corrective Action
Replace the housing if cracked; do not attempt to seal a cracked housing with sealant alone, as structural integrity and moisture protection cannot be reliably restored.
White or Powdery Corrosion Around Fasteners
Likely Cause
Galvanic corrosion from dissimilar metals in contact, such as an aluminium housing fastened with uninsulated stainless steel bolts.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect fastener contact points for white/grey corrosion deposits typical of aluminium galvanic attack; confirm the bolt and housing material combination.
Corrective Action
Clean the affected area, fit insulating washers between dissimilar metals, and reassemble with compatible fastener materials going forward.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Cable Gland Seal Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Loose or improperly torqued cable gland, a damaged gland seal, or a cracked housing wall at the cable entry point.
Effect
Water or corrosion enters the housing interior, damaging internal components and degrading insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Cable gland torque and seal condition check, and inspection for staining or corrosion tracking back to the entry point.
Mounting Fastener Loosening
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Continuous vibration from aircraft and ground vehicle operations acting on fasteners without thread-locking compound.
Effect
Fixture becomes loose and optical alignment drifts away from its commissioned orientation.
Detection Method
Bolt torque check against the specified value, and beam alignment verification against the reference orientation.
Housing Crack from Impact
High Severity
Root Cause
Side impact from a snowplough, mower, or debris strike outside the frangible coupling's designed breakaway load direction.
Effect
Structural integrity and moisture sealing of the housing are compromised, risking water ingress and further mechanical failure.
Detection Method
Full-surface visual inspection for cracks, particularly near mounting points and cable entries.
Galvanic Corrosion at Fasteners
Low Severity
Root Cause
Dissimilar metals in contact, such as an aluminium housing fastened with uninsulated stainless steel bolts.
Effect
White or powdery corrosion develops around fasteners, eventually risking seizure and difficult removal.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of fastener contact points for corrosion deposits, and confirmation of bolt/housing material compatibility.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Opening
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the series circuit at the CCR and verify zero energy before opening the bottom housing, since it contains the fixture's cable termination points and, on many designs, the isolating transformer interface.

Hot Surfaces Near the Transformer Interface
Personnel Safety

Allow the housing to cool before handling after an active period, particularly where the isolating transformer sits close to or within the housing.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep gaskets, fasteners, and cable glands accounted for at all times when the housing is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture.

Reseal to Maintain IP Rating
Compliance

Reinstate gaskets, glands, and fasteners correctly and torque them evenly on closing, since a poorly resealed bottom housing allows moisture ingress into the termination points and transformer interface, which can go unnoticed until a fault develops.

Manual Handling
Personnel Safety

Die-cast or stainless-steel bottom housings can be heavy for their size; use correct lifting technique when removing or refitting the assembly.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify a clean insulation test on the reassembled termination before re-energising the circuit, and confirm the housing is properly seated and sealed before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Gland Torque is a Recurring Check, Not a One-Time Install Step
Sealing

Cable gland compression relaxes over time as the gasket material takes a permanent set under load, especially through repeated thermal cycling. A gland that sealed correctly at installation can loosen enough to admit moisture within a few seasons without any visible sign from outside; re-torque checks belong in the routine maintenance cycle, not just the commissioning checklist.

Isolate Dissimilar Metals at Every Fastener
Corrosion

Galvanic corrosion at the housing-to-fastener interface is often blamed on "bad material" when the actual cause is a missing insulating washer between an aluminium housing and stainless steel bolt. Specify the isolation method explicitly in the installation procedure rather than assuming the fastener supplier has accounted for it.

In-Pavement Housings See a Different Failure Pattern Than Elevated Ones
Design

The same bottom housing design serves flush in-pavement fixtures under direct wheel loading and elevated frangible-mounted fixtures under impact loading, but the dominant failure mode differs: flush housings tend to fail from repeated mechanical fatigue and water pooling around the top cover seal, while elevated housings tend to fail from single-event impact or vibration loosening. Tailor the inspection focus to which mounting configuration is actually in the field.

A Hairline Crack Rarely Stays Hairline
Mechanical

A crack that looks cosmetic on a die-cast or composite housing is a stress concentration point that will propagate under continued vibration and thermal cycling, even without another impact. Replace, don't monitor, a cracked housing once identified — deferring replacement to "watch it next cycle" is a common way a minor defect becomes a moisture-ingress failure.

Loose Mounting Bolts Show Up as Optical Faults First
Troubleshooting

Because the bottom housing establishes the fixture's mechanical reference for optical alignment, a loosening mounting bolt often surfaces first as a photometric aim complaint rather than an obvious mechanical symptom. When a fixture's aim has drifted with no record of impact, check housing mounting torque before assuming the optical assembly itself has failed.

Trapped Water Does More Damage Than Water That Passes Through
Durability

A housing that leaks slightly but drains is often less damaging long-term than one that seals well everywhere except a single low point, because standing water at a trapped low point accelerates corrosion at exactly the location hardest to inspect. Check for drainage provision or trapped-water pockets as part of the design review, not only the overall IP rating.
