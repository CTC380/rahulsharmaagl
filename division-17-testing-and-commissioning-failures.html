Initial Issue

Key principle — A system can appear operational during a normal visual inspection while still failing an electrical, photometric, alignment, functional, integration or acceptance test. Division 17 creates a structured path from Test → Test Result → Failure → Investigation → Corrective Action → Retest → Acceptance.

Diagnostic principle — Not every failed test is an equipment failure. Some findings originate from test equipment, calibration, test procedure, measurement error, configuration or the commissioning process rather than the AGL asset itself — always establish which side of that line a finding sits on before it is logged as a technical failure.

C. Test Failure vs. Equipment Failure

This distinction is built into the Failure Database — every Division 17 record should separate the testing failure from the technical failure it reveals (or fails to reveal):

Scenario 1

Cable insulation breakdown → Insulation-resistance test failure. The technical failure is the cable insulation breakdown; the testing failure is the insulation-resistance test failure.

Scenario 2

Incorrect test setup → Incorrect test result. Here the equipment may actually be healthy — the failure is associated with the testing process, not necessarily the AGL equipment.

Scenario 3

PAPI physically misaligned → PAPI alignment failure → PAPI alignment test failure. Division 11 records the physical/technical PAPI failure; Division 17 records the testing/commissioning failure. This cross-linking is important.

D. Failure Severity

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging:

Critical / Safety-Critical — immediate operational or safety risk

Major / Operationally Critical — significant availability impact

Moderate — reduced performance, not yet critical

Minor / Non-Critical — limited or no operational effect

Degradation / Intermittent / Latent — developing or hidden fault

F. Division 17 vs. the Technical Divisions

Division 17 is the quality-control bridge between the AGL equipment failure database and the AGL Testing & Commissioning Manual. A failed test is a finding, not automatically a verdict — always establish whether the root cause sits with the asset (cross-reference the relevant technical division — CCR, cable/connector, earthing, PAPI, signs, transformer, control system), the test process (procedure, setup, interpretation), or the test equipment (calibration, instrument condition). This prevents an important reliability-reporting error: counting every failed test as an equipment failure when some failures actually originate from test equipment, calibration, test procedure, measurement error, configuration or the commissioning process.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about testing and commissioning failures, as covered in Division 17 of the AGL Failure Database.

Q1What is Division 17 — Testing & Commissioning Failures in the AGL Failure Database?

Division 17 covers failures in testing and commissioning, and links the Failure Database directly with the AGL Testing & Commissioning Manual. It contains 20 published topics (TCF-01 to TCF-20) covering electrical testing, functional and photometric performance, cable and connector testing, control and system integration, FAT/SAT, test-equipment calibration and commissioning deviations.

Q2Which testing and commissioning failures does Division 17 cover?

Insulation-resistance, continuity, earth-resistance, transformer and CCR test failures, functional-test and photometric-test failures, PAPI alignment failure, sign acceptance-test failure, cable-test and connector-test failures, ALCMS integration-test failure, FAT and SAT failures, system integration failure, performance acceptance failure, calibration failure, incorrect test result, failed retest and commissioning deviation.

Q3How are the 20 testing topics grouped?

They fall into six groups: Electrical Testing Failures (5 topics), Functional & Performance Testing (4), Cable & Connector Testing (2), Control & Integration Testing (5), Test Equipment & Data Quality (2) and Retest & Commissioning Control (2).

Q4Is a failed test the same as an equipment failure?

No. A failed test is a finding, not automatically a verdict. Every Division 17 record should separate the testing failure from the technical failure it reveals, or fails to reveal. A cable insulation breakdown produces an insulation-resistance test failure, but an incorrect test setup can produce an incorrect result while the equipment is healthy.

Q5Where can the root cause of a failed test sit?

It can sit with the asset (cross-referencing the relevant technical division), the test process (procedure, setup or interpretation) or the test equipment (calibration or instrument condition). Establishing which one applies prevents an incorrect verdict against the asset.

Q6What test-to-acceptance chains does Division 17 capture?

Division 17 records the full chain rather than the final pass or fail. In the cable chain, insulation deterioration leads to an insulation-resistance test failure, cable investigation, repair or replacement, retest and acceptance. The earth chain runs from electrode or connection deterioration through high earth resistance and an earth-resistance test failure to earthing corrective action, retest and acceptance. A photometry chain is also captured.

Q7What should be recorded for a testing or commissioning failure?

Records include test identification (failure ID, asset, system, test type, procedure reference, date, tester), test-equipment details (instrument ID, type, calibration status and validity, range and setup), test requirement (parameter, acceptance criterion, required and measured values) and failure information (category, mode, pass/fail status, extent).

Q8How are failure severity and operational impact classified?

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging. The classes are Critical / Safety-Critical (immediate operational or safety risk), Major / Operationally Critical (significant availability impact), Moderate (reduced performance, not yet critical), Minor / Non-Critical (limited or no operational effect), and Degradation / Intermittent / Latent (a developing or hidden fault).

Beyond the Failed Test

DON’T JUST RE-TEST. UNDERSTAND WHY THE FIRST RESULT WAS WRONG.

A test or commissioning failure can come from a genuine system fault, a test-method error, or an out-of-tolerance instrument — real troubleshooting means understanding testing procedure and acceptance criteria well enough to know which one it actually was.
