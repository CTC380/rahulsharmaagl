Optical Filter

A transparent or coloured optical element that selectively transmits specific wavelengths of light while blocking others. Used to achieve the precise colours required for threshold (green), end (red), taxiway (blue/green), stop bars (red), and obstruction (red/white) lighting, ensuring compliance with ICAO colour specifications.

Overview

What Is It?

An Optical Filter is a component that modifies the spectral content of light by selectively transmitting certain wavelengths while absorbing or reflecting others. In AGL fixtures, filters are used to produce the required colours — green for threshold and taxiway centreline, red for runway end and stop bars, yellow for clearance bars, and blue for taxiway edge lights. Filters can be absorptive (dyed glass or plastic that absorbs unwanted wavelengths) or dichroic (interference coatings that reflect unwanted wavelengths). Dichroic filters offer higher transmission and better colour stability but are more expensive. The choice depends on the application, cost, and environmental conditions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The optical filter's purpose is to give a fixture the exact colour that carries operational meaning to a pilot — green marking the threshold, red marking the runway end or a stop bar, yellow marking a clearance bar, and so on. These colours are not decorative: a pilot who sees red or green light on a runway is meant to draw a specific conclusion about their position or clearance to proceed, so the filter's job is to hold the transmitted wavelengths within the tight colour-coordinate tolerances that ICAO and FAA specifications require, and to keep doing so despite years of UV exposure and heat that would otherwise fade an absorptive filter or degrade a dichroic coating's thin-film layers. Getting this wrong is not a minor optical defect — a colour that has drifted outside tolerance could be misread by a pilot as a different fixture type entirely.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Optical filters are used in virtually every colour-specific AGL fixture:

  • Threshold Lights (Green): Green filters for runway threshold and wing bar lights.
  • Runway End Lights (Red): Red filters to indicate the end of the runway.
  • Taxiway Centreline Lights (Green): Green filters for centreline guidance.
  • Taxiway Edge Lights (Blue): Blue filters for edge delineation.
  • Stop Bars (Red): Red filters for runway holding position lights.
  • Clearance Bars (Yellow): Yellow filters for taxiway clearance lights.
  • Obstruction Lights (Red/White): Red filters for obstruction marking, often combined with clear sections.
  • PAPI & VASI: Specialised filters that create the red/white transition via colour mixing.
Placement

Installation & Location

The filter is fitted between the light source (or LED module) and the lens or prism, either as a coated layer bonded to another optical element or as a discrete disc held in a retaining groove or clip within the optical assembly. Its position is fixed by the housing's internal geometry, so there is generally no field adjustment; correct installation means confirming the filter is the correct colour and grade for that fixture type and that it's seated fully home in its groove or clip, without gaps that would let unfiltered light leak past the edge. Because colour compliance is safety-critical (a threshold light showing the wrong colour is a serious defect), the filter is always sourced and fitted as a matched replacement part rather than substituted, and the fixture's colour output is verified after any filter is fitted or replaced.

Function

How Does It Work?

An optical filter works by either absorbing or reflecting unwanted wavelengths. Absorptive filters contain dyes or pigments that absorb specific colours, transmitting only the desired wavelengths. They are simple, robust, and inexpensive, but can fade over time due to UV exposure and heat. Dichroic filters use multiple thin-film interference layers that reflect certain wavelengths and transmit others. They offer high transmission (often >90%) and excellent colour stability, but are more delicate and costly.

In AGL fixtures, the filter is typically placed between the light source and the lens or prism, or integrated into the lens itself. The filter must be designed to withstand the fixture's operating temperature, UV radiation, and mechanical stresses while maintaining precise colour coordinates as defined by ICAO Annex 14 and FAA specifications.

Construction

Physical Construction

Absorptive filters are made from glass or plastic dyed throughout its thickness with a pigment tuned to absorb unwanted wavelengths, so colour performance depends on the material's bulk optical density rather than a surface treatment. Dichroic filters instead start as clear optical-grade glass onto which multiple thin-film interference layers are vacuum-deposited, each layer tuned to reflect specific wavelength bands while passing the target colour with minimal loss; because the effect depends on precise, uniform layer thickness, dichroic filters require tighter manufacturing tolerances than dyed equivalents. Whichever type is used, the filter is cut to the exact aperture of the fixture's optical path and mounted in a dedicated slot or retaining ring — either bonded in place or held by a light clip — positioned between the light source and the prism or lens so it intercepts the full beam without introducing stray reflections at its edges.

Pre-Fit

Inspect the filter for chips, delamination, or coating damage at the edge before fitting, since edge defects can let unfiltered light leak past the aperture.

Seat the filter fully home in its retaining groove or clip with no gaps around the edge, since the position is fixed by the housing geometry and there is no field adjustment once fitted.

Correct Optical Path Placement

Colour Output Verification

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Colour Fading (UV Degradation)

Absorptive filters (especially plastic ones) can fade over time due to UV exposure, shifting colour coordinates outside specification. This compromises the visual cue for pilots. Regular photometric testing and replacement of faded filters is essential.

Thermal Stress Cracking

Glass filters can crack due to thermal shock from rapid temperature changes (e.g., halogen lamp heat-up). Use of borosilicate glass or proper thermal management in the fixture design is recommended.

Contamination (Dust, Oil, Moisture)

Filters can become contaminated with dust, oil, or moisture, reducing light transmission and altering colour. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft cloths is required.

Dichroic Coating Delamination

Dichroic filters can suffer from delamination of the thin-film coatings due to humidity or thermal cycling. This leads to reduced transmission and colour shift. Use of high-quality coatings and proper sealing is critical.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Colour Outside Chromaticity Specification
Likely Cause
UV degradation fading an absorptive plastic filter, or dichroic coating delamination from humidity or thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour check against ICAO/FAA chromaticity limits; visually inspect the filter for fading (absorptive types) or coating haze/flaking (dichroic types).
Corrective Action
Replace the filter with an approved part of the correct type; if dichroic coatings are repeatedly delaminating at this location, review sealing and humidity control in the housing.
Cracked Glass Filter
Likely Cause
Thermal shock from rapid heat-up (e.g. halogen lamp start) acting on a filter not made of borosilicate glass.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crack pattern for a heat-shock signature radiating from the lamp-facing side; confirm the filter material specification against what was actually fitted.
Corrective Action
Replace with a borosilicate glass filter as specified, and check the fixture's thermal management (lamp wattage, spacing, ventilation) if cracking recurs.
Reduced Light Transmission / Dim Beam
Likely Cause
Contamination from dust, oil, or moisture film on the filter surface reducing transmission.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect for visible surface contamination or film; run a photometric intensity check to confirm the extent of the shortfall.
Corrective Action
Clean the filter with approved solvents and soft cloths, and re-verify intensity; investigate the contamination source if it recurs quickly.
Hazy or Cloudy Appearance on a Dichroic Filter
Likely Cause
Thin-film coating delamination from prolonged humidity exposure or repeated thermal cycling breaking down the coating's adhesion.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coating under angled light for visible flaking, bubbling, or haze distinct from surface dirt; confirm the filter housing seal is intact.
Corrective Action
Replace the filter, and confirm the housing seal is properly fitted to reduce humidity exposure to the replacement coating.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Filter Removal
Electrical Safety

Isolate the fixture and allow the lamp or LED module to cool before removing the filter; a live circuit or hot lamp behind the filter is a shock and burn hazard during replacement.

Thermal Shock Avoidance
Handling

Do not apply a cold cleaning solution to a hot filter or install a filter immediately after a halogen lamp change; allow the fixture to reach ambient temperature to avoid thermal-shock cracking.

Coating-Safe Handling
Workmanship

Clean the filter with a soft cloth and manufacturer-approved cleaner rather than an abrasive pad or solvent, and avoid touching a dichroic-coated surface directly, since fingerprints and abrasion accelerate coating delamination.

Seal Integrity on Reinstall
Workmanship

Inspect the filter's housing seal or gasket for compression set or cracking before reinstalling, since a poor seal accelerates humidity-driven coating delamination.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for the removed filter, gasket, and fasteners at all times during handling, and secure or remove any loose material before returning the fixture to service to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Verify Chromaticity After Replacement
Compliance

After a filter replacement, verify the light output still meets the applicable chromaticity limits before closing out the work, since a mismatched or degraded filter can put the fixture out of tolerance.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return a fixture to service with a cracked, hazed, or delaminating filter; confirm the filter is fully seated and the fixture meets its photometric requirement before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Dichroic Coatings Degrade Before the Substrate Does
Compliance

A dichroic-coated filter's spectral performance can drift measurably from coating degradation years before the glass or coating shows any visible defect; verify chromaticity at overhaul rather than relying on a visual check of the coating's appearance.

Fingerprints Are a Long-Term Failure Mode, Not Just a Cosmetic Issue
Workmanship

Oils from bare-hand handling can etch into a dichroic coating over time under heat cycling, leaving a permanent mark that a cleaning afterward cannot remove; handle coated filters by the edges with gloves from the first touch, not just during cleaning.

Coating Adhesion Weakens With Repeated Thermal Cycling Near a Hot Source
Handling

Filters mounted close to a hot halogen or high-drive LED source undergo more thermal cycling than the fixture housing itself, which can accelerate coating delamination compared to a filter's rated lifespan under lower-heat conditions.

Humidity Ingress Attacks the Coating Edge First
Durability

Dichroic coating delamination typically starts at the filter's edge where moisture can wick in under a compromised seal, rather than across the coated face; inspect edge condition specifically when checking a filter for early degradation.

A Colour Complaint May Be the Filter, Not the Source
Troubleshooting

A fixture reported as showing an off colour is often assumed to be a lamp or LED module issue first; check filter chromaticity and coating condition before replacing the light source, since a degraded filter produces the same symptom.

Reinstall in the Same Orientation, Even for Symmetric-Looking Filters
Workmanship

Some optical filters have a coating applied to only one face; installing one reversed can change both the spectral output and the coating's exposure to heat from the source side, even if the filter looks identical from either side.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL optical filters: colour control, filter types, chromaticity, standards, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is an optical filter in an AGL fixture?

An optical filter selectively transmits certain wavelengths and absorbs or reflects others to give the fixture its required colour, such as green for threshold and taxiway centreline, red for runway end and stop bars, and yellow for clearance bars.

Q2Why does the exact colour matter?

These colours carry operational meaning to pilots and are not decorative. A pilot who sees red or green light on the runway is meant to draw a specific conclusion, so the colour must stay inside the specified chromaticity limits.

Q3What are the main types of optical filter?

Absorptive filters use dyes or pigments to absorb unwanted colours. They are simple, robust and inexpensive but can fade with UV and heat. Dichroic filters use multiple thin-film coatings that reflect unwanted wavelengths by interference.

Q4Where are optical filters used?

In virtually every colour-specific AGL fixture, including green threshold and wing bar lights, red runway end lights, green taxiway centreline lights, red stop bars and yellow clearance bars.

Q5What are typical optical filter specifications?

Peak transmission is above 80% for green, red and yellow and above 70% for blue. Materials are optical glass or polycarbonate with dyes, or dichroic coated glass, typically 1-3 mm thick, with an operating range of -40°C to +100°C for glass and -40°C to +80°C for polycarbonate.

Q6How is filter colour specified?

Colour is defined by chromaticity boundary equations in the CIE 1931 diagram rather than a simple x/y box. For example, FAA Engineering Brief #67 (ICAO-modified) bounds LED aviation green with three boundary lines for the blue, white and yellow sides.

Q7Which standards apply to optical filters?

ICAO Annex 14 (chromaticity coordinates), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (colour and photometric requirements tested with the qualified fixture, filter included), IEC/TS 61827, and SAE AS25050 (chromaticity and transmission for aeronautical lights and light-transmitting materials).

Q8How often should optical filters be checked?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cleaning dust, oil or moisture film, semi-annual for thermal-stress cracking near mounting edges, and annual for colour and transmission checks and dichroic coating condition.

Q9Why is a fixture's colour outside the chromaticity specification?

Likely causes are UV degradation fading an absorptive plastic filter, or delamination of a dichroic coating from humidity or thermal cycling. Run a photometric colour check against the ICAO/FAA limits and visually inspect the filter for fading or peeling.
