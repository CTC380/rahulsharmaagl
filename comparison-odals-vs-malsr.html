Approach & Landing Systems Comparison

ODALS VS MALSR

Omnidirectional Approach Lighting System vs Medium-Intensity Approach Lighting System with Runway Alignment Indicator Lights — both are FAA approach lighting systems, but they solve the visual approach problem in fundamentally different ways. ODALS is seven omnidirectional flashers (five on the extended centreline plus two at the threshold) with no steady-burning lights; MALSR is a linear array of steady-burning bars with a crossbar and five RAIL flashers.

02 · Definitions

What Is ODALS & MALSR?

ODALS

ODALS stands for Omnidirectional Approach Lighting System. It is an FAA approach lighting system for nonprecision runways, built from seven omnidirectional sequenced flashing lights: five on the extended runway centreline (the first 300 ft from the threshold, then every 300 ft out to 1,500 ft) and two at the threshold, one on each side of the runway. It has no steady-burning bar array like MALSR. The FAA no longer installs ODALS as a new facility.

Main functions

  • Runway identification
  • Circling, offset and straight-in approach guidance
  • Azimuth/alignment information
  • Sequenced flashing visual cues

MALSR

MALSR stands for Medium-Intensity Approach Lighting System with Runway Alignment Indicator Lights. It is a medium-intensity linear approach lighting system that provides a visual extension of the runway centreline into the approach area. It consists of a MALS portion (a green threshold bar, seven five-lamp bars at 200 ft spacing out to 1,400 ft, and a 1,000 ft crossbar) plus five RAIL flashers out to 2,400 ft.

Main functions

  • Medium-intensity steady-burning approach lights
  • Centreline lighting
  • Crossbar
  • Runway Alignment Indicator Lights (RAIL)
03 · Key Difference

Omnidirectional + Flashing vs Linear + RAIL

The easiest way to remember the difference between ODALS and MALSR:

ODALS

Omnidirectional + Flashing + Runway Identification

MALSR

Medium Intensity + Linear Centreline + RAIL

Engineering point: ODALS helps the pilot identify and align with the runway from the approach environment. MALSR provides a structured visual path along the runway approach centreline.

05 · When Each Is Considered

Choosing Between ODALS and MALSR

ODALS is considered where

  • Runway identification is the priority
  • A relatively compact system is needed
  • Available approach land is limited
  • Terrain creates constraints
  • A conventional extended ALS is impractical

MALSR is considered where

  • The approved FAA design requires medium-intensity lighting
  • A linear centreline approach reference is needed
  • RAIL is required
  • Strong runway alignment guidance is needed
  • A more extensive approach lighting installation is appropriate

Current FAA status: FAA Order JO 6850.2C states that ODALS is no longer installed as a new facility. New FAA-standard projects therefore choose from the other listed configurations; ODALS knowledge remains relevant for existing installations and for understanding legacy approach-lighting terminology.

06 · Alignment & Flashing

How Each System Guides the Pilot

ODALS visual function

ODALS provides alignment information through a line of five sequenced flashers on the extended centreline, with two threshold flashers marking the runway end. Because the flashers are omnidirectional they can be seen from circling and offset positions as well as straight-in, but there is no steady bar pattern to give roll or height cues. The sequenced flashing draws the pilot's attention toward the runway environment.

  • Directional/azimuth guidance
  • Sequenced flashing draws attention to the runway
  • Runway-end identification confirms threshold location

MALSR visual function

MALSR provides a strong linear alignment reference. The approach lights visually extend the runway centreline into the approach area, and Runway Alignment Indicator Lights (RAIL) reinforce that alignment with a sequenced flashing pattern.

  • Strong linear alignment
  • Centreline continuation into the approach area
  • RAIL reinforces alignment with sequenced flashing

08 · Electrical Engineering

Electrical Architecture

Both systems require appropriate electrical design, but the architecture differs. ODALS has flasher units only — five on the centreline and two at the threshold — with no steady-burning lamps, while MALSR combines steady-burning bars (through the transformers specified in JO 6850.2C ¶204) with RAIL flasher control. In the FAA-standard arrangement MALSR is fed from a 120/240 V single-phase supply through power and control equipment with three-step intensity control, rather than from a CCR series circuit; ODALS has its own power and control equipment with three flasher intensity steps.

ODALS — simplified concept

Power supply → ODALS power & control equipment (three flasher steps) → five centreline flashers + two threshold flashers (omnidirectional flashing light to FAA-E-2651).

MALSR — simplified concept

120/240 V supply → power & control equipment (three-step intensity) → transformers as specified → steady-burning approach fixtures, plus RAIL flasher control. A CCR series circuit applies only where the approved design specifies one.

The actual circuit configuration must follow the applicable current FAA design.

10 · Photometric Testing

Photometric & Alignment Checks

ODALS verification

  • Flashing-light intensity
  • Flash sequence
  • Runway-end identifier performance
  • Alignment and colour
  • Visibility from the intended approach sector

Because ODALS is designed around an omnidirectional concept, angular performance is particularly important.

MALSR verification

  • Steady-burning intensity
  • RAIL intensity
  • Flash sequence
  • Centreline and crossbar alignment
  • Colour and beam orientation
11 · Installation Footprint

Installation Considerations

One advantage of ODALS is its relatively compact approach installation — a 1,500 ft system needing land 1,700 ft long by 400 ft wide. It can be useful where available approach land is limited, terrain creates constraints, a conventional linear ALS cannot be installed, or a shorter approach lighting system is appropriate.

MALSR requires a more extensive approach-lighting footprint (1,600 × 400 ft for the MALS portion plus a 1,000 ft × 25 ft RAIL corridor, widenable to 100 ft where visibility, obstruction clearance or access require it), needing coordination for centreline, crossbar, RAIL units, light supports, cable routes and approach clearances.

15 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: ODALS is just a shorter MALSR
Fact

No. ODALS has a different system concept and uses runway-end identification and omnidirectional visual guidance rather than a scaled-down linear array.

Myth: ODALS has no flashing lights
Fact

Flashing visual guidance is an important part of ODALS — sequenced flashing lights are central to its concept.

Myth: MALSR is simply a brighter ODALS
Fact

No. MALSR is a medium-intensity linear approach lighting system with RAIL — a different configuration philosophy, not just an intensity upgrade.

Myth: ODALS automatically means a lower approach category
Fact

The approach category is determined by the complete operational system and applicable criteria, not simply by the ALS name.

Myth: All flashing approach systems are equivalent
Fact

The flashing arrangement, light positions, intensity, function and overall system configuration can be very different between systems.

16 · Engineering Selection

Choosing Between ODALS and MALSR

When selecting between ODALS and MALSR, consider:

  • Approach category and runway operational requirements
  • Applicable FAA criteria (JO 6850.2C and AC 150/5340-30) — noting that the FAA no longer installs ODALS as a new facility
  • Available approach area and land constraints
  • Terrain and obstacles
  • Required visual guidance philosophy — identification vs linear alignment
  • Electrical infrastructure
  • Installation and maintenance requirements
  • Airport-specific technical specifications
17 · Engineering Workflow

From Requirement to Commissioning

Determine approach requirement → identify FAA ALS requirement → evaluate approach area → select ODALS/MALSR → prepare approach layout → determine light positions → design power and control circuits → select power/control equipment → select transformers where specified → design control & flashing system → install fixtures → alignment → electrical testing → flasher functional testing → photometric verification → commissioning.

18 · Engineering Verdict

Which System Applies Where?

Engineering Verdict

ODALS and MALSR are not simply two versions of the same approach lighting system. ODALS focuses on omnidirectional approach guidance and runway identification using a relatively compact arrangement. MALSR provides a linear, medium-intensity approach reference with RAIL to strengthen runway alignment.

The final selection should always be based on the current applicable FAA criteria and approved airport design — not on a generic comparison alone.

ODALSIdentify and guide toward the runway using compact, omnidirectional visual guidance.
MALSREstablish a strong linear visual path to the runway with medium-intensity lights and RAIL.

19 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is ODALS just a scaled-down MALSR?

No. ODALS uses an omnidirectional flashing and runway-end identification concept, while MALSR is a linear medium-intensity system with RAIL — they are different system philosophies, not different sizes of the same design.

What is the biggest difference between ODALS and MALSR?

ODALS provides omnidirectional identification and flashing guidance without a conventional long centreline; MALSR provides a strong linear centreline extension reinforced by RAIL.

Do both systems use flashing lights?

Yes, but differently. ODALS uses sequenced flashing as a core identification cue, while MALSR uses RAIL — flashing lights that reinforce an already-established linear centreline.

Which system has a smaller footprint?

ODALS: a 1,500 ft system needing 1,700 × 400 ft of land, versus MALSR's 2,400 ft (1,600 × 400 ft plus a 1,000 × 25 ft RAIL corridor). This makes ODALS useful where approach land is constrained.

Does choosing ODALS mean a lower approach category?

Not automatically. Approach category is determined by the complete operational system and applicable FAA criteria, not simply by the ALS name.

Does MALSR have runway-end identifier lights?

Not as flashing REIL-type units. MALSR marks the runway end with a green threshold bar, and runway-end identification is not its defining feature — the runway is identified through its linear approach lighting and associated runway lighting.

Does the FAA still install ODALS?

No. FAA Order JO 6850.2C states that ODALS is no longer installed as a new facility, although existing installations and the terminology remain relevant.

Applicability Note

These are FAA-defined systems. On ICAO/DGCA-regulated aerodromes (including Indian airports), approach lighting is specified as simple or precision (CAT I/II/III) approach lighting under ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, so FAA names such as MALS, MALSF, MALSR and ODALS do not apply directly.
