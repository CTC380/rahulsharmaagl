Low Intensity Obstruction Light

A steady‑burning red aviation warning light used on structures up to 45 metres in height, providing nighttime obstruction marking. Low intensity obstruction lights are the most common type of aviation warning light, offering reliable, low‑power visual warning for towers, masts, chimneys, buildings, and other obstacles that pose a hazard to low‑flying aircraft.

Overview

What Is It?

A Low Intensity Obstruction Light is a steady‑burning red aviation warning light installed on structures that are less than 45 metres (150 feet) in height, or as an intermediate light on taller structures. It provides a continuous red visual warning to pilots during nighttime operations, indicating the presence of an obstacle that could pose a collision hazard.

These lights are the most widely used type of obstruction lighting, found on communication towers, wind turbines, chimneys, buildings, and other vertical structures. They are characterised by their low power consumption, long service life (particularly in LED variants), and simple, reliable operation. Low intensity lights are typically rated at 32 candela minimum intensity, making them visible at distances of several kilometres in clear conditions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Low intensity lighting exists to give shorter structures — those under 45 metres — a simple, minimum-cost warning proportionate to the actual level of hazard they present, rather than requiring every vertical structure regardless of height to carry the same lighting infrastructure as a major tower. A steady-burning signal, rather than a flashing one, is sufficient at this height because the hazard a short structure presents is comparatively localized and doesn't require the extra conspicuity a flashing pattern provides for taller, more distant obstacles. Its purpose is also served by simplicity and low power draw: because this is by far the most numerous category of obstruction light, deployed across huge numbers of towers, chimneys, and buildings, a design that is inexpensive, reliable, and low-maintenance is what makes obstruction marking practical at this scale in the first place.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Low intensity obstruction lights are installed on a wide range of structures:

  • Communication towers – mobile phone masts, radio/TV broadcast towers.
  • Wind turbines – as intermediate or top lights.
  • Chimneys and smokestacks – industrial and power plant structures.
  • Buildings – high‑rise structures that exceed height thresholds.
  • Transmission towers – electrical power line pylons.
  • Masts and poles – meteorological, navigational, and utility masts.
  • As intermediate lights on tall structures – in combination with medium or high intensity lights.
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are mounted at the top of the structure and, where used as an intermediate light on a taller structure, at the levels specified by the applicable lighting scheme, typically on a simple bracket suited to the structure type — a pole-top mount on masts and poles, a facade bracket on buildings, or a lattice-arm mount on towers. Given the low power draw and light weight of these fixtures, mounting hardware is comparatively simple, though it still needs to hold the light clear of the structure's own members so it isn't shadowed from any direction. Cabling is run along the structure back to a photocell or timer, often self-contained within the fixture itself in modern LED variants, which simplifies installation on structures that lack an existing power and control infrastructure. Because this is the most numerous category of obstruction light, installations are frequently standardized across large numbers of similar structures (towers, poles, short buildings) using the same bracket and wiring arrangement at each site.

Function

How Does It Work?

Low intensity obstruction lights operate on a simple but essential principle:

  • Light Source: Traditionally using a low‑wattage incandescent or halogen lamp (typically 10–25W), modern systems increasingly use high‑brightness LEDs for improved energy efficiency and longer life.
  • Colour: The light emits a steady red beam, which is the internationally recognised colour for obstruction warning. Red is chosen for its high visibility against the night sky and its minimal impact on night vision.
  • Beam Pattern: Most low intensity obstruction lights are omnidirectional, emitting light uniformly in all horizontal directions (360°) to ensure visibility from any approach angle.
  • Operation: The light operates automatically, typically controlled by a photoelectric sensor (day/night switch) that turns the light on at dusk and off at dawn. Some systems are controlled remotely via a central monitoring system.
  • Power: Low intensity lights are typically powered from the local mains supply (110/230V AC) or via a dedicated solar‑powered system for remote locations.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Low intensity obstruction lights carry the simplest control architecture on the airfield: there is no shared circuit or central controller involved in day-to-day operation. Each fixture is powered directly from local mains or a dedicated solar-battery system, and a photoelectric day/night sensor built into (or wired directly to) the fixture is the entire control loop — it switches the lamp on at dusk and off at dawn with no other input required. Where a taller structure layers a low intensity light in among medium or high intensity fixtures above (as shown opposite), each level still runs its own independent photocell cycle rather than being synchronised to the others, because a steady always-on-at-night signal doesn't need coordination the way flashing tiers do. Some installations wire the light into a central or remote monitoring system so a lamp failure is reported to maintenance staff without a site visit, but that link is purely for status reporting — remove it and the fixture keeps switching on its local photocell exactly as before.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is a compact, weatherproof unit housed in a moulded polymer or die-cast metal enclosure, sized for straightforward surface- or pole-mounting on shorter structures, guy wires, or building edges where a steady-burning low-intensity marker is sufficient. A red lens covers the LED array or low-wattage incandescent/halogen lamp, gasket-sealed to achieve an IP65/IP67 rating that keeps out dust and driven rain despite the fixture's often unsheltered mounting position. The housing integrates a simple mounting bracket or threaded base allowing quick attachment and orientation for omnidirectional beam coverage, with a compact internal compartment for any driver electronics and a sealed cable gland for the incoming power connection, whether mains AC, low-voltage DC, or an integrated solar-and-battery supply. Given the light's typically lower mounting height and easier access, the housing favours simplicity and low maintenance over the heavier sealing and structural bracing used on high-intensity fixtures, while still using corrosion-resistant fasteners and UV-stable materials for long outdoor service life.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lamp Burnout (Incandescent Systems)

Traditional incandescent or halogen lamps have a limited life (typically 2,000–5,000 hours) and require regular replacement. Failure to replace burned‑out lamps can leave structures unmarked and create a significant aviation hazard. LED systems largely eliminate this issue.

Photocell Failure

The day/night sensor (photocell) can fail due to dirt, moisture, or age, causing the light to stay on during the day or not turn on at night. Regular inspection and cleaning of the photocell are essential.

Lens Contamination

Dust, dirt, bird droppings, and environmental pollutants can reduce light output and compromise visibility. Routine cleaning with appropriate materials is necessary to maintain performance.

Moisture Ingress

Seals and gaskets can degrade over time, allowing moisture to enter the housing. This can cause corrosion, short circuits, or optical degradation. Regular inspection of seals and prompt replacement is recommended.

LED Degradation

Even LED systems experience gradual lumen depreciation over time. After several years of operation, light output may fall below the minimum required intensity (32 cd). Periodic photometric testing is recommended to verify compliance.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Light Does Not Illuminate at Night
Likely Cause
Failed or burned-out lamp (incandescent systems) or a photocell stuck in the "day" position preventing the circuit from energising.
Diagnostic Steps
Bridge or bypass the photocell to confirm whether it is holding the circuit open; check lamp continuity or LED module supply voltage.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp or LED module as required, and replace the photocell if it fails to switch correctly against a reference light level.
Light Stays On During Daytime
Likely Cause
Photocell contamination or failure is preventing it from detecting daylight and opening the circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Clean the photocell lens and re-test switching against ambient light; check for stuck relay contacts in the control circuit.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the photocell and confirm the control relay operates correctly through a full day/night cycle.
Reduced Light Output Below Minimum Intensity (32 cd)
Likely Cause
Gradual LED lumen depreciation or lens contamination has brought output below the required minimum.
Diagnostic Steps
Take a photometric reading and compare against the 32 cd minimum; inspect the lens for dirt, pitting, or UV yellowing.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with an approved cleaner; if intensity remains below minimum after cleaning, replace the LED module and re-verify with a follow-up reading.
Water Ingress and Corrosion
Likely Cause
Degraded gaskets or a cracked lens seal has allowed moisture into the housing, leading to corrosion or short circuits.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect seals and gaskets for hardening or cracking; check for condensation, corrosion, or tracking marks inside the housing.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded seals and gaskets, dry out the housing, and megger the circuit before re-energising.
Frequent Lamp Replacement (Incandescent Systems)
Likely Cause
Voltage above the rated supply, or vibration-induced filament fatigue, is shortening lamp life below the expected 2,000-5,000 hour range.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure supply voltage at the fixture against the rated value; check mounting for excess vibration.
Corrective Action
Correct supply voltage if out of tolerance, fit vibration-damping mounts, or consider converting to an LED retrofit module to eliminate filament fatigue.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Solar-Powered Units Fail Quietly Through Winter
Reliability

A solar-powered low-intensity light can run down its battery reserve gradually across a run of short, overcast winter days without any single fault event, then go dark on the one night the reserve finally runs out. Check panel output and battery state of charge with the season's actual daylight hours in mind, not just against a single generic charge-verification pass/fail.

Vegetation and Debris Shading Are the Most Common Real-World Fault
Inspection

On low, easily-reached structures, the single most common cause of a "failed" light is shading of the solar panel or photocell by nearby growth or accumulated debris rather than an actual component fault. Check for physical shading first on a reported outage before escalating to electrical diagnostics on this fixture type.

As an Intermediate Light, Its Failure Mode Depends on the Parent Structure
Design

When this fixture serves as an intermediate marker on a taller structure rather than a standalone low obstruction, its failure removes only one level of the overall marking scheme rather than darkening the structure entirely, and needs to be assessed against the full-height marking requirement, not treated as an isolated fault.

Steady-Burning Function Test Catches What an Intensity Reading Alone Misses
Testing

A light can measure within intensity spec on a momentary check yet still exhibit a subtle flicker or intermittent dropout under sustained operation, particularly as an LED driver or lamp ages. Run the steady-burning function test over a real observation period rather than relying on a single instantaneous photometric reading.

Lens Cleaning Frequency Should Match the Site, Not a Fixed Interval
Maintenance

A low-mounted light near a road, farm, or industrial site accumulates dirt and grime at a very different rate from the same fixture on a clean rooftop, so a single fixed lens-cleaning interval either over- or under-services most installations. Set the interval from observed fouling rate at each site rather than a generic schedule.

Lamp/LED Replacement Checks Are Worth Doing Even Without a Reported Fault
Preventive

Because these fixtures are numerous and individually low-consequence, a single failed unit can go unreported far longer than a high-intensity fixture would. Include a proactive lamp/LED condition check in routine rounds rather than relying solely on outage reports to identify failed units across a large population of low-intensity lights.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about low intensity obstruction lights: when they are used, colour, intensity, power options, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a low intensity obstruction light?

A low intensity obstruction light is a steady-burning red aviation warning light fitted to structures under 45 m (150 ft) in height, or as an intermediate light on taller structures. It is the most widely used type of obstruction lighting, with low power consumption and long life, particularly in LED versions.

Q2Which structures use low intensity obstruction lights?

Communication towers, wind turbines, chimneys, buildings, transmission towers, and masts and poles such as meteorological and utility masts. They are also used as intermediate lights alongside medium or high intensity lights on tall structures.

Q3What colour and intensity are low intensity obstruction lights?

They show steady aviation red with a minimum intensity of 32 cd per ICAO and up to about 200 cd depending on the model. Red is used for its visibility against the night sky and its minimal effect on night vision.

Q4Does a low intensity obstruction light flash?

No. It is steady-burning, which is sufficient because a structure under 45 m presents a comparatively local hazard. The light runs at constant intensity and is switched dusk to dawn by a photocell.

Q5What is the beam pattern of a low intensity obstruction light?

It is omnidirectional with 360° horizontal coverage. The vertical beam is wide and low-angle, typically 10° to 30° above horizontal, suited to close-range marking.

Q6How are low intensity obstruction lights powered?

They can run on 110–230 V AC mains, 12/24 V DC or a solar panel with battery storage. Power consumption is only 5–25 W for LED and 15–50 W for incandescent, low enough for solar or battery operation at remote sites.

Q7Which standards apply to low intensity obstruction lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment) and FAA AC 70/7460-1 (obstruction marking and lighting). Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q8How often should low intensity obstruction lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for steady-burning function tests and solar panel and battery checks, and annual for photometric intensity (at or above 32 cd), seal and weatherproofing and cable and power connection inspection. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9How long do low intensity obstruction light lamps last?

LED versions last 50,000–100,000 hours. Incandescent or halogen lamps typically last only 2,000–5,000 hours, and an unreplaced burnt-out lamp leaves the structure unmarked, so LED is now the usual choice.

Q10Why does a low intensity obstruction light not come on at night or stay on in the day?

Both faults usually trace to the photocell. A photocell stuck in the day position keeps the circuit open, while a dirty or failed one stays on in daylight. Bridge or bypass the photocell to confirm, clean its lens, and check lamp continuity or LED module supply voltage.
