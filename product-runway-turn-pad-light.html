Product Overview

Runway Turn Pad Light

What It Is

Runway turn pad lights provide visual guidance along a runway turn pad and help an aircraft complete a turn and align with the runway centreline. A turn pad is a defined pavement area at or near the runway end that allows an aircraft to manoeuvre through a 180-degree turn where required.

Turn-pad lighting differs from conventional runway edge lighting: its purpose is continuous visual guidance through the turning manoeuvre, particularly where visibility conditions require enhanced visual information.

Selected According To

  • Turn-pad geometry
  • Runway configuration
  • Pavement marking arrangement
  • Visibility / RVR requirements
  • Straight and curved sections
  • Light spacing
  • Optical direction

  • Series-circuit characteristics
  • Inset or approved fixture configuration
  • Mounting and pavement requirements
  • Environmental conditions
  • Project technical specification

The final configuration should always be established from the approved aerodrome lighting layout and applicable standard.

Function of Runway Turn Pad Lighting

A Clear Visual Path Through the Turning Manoeuvre

What the Lights Do

During a 180-degree turn the aircraft heading changes continuously, so the lighting must give visual information that follows the geometry of the marked turn path.

It should be treated as a complete visual-aid system, not simply a light fixture.

An Effective System Provides

  • Continuous visual guidance
  • Clear identification of the intended turn path
  • Directional visibility from the aircraft
  • Correct light colour
  • Appropriate intensity
  • Adequate spacing
  • Consistent optical alignment
  • Compatibility with the AGL electrical circuit
  • Reliable performance in airport conditions
Light Colour & Optics

Green, Fixed, Unidirectional

ICAO Characteristics

ICAO specifies runway turn-pad lights as fixed, unidirectional green lights, with beam dimensions arranged so that the lights are visible only from aircraft on or approaching the turn pad.

Optical directionality is therefore a defining feature of the product.

Check the Fixture For

  • Green light output
  • Unidirectional operation
  • Horizontal beam coverage
  • Vertical beam coverage
  • Optical axis
  • Intensity
  • Colour / chromaticity
  • Viewing sector
  • Direction of intended aircraft movement

Do not select on colour alone. The complete optical distribution and viewing geometry must match the approved design.

Turn Pad Lighting Layout

Lights Follow the Marked Turn Path

Layout Concept

Lights follow the geometry of the runway turn-pad marking, from the entry, around the curve, and into alignment with the runway centreline.

Turn Pad Entry → Curved Turn Path → Runway Centreline

Actual fixture positions must be determined from the approved turn-pad marking and project lighting layout.

Coordinate The Layout With

  • Turn-pad radius
  • Runway centreline
  • Pavement edge
  • Turn-pad marking
  • Aircraft design group
  • Aircraft turning path
  • Entry / exit geometry
  • Existing runway lighting
  • Adjacent taxiway lighting

The layout should follow the approved pavement geometry, not be determined independently from the marking and aircraft movement path.

Turn Pad Edge Lighting

Guidance Lights vs Edge Lights

Do Not Confuse the Two

Turn-pad guidance lighting is not turn-pad edge lighting. ICAO identifies taxiway edge lights, with blue fixed characteristics, at the edges of turn pads intended for night use.

Turn Pad Guidance Lights → Green, directional
Turn Pad Edge Lights → Blue, edge / perimeter

Project Scope

A project may include both systems. The required combination should be determined from the approved project lighting design.

Safety

Electrical Safety — Isolation and Lock-Out

WARNING — never open a live series circuit. An open series circuit driven by a constant-current regulator can produce dangerously high voltage. Opening, cutting or disconnecting any part of an energised circuit can cause serious injury or death and damage equipment.

  • De-energise the circuit at the regulator and apply the site lock-out / tag-out (LOTO) procedure before any work
  • Where the circuit must be isolated or shorted, use the series circuit cut-out switch or approved isolation device — not an improvised bypass
  • Verify the circuit is de-energised and the output is isolated before touching conductors, connectors, splices, transformers or fixtures
  • Do not disconnect or reconnect a fixture's secondary connector while the series circuit is energised: an open secondary on a series isolating transformer can develop hazardous voltage and damage the transformer
  • Work on the movement area only under the airport's permit-to-work system, with the runway or taxiway closed as required, using qualified personnel and the manufacturer's instructions
  • Follow the applicable national electrical safety regulations and site procedures

FAQ

Runway Turn Pad Light — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a runway turn pad light?

Runway turn pad lights provide continuous visual guidance along a runway turn pad, helping an aircraft complete a turn and align with the runway centreline. A turn pad is a defined pavement area at or near the runway end that allows an aircraft to manoeuvre through a 180-degree turn where required. The lighting should follow the geometry of the marked turn path and be treated as a complete visual-aid system, not simply a light fixture.

When are turn pad lights required?

ICAO specifies turn-pad lights for continuous guidance on turn pads intended for use in RVR conditions below 350 m, and recommends them for turn pads intended for use at night.

What colour are runway turn pad lights?

ICAO specifies runway turn-pad lights as fixed, unidirectional green lights, with beam dimensions arranged so that the lights are visible only from aircraft on or approaching the turn pad. Optical directionality is a defining feature, so do not select on colour alone, because the complete optical distribution and viewing geometry must match the approved design.

Where are turn pad lights placed, and how far apart?

Under ICAO provisions the lights are normally on the runway turn-pad marking, or offset by not more than 30 cm where a light cannot practicably be on the marking. Longitudinal spacing is not more than 15 m on straight sections and not more than 7.5 m on curved sections. Closer spacing on curves gives a more continuous indication of the aircraft’s changing direction, and the actual fixture positions must come from the approved turn-pad marking and project lighting layout.

Are turn pad guidance lights the same as turn pad edge lights?

No. Turn-pad guidance lights are green and directional, while turn-pad edge lights are blue and define the edge or perimeter. ICAO identifies taxiway edge lights, with blue fixed characteristics, at the edges of turn pads intended for night use. A project may include both systems, and the required combination should be determined from the approved lighting design.

How does a turn pad light differ from a taxiway edge light?

A turn pad light gives continuous guidance through the turn, is typically green and unidirectional, and sits on the turn-pad marking. A taxiway edge light identifies the edge or perimeter, is typically blue and omnidirectional unless the design specifies otherwise, and sits on the turn-pad or taxiway edge. They are not interchangeable.

Are turn pad lights inset or elevated?

They are supplied in an inset configuration, where the fixture is installed within the pavement and aligned with the turn-pad marking, and the fixture and housing should be treated as one coordinated installation assembly. A typical inset assembly includes the light fitting, in-pavement housing, optical lens, LED module, sealing gasket, mounting hardware, drainage arrangement, secondary cable, isolating transformer and primary connector. For inset installations, focus on water ingress, drainage, sealing, the pavement interface and mechanical loading.

How are turn pad lights powered?

A typical arrangement is a CCR feeding a primary AGL cable, an isolating transformer, a secondary cable and the turn pad light. Before procurement confirm the CCR current, number of fixtures, fixture electrical load, transformer rating, primary and secondary cable, connector configuration, intensity steps, monitoring arrangement and control-system interface. Poor electrical coordination can cause reduced light output, incorrect intensity, circuit instability, transformer stress, premature fixture failure or monitoring abnormalities.

What should be checked during installation and commissioning?

Check the approved turn-pad marking and geometry, runway centreline and pavement edge, fixture spacing on straight and curved sections, optical direction and beam axis, the aircraft viewing direction, and alignment with the turn-path geometry, as well as the housing and fixture seating, pavement cut-out and level, drainage, sealing and mounting. Commissioning covers circuit continuity, insulation resistance, transformer verification, series-circuit current, CCR operation, intensity steps, monitoring and photometric verification of colour, intensity, optical direction and beam distribution.

What are the common faults with turn pad lights?

A light that is off can come from an open series circuit, light-source failure, transformer failure, connector failure, a secondary cable fault or an internal fixture failure. Low output can come from an incorrect CCR setting, optical contamination, LED degradation, damaged optics or an electrical mismatch. An incorrect colour can come from incorrect fixture configuration, light-source degradation, optical damage, contamination or an incorrect replacement component, and an incorrect direction from incorrect installation or a misoriented optical assembly. Poor curve guidance can come from excessive spacing, incorrect curve alignment, a missing fixture or an incorrect layout.

Which standards apply to runway turn pad lights?

References include ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I (Chapter 5 on runway turn-pad lights), FAA AC 150/5340-30J, FAA AC 150/5345-46F, DGCA India requirements and the airport-specific AGL specification and approved layout. ICAO adopted Amendment 18 to Annex 14 Vol. I in 2025, with an applicable date of 27 November 2025 for the relevant visual-aid provisions, so check the applicable edition or amendment and the exact FAA fixture classification for each project.

Is it safe to work on a runway turn pad light while the series circuit is energised?

No. An open series circuit driven by a constant-current regulator can produce dangerously high voltage, and opening, cutting or disconnecting any part of an energised circuit can cause serious injury or death. Do not disconnect or reconnect a fixture’s secondary connector while the series circuit is energised, because an open secondary on a series isolating transformer can develop hazardous voltage and damage the transformer. De-energise the circuit at the regulator, apply the site lock-out / tag-out procedure, use the series circuit cut-out switch or an approved isolation device, and work on the movement area only under the airport’s permit-to-work system, with the runway or taxiway closed as required. See the electrical safety warning on this page.
