Cable Gland

A mechanical cable entry device that secures and seals the electrical cable as it passes through the housing of an AGL fixture. Provides strain relief, environmental sealing (IP67/IP68), and protection against moisture, dust, and mechanical pull forces.

Overview

What Is It?

A Cable Gland (also known as a cable connector or cable fitting) is a mechanical device designed to attach and secure the end of an electrical cable to the housing of an AGL fixture, junction box, or control panel. It provides three essential functions: strain relief (preventing the cable from being pulled out of the fixture), environmental sealing (preventing moisture, dust, and contaminants from entering the housing), and electrical continuity (often providing a conductive path for screen/earthing). Cable glands are typically made from brass (nickel-plated), stainless steel, or engineering plastics, with elastomeric seals (O-rings, compression seals) that conform to the cable outer sheath to create a watertight and dust-tight seal. In AGL applications, glands must meet IP67/IP68 ingress protection ratings and withstand harsh environmental conditions, including temperature extremes, UV radiation, and chemical exposure.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Every sealed AGL enclosure has to have at least one hole cut into it somewhere for the power cable to get in, and that single opening is where the whole enclosure's IP67/IP68 rating is won or lost. The cable gland's purpose is to close that gap without compromising it: its elastomeric seals grip the cable's outer sheath tightly enough to keep the enclosure watertight and dust-tight at that entry point, while its mechanical clamp takes up any pulling, twisting, or vibration in the cable run so those forces are absorbed by the gland and housing rather than transmitted down the cable to the internal terminations. Where continuity of an earth or screen connection matters, the gland can also carry that electrical path — meaning a poorly fitted or wrong-size gland can defeat the fixture's environmental protection and its earthing at the same time, from a single point of failure.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Cable glands are used at every point where an electrical cable enters an AGL enclosure. Common applications include:

  • Fixture Cable Entries: At the bottom housing of runway, taxiway, threshold, and approach lights.
  • Junction Boxes: For cable terminations and splices in the series circuit.
  • Isolating Transformer Housings: For primary and secondary cable entries.
  • CCR Cabinets: For power input and output cable entry points.
  • ALCMS Control Panels: For communication and power cable entries.
  • Connector Enclosures: For L-823 connector assemblies.
  • Obstruction Light Housings: For power and control cable entries.
  • Wind Cone and Beacon Housings: For power supply cable entry.
Placement

Installation & Location

The gland fits into a threaded or knockout port in the enclosure wall — a fixture's base can, a junction box, or a transformer housing — with the cable's outer sheath clamped inside the gland body so the seal grips the cable jacket rather than the conductors themselves. Gland size is matched to the cable's actual outer diameter rather than a generic fitting, since an oversized gland can't seal properly and an undersized one will crush the cable jacket; orientation is set, where the enclosure allows, so the cable enters from below or the side rather than directly from above, reducing the chance of water tracking down the cable into the gland. Torque on the gland's compression nut is checked to the manufacturer's value and re-checked after the cable has settled in, since underlying cable stress can loosen the gland's grip within the first weeks after installation.

Function

How Does It Work?

A cable gland works by clamping the cable's outer sheath when the gland nut is tightened. The internal components work together to create a secure and sealed cable entry:

  • Compression Seal: An elastomeric sealing ring (grommet) is compressed against the cable sheath when the gland nut is tightened, creating a watertight seal that conforms to the cable's outer diameter.
  • Strain Relief: The clamping action of the gland body and nut prevents the cable from being pulled out of the housing, protecting the internal electrical connections from mechanical stress.
  • O-Ring Seal (Housing Interface): An O-ring or flat gasket between the gland body and the housing provides a seal against moisture ingress at the threaded interface.
  • Screen/Earth Continuity: In metallic glands, a conductive path can be established through the gland body to provide earthing for screened cables and EMI protection.
  • Swivel or Fixed: Some glands feature a swivel design allowing cable orientation adjustment, while others are fixed-angle.

Proper installation requires matching the gland's cable diameter range to the actual cable diameter, and tightening the nut to the specified torque to achieve the required IP rating without damaging the cable.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The cable gland has no control-logic role — it is a passive sealing point at the boundary between the external field cable run and the protected internal space of whatever enclosure it serves (a base can, CCR cabinet, or junction box). It clamps the cable's outer sheath and seals the entry hole, so its job is to keep the enclosure's interior dry and to relieve strain on the cable's conductors right at the point they cross into the enclosure. Every enclosure containing a cable-fed component in the AGL system uses this same boundary role — the gland has no signal of its own, and its condition (seal integrity, correct cable-diameter match) is checked visually or by inspection rather than through the ALCMS.

A passive seal at the enclosure boundary — its condition is confirmed by inspection, not by any signal to the ALCMS.

Construction

Physical Construction

The gland body is machined from nickel-plated brass, stainless steel, or moulded polyamide, with a threaded barrel (PG or metric) that screws into the fixture housing or junction box wall and a compression nut at the cable-entry end. Inside, a soft elastomeric sealing ring, silicone, EPDM, or nitrile, is compressed radially around the cable jacket as the nut is tightened, forming the primary seal against water and dust ingress while a separate O-ring seals the gland body to the enclosure wall. An internal shoulder or clamping insert grips the cable to provide strain relief, so pulling forces on the cable are transferred to the gland body rather than the internal terminations. Where specified, a captive metal cone or braid clamp makes contact with the cable's outer screen to provide a bonded earth connection through the gland body itself. Properly torqued and seated against its enclosure gasket, the assembly achieves its rated IP67/IP68 protection.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Seal Compression Check
Annual

Verify the gland nut is torqued so the internal seal compresses fully around the cable, maintaining the IP67/IP68 rating.

Elastomer Seal Condition
Annual

Inspect silicone, EPDM, or nitrile sealing elements for hardening, cracking, or compression set, replacing where they no longer conform to the cable jacket.

Strain Relief Verification
Annual

Confirm the gland still grips the cable to its rated pull-out force, since a loosened strain relief transfers load onto internal terminations.

Thread & Corrosion Check
Annual

Check gland body threads and housing interface for corrosion or cross-threading damage that could compromise the ingress seal.

Earth / Screen Continuity Test
Annual

Where fitted, verify the earth tail or screen clamp maintains continuity to the enclosure earth point.

Cable Diameter Match Review
Annual

Confirm the installed cable diameter still falls within the gland's rated clamping range after any cable replacement or re-termination work.

Chemical Exposure Inspection
Annual

Check for swelling or discoloration from de-icing fluid, jet fuel, or hydraulic fluid exposure that can degrade seal material faster than its rated life.

UV & Material Degradation Check
Annual

Inspect plastic-bodied glands for UV embrittlement or chalking, particularly on exposed above-ground fixtures.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Moisture Ingress via Gland Seal

If the gland nut is not sufficiently tightened, or if the cable diameter is outside the gland's clamping range, moisture can enter the fixture through the cable entry. This is one of the most common causes of internal corrosion and short circuits. Always verify cable diameter matches the gland's specified range and tighten to the correct torque.

Corrosion of Metal Glands

In coastal or high-humidity environments, brass glands with nickel plating can corrode over time, especially if the plating is damaged. Use stainless steel glands in such environments for maximum durability.

UV Degradation of Plastic Glands

Plastic (polyamide) glands exposed to direct sunlight can become brittle and crack due to UV degradation. Use UV-stabilised plastic glands or metal glands for outdoor airfield installations.

Over-Tightening Damage

Excessive tightening of the gland nut can crush or deform the cable, damage the internal seal, or strip the threads. Always use a calibrated torque wrench and follow the manufacturer's specified torque values.

Incorrect Gland Selection

Using a gland with the wrong thread type (e.g., PG vs Metric) or the wrong cable diameter range can lead to poor sealing and mechanical failure. Always check the fixture's specifications and the cable's outer diameter before selecting a gland.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Moisture or Corrosion Found Inside the Fixture at the Cable Entry
Likely Cause
The gland nut was not sufficiently tightened, or the cable's outer diameter falls outside the gland's specified clamping range.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the cable's outer diameter against the gland's rated clamping range; inspect the gland nut for correct tightness and the seal for visible gaps.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with a gland matched to the actual cable diameter and tighten to the manufacturer's specified torque to restore a sealed entry.
Corroded or Discoloured Metal Gland
Likely Cause
Nickel-plated brass gland plating has been damaged or worn, exposing the base metal to a coastal or high-humidity environment.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the gland for pitting, discolouration, or visible plating damage; check the site's environmental classification against the gland material specified.
Corrective Action
Replace with a stainless steel gland rated for coastal/marine environments where corrosion is confirmed.
Plastic Gland Cracked or Brittle
Likely Cause
UV degradation from prolonged direct sunlight exposure has embrittled a standard polyamide gland.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the gland body for cracking, chalking, or brittleness; check whether the gland is UV-stabilised or a standard indoor-rated type.
Corrective Action
Replace with a UV-stabilised plastic gland or a metal gland for outdoor airfield installations exposed to direct sunlight.
Cable Crushed, Seal Damaged, or Threads Stripped at the Gland
Likely Cause
Over-tightening of the gland nut during installation, often from not using a calibrated torque wrench.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the cable jacket at the entry point for crushing or deformation; check the gland threads and seal for damage; confirm whether a calibrated torque wrench was used at installation.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged gland and, if crushed, the affected cable section; reinstall using a calibrated torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value.
Poor Seal or Mechanical Failure Despite Correct Torque
Likely Cause
The wrong gland was selected — mismatched thread type (e.g., PG vs. Metric) or an incorrect cable diameter range for the application.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the gland's thread type against the fixture's entry point specification, and confirm the cable's outer diameter falls within the gland's rated range.
Corrective Action
Replace with a gland correctly matched to both the fixture's thread type and the cable's actual outer diameter.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Seal Failure from Diameter Mismatch
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable outer diameter falls outside the gland's rated clamping range, or the gland nut was left under-tightened at installation.
Effect
Moisture works past the seal and enters the fixture at the cable entry, leading to internal corrosion or an electrical fault.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for moisture or corrosion inside the fixture near the entry, and verification of cable diameter against the gland's rated range.
Metal Gland Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Nickel-plated brass gland plating worn or damaged, exposing the base metal to a coastal or high-humidity environment not matched to the material rating.
Effect
Progressive pitting and discolouration of the gland body, eventually threatening the seal and the mechanical connection at the entry.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for pitting, discolouration, or plating damage, cross-checked against the site's environmental classification.
Plastic Gland Embrittlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure on a standard (non-UV-stabilised) polyamide gland used in an outdoor airfield installation.
Effect
Gland body cracks or becomes brittle, compromising the seal and the mechanical grip on the cable.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for cracking or chalking on the gland body, and confirmation of whether the gland is UV-stabilised.
Over-Torque Damage at Installation
High Severity
Root Cause
Gland nut tightened without a calibrated torque wrench, applying excess clamping force during installation.
Effect
Cable jacket crushed at the entry point, seal deformed, or gland threads stripped, undermining both the seal and the mechanical hold.
Detection Method
Inspection of the cable jacket for crushing, and of the gland threads and seal for damage, at scheduled maintenance.
Gland Type Mismatch
Low Severity
Root Cause
Wrong gland selected for the application — mismatched thread type (e.g. PG vs. Metric) or a clamping range that doesn't fit the actual cable.
Effect
Poor seal or mechanical failure at the entry despite the gland nut being correctly torqued.
Detection Method
Verification of the gland's thread type against the fixture's entry specification, and the cable's outer diameter against the gland's rated range.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the circuit and verify zero energy at the connector before removing or refitting a cable gland at any fixture, junction box, or panel entry.

Correct Gland Torque & Sizing
Environmental Sealing

Fit the gland size matched to the cable outer diameter and torque to specification; under-torquing leaves an IP67/68 seal ineffective, while over-torquing can crush the cable jacket and damage the conductors inside.

Verify Seal Before Closing Up
Compliance

Inspect the O-ring or compression seal for damage before reassembly, and confirm the gland seats fully against the housing to restore the enclosure's ingress protection rating.

Avoid Dissimilar-Metal Contact
Corrosion Control

Match gland material to the housing and cable armour where specified, since mixed metals in a damp environment can set up galvanic corrosion that degrades the seal and earth continuity over time.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the circuit after gland replacement or re-termination before restoring power, since a nicked conductor or damaged seal can fault soon after re-energisation.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools when working on series-circuit terminations, and confirm any screen/earth continuity path through the gland is restored before reconnecting.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for old glands, seals, and fasteners removed during the work, and clear the site before closing out.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Gland Size Must Match the Actual Cable OD, Not the Nominal Size
Sealing

A gland selected against a cable's nominal diameter rather than its actual measured outer diameter — which varies by manufacturer and batch — can leave the compression seal under- or over-tightened, defeating the watertight function even when installed 'correctly.'

Verify Earthing Continuity Independently of Mechanical Tightness
Electrical

A gland that's mechanically snug and appears well-installed can still fail to provide reliable screen/earth continuity if the conductive path wasn't properly seated during assembly. Test continuity with a meter, don't assume it from correct-looking mechanical assembly.

Re-Torque Compression Nuts After the Cable's First Thermal Cycle
Durability

Cable sheathing can cold-flow slightly under compression during its first significant thermal cycle after installation, leaving the gland's grip looser than at initial torque. A follow-up check after the first season catches this before it becomes a strain-relief or sealing failure.

Match Gland Material to the Housing to Avoid Galvanic Corrosion
Material

Installing a brass gland into a housing designed for stainless (or vice versa) sets up galvanic corrosion at the threaded interface, which is difficult to spot until the gland seizes or the seal fails. Confirm material compatibility before substituting a gland from a different batch or supplier.

Bend Radius at the Gland Entry Affects Long-Term Seal Integrity
Installation

A cable bent too sharply immediately at the gland entry puts continuous lateral stress on the seal that a straight entry wouldn't; maintain the manufacturer's minimum bend radius from the gland face, not just from the enclosure wall.

Record Gland Type and Size at Installation for Future Cable Swaps
Documentation

When a cable is later replaced with a different type or diameter, having the original gland specification on record prevents a mismatch being installed by a crew unfamiliar with the original design intent.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the cable gland: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a cable gland?

A cable gland (also called a cable connector or cable fitting) attaches and secures the end of an electrical cable to the housing of an AGL fixture, junction box or control panel. It provides strain relief, environmental sealing against moisture and dust, and often a conductive path for screen or earthing.

Q2How does a cable gland seal and hold a cable?

Tightening the gland nut compresses an elastomeric seal against the cable sheath for a watertight seal and clamps the cable to prevent it pulling out. An O-ring or flat gasket seals the interface between the gland body and the housing.

Q3Where are cable glands used in AGL?

At every point where a cable enters an AGL enclosure: fixture cable entries, junction boxes, isolating transformer housings, CCR cabinets and ALCMS control panels.

Q4What materials and thread sizes are typical?

Materials are nickel-plated brass, 316L stainless steel or polyamide, with elastomer seals in silicone, EPDM or nitrile rubber. Threads are PG (PG7 to PG21) or metric (M16 to M32), with cable diameters from 3-8 mm (PG7) up to 18-28 mm (PG21).

Q5What ingress protection and strain relief do AGL cable glands provide?

They typically meet IP67 or IP68 when properly installed, operate from -40°C to +100°C depending on material, and provide strain relief of up to 100 N depending on gland size and cable type.

Q6Why must the gland match the cable diameter?

The compression seal must conform to the cable's outer sheath. A diameter mismatch is a leading cause of seal failure, and choosing the wrong gland type is a common installation error.

Q7Which standards apply to cable glands?

The main references are IEC 60529 (IP codes), IEC 62444 (cable gland performance), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (fixture cable entry requirements) and UL 514B for the US market. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should cable glands be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover seal compression, elastomer seal condition, strain relief, threads and corrosion, earth and screen continuity, cable diameter match, chemical exposure and UV and material degradation. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with cable glands?

Common problems are moisture ingress through the gland seal, corrosion of metal glands, UV degradation of plastic glands, over-tightening damage (crushed cable, damaged seal or stripped threads) and incorrect gland selection. Moisture or corrosion found inside a fixture at the cable entry is a typical symptom.
