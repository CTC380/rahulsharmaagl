NOTAMs (Notice to Airmen)

An AGL engineering and operational guide to aerodrome notices. A NOTAM is an aeronautical notice issued through the appropriate aviation information system to communicate the establishment, condition, change or removal of an aeronautical facility, service, procedure or hazard — when that information is timely and operationally significant. For Airfield Ground Lighting, a NOTAM is not simply an engineering maintenance record. It is an operational safety communication.

Overview

1. What Is a NOTAM?

NOTAM stands for Notice to Airmen. Its purpose is to provide timely operational information to aviation users about conditions that may affect flight operations. The exact circumstances requiring a NOTAM are determined by applicable aeronautical information regulations and the responsible aerodrome/aviation authority — not by an individual engineer's judgement alone.

Examples of what a NOTAM can cover:

Runway availability Taxiway availability Aerodrome lighting Approach lighting PAPI Runway edge lighting Runway centreline lighting Taxiway lighting Airfield signs Navigation facilities Aerodrome operational restrictions Temporary hazards Construction or maintenance activities
2. Why It Matters

Why NOTAMs Matter in AGL

An AGL defect can be more than a maintenance problem — it can change what visual guidance an aircraft actually receives. The engineering team identifies and communicates the technical condition through the airport's established reporting process; the authorized aeronautical information function determines and issues the operational notice.

4. Potential Triggers

When Can AGL Problems Become NOTAM-Relevant?

Whether a particular condition requires a NOTAM depends on its operational significance and the applicable aeronautical information procedures — not on the fact that a fault exists. Potential examples include:

Runway Lighting

  • Unavailable
  • Partially unavailable
  • Edge lights affected
  • Centreline lights affected

Taxiway Lighting

  • Unavailable
  • Stop bars affected
  • Temporary configuration changes

Approach Lighting

  • Unavailable or degraded system
  • Significant lighting restrictions

PAPI

  • Unavailable
  • Under maintenance / restriction

Airfield Signs

  • Unavailable
  • Lighting-related works affecting operations

The Governing Question

Not "is something wrong?" but "does this change published or expected operational conditions, per applicable procedure?"

9. A Subtle Trap

NOTAM and PAPI: "Light ON" ≠ "Correctly Serviceable"

PAPI is an important visual approach aid. Potential conditions include complete outage, one or more light units unavailable, misalignment, maintenance or an operational restriction. However, a PAPI that is physically illuminated is not necessarily operationally correct.

Light ON does not automatically mean PAPI correctly aligned and serviceable. Technical verification should therefore accompany operational reporting.

Same Illuminated Unit, Two Different Technical States

A powered, visibly lit PAPI can still present a false glide-path indication — the fault is optical, not electrical, so it will not show up as "light off"

10. Airfield Signs & 11–12. Planned vs Emergency

Signs, Planned Maintenance & Emergency Failure

Illuminated Signs

Operational importance depends on location, sign function and applicable airport procedures.

  • Sign completely unavailable
  • Sign partially illuminated
  • Incorrect sign displayed
  • Sign electrical failure
  • Sign undergoing replacement or temporary removal

Emergency AGL Failure

Speed matters, but accuracy matters equally. Never communicate an unverified technical assumption as an operational fact.

  • Immediate detection → confirm condition
  • Notify airport operations
  • Assess operational impact
  • Initiate applicable aeronautical information procedure
  • Rectify → test → restore → update/cancel

Planned maintenance workflow: Maintenance Plan → Affected AGL Identified → Operational Impact Assessed → Airport Operations / ATC / AIS Coordination → Required Notice or Restriction → Maintenance Work → Testing → Restoration → Notice Cancellation/Update if applicable.

13 & 14. Reporting Discipline

Engineering Information Required — and Why Exact Location Is Critical

Avoid vague statements such as "Runway lights not working." When reporting an AGL condition internally for possible aeronautical information action, capture the following.

Airport Date/time Exact location Runway/taxiway identifier Lighting system Affected component Number of affected units Percentage/extent Failure condition Operational status if known Maintenance status Expected restoration time Engineer/technician Test results Supporting records
The exact information required for a NOTAM request depends on the applicable national and airport procedures. Engineering records should identify runway designation, lighting type, affected section and units, direction/location, circuit identification and nature of defect — accurate location information reduces the risk of operational misunderstanding.

17, 18 & 19. Don't Confuse These

NOTAM vs Fault Log, SNOWTAM & AIP

Several documents look related but serve distinct purposes — and can originate from the same event without being interchangeable.

DocumentWhat It Communicates
AGL Fault LogEquipment, failure, measurements, diagnosis, repair, technician, test results.
NOTAMOperationally significant condition, affected facility, relevant period, operational status, restoration/update information.
SNOWTAMA specialized aerodrome condition report tied to runway surface conditions from snow, slush, ice, frost or related contamination. AGL failures are normally handled through the NOTAM/aeronautical information process, not treated as SNOWTAM information.
AIPRelatively permanent aeronautical information of lasting operational relevance.

A useful principle: AIP = Published Aeronautical Information. NOTAM = Time-Critical Change / Condition Information. The exact publication mechanism is governed by the applicable State AIS framework.

20, 21 & 22. Restoration Discipline

"Lights ON" Does Not Equal "System Restored"

Restoration is not complete simply because the technician repairs the equipment. A fixture may illuminate while still having incorrect intensity, incorrect colour, poor alignment, intermittent operation, incorrect circuit configuration, damaged optics or other performance problems.

23, 24 & 25. Diagnosing Extent

CCR, Series-Circuit & Individual Light Failures

CCR Failure

Can affect an entire lighting circuit — runway, taxiway, approach or PAPI outage, or reduced intensity capability.

Trace: Which CCR → Which circuit → Which lighting system → What operational effect?

Series-Circuit Failure

A single fault can affect multiple lighting units.

  • Cable fault
  • Earth fault
  • Open circuit
  • Transformer failure
  • Connector failure
  • CCR problem

Individual Light Failure

A single failed fixture does not automatically mean a NOTAM is required. The engineering/operations team must determine:

  • Location
  • Function
  • Number of failed units
  • Remaining serviceable lights
  • Operational significance
  • Applicable reporting criteria

Technical failure ≠ Automatically NOTAM.

28, 29 & 30. Data Discipline

NOTAM Data Accuracy & Time Management

Incorrect NOTAM information can create operational confusion. Common information errors include:

Wrong runway designation Wrong taxiway Wrong lighting type Incorrect effective time Incorrect end time Incomplete affected area Failure to update restoration Failure to cancel an obsolete notice

Engineering should clearly record detection time, notification time, maintenance start, maintenance completion, testing completion and restoration confirmation — creating a traceable timeline. Aeronautical information commonly uses UTC for standardized time reporting; engineering records may use local time, but the responsible procedure should be followed when providing information for aeronautical information processing. A time-zone mistake can create an operationally significant error.

32 & 41. Decision Support

NOTAM Quality Control & the AGL NOTAM Decision Framework

Before information is passed to the authorized NOTAM function, check: correct airport, correct facility, correct runway/taxiway, correct lighting system, correct affected area, condition verified, start time recorded, expected restoration established if appropriate, maintenance team and operations informed, and supporting test records available.

Use This as an Internal Engineering Decision Aid

Step 1 — Is there an AGL abnormality? NO → Normal operation. YES → Continue.

Step 2 — Is the condition verified? NO → Investigate. YES → Continue.

Step 3 — Does it affect an aeronautical facility or operational capability? NO → Maintenance record. YES → Continue.

Step 4 — Is the condition operationally significant under applicable procedures? NO → Normal maintenance/control process. YES → Notify the authorized airport/AIS function.

Step 5 — Is NOTAM or another aeronautical information action required? Decision by authorized function.

Step 6 — Repair and verify.

Step 7 — Update/cancel the applicable information as required.

The Decision Framework as a Branching Path

Most abnormalities exit early as routine maintenance — only conditions that clear every gate reach the authorized NOTAM function

47. Terminology Discipline

NOTAM vs NOTAM-Like Internal Alerts

Don't mix terminology. An airport may have internal maintenance alerts, engineering notifications, operations alerts, control-room alarms or work permits — these are not necessarily NOTAMs. A NOTAM is part of the formal aeronautical information framework.

40. The Bigger Picture

NOTAM and Safety Management

NOTAMs form part of a broader operational safety system. They help ensure that aviation users are informed when aerodrome conditions differ from expected conditions. For AGL engineering, this creates the principle: a defect that changes operational capability must be communicated through the airport's approved safety and aeronautical information process.

49. The Core Principle

The Final Principle

53. Final Engineering Principle

AGL Failure → Operational Impact → Verified Information → Authorized Notification → Corrective Action → Verified Restoration.

A NOTAM does not repair an AGL system. It communicates an operationally significant condition while the technical team works to restore the facility. For AGL engineers, the objective is not merely "issue a NOTAM." The objective is to identify the real condition, communicate it accurately through the approved process, restore the system correctly, verify serviceability, and ensure the aeronautical information remains current.

Airfield Lighting NOTAM Procedure

Airfield lighting NOTAM procedure

The airfield lighting NOTAM procedure begins when a lighting fault or planned work affects what pilots can rely on. FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is a NOTAM?

NOTAM stands for Notice to Airmen. It communicates the establishment, condition, change or removal of an aeronautical facility, service, procedure or hazard when that information is timely and operationally significant. The circumstances requiring one are determined by applicable aeronautical information regulations and the responsible authority, not by an individual engineer's judgement alone.

Q2Why do NOTAMs matter for airfield ground lighting?

An AGL defect can be more than a maintenance problem, since it can change the visual guidance an aircraft actually receives. Engineering identifies and communicates the technical condition through the airport's reporting process, and the authorized aeronautical information function determines and issues the operational notice.

Q3What is the difference between a NOTAM and a maintenance report?

A maintenance report is a technical record for engineering and maintenance use, containing technical details of inspections or failures. A NOTAM is operational information for aviation users, using standardized aeronautical information published through authorized channels. One should not be treated as automatically equivalent to the other.

Q4Who decides whether a NOTAM is issued for an AGL fault?

Authorized operational and aeronautical information personnel determine and issue the notice. Engineering identifies and verifies the condition and reports it through the established process.

Q5When can an AGL problem become NOTAM-relevant?

It depends on operational significance and the applicable procedures, not simply on a fault existing. Examples include unavailable or partially unavailable runway lighting, affected taxiway lighting or stop bars, unavailable or degraded approach lighting, PAPI outages or restrictions, and unavailable airfield signs. The governing question is whether the condition changes published or expected operational conditions.

Q6What is the AGL-to-NOTAM chain?

AGL fault, detection, technical verification, operational impact assessment, report to the authorized airport function, NOTAM decision, NOTAM issuance, maintenance or rectification, verification, and finally NOTAM cancellation or update.

Q7Does a lit PAPI mean it is serviceable?

No. A PAPI that is physically illuminated is not necessarily operationally correct. A powered, visibly lit PAPI can still present a false glide-path indication, where the fault is optical rather than electrical, so technical verification should accompany operational reporting.

Q8How precisely should approach lighting faults be reported?

Identify exactly which portion of the system is affected, such as centreline lighting, crossbars, sequenced flashing components or other elements, rather than simply reporting that the approach lights are faulty.

Q9What should happen during an emergency AGL failure?

Speed matters, but accuracy matters equally. Confirm the condition, notify airport operations, assess the operational impact, initiate the applicable aeronautical information procedure, then rectify, test, restore and update or cancel the notice. Never communicate an unverified technical assumption as an operational fact.

Q10How is planned AGL maintenance coordinated with NOTAMs?

The workflow runs from the maintenance plan and identification of affected AGL, through operational impact assessment and coordination with airport operations, ATC and AIS, to any required notice or restriction, then the maintenance work, testing, restoration and notice cancellation or update if applicable.
