Lens

The transparent optical window that protects the light source while shaping the output beam. Made from high-grade glass or polycarbonate, the lens ensures photometric performance, environmental sealing, and durability in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Lens in AGL fixtures is a transparent cover that serves multiple critical functions: it protects the internal optical components (prism, reflector, light source) from environmental contamination and mechanical damage, while also contributing to the beam-shaping process. Lenses can be flat, curved, or Fresnel-type, and are designed to withstand extreme temperatures, UV radiation, impacts from foreign objects, and the weight of aircraft (in inset fixtures). The lens material is typically high-transparency glass (borosilicate or tempered) or optical-grade polycarbonate with hard coatings to resist scratching and UV degradation.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The lens's purpose is to let a fixture keep producing light while shielding everything behind it from the environment — a role it has to fulfil for years without compromising the beam pattern pilots rely on for guidance. As the outermost optical surface, it takes the brunt of UV exposure, temperature extremes, foreign-object impacts, and, in inset fixtures, the direct weight of aircraft tyres, so its protective function is what keeps the prism, reflector, and light source behind it performing to their original photometric design rather than degrading from moisture or physical damage. Where the lens also refracts light or carries an integrated colour filter, this protective role has to be balanced precisely against optical performance, since any distortion, fogging, or crazing of the surface would blur or misdirect the beam it is meant to be safeguarding.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Lenses are used in every AGL lighting fixture that requires a controlled light beam and environmental protection. Common applications include:

  • Runway Edge Lights: Flat or slightly curved lenses to protect the prism and seal the fixture.
  • Inset Lights: Heavy-duty tempered glass lenses rated for the full aircraft wheel-load environment (≈27,000 kg / 59,400 lb, governing case a fully loaded A380-800 tyre, per FAA Engineering Brief 83A).
  • Taxiway Lights: Polycarbonate or glass lenses with colour filters for edge and centreline guidance.
  • Approach Lighting: High-transmission lenses for barrettes and flashing lights.
  • PAPI & VASI: Lenses with built-in colour filters to create the red/white transition.
  • Obstruction Lights: Lenses that provide 360° visibility with red or white output.
Placement

Installation & Location

The lens is seated over the prism or reflector on a gasket, then clamped in place by the top cover or a threaded retaining ring, with even bolt torque applied around the perimeter so the seal compresses uniformly and the lens sits flat without local stress points that could crack tempered glass. Orientation matters where the lens carries a colour filter or a directional cut-off (as in a uni-directional threshold or end light lens): it's fitted with its coloured or optically-active face aligned exactly to the manufacturer's index mark, since a lens rotated even slightly can change the light's colour boundary or beam direction. Once fitted, the outer face sits flush with (or proud of, per the design) the top cover so it can shed water and withstand wheel loads on inset fixtures, and it's inspected for correct seating before the fixture is returned to service.

Function

How Does It Work?

The lens works by transmitting light from the source while providing a physical barrier against the environment. In many fixtures, the lens also acts as a secondary optical element, refracting light to fine-tune the beam pattern. Lenses can be:

  • Flat: Minimal optical effect, primarily for protection.
  • Curved: Adds a lensing effect to modify beam spread.
  • Fresnel: Stepped surface to reduce weight and thickness while maintaining optical power.
  • Coloured: Integrated colour filter to produce green, red, yellow, or blue light.

The lens is sealed with a gasket or O-ring to maintain IP67/IP68 water ingress protection. It is also designed to resist fogging, cracking, and chemical attack from de-icing fluids and jet fuel.

Construction

Physical Construction

Lenses are moulded or precision-ground from borosilicate or tempered glass, or from optical-grade polycarbonate, with the surface profile — flat, curved, or Fresnel-faceted — dictated by how much beam-shaping work the lens itself is expected to do alongside the fixture's prism or reflector. A hard coating is applied to the outer face to resist UV degradation and scratching from windblown grit, since surface haze on the lens directly reduces photometric output over time regardless of the light source behind it. The lens seats into a machined groove or retaining lip in the housing or top cover, held in place by a threaded ring, retaining clips, or an adhesive bead, with a gasket or O-ring compressed at the seating line to complete the fixture's IP67/IP68 seal. On inset fixtures the lens (and the surrounding top-cover structure it sits in) is additionally load-rated to survive direct aircraft wheel loading without cracking, which drives both material thickness and the profile's edge support design.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Component Verification
Pre-Fit

Confirm the lens material, colour, and part number match the fixture model before fitting, since a mismatched lens changes the beam colour or optical performance without any visible sign at assembly.

Index Mark Alignment
Fit

Where the lens carries a colour filter or directional cut-off, align its optically-active face exactly to the manufacturer's index mark, since a lens rotated even slightly can shift the colour boundary or beam direction.

Gasket / O-Ring Seating
Fit

Seat the lens on a clean, undamaged gasket or O-ring free of grit or old sealant residue, since any debris at the seating line compromises the IP67/IP68 seal regardless of correct torque.

Even Perimeter Torque
Fit

Torque the top cover or retaining ring evenly around the full perimeter in the manufacturer's specified sequence, so the seal compresses uniformly and the lens sits flat without local stress points that could crack tempered glass.

Load Rating Confirmation (Inset Fixtures)
Fit

On inset fixtures, confirm the lens and its edge support in the top cover are the load-rated variant before fitting, since a standard lens cannot survive direct aircraft wheel loading.

Flush Seating Check
Post-Fit

Confirm the outer face sits flush with, or proud of per design, the top cover so it sheds water correctly and, on inset fixtures, carries wheel loads as intended.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record lens part number, orientation, and torque values before the fixture is returned to service, since correct seating is not verifiable once the fixture is closed up.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Scratching & Hazing

Lenses can become scratched from abrasive cleaning, sand, and debris. This reduces light output and can scatter light. Use only approved cleaning methods and soft cloths. Replace lenses that are heavily scratched or hazed.

Thermal Stress Cracking

Rapid temperature changes (e.g., halogen lamp heat-up in cold conditions) can cause thermal stress cracking in glass lenses. Use tempered glass and ensure proper thermal design to mitigate this risk.

Gasket Failure & Moisture Ingress

The gasket or O-ring sealing the lens can degrade over time, allowing moisture ingress. This causes fogging, internal corrosion, and electrical faults. Regular gasket inspection and replacement is recommended.

Colour Fading in Polycarbonate Lenses

Polycarbonate lenses with integral colour can fade under UV exposure. Use UV-stabilised materials or replace with glass lenses for critical colour applications.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced or Scattered Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens surface scratched or hazed from abrasive cleaning, sand, or airborne debris, scattering light outside the intended beam pattern.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens surface under strong light for scratches or haze; run a photometric intensity/beam-pattern check against the rated values.
Corrective Action
Clean lightly hazed lenses with approved solvents and soft cloths only; replace lenses that are heavily scratched or hazed beyond cleaning.
Cracked Lens
Likely Cause
Thermal stress cracking from rapid temperature change (e.g. halogen lamp heat-up in cold conditions) if a non-tempered glass lens is fitted.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crack pattern for a thermal-shock signature (radiating from a hot spot) versus impact damage; confirm whether the lens material is tempered glass as specified.
Corrective Action
Replace with a tempered glass lens as specified, and verify the fixture's thermal design (lamp wattage, ventilation) is within design limits if cracking recurs.
Fogging or Moisture Inside the Fixture
Likely Cause
Gasket or O-ring sealing the lens has degraded, allowing moisture ingress that fogs the optic and risks internal corrosion.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the lens seal (gasket/O-ring) for compression set, cracking, or gaps; check for condensation or corrosion evidence inside the housing.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded gasket or O-ring, dry out the housing, and re-verify the seal is holding after the next weather cycle.
Colour Faded on a Coloured Polycarbonate Lens
Likely Cause
UV exposure fading the integral colour pigment in a polycarbonate lens over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour check against ICAO/FAA chromaticity limits; compare the lens colour to an unexposed reference sample if available.
Corrective Action
Replace with a UV-stabilised polycarbonate lens, or upgrade to a glass lens with a separate colour filter for critical colour applications.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Lens Removal
Electrical Safety

Isolate the fixture and allow the lamp or LED module to cool before removing the lens; a live circuit or hot lamp behind the lens is a shock and burn hazard during replacement.

Thermal Shock Avoidance
Handling

Do not apply a cold cleaning solution to a hot lens or install a lens immediately after an incandescent lamp change; allow the fixture to reach ambient temperature to avoid thermal-shock cracking.

Cleaning Method
Workmanship

Clean the lens with a soft cloth and manufacturer-approved cleaner rather than an abrasive pad or solvent; abrasive cleaning scratches or hazes the surface and degrades the beam pattern.

Seal Integrity on Reinstall
Workmanship

Inspect the lens gasket or O-ring for compression set or cracking before reinstalling, and replace it if degraded, since a poor lens seal invites moisture ingress and internal corrosion.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for the removed lens, gasket, and fasteners at all times during handling, and secure or remove any loose material before returning the fixture to service to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Verify Photometric Performance
Compliance

After a lens replacement, verify beam pattern and chromaticity where applicable before closing out the work, since a mismatched or damaged lens can put the fixture out of tolerance.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return a fixture to service with a cracked, hazed, or improperly seated lens; confirm the lens is fully seated and the fixture meets its photometric requirement before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Rapid Cooling Cracks Glass Lenses the Same Way Rapid Heating Does
Handling

A hot lens exposed to cold night air or a draft during changeover can crack from rapid cooling just as readily as from a cold cleaner applied to a hot lens; let ambient conditions equalize from both directions, not just after removing a heat source.

Track Colour Shift From UV Aging on Polycarbonate Lenses
Compliance

Polycarbonate lenses can yellow slightly with years of UV exposure, gradually shifting the fixture's chromaticity even though the lens remains structurally sound and passes a visual crack check; treat aged polycarbonate lenses as a chromaticity risk at overhaul, not just a mechanical one.

A Beam Pattern Fault Can Be the Lens, Not the Optic Behind It
Troubleshooting

A hazed or internally stress-cracked lens can scatter light and distort beam pattern even when the reflector or prism behind it is correctly aligned; rule out the lens itself before re-aiming or replacing deeper optical components.

Confirm Lens Orientation on Fixtures With Directional Optics
Workmanship

Some lenses have a directional prism pattern molded in rather than being fully symmetric; reinstalling one rotated from its original orientation can shift the beam pattern even though the lens looks correctly seated.

Gasket Compression Set Outlasts the Lens's Visual Condition
Durability

A lens gasket can lose sealing force well before the lens itself shows any wear, so a lens that looks fine on removal doesn't mean its gasket is still doing its job; replace the gasket at every lens service as routine, not only when moisture ingress is already suspected.

FOD Risk From a Cracked Lens Starts Before It Fully Fails
Operational

A lens with a hairline crack found during inspection is already at risk of shedding fragments under vibration or thermal cycling, well before it fails completely; treat a cracked lens as an immediate replacement rather than a monitor-and-revisit item.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL fixture lenses: materials, load ratings, photometrics, standards, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is a lens in an AGL fixture?

A lens is a transparent cover that protects the internal optical components (prism, reflector, light source) from contamination and mechanical damage while also contributing to beam shaping. It can be flat, curved or Fresnel-type and must withstand temperature extremes, UV, foreign-object impacts and, on inset fixtures, aircraft wheel loads.

Q2What does the lens do in an airfield light?

It lets the fixture keep producing its designed beam for years while shielding everything behind it. As the outermost optical surface it takes the UV, thermal, chemical and impact exposure, and it can also refract light to fine-tune the beam pattern.

Q3What is the difference between flat, curved and Fresnel lenses?

Flat lenses have minimal optical effect and mainly protect the fixture. Curved lenses add a lensing effect that modifies beam spread. Fresnel lenses use a stepped surface to reduce weight and thickness while still shaping the beam.

Q4What materials are AGL lenses made from?

Typical materials are tempered glass, borosilicate glass and optical-grade polycarbonate. Light transmission is above 90% for clear glass and above 85% for coloured lenses, and the outer face carries a hard, UV-resistant coating to resist scratching and haze.

Q5What load can an inset fixture lens withstand?

Inset lenses are rated for the full aircraft wheel-load environment, approximately 27,000 kg (59,400 lb), with the governing case a fully loaded A380-800 tyre per FAA Engineering Brief 83A. Elevated fixture lenses are typically rated IK10 (20 J) for impact.

Q6What ingress protection does a sealed lens provide?

When seated on its gasket or O-ring and clamped evenly by the top cover or retaining ring, the lens assembly typically achieves IP67 or IP68. Even bolt torque around the perimeter keeps the seal compressed uniformly.

Q7Which standards apply to AGL lenses?

ICAO Annex 14 (photometric and mechanical requirements), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (Specification for Runway, Taxiway, Heliport, and Vertiport Light Fixtures), IEC/TS 61827 (luminaire characteristics) and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program).

Q8How often should AGL lenses be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for cleaning rubber deposits and residue, semi-annual for thermal stress micro-crack inspection, and annual for seal integrity and, on polycarbonate lenses, checking for UV-driven yellowing or colour fade.

Q9Why do glass lenses crack from thermal shock?

Rapid temperature change stresses the glass, for example a cold cleaning solution on a hot lens, a lens installed straight after an incandescent lamp change, or a hot lens exposed to cold night air. Let the fixture reach ambient temperature and use tempered glass where thermal cycling is severe.

Q10Why has a fixture's light output dropped or become scattered?

A scratched or hazed lens scatters light outside the intended beam pattern. Inspect the lens under strong light and run a photometric intensity or beam-pattern check. Lightly hazed lenses can be cleaned with approved solvents and soft cloths, while heavily scratched ones should be replaced.

Q11What precautions apply when removing or refitting a lens?

Isolate the fixture and let the lamp or LED module cool before removing the lens, because a live circuit or hot lamp is a shock and burn hazard. When refitting, confirm the part number, colour and index mark match the fixture, since a mismatched or rotated lens changes beam colour or direction.
