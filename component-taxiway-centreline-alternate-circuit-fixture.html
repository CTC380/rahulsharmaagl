Taxiway Centreline Alternate-Circuit Fixture

A specialised centreline lighting fixture designed with dual-circuit capability, enabling alternating operation between two independent series circuits. This configuration provides redundancy, allows for staggered lighting patterns, and supports advanced guidance features such as directional sequencing or variable spacing for enhanced pilot awareness during ground movement.

Overview

What Is It?

A Taxiway Centreline Alternate-Circuit Fixture is a specialised centreline lighting fixture designed with dual-circuit capability, enabling alternating operation between two independent series circuits. This configuration provides redundancy, allows for staggered lighting patterns, and supports advanced guidance features such as directional sequencing or variable spacing for enhanced pilot awareness during ground movement. The fixture typically contains two separate light sources (or two sets of LEDs) connected to two different isolating transformers and series circuits, allowing it to be illuminated by either circuit independently or in combination.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The taxiway centreline alternate-circuit fixture's purpose is to keep centreline guidance available even if one of its two feeding circuits fails, which matters specifically on the busy or precision taxiways where a gap in the centreline line could genuinely disrupt ground movement during low-visibility operations. Beyond straightforward redundancy, its dual-circuit design also enables staggered or sequenced lighting patterns that a single-circuit fixture couldn't produce — letting an airport reduce the visual 'dash' effect of ordinary centreline spacing or add directional sequencing through complex intersections, capabilities that exist specifically to sharpen guidance precision where taxiway routing gets complicated.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Taxiway centreline alternate-circuit fixtures are installed on precision taxiways and busy airports where enhanced guidance, redundancy, or advanced lighting patterns are required. They are used on:

  • Precision taxiways at major international airports requiring redundancy
  • Complex taxiway intersections where directional guidance is beneficial
  • Low visibility operations (CAT II/III) requiring enhanced centreline guidance
  • Rapid exit taxiways where sequential lighting can guide pilots
  • Apron taxi lanes with complex routing requirements
  • Military airfields requiring flexible and redundant lighting configurations
Placement

Installation & Location

The fixture is installed flush into the pavement along the taxiway centreline at the same spacing as standard centreline fixtures, with its housing sized to accommodate the two separate light sources and their associated wiring within a single base can. Each of the fixture's two circuits is wired back to its own isolating transformer and series loop, run as physically separate cable paths from the fixture through to the field distribution point, so a fault on one circuit cannot propagate into the other and defeat the redundancy the fixture is installed to provide. Positioning along the centreline is tied to the same surveyed reference used for standard centreline fixtures, ensuring the alternate-circuit units integrate seamlessly into the existing lighting pattern rather than creating a visible discontinuity. Where the fixture is used to support sequenced or directional lighting patterns, its position within the overall array is set out specifically to achieve the intended visual effect, rather than at a generic centreline interval alone.

Function

How Does It Work?

Taxiway centreline alternate-circuit fixtures operate using a dual-circuit design that provides flexibility and redundancy:

  • Dual-Circuit Design: The fixture contains two independent light sources (LED arrays or lamps) connected to separate isolating transformers and series circuits (e.g., Circuit A and Circuit B).
  • Alternating Operation: The fixture can be illuminated by Circuit A only, Circuit B only, or both circuits simultaneously, depending on the desired lighting pattern.
  • Staggered Patterns: By alternating which fixtures are connected to which circuit, a staggered centreline pattern can be created (e.g., every other light on Circuit A, the rest on Circuit B), reducing the visual "dash" effect and providing a more continuous guidance line.
  • Directional Sequencing: In advanced configurations, the circuits can be switched in sequence to provide directional guidance, with lights progressively illuminating along the taxiway to indicate the correct path.
  • Redundancy: If one circuit fails, the fixture can still be illuminated by the other circuit, ensuring continuous centreline guidance.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Where a standard taxiway centreline fixture answers to one CCR, this fixture answers to two independent ones — Circuit A and Circuit B each run their own loop, their own constant current, and their own loop-level fault detection, joined only at the point where both feed the same physical light. This lets the ALCMS (or, at complex airports, an A-SMGCS route-sequencing system) command a taxiway centreline segment into one of several states — Circuit A only, Circuit B only, or both together — without needing per-fixture addressing, simply by deciding which of the two loops is energised. The practical uses are redundancy (if Circuit A develops a loop fault, ALCMS can switch the segment onto Circuit B alone rather than lose the guidance line entirely) and directional or route-specific patterning (staggering which fixtures sit on which circuit to signal a particular taxi route). Because the two circuits are electrically independent, a fault on one is reported and diagnosed exactly as it would be on any single-circuit fixture, with no cross-loop ambiguity for the CCR to resolve.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture houses two independent green LED arrays or filtered 45W–65W halogen lamps — one per circuit — within a single die-cast aluminium body, typically 200–250 mm in diameter, anodised or powder-coated against fuel spillage, de-icing fluid, and UV exposure. Each light source has its own gasket-sealed optical prism and its own set of internal leads, kept physically and electrically separate all the way back to two independent L-823 splice connectors, so a fault on Circuit A cannot bridge into Circuit B inside the fixture. The flush-mounted body seats into an L-868 base can rated for a 100,000 kg (220,000 lb) aircraft wheel load, with the top surface load-bearing regardless of which circuit is illuminated. Each circuit's leads route to its own dedicated 45W–65W isolating transformer housed separately nearby, and O-ring seals throughout give the assembly its IP67/IP68 rating.

Installation Requirements

Base Can Recess & Grade
Civil

Recess the base can into the pavement flush with the taxiway centreline, sized to accommodate the two separate light sources and their associated wiring within a single housing.

Centreline Survey Set-out
Civil

Set out fixture positions against the same surveyed reference used for standard centreline fixtures, and where the fixture supports a sequenced or directional pattern, position it to achieve the intended visual effect rather than a generic interval.

Physically Separate Circuit Paths
Electrical

Run each of the fixture's two circuits as physically separate cable paths from the fixture through to the field distribution point, so a fault on one circuit cannot propagate into the other.

Dual Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal both circuits' connectors per manufacturer procedure, keeping each splice submersible-rated before the base can is closed.

Dual Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm each circuit's isolating transformer is wired back to its own isolating transformer and series loop, matched to the fixture and that loop's rated current step.

Load-Rated Housing Seating
Mechanical

Seat the fixture housing fully home in the base can and confirm it sits flush and level, since the pavement-mounted design must carry full aircraft wheel load.

Circuit Independence Check
Electrical

Verify continuity on each circuit separately before backfill, confirming no cross-connection exists between the two paths.

Pre-Sealing Sign-off
Documentation

Record base can position, both circuits' cable routes, and connector seals as-built before the pocket is finally sealed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test (Both Circuits)
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test each circuit's isolating transformer and series loop independently before either circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum on both.

Loop Continuity Test (Both Circuits)
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity of each series loop independently and confirm there is no cross-connection between Circuit A and Circuit B inside the fixture.

Independent Circuit Energisation Test
Energisation

Energise Circuit A alone, then Circuit B alone, confirming the fixture illuminates correctly and independently on each before either is combined.

Circuit Isolation & Balance Verification
Energisation

Confirm the two circuits remain electrically isolated from one another and that energising one circuit does not induce voltage on the other.

CCR Brightness Step Response (Both Circuits)
Energisation

Cycle each circuit's CCR through every intensity step and confirm the fixture responds correctly on each, ruling out a marginal transformer on either side.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity and colour on each circuit separately with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances.

Base Can Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm base can depth and load-bearing pavement seal meet as-built tolerances before the surrounding surface is finally reinstated.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, and photometric readings for both circuits alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Circuit Imbalance

Alternate-circuit fixtures require careful balancing of the two circuits to ensure consistent intensity. Differences in cable length, transformer characteristics, or LED driver performance can cause one circuit to appear dimmer than the other. Regular photometric testing and circuit balancing are essential.

Complex Wiring and Troubleshooting

Dual-circuit fixtures require more complex wiring and connector arrangements, making installation and troubleshooting more challenging. Proper labelling, documentation, and testing are essential to avoid confusion and ensure correct circuit identification.

Green Filter/LED Degradation

Green light sources are susceptible to UV degradation and thermal stress. In alternate-circuit fixtures, degradation may occur unevenly between the two circuits, causing visible differences in colour or intensity. Regular photometric testing and replacement of LED modules is essential.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

One Circuit Dark, Other Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
An open-circuit fault (failed transformer, connector, or cable break) confined to only one of the two series loops feeding this dual-circuit fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm which circuit is de-energised by checking CCR status for each loop; megger the affected loop from the CCR outward to isolate the fault.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted transformer, connector, or cable section on that loop, then re-verify both circuits energise independently.
Visible Intensity Mismatch Between Circuits
Likely Cause
Differing cable lengths, transformer characteristics, or uneven LED driver performance between the two circuits.
Diagnostic Steps
Take independent photometric readings on each circuit and compare against the design intensity; check both circuits are set to matching CCR steps.
Corrective Action
Balance CCR output steps between circuits, and replace the underperforming transformer or LED module to bring intensity back in line.
Colour or Intensity Drift Specific to the Green Circuit
Likely Cause
UV and thermal degradation of the green LED module or filter, developing unevenly relative to the other circuit.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour and intensity check on the green circuit alone and compare to spec and to the paired circuit's reading.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded green LED module or filter and re-verify colour and intensity against ICAO/FAA thresholds.
Confusion During Fault-Finding on Dual Circuits
Likely Cause
Inadequate labelling or documentation of the two circuits at the base can has led to the wrong loop being tested or isolated.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify circuit identification against as-built wiring records before beginning any test; confirm labelling at the base can matches records.
Corrective Action
Relabel connectors and update documentation on site, then repeat the fault-finding procedure with the correct circuit confirmed.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Both Series Circuits
Electrical Safety

The fixture is fed by two independent transformers and series loops; de-energise and lock out both CCRs and verify zero energy on each circuit before opening the base can, since isolating only one leaves the fixture live.

Circuit Identification & Tagging
Electrical Safety

Positively identify and tag each of the two circuits at the fixture and back to its transformer before disconnecting anything, to avoid cross-connecting the alternate circuits during reassembly.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM for any period where the fixture, or the sequencing pattern it supports, will be dark or non-standard, keeping personnel and vehicles clear of active taxiway traffic.

Insulation Test Each Circuit Separately
Electrical Safety

Megger test each of the two series loops independently after any work, since a fault on the circuit not currently selected can go undetected until it is switched into use.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for at all times when the base can is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on either series-circuit connection, and confirm the fixture and base can are bonded/grounded before reconnecting.

Verify Correct Sequencing on Restoration
Compliance

After reassembly, confirm both circuits energise correctly and that the intended alternating or sequenced pattern is restored before closing out the work and cancelling the NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Label Both Circuits at Every Point, Not Just the Fixture
Electrical

With two independent series loops feeding one fixture, a mislabeled junction anywhere along either run — not just at the base can — can lead to a cross-connection during a completely unrelated repair months later; extend circuit tagging discipline to every accessible point on both loops.

Verify the Sequencing Logic End-to-End, Not Fixture-by-Fixture
Compliance

Confirming that this one fixture correctly alternates its output isn't the same as confirming the whole route's alternating pattern remains coherent; walk the full sequenced section after any work here, since one fixture's timing can look correct in isolation while conflicting with its neighbours.

Test Both Circuits, Even the One Not Currently Selected
Troubleshooting

A fault on the standby circuit can sit undetected indefinitely if only the active circuit gets tested; make megger testing both loops routine, not just the one currently in service.

Two Transformers Means Two Chances for a Mismatch
Electrical

Each of the two isolation transformers feeding this fixture should be sized to its own circuit's actual load; reusing one transformer's rating for the other's replacement assumes a symmetry that dual-circuit designs don't always have.

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven brightness even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

Base Can Seal Ages Faster With Two Cable Entries
Durability

A dual-circuit base can has twice the cable glands and connector points of a standard fixture, and each is a potential water-ingress path; inspect and reseal every entry point at overhaul, not just the one most recently worked on.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about taxiway centreline alternate-circuit fixtures: dual-circuit operation, redundancy, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a taxiway centreline alternate-circuit fixture?

It is a taxiway centreline light with two independent light sources fed from two separate series circuits (Circuit A and Circuit B). The fixture can be lit by either circuit alone or by both together.

Q2Why use an alternate-circuit centreline fixture?

Redundancy. If one circuit fails, the other keeps the centreline lit, which matters on busy or precision taxiways where a gap in centreline guidance is a safety risk.

Q3How does alternating operation work?

Alternating which fixtures connect to which circuit creates a staggered centreline, for example every other light on Circuit A and the rest on Circuit B. In advanced setups the circuits can be switched in sequence to give directional guidance.

Q4What colour is the fixture and how bright is it?

Green, steady, at roughly 200 to 2,000 cd per circuit depending on the setting. Each circuit is CCR-controlled through 2.8 A to 6.6 A steps, and intensity is matched across A and B.

Q5Where are these fixtures used?

On precision taxiways and busy airports needing redundancy, complex intersections, CAT II/III low-visibility operations, rapid exit taxiways and apron taxi lanes with complex routing.

Q6Which standards apply to alternate-circuit fixtures?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5345-46, with IEC 61822 for the constant current regulators and IEC 61821 for maintenance of the series circuits. Always verify the current edition.

Q7How often should these fixtures be checked?

Typical checks are an annual photometric and circuit balance check, annual wiring and documentation audit, annual green LED and filter inspection and quarterly lens cleaning. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q8What should I check if only one circuit is dark?

Check the CCR status for each loop to see which circuit is de-energised, then megger the affected loop from the CCR outward. The usual cause is an open circuit from a failed transformer, connector or cable break confined to that loop.
