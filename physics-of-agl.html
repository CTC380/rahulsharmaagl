The Physics of AGL

Resistance, reactance and regulation — the electrical behaviour behind an airfield series circuit. CCRs, cables, isolating transformers, connectors and fixtures are the components you can see. Underneath every one of them is a set of electrical principles that decides how current, resistance, reactance, impedance, voltage and power interact — and why an AGL circuit behaves the way it does.

Overview

What Is the Physics of AGL?

The physics of AGL is the study of how electrical energy behaves as it travels through an airport lighting system. An AGL series circuit is not simply a wire carrying electricity — it is an electrical system containing conductors, cable resistance, cable inductive reactance, isolation transformers, fixture loads, connector interfaces, distributed impedance, CCR regulation, and ground and insulation characteristics.

Understanding these principles helps an engineer explain why CCR output voltage changes with load, why cable losses occur, why transformer behaviour changes with loading, why series-circuit current must be controlled, why open circuits create high-voltage conditions, why faults affect circuit behaviour, and why electrical measurements do not always directly indicate optical performance.

A System, Not a Wire

Resistance, reactance, transformers, fixtures and connectors all combine to determine how the circuit actually behaves.

Electrical → Optical

The fundamental chain: Electrical Input → Series Circuit → Transformer → Fixture → Optical Output.

Foundation, Not Trivia

These aren't abstract formulas — they're the reasoning an engineer uses to explain and troubleshoot real circuit behaviour.

Series Circuit Architecture

Why AGL Uses a Series Circuit

Traditional airport lighting commonly uses a constant-current series circuit. Instead of supplying each fixture from an individual parallel voltage source, the lighting loads are connected in series.

CCR

Regulates circuit current

Cable

Carries controlled current

Isolation Transformer

Interfaces the fixture

Fixture

Converts energy to light

Return Path

Back through cable to CCR

The lighting system is designed around controlled current rather than simply maintaining a fixed voltage at every fixture. In an ideal series circuit, the same current flows through every point in the series path: I₁ = I₂ = I₃ = … = Iₙ. This is one of the most important differences between AGL series circuits and conventional parallel lighting systems.

Current Is the Same at Every Point in the Loop

Voltage varies fixture-to-fixture across the loop, but a healthy series circuit reads the same current everywhere it's measured

Resistance

Resistance in the AGL Circuit

Resistance is the opposition to current caused by the resistive properties of an electrical element. In an AGL system it exists in cable conductors, transformer windings, connector contacts, fixture wiring, terminations and joints — and it produces electrical losses.

Resistive Power Loss

P = I²R

Circuit current is intentionally held within a controlled operating range, so this relationship matters throughout the circuit.

Conductor Resistance

R = ρL / A

Longer cable → higher resistance. Larger conductor area → lower resistance.

Temperature Effect

Rₜ = R₀[1 + α(T − T₀)]

Resistance of most conductors rises with temperature — consider test temperature before calling a reading abnormal.

Total Circuit Resistance

A simplified total is Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + … + Rₙ. A small resistance at one connector may look insignificant, but a high-resistance connection can produce local heating, voltage loss, power loss and reliability problems.

Contact Resistance

A healthy connection has very low contact resistance. Poor mating, contamination, corrosion, mechanical damage, improper termination, water ingress or a loose connection can all increase it — and even a relatively small increase can produce significant I²R heating when current is high.

AC Behaviour

Reactance & Impedance

Resistance is only one part of AC circuit opposition. The other major concept is reactance — opposition to AC caused by inductance or capacitance. Resistance and reactance combine to form impedance (Z), a far more useful description of an AC AGL circuit than resistance alone.

Inductive Reactance

Xₗ = 2πfL

Increases with frequency and inductance. Relevant to transformer windings, cable characteristics, magnetic equipment and certain AGL loads.

Capacitive Reactance

Xc = 1/(2πfC)

Decreases as frequency increases. In long cable runs, distributed capacitance can influence circuit behaviour.

Impedance

|Z| = √(R² + X²)

For a series R-L circuit, Z = R + jX. This is what the CCR actually "sees" — not a single resistor.

Real AGL circuits contain both. A simplified circuit is: Cable Impedance + Transformer Impedance + Fixture-Related Reflected Impedance + Other Circuit Effects.

Phase Angle

For an AC impedance Z = R + jX, the phase angle is φ = tan⁻¹(X/R) — it describes the relationship between voltage and current, and it's the underlying reason phase angle helps explain reactive power, power factor, transformer behaviour and AC circuit performance generally.

The Impedance Triangle

R and X combine as perpendicular vectors, not as a simple sum — Z is the hypotenuse, and φ is the phase angle between voltage and current

Common Misunderstanding

Why CCR Output Voltage Changes

A constant-current regulator maintains the required circuit current — it does not maintain a fixed voltage. If circuit impedance changes, the voltage required to maintain that current also changes: V ≈ I × |Z|. Try it below.

Adding transformers or lengthening cable increases the required CCR voltage; removing loads or shortening cable decreases it. This is normal series-circuit physics, not a fault.

Adding a Load

An additional compatible load increases total circuit impedance. If current is held constant, V = I × Z means the CCR must produce a higher voltage. This is normal series-circuit physics, not a defect.

Removing a Load

Removing a transformer or fixture reduces total circuit impedance, so the CCR may settle at a lower output voltage while still holding the same regulated current.

Fault Physics

What Happens During a Fault?

Different fault types create different electrical signatures. Click a fault to see how it behaves.

Open Circuit

Interrupted Path

  • Series current path is broken — CCR → Cable → OPEN
  • Circuit current falls toward zero
  • CCR may enter a protective or open-circuit response state
  • Requires controlled, appropriate testing procedures

Short Circuit

Very Low Impedance

  • Z drops toward a very low value
  • A fixed-voltage source could see very high current
  • A CCR responds differently — it's current-regulated with its own protection
  • Never assume every CCR behaves identically here

Earth Fault

Unintended Path to Earth

  • Depends on fault impedance, insulation, cable construction
  • Also depends on CCR design, earthing arrangement, monitoring
  • Can cause abnormal current distribution
  • Can cause insulation degradation or protective action

High-Resistance Connection

Additional Local Impedance

  • Produces I²R heating at the joint itself
  • Connection can progressively get hotter as current flows
  • May lead to deterioration, intermittent operation, eventual open circuit
  • Visual inspection alone may not catch it

Water Ingress

Insulation & Leakage Change

  • Affects insulation resistance and leakage current
  • Affects connector and cable condition
  • Can affect transformer insulation
  • Environmental sealing is an electrical reliability issue, not just mechanical

Transformer Failure

Impedance & Load Change

  • Circuit impedance can shift
  • Load behaviour on the series circuit can change
  • Can influence required CCR output voltage
  • Should be investigated together with circuit history
Cable Physics

Why Cable Length & Size Matter

Cable resistance produces loss according to P = I²R — so cable losses scale with the square of current, not linearly with it.

Longer Cable

Higher resistance → higher voltage drop → greater resistive loss. Cable route and circuit length are a core AGL design input.

Larger Conductor

Larger cross-sectional area → lower resistance → lower resistive voltage drop, within the applicable cable specification.

Current Doubles → Loss ×4

If current increases by a factor of two, I² becomes 4I² — so resistive loss becomes roughly four times greater at the same resistance.

A Cable Is Not Just a Conductor

Long AGL cables have distributed electrical properties — resistance, inductance and capacitance. Long series circuits can therefore exhibit behaviour that simple DC resistance calculations alone cannot fully explain, including resonance effects in systems containing both inductance and capacitance (where Xₗ = Xc).

Power

Power & Power Factor

AC systems require separating real power, reactive power and apparent power — and AGL systems containing magnetic components such as transformers can exhibit reactive behaviour.

Apparent Power

S² = P² + Q²

P = real power (W), Q = reactive power (VAR), S = apparent power (VA).

Power Factor

PF = P/S = cos φ

Indicates how effectively apparent power converts into real power.

Transformer Ratio

V₁/V₂ = N₁/N₂

Ideal turns ratio; real transformers differ due to losses and non-ideal characteristics.

Input currentEquipment loadingPower consumption Electrical infrastructureGenerator capacityDistribution equipment
The Power Triangle

Real power (P) does the useful work of lighting the fixture; reactive power (Q) is exchanged with magnetic components; apparent power (S) is what the supply and generator must actually be sized for

Transformers & Fixtures

Transformer & Fixture Load Physics

An AGL isolating transformer is not an ideal transformer — it has winding resistance, leakage reactance, magnetizing characteristics, core losses and its own insulation system, and it contributes to overall series-circuit impedance.

CCR → Transformer → Fixture

The transformer transfers energy from the series circuit to the fixture — it does not behave like a conventional voltage transformer connected to a fixed-voltage supply. Its role is specifically adapted to the series-circuit architecture.

  • P₁ ≈ P₂ for an ideal transformer
  • P₂ < P₁ for a real transformer (copper losses, core losses, leakage)
  • Efficiency: η = Pout / Pin

Incandescent vs LED Loads

An incandescent lamp is primarily a resistive load, though its resistance changes substantially with temperature. An LED system may include electronic components and may not behave electrically like a simple resistor.

  • Fixture compatibility with the series circuit must be engineered
  • Never assumed from lamp wattage alone

Applied Physics

Physics in Design, Commissioning & Maintenance

These electrical principles aren't just theory — they shape how an AGL circuit is designed, how it's baselined at commissioning, and how it's maintained afterward.

Design

Electrical design must consider circuit length, cable characteristics, transformer characteristics, fixture load, CCR rating, voltage capability, circuit impedance, environmental conditions, protection and testing requirements — so the circuit operates reliably within equipment and system limits.

Commissioning

During commissioning the engineer should establish an electrical baseline — CCR current, CCR output voltage, circuit resistance, insulation resistance, transformer measurements, equipment status, functional response and photometric performance — as reference data for future troubleshooting.

Maintenance

Maintenance should identify changes in system behaviour, not just failed components — e.g. a gradual CCR voltage increase with no obvious fixture failure should prompt investigation of circuit impedance. This is a more predictive approach to AGL maintenance.

Physics of AGL Reliability

Reliability is influenced by the complete engineering chain, not electrical physics alone: electrical design, component quality, installation quality, environmental protection, electrical regulation, mechanical integrity, maintenance and photometric verification. The physics provides the foundation — system reliability depends on all of it together.

Diagnostic Model

A Physics-Based Troubleshooting Sequence

A useful troubleshooting philosophy is to not ask only "which component failed?" — ask "what changed in the electrical behaviour of the system?" This avoids replacing components based on a single measurement alone.

Confirm CCR operating step

Establish the intended operating point before judging any reading against it.

Verify actual circuit current

Current is the controlled quantity — confirm it's actually where it should be.

Record CCR output voltage

Voltage is the circuit's response to impedance, not a fixed target.

Compare with historical & design values

A trend against baseline is far more informative than a single snapshot.

Confirm circuit configuration

Check for added, removed or reconfigured loads before assuming a fault.

Check transformer population

Every series-connected transformer contributes to total circuit impedance.

Inspect connectors & joints

High-resistance connections are a common, easily-missed contributor.

Investigate cable condition

Consider insulation resistance and physical cable condition, not just continuity.

Perform component-level testing

Isolate individual transformers, connectors or cable sections as needed.

Verify fixture performance

Confirm the fixture itself is operating as expected electrically.

Perform photometry where required

Electrical health does not by itself confirm optical performance.

Test Yourself

Common Misunderstandings — Quick Check

These are recurring misunderstandings in AGL electrical work. Pick the correct answer for each.

1. "The CCR should always produce the same voltage."

True — voltage should be fixed regardless of circuit
False — voltage varies with circuit impedance at constant current

2. "Continuity means the circuit is healthy."

True — a continuous path guarantees healthy performance
False — continuity only proves an electrical path exists

3. "Low resistance always means good."

True — lower is always better in every case
False — measured resistance must be interpreted against expected values and test conditions

4. "Correct current means correct light output."

True — correct current guarantees correct photometry
False — optical performance requires separate photometric verification

5. "A cable is just a conductor."

True — only resistance matters for a cable
False — an AGL cable has resistance, inductance and capacitance, especially over length

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is the physics of AGL?

It is the study of how electrical energy behaves as it travels through an airport lighting system. An AGL series circuit contains conductors, cable resistance and inductive reactance, isolation transformers, fixture loads, connector interfaces, distributed impedance, CCR regulation, and ground and insulation characteristics. Understanding this explains real circuit behaviour and supports troubleshooting.

Q2Why does AGL use a series circuit?

Traditional airport lighting commonly uses a constant-current series circuit, with loads connected in series instead of each fixture having its own parallel voltage source. In an ideal series circuit the same current flows through every point of the loop, so a healthy circuit reads the same current everywhere it is measured.

Q3Why does CCR output voltage change with the circuit?

A CCR maintains circuit current, not a fixed voltage. If circuit impedance changes, the voltage needed to maintain that current changes too, roughly V = I × |Z|. Adding transformers or lengthening cable raises the required voltage, and removing loads lowers it. This is normal series-circuit behaviour, not a fault.

Q4What is the difference between resistance, reactance and impedance?

Resistance opposes current and produces real power loss. Reactance is opposition to AC caused by inductance or capacitance and depends on frequency. Together they form impedance (Z), which describes an AC AGL circuit far better than resistance alone and is what the CCR actually sees.

Q5Why does a high-resistance connection matter?

Even a small increase in contact resistance can produce significant I²R heating when current is high. Causes include poor mating, contamination, corrosion, mechanical damage, improper termination, water ingress or loose connections. The connection can progressively get hotter and lead to deterioration, intermittent operation and eventual open circuit, and visual inspection alone may not catch it.

Q6How does temperature affect resistance readings?

Resistance of most conductors rises with temperature, so the test temperature should be considered before calling a reading abnormal.

Q7What happens electrically during an open circuit?

The series current path is broken and circuit current falls toward zero. The CCR may enter a protective or open-circuit response state, and the page notes this requires controlled, appropriate testing procedures.

Q8How does a CCR respond to a short circuit?

A fixed-voltage source could see very high current, but a CCR is current-regulated with its own protection and responds differently. The page cautions never to assume every CCR behaves identically.

Q9What affects an earth fault?

Fault behaviour depends on fault impedance, insulation and cable construction, and also on CCR design, earthing arrangement and monitoring. It can cause abnormal current distribution, insulation degradation or protective action.

Q10Why don't electrical measurements always show optical performance?

The chain runs from electrical input through the series circuit and transformer to the fixture and optical output, and healthy electrical readings do not by themselves prove the required optical performance. That is why photometry and inspection complement electrical testing.
