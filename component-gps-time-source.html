GPS Time Source

A standalone GPS/GNSS receiver that provides an ultra‑precise, disciplined time reference for airfield lighting control systems. The GPS Time Source delivers Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) with sub‑microsecond accuracy, generating pulse‑per‑second (PPS) signals and time‑of‑day data for synchronizing ALCMS servers, PLCs, communication gateways, and network switches — ensuring consistent timestamps across the entire AGL infrastructure.

Overview

What Is It?

A GPS Time Source — also known as a GPS‑disciplined oscillator (GPSDO), GPS receiver with time output, or GNSS time server — is a dedicated device that receives signals from the Global Positioning System (GPS) or other GNSS constellations (GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) and generates a highly accurate, stable time reference. Unlike a full NTP server, the GPS Time Source typically provides raw timing signals — such as a pulse‑per‑second (PPS) output and a serial time‑of‑day message (NMEA or proprietary) — which can be used directly by other equipment (e.g., PLCs, RTUs, data loggers) or fed into an NTP server to distribute time over the network.

Key characteristics of the GPS Time Source include:

  • High‑Precision Timing: Provides a 1 PPS (pulse‑per‑second) output with an accuracy of typically ±50 ns to ±100 ns relative to UTC, making it suitable for demanding time‑sensitive applications.
  • Multi‑Constellation Support: Modern devices support GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou, ensuring reliable time reception even in obstructed environments or during satellite outages.
  • Disciplined Oscillator: An internal oven‑controlled crystal oscillator (OCXO) or temperature‑compensated crystal oscillator (TCXO) is disciplined by the GPS signal, providing excellent holdover performance (e.g., < 1 µs per hour) when the satellite signal is lost.
  • Multiple Output Interfaces: Typically provides a 1 PPS output (BNC or TTL), an RS‑232/RS‑422 serial port for time‑of‑day messages (NMEA 0183 or proprietary), and optionally an IRIG‑B or NTP output for integration with various systems.
  • Time‑of‑Day Data: Outputs UTC date and time via serial data streams, which can be parsed by connected devices to set their internal clocks with high accuracy.
  • Antenna Input: Includes a dedicated antenna input (SMA or TNC) with 5 VDC antenna power and built‑in surge protection for outdoor antenna installations.
  • Compact and Rugged: Often packaged as a small, DIN‑rail mountable module or a compact enclosure, suitable for installation in control cabinets, equipment shelters, and outdoor enclosures.

In airfield lighting systems, the GPS Time Source is a foundational component for time synchronization. It provides the raw timing signals that are used by the ALCMS server, PLCs, and other devices to maintain accurate time, enabling precise sequence‑of‑events (SOE) recording, alarm correlation, and coordinated system responses. It is often paired with an NTP server or integrated directly into a time synchronization module.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The GPS time source exists to give every device across the AGL network the same, unquestionable definition of "when." Individually, a CCR fault, a network event, and an operator action can each be timestamped by their own local clock well enough for routine logging — but when an incident investigation needs to establish the true order in which several such events occurred across different devices, clocks that have each drifted by even a fraction of a second can make that reconstruction unreliable or simply wrong. By disciplining every connected system to the same GPS-derived reference, accurate to tens of nanoseconds, the time source is what makes a sequence-of-events reconstruction actually trustworthy rather than approximately plausible, which matters directly for fault analysis, alarm correlation, and any compliance record that depends on proving what happened first.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The GPS Time Source is deployed in AGL systems where high‑precision time synchronization is critical. Key applications include:

  • ALCMS Server Time Synchronization: Providing the primary time reference for the ALCMS server, ensuring that all alarms, events, and operator actions are timestamped with sub‑millisecond accuracy.
  • PLC and Remote I/O Synchronization: Feeding a PPS signal and time‑of‑day data to PLCs and remote I/O units, enabling precise sequence‑of‑events (SOE) logging with 1 ms or better resolution.
  • CCR Communication Interfaces: Synchronizing CCR communication modules to timestamp status changes and alarms from Constant Current Regulators with high precision.
  • SCADA Integration: Providing a disciplined time source to SCADA systems, ensuring that data from multiple sources is time‑aligned for accurate analysis.
  • Approach Lighting Sequencers: Synchronizing sequenced flashing lights and approach lighting systems to ensure precise timing and coordinated operation.
  • RWSL (Runway Status Light) Systems: Providing accurate time for RWSL control logic, ensuring that light state changes occur at precisely the right moments during aircraft movements.
  • Network Time Servers: Serving as the external reference for NTP servers, which then distribute time to all network‑connected devices in the AGL network.
  • Data Acquisition Systems: Providing precise timestamps for data acquisition systems monitoring airfield sensors, weather stations, and power quality monitors.
Placement

Installation & Location

The receiver unit itself is DIN-rail or rack mounted in the control cabinet, equipment shelter, or control room closest to the devices it will feed, with its PPS and serial time-of-day outputs run on short, direct cabling to the connected PLC, RTU, or NTP server to preserve timing accuracy. Its GPS/GNSS antenna is a separate outdoor component, mounted with a clear, unobstructed view of the sky — typically on a roof, mast, or external wall bracket — and connected back to the receiver by a coaxial antenna cable that includes in-line surge protection at the building entry point, since the antenna cable is the one part of the installation directly exposed to lightning risk. Cable length between antenna and receiver is kept within the manufacturer's rated limit to avoid excessive signal loss, and where multiple receivers in the same facility need a common reference, a splitter or separate antenna runs are used rather than daisy-chaining receivers off one another. Power is typically drawn from the same control cabinet supply as the device it serves, ideally UPS-backed, since a power interruption to the receiver during a GPS outage also removes the holdover oscillator's ability to keep counting accurately.

Function

How Does It Work?

The GPS Time Source operates as a precision timing generator, leveraging satellite signals to produce a disciplined time reference. Key functional aspects include:

  • Satellite Signal Reception: The device receives GPS/GNSS signals via a dedicated antenna. The antenna captures L1 (1575.42 MHz) signals from multiple satellites. The receiver decodes the navigation messages to determine the precise time and position.
  • Time and Frequency Extraction: The receiver extracts the highly stable carrier frequency (which is disciplined by atomic clocks on the satellites) and the time‑of‑week information from the navigation message. This provides a reference frequency and a known time epoch.
  • Disciplined Oscillator: The internal oscillator (typically an OCXO or TCXO) is disciplined by the satellite‑derived frequency. A phase‑locked loop (PLL) or frequency‑locked loop (FLL) adjusts the oscillator frequency to match the satellite signal, ensuring that the oscillator's output is precisely 10 MHz or a similar reference frequency.
  • Pulse‑Per‑Second (PPS) Generation: The disciplined oscillator is used to generate a 1 PPS signal. The PPS pulse is synchronized to the GPS second, with a rising edge that occurs at the start of each UTC second. The jitter of the PPS is typically less than 50 ns, making it suitable for high‑precision synchronization.
  • Time‑of‑Day Data Output: The receiver outputs date and time information via a serial port (e.g., RS‑232) using standard NMEA 0183 sentences (e.g., $GPGGA, $GPRMC, $GPZDA). These sentences contain the current UTC time, date, and optionally position information. Connected devices can parse these messages to set their internal clocks.
  • Holdover Operation: When the satellite signal is lost (e.g., due to antenna obstruction or atmospheric conditions), the device enters holdover mode. The disciplined oscillator continues to provide a stable time reference, and the PPS output remains accurate for a period determined by the oscillator's stability (e.g., hours or days). The device may also provide a warning or alarm output to indicate that the reference is lost.
  • Antenna Management: The device supplies power (typically 5 VDC) to the active GPS antenna via the coaxial cable. It also includes built‑in surge protection and a low‑noise amplifier (LNA) to compensate for cable losses. The antenna status (connected, signal strength, number of satellites) is monitored and can be reported via the serial port or status LEDs.
  • Alarm and Status Indication: Front‑panel LEDs or a display indicate the device status, including power, signal lock, holdover mode, and fault conditions. Some models provide a relay output to indicate loss of synchronization for remote alarming.

The GPS Time Source is typically powered from a 24 VDC or 120/230 VAC supply, with optional redundancy for critical installations. The antenna must be installed with a clear view of the sky, and the cable run should be kept as short as possible to minimize signal attenuation and delay.

Construction

Physical Construction

The receiver electronics are housed in a compact DIN-rail-mount enclosure or a small standalone panel-mount case, built around a multi-constellation GNSS receiver board, an internal oscillator (TCXO or OCXO, depending on the holdover-stability grade), and output-conditioning circuitry for the 1PPS, frequency, and serial time messages. The front or top face carries an SMA or TNC antenna connector supplying 5 VDC to a remote active antenna, BNC or terminal-block outputs for the timing signals, a serial port for NMEA data, and status LEDs showing power, satellite lock, holdover, and fault state at a glance. The GNSS antenna itself is a separate, weatherproof puck- or dome-style unit mounted outdoors with a clear view of the sky — typically on a mast or rooftop bracket — and connected back to the receiver over coaxial cable, with surge protection fitted at the cable entry to the equipment room given the antenna's outdoor exposure. The receiver's own housing is rated for indoor cabinet use (IP20/IP40), relying on the enclosing control cabinet for weather protection, while the internal oscillator is thermally isolated within the case to hold its holdover accuracy if GNSS lock is lost.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Antenna Obstruction

GPS signals can be blocked by buildings, vegetation, or even snow accumulation on the antenna. Ensure the antenna is installed with a clear, unobstructed view of the sky. Use a site survey tool to verify signal strength before final mounting. For outdoor installations, consider a heated antenna to melt snow and ice.

Cable Attenuation and Delay

Long antenna cables can introduce signal attenuation and delay, affecting time accuracy. Use low‑loss coaxial cable (e.g., LMR‑400) and keep the cable run as short as possible. For runs longer than 30 meters, consider using an in‑line amplifier or a fiber optic extension. Account for cable delay (approximately 5 ns per meter) when high precision is required.

Loss of GPS Lock

GPS lock can be lost due to satellite outages, atmospheric conditions, or receiver malfunctions. The device will enter holdover mode, which may drift over time. Monitor the holdover duration and accuracy, and consider a secondary time source (e.g., a second GPS receiver or a network time reference) for redundancy in critical applications.

Multipath Interference

Signals reflecting off buildings or other structures can cause multipath interference, degrading timing accuracy. Use an antenna with good multipath rejection, and position the antenna away from reflective surfaces. If multipath is unavoidable, consider using a choke ring antenna or a high‑quality timing antenna.

Power Supply Noise

Noise on the power supply can affect the oscillator performance and degrade timing accuracy. Use a clean, regulated power supply with adequate filtering. Avoid sharing the power supply with heavy inductive loads (contactors, motors) without proper decoupling.

Interference from Nearby Transmitters

Nearby radio transmitters (e.g., radar, cellular, or other RF sources) can interfere with GPS reception. Ensure the antenna is located away from high‑power transmitters. If interference is suspected, use a spectrum analyzer to identify the source and reposition the antenna or add a filter.

Firmware and Configuration Errors

Incorrect configuration of the serial output (baud rate, parity, message type) can prevent connected devices from parsing the time data. Verify the settings against the receiving device's requirements. Keep the firmware updated to benefit from performance improvements and bug fixes.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Weak or No Satellite Signal
Likely Cause
Antenna obstruction from buildings, vegetation, or snow/ice accumulation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check signal strength/satellite count on the receiver's diagnostic screen; inspect the antenna's sky view for new obstructions; check for snow or ice buildup in winter conditions.
Corrective Action
Clear obstructions or relocate the antenna for a clear sky view, and fit a heated antenna where snow/ice accumulation is a recurring problem.
Timing Accuracy Degraded Despite Good Signal
Likely Cause
Cable attenuation or delay from a long or poor-quality antenna cable run.
Diagnostic Steps
Check cable length and type against the recommended maximum for the cable grade; measure signal loss at the receiver input; calculate expected propagation delay (~5 ns per metre) and compare against observed offset.
Corrective Action
Replace with low-loss coaxial cable, shorten the run where possible, or add an in-line amplifier or fibre optic extension for long runs; account for known cable delay in high-precision applications.
Device Enters Holdover and Drifts Over Time
Likely Cause
Loss of GPS lock due to satellite outage, atmospheric conditions, or a receiver fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the receiver's lock status history and holdover duration; monitor drift rate during holdover against the manufacturer's specification.
Corrective Action
Investigate and resolve the cause of lock loss (antenna, cable, interference), and add a secondary time reference for redundancy in critical applications.
Timing Jitter or Repeatable Small Errors
Likely Cause
Multipath interference from signals reflecting off nearby structures.
Diagnostic Steps
Check antenna positioning relative to reflective surfaces; review the antenna's multipath rejection specification; compare timing stability at different candidate mounting locations if possible.
Corrective Action
Reposition the antenna away from reflective surfaces, or upgrade to a choke ring or high-quality timing antenna with better multipath rejection.
Oscillator Performance Unstable
Likely Cause
Noise on the power supply affecting the internal oscillator, especially where the supply is shared with inductive loads.
Diagnostic Steps
Check power supply quality and filtering at the unit; identify any shared loads such as contactors or motors on the same supply branch.
Corrective Action
Fit a clean, regulated, adequately filtered power supply dedicated to the GPS time source, decoupled from inductive loads.
Signal Reception Intermittently Drops Out
Likely Cause
Interference from nearby high-power RF transmitters (radar, cellular, or other sources).
Diagnostic Steps
Use a spectrum analyser to check for interference near the GPS frequency band; correlate dropout timing with known nearby transmitter activity.
Corrective Action
Relocate the antenna away from the interference source or add appropriate filtering; escalate to site management if a third-party transmitter is the cause.
Connected Devices Not Receiving or Misreading Time Data
Likely Cause
Incorrect serial output configuration (baud rate, parity, message type) or outdated firmware.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the GPS unit's serial output settings against the receiving device's required configuration; check firmware version against the latest release.
Corrective Action
Correct the serial output configuration to match the receiving device and update firmware to the latest stable version.

Engineering & Expert Notes

By Rahul Sharma

Signal Lock Doesn't Guarantee Accuracy, Only Reception
Verification

A receiver can show a locked fix while tracking a marginal number of satellites in poor geometry, producing a technically valid but degraded-accuracy time output; check satellite count and dilution-of-precision indicators, not just the lock status light.

Sky View Obstruction Degrades Gradually, Not Suddenly
Environmental

Growing vegetation or new construction near the antenna reduces visible sky incrementally, and the receiver can compensate by tracking fewer, lower-elevation satellites for a long time before losing lock outright; a periodic sky-view survey catches this before it becomes an outage.

Holdover Quality Depends on the Oscillator, Which Ages
Compliance

An internal oscillator's holdover accuracy during a lost GPS signal degrades as the oscillator ages, even on a unit that met its holdover spec when new; factor unit age into how long you can trust holdover to bridge an outage.

Antenna Surge Protection Needs Independent Verification From Power-Side Protection
Electrical

A unit can have solid surge protection on its power input while the antenna feed, exposed at height, remains a separate and often-overlooked lightning exposure path; verify both protection paths independently rather than assuming one covers the other.

A Timing Fault Here Surfaces as Confusion Everywhere Else
Design

Because sequenced approach lighting and ALCMS event logs depend on this unit as their time reference, a subtle drift doesn't just affect this unit's own display; it can desynchronise lighting sequencing or misorder logged events across the system in ways that are confusing to diagnose after the fact.

Cable Length Affects Signal Timing, Not Just Signal Strength
Installation

An antenna cable run longer than the manufacturer's recommended maximum can introduce enough propagation delay to measurably skew the PPS timing output, even while signal strength remains adequate for reception; verify cable length against spec, not just that the unit shows a locked fix.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the GPS time source: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a GPS time source?

A GPS time source (also called a GPS-disciplined oscillator, GPSDO or GNSS time server) is a dedicated device that receives GPS or other GNSS signals and generates a highly accurate, stable time reference. It typically provides a pulse-per-second (PPS) output and a serial time-of-day message rather than acting as a full NTP server.

Q2Why does an AGL system need a GPS time source?

So that alarms, events and operator actions in the ALCMS are timestamped consistently across systems. It gives the ALCMS server its primary time reference and can feed PPS and time-of-day data to PLCs and remote I/O units for precise synchronisation.

Q3How accurate is a GPS time source?

The 1 PPS output is typically accurate to ±50 ns to ±100 ns relative to UTC with GPS lock, and about ±1 µs over 24 hours in holdover.

Q4How does a GPS time source work?

An antenna receives GNSS signals (for example GPS L1 at 1575.42 MHz), and the receiver extracts the time-of-week and a stable reference frequency disciplined by the satellites' atomic clocks. It then disciplines a local oscillator to generate the PPS and time messages.

Q5What is holdover?

The mode the device enters if it loses GPS lock, keeping time from its local oscillator. Typical holdover stability is ±1 µs per hour for an OCXO and ±1 ms per day for a TCXO, so extended holdover leads to drift.

Q6What outputs does a GPS time source provide?

A 1 PPS TTL signal (50 Ω, BNC, 10 ms pulse width configurable), an optional 10 MHz, 5 MHz or 1 MHz frequency output, a serial RS-232 or RS-422 time-of-day message in NMEA 0183 or proprietary format, and a relay alarm output for loss of sync.

Q7How is a GPS time source powered?

By 24 V DC (18-32 V) or 120/230 V AC via a power supply module, drawing 2 W to 5 W typically and up to 10 W with an active antenna. The antenna is powered at 5 V DC over the coax. It is a low-voltage auxiliary device, not part of the airfield series circuit.

Q8Which standards apply?

Relevant references are NMEA 0183, IEC 61850 (time synchronisation), IEEE 1588 (PTP) and FAA AC 150/5345-56 (L-890 ALCMS), whose sequence-of-events logging depends on a synchronised time source. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should a GPS time source be checked?

Typical intervals are quarterly for firmware and configuration review, semi-annual for holdover performance checks, and annual for antenna site survey, cable and connector inspection and power supply quality checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with GPS time sources?

Common problems are antenna obstruction, cable attenuation and delay, loss of GPS lock, multipath interference, power supply noise, interference from nearby transmitters and firmware or configuration errors. Symptoms include weak or no satellite signal, degraded timing accuracy, drift while in holdover or intermittent dropouts.
