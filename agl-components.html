Overview

What Are AGL Components?

An Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) system is built from interconnected electrical, optical, mechanical and control components. Each has a specific function, and the performance of one can directly affect the others. Understanding them supports design, selection, procurement, installation, testing, commissioning, photometric verification, maintenance, troubleshooting and rehabilitation.

Architecture

AGL System Architecture

Power path

Airport power supply feeds the CCR, which drives the primary circuit. Each light on the circuit is fed through its own isolation transformer.

Control path

ATC and airport operations command the ALCMS, which controls the CCRs and receives monitoring information. The CCRs feed the AGL series circuits and airport light fixtures.

      Electrical

      Electrical AGL Components

      Constant Current Regulator (CCR)

      Maintains the selected current through the series lighting circuit. May also provide multiple intensity steps, local and remote control, monitoring, fault indication, protection and an ALCMS interface, so it is both a power and, in monitored systems, a control component. CCR working principle

      Isolating transformer

      Connects an individual light to the series circuit while separating primary and secondary. Match primary and secondary current, wattage, frequency, insulation, environmental protection, connectors and fixture compatibility. Airport isolating transformer

      Primary cable

      Carries the controlled series current. Select for voltage rating, insulation, conductor size, environment, circuit length, temperature, mechanical protection and the airport specification. A primary fault can affect multiple fixtures. Cables and connectors

      Secondary cable

      Links the transformer secondary to the fixture, generally for one fixture. Common problems: connector failure, cable damage, insulation deterioration, water ingress, mechanical damage and high-resistance connections.

      Connectors

      Join primary cable to transformer, transformer to secondary lead, and secondary lead to fixture. Primary connectors serve the series circuit; secondary connectors serve the transformer-to-fixture link. A mated connector can still have poor contact or moisture.

      Junction boxes

      Protected space for connections, and for transformers in some designs. Select for installation environment and loading. Junction boxes and housings

      Mechanical

      Mechanical AGL Components

      Mechanical infrastructure keeps equipment positioned and protected. It influences fixture elevation, alignment, stability, drainage, maintenance access, vehicle and aircraft loading, and frangibility where applicable.

      Light bases

      The mounting interface between airfield infrastructure and the fixture; depending on design, also a transformer housing, junction box or cable-access enclosure. Consider loading, diameter, depth, material, corrosion protection, drainage, fixture interface, cable entry and alignment.

      Transformer housings

      Physical protection for an isolation transformer. Consider transformer size, depth, drainage, cable routing, connector access, mechanical protection and environment. Select housing and transformer as a compatible system.

      Frangible supports

      Designed to break away under applicable conditions rather than become an unacceptable obstacle hazard. Used with elevated runway, taxiway and approach lights, signs and PAPI. Consider fixture weight, mounting height, support arrangement, break point, foundation and nearby aircraft movement.

      Mounting assemblies

      Mounting plates, baseplates, bolts, spacer rings, extensions, adjustment mechanisms, brackets, frangible couplings and elevation adjustment components. Incorrect mounting causes misalignment, wrong elevation, instability and poor photometric performance.

      Control

      Control and Monitoring Components

      Modern AGL systems increasingly use centralized control and monitoring: ALCMS, CCR control interfaces, remote-control panels, monitoring equipment, circuit selectors, PLC and RTU interfaces, communication equipment, status panels and alarms.

      ALCMS

      Centralized control and monitoring of lighting circuits: circuit on/off, intensity selection, status monitoring, fault alarms, event logging, equipment status, remote control and interfaces with airport operational systems. It is part of the control and monitoring architecture, not only a user interface.

      CCR control

      The interface between the control system and the regulator: start and stop, brightness selection, local or remote selection, status, fault indication and monitoring. Commissioning should verify both the command and the feedback.

      Remote control

      Lets authorized staff operate lighting without being at the CCR cabinet: on/off, intensity step, status and alarms. May sit in ALCMS, a control desk, SCADA or an airport operations control system.

      Monitoring

      Covers CCR status, circuit status, lighting failures, alarms, communication status, control-system faults and equipment availability, so staff can respond to failures sooner.

      Selecting, Specifying and Replacing Components

      Electrical compatibility

      Does it match the circuit?

      Optical compatibility

      Does it give the required optical performance?

      Mechanical compatibility

      Does it fit and withstand the application?

      Environmental suitability

      Can it operate in the actual airport environment?

      Regulatory compliance

      Does it meet project and authority requirements?

      Maintainability and lifecycle

      Can it be inspected and replaced efficiently, with spares and support available?

      A procurement specification identifies

      • Component type and application
      • Electrical, mechanical and environmental requirements
      • Dimensions, materials and connectors
      • Test requirements and approvals or certification
      • Documentation, spare parts and warranty

      Compatibility pairs to check

      • CCR and transformer: current
      • Transformer and fixture: wattage, current, secondary
      • Fixture and base: mechanical interface
      • Fixture and connector: electrical and physical
      • ALCMS and CCR: control and communication
      • LED fixture and existing CCR: electrical load
      A component that meets its own specification can still be unsuitable if it is incompatible with the installed system.

      Replacement

      Select by application, electrical characteristics, mechanical interface, optical characteristics, environmental requirements and applicable approval, not by appearance. A transformer chosen only by wattage can be wrong if the primary and secondary current arrangement or frequency differs, and a runway fixture replaced without checking its optics can create a photometric problem.

      Lifecycle

      Projects, Lifecycle and Asset Management

      New airports: specify as a system

      Coordinate electrical (CCRs, cables, transformers, connectors, junction boxes), lighting (runway, taxiway, approach, PAPI, signs, stop bars, runway guard lights), mechanical (bases, housings, foundations, mounting, frangible supports) and control (ALCMS, remote controls, monitoring, communications).

      Rehabilitation: survey before replacing

      Assess existing equipment, age, condition, electrical and photometric performance, spare-parts availability and compatibility with new equipment, then decide to retain or replace. This avoids replacing components that can stay in reliable service.

      Reference

      Standards for AGL Components

      The applicable standard depends on the product and project, and can involve ICAO, the national civil aviation authority, FAA where applicable, airport technical specifications, project specifications, manufacturer requirements and applicable electrical standards. FAA publishes specialist documents for CCRs, light fixtures, isolation transformers, PAPI systems and light bases and transformer housings.

      For an Indian airport project, the governing project documents and the applicable DGCA and ICAO framework control the final equipment requirements. Do not assume one certification scheme applies to every airport worldwide, and confirm the current edition of any reference before relying on it.

      Keyword Reference

      AGL System Components, Airport Lighting Components & Equipment Selection

      The main building blocks of an airfield lighting system and how to choose them.

      AGL system components

      AGL system components are the equipment that makes up an airfield lighting installation: CCRs, series circuit cables, isolating transformers, connectors, light fixtures, bases, signs, PAPI units, control and monitoring equipment, and the civil items that support them. Each must suit the others and the standard being followed.

      Airport lighting components

      Airport lighting components is the wider term for everything used in airside lighting, including approach, runway, taxiway, apron and obstruction lighting. Most systems share the same electrical backbone of CCRs, series circuits and transformers, with different fixtures for each application.

      AGL equipment selection

      AGL equipment selection means choosing components that meet the specification, work together and can be supported over the system's life. Check approval to the relevant standard, compatibility with the CCR and series circuit, installation location, spares and maintenance support. See Selecting, Specifying and Replacing Components above.

      Keyword Reference

      Runway, Taxiway & Approach Lighting Components, Beacons and Light Types

      Components by application, and the main fixture types.

      Runway lighting components

      Runway lighting components include edge, threshold, end, centreline and touchdown zone fixtures, with their transformers, connectors and bases, all fed from series circuits. Fixtures differ in colour, intensity and mounting, and are chosen to the runway category and standard.

      Taxiway lighting components

      Taxiway lighting components include edge and centreline fixtures, stop bars, guard lights and signs, with their transformers, connectors and bases. Colours and patterns follow the standard, and the components must work with the control and monitoring arrangements for the taxiway system.

      Approach lighting components

      Approach lighting components include centreline barrettes, crossbars, side rows and sequenced flashing lights, with their support structures, transformers, flashers and control equipment. These are mostly elevated units, so structure and alignment are part of the component choice.

      Airport beacon lighting

      Airport beacon lighting is the rotating or flashing light on or near an aerodrome that helps pilots find it from the air. Beacon colour and flash pattern follow the standard, and the unit needs a reliable supply and regular checks of its lamp or LED, drive and alignment.

      In-pavement airport lights

      In-pavement airport lights are mounted flush with the pavement surface, as with many runway centreline, touchdown zone and taxiway lights. They must withstand aircraft and vehicle loads, so the fixture, base and seals are chosen for strength and sealing, and installed and levelled carefully.

      Recessed airfield lights

      Recessed airfield lights sit in a base set into the pavement or ground, with the optical surface at or near the surface. The base, drainage, sealing and cable entry need care at installation, because water ingress is a common cause of later faults.

      Omnidirectional airfield lights

      Omnidirectional airfield lights show light in all directions around the fixture, instead of in one direction. They are typical for taxiway edge lights and some other uses. Check the fixture's approval, colour and intensity against the standard for the application.

      FAQ

      AGL Components FAQ

      Q1What are the main components of an AGL system?

      The main categories are electrical, lighting, mechanical and control components. Typical components include CCRs, transformers, cables, connectors, runway and taxiway lights, PAPI, signs, light bases, junction boxes and ALCMS.

      Q2What is the most important electrical component in AGL?

      In a series-circuit system the CCR is one of the most important, because it controls the series-circuit current.

      Q3What does an AGL isolation transformer do?

      It electrically separates the primary series circuit from the individual light fixture while transferring power to the fixture.

      Q4What is an airport light base?

      The physical mounting interface for an AGL fixture. Depending on design it can also serve as a transformer housing or junction box.

      Q5What is an airport junction box?

      A protected enclosure for electrical connections and, in some designs, for transformer and fixture interfaces.

      Q6What components are used in runway lighting?

      Edge lights, threshold lights, runway end lights, centreline lights, touchdown-zone lights and approach lighting.

      Q7What components are used in taxiway lighting?

      Taxiway edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, runway guard lights and associated signs.

      Q8What are the components of a PAPI?

      Four light units, optical systems, mounting and aiming arrangements, electrical connections, and associated control and infrastructure.

      Q9What is ALCMS?

      ALCMS stands for Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System. It provides centralized control, status monitoring and alarm functions for airport lighting.

      Q10What is the difference between primary and secondary AGL cable?

      The primary cable forms part of the airport series circuit. The secondary connection normally links an isolation transformer to the individual light fixture.

      Q11Why are isolation transformers used in AGL?

      They provide electrical separation between the airport series circuit and the individual fixture.

      Q12Why are AGL connectors important?

      They maintain electrical continuity and environmental and mechanical integrity at connections within the lighting circuit.

      Q13Which AGL components require photometric testing?

      Lighting fixtures and visual aids such as runway lights, taxiway lights, approach lights and PAPI may require it, depending on the applicable requirements.

      Q14Which AGL components require electrical testing?

      CCRs, cables, transformers, connectors, control equipment and associated circuits can require electrical testing, depending on the project and maintenance procedure.

      Q15How do AGL components work together?

      The CCR controls the series circuit, the cable distributes power, isolation transformers interface with individual fixtures, the fixtures generate the optical output, mechanical components position the equipment, and control systems operate and monitor the installation.

      Q16How should an AGL component be selected?

      Consider electrical compatibility, optical performance, mechanical requirements, environmental conditions, regulatory and project requirements, installation interface, maintainability and lifecycle support.

      Q17Can a component from one manufacturer be connected to another manufacturer's system?

      Potentially, but verify compatibility first: electrical, mechanical, optical, control and certification requirements should all be considered.

      Q18Are AGL components standardized?

      Many categories have established international, national or authority-specific requirements, but exact dimensions, ratings, interfaces and approvals vary by standard and application.

      Q19What is the difference between an AGL manufacturer and an AGL supplier?

      A manufacturer produces the equipment. A supplier may manufacture, distribute or source it, and its technical responsibility depends on the contract.

      Field Insight

      Engineering & Expert Notes

      RS
      By Rahul Sharma
      Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
      Learn the System Path Before the Parts List
      Understanding

      Follow supply to CCR, cable, transformer, fixture and control. Knowing how each component connects explains why a fault in one place shows up somewhere else.

      Part Numbers and Standards References Belong in Your Records
      Specification

      Record the approved type for each installed item. When the part is later replaced, the correct specification should be known without relying on memory or a worn label.

      Compatibility Matters More Than a Visual Match
      Interfaces

      Connectors, transformers and fixtures can look interchangeable but differ in rating or approval. Verify the interface rather than assuming that a part that fits will perform correctly.

      Mechanical Items Deserve the Same Attention as Electrical Ones
      Mechanical

      Bases, frangible couplings and mountings carry load, drainage and safety functions. Their condition affects electrical performance even though they carry no current.

      Plan Spares Around Failure Rates, Not Convenience
      Spares

      Stock the items that fail most and take longest to obtain. Review usage periodically and avoid keeping obsolete types that no longer match the installed base.

      Update the Asset Register When Anything Changes
      Records

      A register that no longer reflects the field slows fault finding and audits. Capture swaps, upgrades and relocations at the time the work is closed out.
