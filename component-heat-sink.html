Heat Sink

The thermal management component that dissipates excess heat from the light source (LED or halogen lamp), ensuring stable junction temperatures, prolonging component life, and maintaining consistent light output in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Heat Sink is a passive thermal management component designed to absorb and dissipate excess heat generated by the light source (LED module or halogen lamp) in an AGL fixture. It typically consists of a metal body (aluminium or copper) with a large surface area — often featuring fins, pins, or ridges — that increases the rate of heat transfer to the surrounding air. The heat sink is thermally coupled to the light source via a thermal interface material (TIM) to minimise thermal resistance. By maintaining the light source's junction temperature within its specified limits, the heat sink prevents premature degradation, colour shift (in LEDs), and catastrophic failure, ensuring reliable performance over the fixture's service life.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The heat sink's purpose is to protect the light source from the heat it generates so the fixture keeps meeting its photometric and colour requirements over its rated service life. An LED module or halogen lamp that runs too hot will degrade faster, drift in colour (a particular concern for LEDs, where colour coordinates must stay within tight ICAO/FAA tolerances), or fail outright — any of which can compromise the pilot's ability to correctly identify the fixture's signal or leave a gap in the lighting pattern. By conducting heat away from the light source and dissipating it to the surrounding air, the heat sink keeps the junction temperature within its design limit, which is what allows modern LED fixtures to achieve their quoted 50,000-hour service life rather than failing early from thermal stress.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Heat sinks are used in virtually every modern AGL fixture where thermal management is critical, including:

  • LED Runway Edge & Centreline Lights: High-performance finned heat sinks for high-power LED modules.
  • LED Threshold & End Lights: Heat sinks for coloured LED modules to maintain colour stability.
  • LED Taxiway Lights: Compact heat sinks for lower-power LED modules.
  • LED Approach Lighting: Heat sinks for high-flux LED barrettes and flashing lights.
  • LED PAPI & VASI: Precision heat sinks for glide slope LED units.
  • LED Obstruction Lights: Heat sinks for high-intensity LED obstruction beacons.
  • Halogen Fixtures: Heat sinks or heat spreaders that dissipate heat from the lamp base.
  • LED Drivers: Heat sinks for driver electronics to ensure reliable operation.
Placement

Installation & Location

The heat sink is bolted or thermally bonded directly to the back of the LED module or driver board, with a thin, even layer of thermal interface material (paste or pad) applied between the two surfaces to eliminate air gaps that would otherwise insulate the joint and trap heat. Fasteners are torqued evenly across the mounting pattern so the interface pressure is uniform, since an uneven or over-tightened joint can crack the LED substrate or squeeze out the thermal compound entirely. The sink's fins are orientated inside the housing to allow whatever airflow or convection path the fixture design provides, and it's positioned so it doesn't obstruct the beam path, cable routing, or the gasket seal when the housing is closed back up.

Function

How Does It Work?

A heat sink works on the principles of conduction, convection, and radiation:

  • Conduction: Heat flows from the light source through the thermal interface material (TIM) into the heat sink base via direct contact.
  • Convection: The heat sink's extended surface area (fins) transfers heat to the surrounding air via natural or forced convection.
  • Radiation: Heat is also radiated from the heat sink surface, especially when dark anodized or painted with high-emissivity coatings.

The heat sink is designed to have a low thermal resistance (°C/W) to maintain the junction temperature (Tj) below the maximum specified for the LED or component. In LED AGL fixtures, typical junction temperatures are kept below 85°C to achieve a 50,000-hour L70 lifetime. The heat sink is often integrated into the fixture housing or is a separate component clamped to the LED module with spring-loaded fasteners to ensure consistent thermal contact.

Construction

Physical Construction

Heat sinks for AGL fixtures are typically die-cast or extruded aluminium, machined flat on the mounting face to minimise the air gap against the LED module's metal-core PCB. A thermal interface material — thermal paste, a phase-change pad, or a thermally conductive adhesive — fills the microscopic surface irregularities between the two flat faces, since even a thin air gap here dominates the overall thermal resistance. The opposite face is finned, pinned, or ridged to maximise surface area for convective heat transfer to the surrounding air inside the sealed fixture housing, with fin spacing and orientation chosen to work within a closed, non-fan-cooled enclosure. The heat sink body doubles as part of the fixture's structural stack in many designs, bolting directly between the LED module and the housing so thermal and mechanical mounting are handled by the same fasteners.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Surface Condition Inspection
Pre-Installation

Inspect fins and mounting surfaces for shipping damage, deformation, or debris before the heat sink is fitted, since a damaged mounting face compromises thermal contact.

Thermal Interface Material Application
Mechanical

Apply thermal paste, phase-change pad, or thermally conductive adhesive evenly at the correct thickness across the mounting face, eliminating air gaps between the heat sink and the LED module.

Fastener Torquing
Mechanical

Torque mounting screws or clamps to the specified value, since inconsistent clamping pressure directly affects thermal contact and long-term performance.

Fin Clearance Check
Mechanical

Confirm fin spacing and orientation are unobstructed within the sealed fixture housing, since the design relies on convective heat transfer without active airflow.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record thermal interface application and fastener torque values as-built before the fixture is closed up and energised for thermal performance testing.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Thermal Interface Degradation

Thermal grease can dry out or pump out over time, increasing thermal resistance and causing LED junction temperature to rise. This leads to accelerated lumen depreciation and colour shift. Regular inspection and re-application of TIM during lamp replacement is recommended.

Dust & Debris Accumulation

Dust and dirt can accumulate on the heat sink fins, reducing heat transfer efficiency. In the airfield environment, this is a common issue. Regular cleaning with compressed air or a soft brush is essential to maintain thermal performance.

Corrosion of Aluminium Heat Sinks

In coastal or high-humidity environments, aluminium heat sinks can corrode, especially if the anodized coating is damaged. This increases thermal resistance and reduces heat dissipation. Use marine-grade anodizing or stainless steel alternatives in such conditions.

Mechanical Damage to Fins

The heat sink fins can be bent or damaged during maintenance or due to impact. Damaged fins reduce surface area and cooling efficiency. Carefully straighten or replace the heat sink if damaged.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

LED Running Hot / Premature Lumen Depreciation
Likely Cause
Thermal interface material (TIM) has dried out or pumped out, increasing thermal resistance between the LED/driver and the heat sink.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure LED junction or case temperature against the expected value for ambient conditions; if accessible, inspect the TIM layer for dryness, cracking, or migration away from the contact area.
Corrective Action
Clean the mating surfaces and re-apply fresh TIM per the manufacturer's specification during the next lamp/module replacement; add TIM re-application to the standard service procedure.
Reduced Cooling Performance Over Time
Likely Cause
Dust and debris accumulation on the fins reducing effective surface area for heat transfer.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect fins for dust buildup or debris packed between fin channels; compare case temperature to a known-clean baseline if available.
Corrective Action
Clean the fins with compressed air or a soft brush, taking care not to bend them, and schedule regular cleaning as part of routine maintenance in dusty environments.
Visible Corrosion or Pitting on Aluminium Fins
Likely Cause
Damaged anodized coating exposing bare aluminium to coastal salt air or high humidity.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the anodized coating for scratches or wear that expose bare metal; check the extent of pitting or white corrosion product on the fins.
Corrective Action
Replace with a marine-grade anodized or stainless steel heat sink for coastal/high-humidity sites; light surface corrosion can sometimes be cleaned, but pitted fins should be replaced to restore thermal performance.
Bent or Deformed Fins
Likely Cause
Physical impact or mishandling during maintenance work on the fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually assess the extent of fin deformation and whether airflow channels between fins remain open.
Corrective Action
Carefully straighten lightly bent fins if channels remain functional, or replace the heat sink if deformation is significant enough to restrict airflow.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Hot Surface Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow the heat sink to cool after the fixture has been operating before handling; it is designed to run hot by function and can retain enough heat to burn bare skin shortly after power-off.

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the fixture circuit before removing the heat sink or disturbing its thermal interface to the LED module or lamp.

Thermal Interface Material Reapplication
Mechanical

Clean and reapply thermal interface material correctly whenever the heat sink is removed and reseated; an air gap or degraded TIM raises junction temperature and shortens the light source's service life even though the fixture still appears to work.

Fin Clearance
Mechanical

Keep the fin array free of dirt, insect nests, and debris buildup during maintenance; blocked airflow reduces heat dissipation even when the heat sink itself is undamaged.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for electrical work and heat-resistant gloves when handling a heat sink that has recently been in service.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the heat sink is firmly mounted with correct thermal contact before closing out the work; loose mounting is a common cause of premature light-source failure that is easy to overlook.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Working Fixture Doesn't Mean the Thermal Path Is Good
Diagnostics

An LED module with a degraded thermal interface will typically still light correctly at first power-on; the effect shows up as accelerated lumen depreciation and colour shift over months, not an immediate failure, so a fixture reported as "working fine" during a quick visual check can still be running its light source hot.

Insect and Debris Buildup Is a Slow, Cumulative Fault
Maintenance

Fin-array airflow blockage from insects or wind-blown debris accumulates gradually over a season rather than appearing all at once; include a fin-clearance check as a standard step at every scheduled visit rather than only investigating it after a heat-related failure has already occurred.

TIM Reapplication Quality Varies More Than the Material Choice
Installation

How evenly thermal interface material is applied and how firmly the mating surfaces are clamped matters at least as much as which TIM product is used; a technically correct compound applied unevenly or with residual old material left underneath can still leave an effective air gap.

Retrofit Compatibility Isn't Just About Physical Fit
Retrofit

A heat sink that physically bolts onto a retrofit LED module doesn't necessarily have the thermal capacity that module needs; check the manufacturer's thermal resistance rating against the module's actual heat output rather than assuming a mechanically compatible part is thermally adequate.

Mounting Torque Affects Thermal Contact, Not Just Mechanical Security
Mechanical

Under-torqued mounting fasteners can leave a heat sink mechanically secure enough to pass a simple wiggle test while still not achieving full contact pressure across the mating surface; torque to the specified value rather than "snug," since thermal contact quality depends on even clamping pressure.

Corrosion at the Fin Base Reduces Effective Surface Area
Durability

Corrosion or oxidation building up between fins near the base can reduce effective heat-dissipating surface area well before it's visually obvious from a casual glance at the fin tips; inspect the base of the fin array specifically in coastal or high-humidity locations, not just the exposed outer fins.
