PAPI Azimuth Adjustment

The precision horizontal orientation mechanism that aligns each PAPI light unit with the runway centreline. This adjustment ensures that the glide slope beam is directed along the correct approach azimuth, preventing lateral displacement that could mislead pilots during final approach.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Azimuth Adjustment is the mechanical system that allows the horizontal (left/right) orientation of a PAPI light unit to be set precisely. It is typically integrated into the mounting base or the frangible pole interface and provides a controlled means of rotating the entire PAPI unit around its vertical axis.

Proper azimuth alignment is essential because the PAPI beam must be directed exactly along the runway centreline. If a unit is misaligned horizontally, the pilot may see the glide slope indication incorrectly offset to one side, leading to a lateral deviation on approach. The azimuth adjustment mechanism allows fine‑tuning, typically with a range of ±5° and a resolution of a few arc‑minutes.

The mechanism usually comprises:

  • Pivot point – a central bearing or bolt that allows rotation.
  • Adjustment screws or slides – threaded rods or slotted plates that push or pull the unit to achieve the desired angle.
  • Locking system – bolts, clamping plates, or locknuts that secure the setting once aligned.
  • Index or scale – markings to indicate the angular position, aiding in setting and verification.
Purpose

Purpose & Function

The azimuth adjustment exists to keep the PAPI's glide slope indication valid for the aircraft actually flying the approach, not just for one that happens to be perfectly on the extended runway centreline. Because the elevation angle that separates red from white is set to only fractions of a degree, even a small horizontal misalignment can shift what that beam boundary looks like from a normal approach position — a pilot slightly left or right of centreline would then be reading an indication calibrated for a different sightline than the one they're actually on. By locking the unit's rotation precisely onto the centreline, the mechanism ensures the red/white transition means the same thing regardless of the aircraft's lateral position within the normal approach corridor, which is what lets PAPI be trusted as an absolute glide slope reference rather than one that only works from a single exact position in space.

Architecture

Key Components of the Adjustment Mechanism

The azimuth adjustment mechanism is built around several functional elements:

Pivot Bearing

A robust bearing or bushing that allows the PAPI unit to rotate smoothly around its vertical axis. Often a large-diameter bolt or a precision ground pin.

Adjustment Screws

Threaded rods (often with fine pitch) that push against the unit’s base or a slide plate. Turning the screw rotates the unit left or right.

Slotted Plates

An alternative to screws, using slotted holes and eccentric cams to provide coarse and fine adjustment in a compact package.

Locking Mechanism

Clamping bolts or locknuts that secure the adjustment after alignment, preventing drift due to vibration or wind loading.

Index Scale

Engraved or printed markings (often in degrees or arc‑minutes) that indicate the current azimuth angle, aiding in setting and recording.

Corrosion Protection

Stainless steel or plated components, with anti‑seize lubricants, to ensure the mechanism remains adjustable throughout its service life.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The azimuth adjustment is a standard feature on every PAPI installation, regardless of size or configuration:

  • Permanent PAPI systems – on precision runways, where the unit must be precisely aligned to the runway centreline.
  • Helicopter PAPI – similarly, for helipad approach paths, though the azimuth reference may be to a specific helipad centreline.
  • Portable PAPI – with lightweight, quick‑adjust mechanisms for rapid deployment.
  • Retrofit installations – when replacing old units, the azimuth adjustment allows accommodation of existing pole or foundation positions.
  • Maintenance and recalibration – used whenever a unit is reinstalled or after any event that could disturb horizontal alignment.
Placement

Installation & Location

The adjustment mechanism is built into the PAPI unit's mounting base or the frangible pole interface, installed as the unit is initially set up on its surveyed foundation so the pivot point sits directly beneath the optical housing's centre of rotation. Coarse azimuth is set first, using the surveyed runway centreline as the reference, positioning the unit's rough orientation before the fine adjustment screws or slotted plates are used to bring the beam onto the precise heading required. Once aligned, the locking bolts or clamping plates are torqued to the manufacturer's specification and the index scale reading is recorded as part of the commissioning documentation, giving a baseline to check against during future inspections. Because the mechanism sits at the base of an outdoor, frangible-mounted fixture, its components are specified in corrosion-resistant materials and treated with anti-seize lubricant at installation, so the adjustment remains free to move on a future recalibration rather than seizing in place over years of exposure.

Function

How Does It Work?

The azimuth adjustment process is straightforward but requires precision and care:

  • Initial Setup: The PAPI unit is placed on its mounting base and loosely bolted, allowing rotation. The adjustment screws or slides are in their neutral positions.
  • Reference Sighting: A theodolite or alignment telescope is set up at a known position on the runway centreline (often at the threshold). The instrument is sighted on a reference mark on the PAPI unit (or on a special alignment target attached to the unit).
  • Measuring Deviation: The theodolite measures the horizontal angle between the PAPI unit’s current orientation and the desired direction (runway centreline). The deviation is noted.
  • Adjustment: The adjustment screws are turned to rotate the unit. One screw pushes the unit clockwise, the other counter‑clockwise (or a single screw with a spring return). The adjustment is made incrementally, with the theodolite re‑checked after each turn.
  • Locking: Once the unit is aligned to within the required tolerance (typically ±0.5°), the locking bolts or nuts are tightened securely. The theodolite is used one final time to confirm that the alignment did not shift during locking.
  • Documentation: The final azimuth angle (often relative to the runway bearing) is recorded for maintenance logs and future reference.

The adjustment range is typically ±5°, which is sufficient to accommodate installation tolerances and minor pole misalignments. The fine‑pitch screws allow adjustments as fine as 0.01° (36 arc‑seconds).

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Azimuth adjustment has no electrical control logic of its own — it's a mechanical, technician-closed loop performed with a theodolite or alignment telescope rather than a command from the ALCMS. A reference sighting establishes the current bearing, the deviation from the runway centreline is measured, and the adjustment screws are turned incrementally with the theodolite re-checked after each turn, until the unit sits within the required tolerance (typically ±0.5°).

Once locked, the azimuth setting is a static mechanical state rather than something the airfield's control system monitors or adjusts in service — the PAPI's electrical control chain (CCR, intensity steps, fault reporting) operates entirely independently of this angle, which is why a drifted azimuth doesn't register as an electrical fault and can only be caught by a physical re-check.

Construction

Physical Construction

The mechanism is built around a central pivot — a bearing or bolted joint — that lets the whole PAPI unit rotate horizontally on top of its mounting base or frangible pole interface. Threaded adjustment screws or a slotted, curved plate engage the unit's base plate on either side of the pivot, so turning a screw pushes or pulls that side of the fixture through a small, controlled arc while the pivot holds the centre fixed. Once the correct angle is dialled in, a set of locking bolts or clamping plates is torqued down to clamp the base plate rigidly against the mounting surface, removing play that could let wind or vibration walk the setting off angle over time. An engraved index mark or graduated scale sits alongside the adjustment screws so the current position can be read and logged without needing to remeasure with a separate tool, and all fasteners and moving parts are corrosion-resistant metal to keep the mechanism free-moving after years of outdoor exposure.

Full-Range Travel Check
Mechanical

Run the mechanism through its full ±5°/±10° range before final siting to confirm smooth travel with no binding, and that it can reach the design azimuth without running out of adjustment.

Survey Benchmark Reference

Establish the runway centreline extended survey bearing before adjustment, since the azimuth setting is referenced against this benchmark rather than an arbitrary heading.

Mechanical

Turn the threaded adjustment screws to dial in the design azimuth against the survey bearing, using a theodolite or calibration tool for the precise setting.

Locking Bolt Torquing
Mechanical

Pre-Verification Sign-off
Documentation

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Adjustment Screws

Threaded rods can seize due to rust or galling, making adjustment impossible. Use stainless steel screws and apply anti‑seize lubricant during installation. Periodic exercise of the screws helps prevent seizing.

Locking Bolt Relaxation

Vibration can cause locking bolts to loosen over time, allowing the unit to drift out of azimuth. Always use locking washers or thread‑locker (medium strength). Re‑torque bolts during routine maintenance.

Incorrect Reference Sighting

If the theodolite is not set up precisely on the runway centreline, the alignment will be incorrect. Always verify the survey reference points and use a properly calibrated instrument.

Scale Misreading

If the index scale is worn, dirty, or misaligned, it can lead to errors. Clean the scale and use a magnifying glass if necessary. For critical alignments, rely on the theodolite reading rather than the scale.

Over‑tightening

Tightening the locking bolts excessively can distort the mounting base or damage the pivot bearing, affecting future adjustment. Use a torque wrench to specified values.

Lack of Maintenance Records

Without proper documentation of azimuth settings, it's difficult to detect drift over time. Keep a log of all alignment checks and adjustments, including the date, measured angle, and any corrective action taken.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Adjustment Screw Won't Turn
Likely Cause
Corrosion or galling of the threaded adjustment rod.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the screw and threads for rust or seizing, and check whether anti-seize lubricant was applied at installation.
Corrective Action
Apply penetrating lubricant and carefully free the screw, or replace with a stainless steel screw and anti-seize compound; exercise it periodically to prevent recurrence.
Unit Found Drifted Out of Azimuth Between Checks
Likely Cause
Locking bolts have relaxed under vibration.
Diagnostic Steps
Check locking bolt torque against specification and inspect for the absence of locking washers or thread-locker.
Corrective Action
Re-torque the locking bolts to specification and fit locking washers or medium-strength thread-locker.
Azimuth Setting Doesn't Match the Runway Centreline
Likely Cause
Incorrect reference sighting — the theodolite not precisely set up on the centreline, or an uncalibrated instrument used.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the survey reference points and confirm the theodolite's calibration status.
Corrective Action
Re-set up the theodolite on verified reference points, recalibrate or replace the instrument if out of tolerance, and repeat the sighting.
Index Scale Reading Doesn't Match Instrument Reading
Likely Cause
A worn, dirty, or misaligned index scale.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the scale for dirt, wear, or misalignment, and compare it against a fresh theodolite reading.
Corrective Action
Clean the scale, and for critical alignments rely on the theodolite reading rather than the scale; replace the scale if worn.
Mounting Base Shows Distortion After Adjustment
Likely Cause
Over-tightening of the locking bolts distorting the mounting base or damaging the pivot bearing.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the base and pivot bearing for distortion or damage, and check whether a torque wrench was used at the specified value.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged base/bearing, and re-tighten using a torque wrench to the specified value going forward.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Adjustment Mechanism Seizure
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion binding the screw or gear threads, or dried-out lubricant allowing dirt ingress into the adjustment mechanism.
Effect
Adjustment screw won't turn, preventing azimuth correction during a scheduled or corrective alignment visit.
Detection Method
Functional check of adjustment travel during scheduled maintenance, and re-lubrication of the mechanism per the maintenance schedule.
Locking Mechanism Creep
High Severity
Root Cause
A locking mechanism that does not fully re-engage after adjustment, or gradual loosening under wind and thermal cycling loads.
Effect
Unit found drifted out of azimuth between checks, degrading the accuracy of the approach guidance presented to pilots.
Detection Method
Periodic azimuth verification against the runway centreline survey, and inspection of the locking mechanism for full engagement.
Reference Survey Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The runway centreline reference point shifted due to pavement work or resurfacing since the PAPI's last formal alignment.
Effect
Azimuth setting doesn't match the runway centreline, even though the adjustment mechanism itself is functioning correctly.
Detection Method
Cross-check of the current azimuth setting against an up-to-date runway centreline survey.
Index Scale Wear or Misreading
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wear or fading of the index scale markings, or an index pointer that has shifted relative to the scale over time.
Effect
Index scale reading doesn't match instrument reading, causing confusion during field verification of the current setting.
Detection Method
Cross-check of the index scale reading against an independent instrument reading, and inspection of the scale and pointer for damage.
Mounting Base Distortion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Over-torquing during a prior adjustment, or metal fatigue in the base accumulated over repeated adjustment cycles.
Effect
Mounting base shows distortion after adjustment, which can introduce a small but persistent azimuth error going forward.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the mounting base for distortion after each adjustment, and dimensional check against the as-installed baseline.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Glide Path Safety Impact
Operational

Azimuth alignment keeps the PAPI beam oriented parallel to the runway centreline; an uncorrected drift changes the visual guidance a pilot sees on approach, so treat a confirmed mismatch as a priority correction.

Confirm Fixture State Before Adjustment
Personnel Safety

Follow the aerodrome's electrical safety procedure for the associated PAPI unit and base can before working at the mounting interface, since the adjustment mechanism is integrated into or near the fixture's mounting base.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Do not over-torque or rigidly fix the adjustment mechanism in a way that compromises the frangible mounting interface; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact per the frangibility requirement.

Verify Full Lock Engagement
Mechanical

Confirm the locking mechanism has fully re-engaged after every adjustment; partial engagement is a recurring cause of the unit drifting out of azimuth between scheduled checks under wind and thermal cycling loads.

Confirm Reference Survey Is Current
Verification

Before adjusting, verify the runway centreline reference point used for the setting is still valid, particularly after any pavement work or resurfacing that could have shifted it independently of the PAPI unit itself.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Adjustment work on an in-service PAPI unit requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the unit will be off-aim or out of service during the correction.

Mechanism Care
Mechanical

Re-lubricate the adjustment mechanism per the maintenance schedule and keep it protected from dirt ingress, since corrosion and dried-out lubricant are the leading causes of the mechanism seizing when correction is needed.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify the corrected azimuth against the runway centreline survey and confirm the lock is fully engaged before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Partial Lock Engagement Feels Identical to Full Engagement by Hand
Mechanical

A locking mechanism that hasn't fully seated can feel secure when checked by hand immediately after adjustment, only to work loose under wind and thermal cycling over subsequent days; verify full engagement by the manufacturer's specified method, such as a witness mark or torque check, not by feel alone.

A Shifted Centreline Reference Can Make a Correctly-Adjusted Unit Look Wrong
Verification

Pavement work or resurfacing can move the physical reference point used for azimuth setting without moving the PAPI unit itself; a unit reported as "out of azimuth" is sometimes actually correctly aligned to a reference that has since shifted, so verify the reference survey's currency before adjusting the unit.

Frangibility and Adjustment Range Can Work Against Each Other
Mechanical

A mechanism over-tightened to prevent azimuth drift can compromise the frangible mounting's ability to shear cleanly on impact; treat the two requirements as a design trade-off to verify together, not lock engagement alone.

Dried-Out Lubricant Seizes at the Worst Possible Time
Durability

An adjustment mechanism that hasn't been re-lubricated on schedule tends to seize gradually and often isn't discovered until a correction is actually needed, turning a routine adjustment into an emergency mechanical intervention; keep re-lubrication on a fixed schedule regardless of whether a correction has been needed recently.

Wind and Thermal Cycling Cause Slow Drift, Not Sudden Failure
Environmental

Repeated micro-movement from wind loading and daily thermal expansion and contraction gradually walks a marginally-engaged lock out of position over weeks or months; a unit found off-azimuth without any obvious cause is often explained by this cumulative effect rather than a single event.

Record the As-Left Azimuth Value, Not Just 'Corrected'
Compliance

Logging the actual measured azimuth value after correction, rather than simply noting the unit was adjusted, gives the next technician a baseline to compare against and helps distinguish a genuinely new drift from a measurement that was always at the edge of tolerance.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about PAPI azimuth adjustment: purpose, procedure, tolerances, specifications, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is PAPI azimuth adjustment?

It is the mechanical system that sets the horizontal (left/right) orientation of a PAPI light unit. It is usually built into the mounting base or the frangible pole interface and rotates the whole unit about its vertical axis so the beam is aimed along the runway centreline.

Q2Why does PAPI azimuth alignment matter?

The elevation angle that separates red from white is set to fractions of a degree, so even a small horizontal misalignment can shift what the beam boundary looks like from a normal approach position. A pilot slightly left or right of centreline would then read an indication calibrated for a different sightline.

Q3What are the parts of an azimuth adjustment mechanism?

A pivot point (a central bearing or bolt), adjustment screws or slides that push or pull the unit to the desired angle, a locking system of bolts, clamping plates or locknuts, and an index scale that indicates angular position to help with setting and verification.

Q4How is PAPI azimuth adjusted?

The unit is loosely bolted to its base, then a theodolite or alignment telescope on the runway centreline (often at the threshold) is sighted on a reference mark or target on the unit to measure the deviation. The adjustment screws are turned incrementally, re-checking after each turn, then the locking bolts are tightened and the alignment is confirmed once more and recorded in the maintenance log.

Q5What azimuth adjustment range and tolerance are typical?

The typical range is ±5° (±10° in extended versions), with alignment locked to within about ±0.5°. Fine-pitch screws give resolution of 0.02° to 0.05° per turn and a minimum increment below 0.01°.

Q6What are typical azimuth adjustment specifications?

Locking is by clamping bolts (M8 to M12) or eccentric cams at 20 to 40 Nm. Materials are 316L stainless steel, aluminium alloy or galvanised steel, operating from -40°C to +70°C, with a load capacity of about 50 kg vertical and 30 kg horizontal wind load. The mechanism weighs about 1 to 3 kg.

Q7Is azimuth adjustment fitted to every PAPI?

Yes, it is a standard feature on PAPI installations, including permanent systems, helicopter PAPI (referenced to the helipad centreline where applicable), portable units with quick-adjust mechanisms, and retrofits where existing pole or foundation positions must be accommodated.

Q8Which standards apply to PAPI azimuth adjustment?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (PAPI azimuth alignment tolerance), FAA AC 150/5345-28 (PAPI installation and alignment procedures) and FAA AC 150/5345-46 (photometric performance and alignment verification), plus ISO 9001 for manufacturing quality and ASTM B117 for salt-spray corrosion testing. Always verify the current edition.

Q9How often should PAPI azimuth be checked?

Typical practice is a semi-annual azimuth verification against the reference, an annual adjustment screw corrosion check and locking bolt torque check, and a logged record at each adjustment. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common azimuth adjustment problems?

Seized or galled adjustment screws from corrosion, locking bolts loosened by vibration (allowing drift), incorrect theodolite reference sighting, misreading a worn or dirty index scale, over-tightening that distorts the base or damages the pivot bearing, and missing adjustment records that make drift hard to detect.

Q11What precautions apply when adjusting PAPI azimuth?

Follow the aerodrome's electrical safety procedure for the unit and base can, and confirm the runway centreline reference survey is still valid. Do not over-torque or rigidly fix the mechanism in a way that defeats the frangible mounting, confirm the lock has fully re-engaged, and coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM where the unit will be off-aim.
