HV Tester

A high-voltage test instrument used to perform dielectric withstand and insulation breakdown tests on AGL cables, transformers, switchgear, and other electrical equipment. HV testers apply voltages up to several kilovolts to verify insulation integrity and ensure safety under overvoltage conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

An HV Tester (High Voltage Tester), also known as a dielectric strength tester or hipot tester, is an instrument that applies a high AC or DC voltage to a device under test to assess the integrity of its insulation. In AGL systems, HV testers are used to:

  • Verify dielectric strength of cables, transformers, and fixtures
  • Detect insulation weaknesses that could lead to breakdown during lightning strikes or switching surges
  • Perform production tests on new equipment before commissioning
  • Confirm repair quality after cable splicing or transformer rewinding
  • Comply with safety standards by ensuring equipment can withstand transient overvoltages

HV testers are available in portable and benchtop configurations, with output voltages ranging from a few hundred volts up to 100 kV or more, depending on the application. They are essential for ensuring the long-term reliability and safety of airfield lighting systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The HV tester exists to answer a question a standard low-voltage test can't: will this cable, transformer, or fixture's insulation actually hold up under the high-voltage stress of a lightning strike or switching surge, not just under normal operating conditions. Insulation that performs fine at everyday voltages can still have microscopic weaknesses that only reveal themselves under the kind of transient overvoltage an airfield's exposed, outdoor lighting infrastructure will eventually experience.

By deliberately applying that higher stress in a controlled test environment, the instrument finds those latent weaknesses before they find themselves during an actual lightning event or surge in the field — which is why it's applied both to new equipment before commissioning and to repaired cables or transformers, confirming that a splice or rewind hasn't introduced a new insulation vulnerability.

Application

Where Is It Used?

HV testers are used in various phases of AGL system lifecycle management:

  • Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT): Testing newly manufactured cables, transformers, and fixtures before shipment.
  • Installation Commissioning: Verifying insulation integrity after installation, especially for underground cables and transformers.
  • Preventive Maintenance: Periodic high-voltage testing to detect insulation aging, moisture ingress, or mechanical damage.
  • Fault Diagnosis: Identifying insulation breakdown that causes earth faults or circuit failures.
  • Post-Repair Verification: Confirming that repairs have restored insulation strength.
  • Type Testing: Qualifying new equipment designs to meet ICAO, FAA, and IEC requirements.

Testing is performed on de-energized circuits, with strict safety protocols including lockout/tagout, grounding, and barrier protection.

Care

Storage & Handling

Because the instrument generates hazardous voltages, it is stored in a locked case or cabinet when not in use, with high-voltage leads and probes kept separate from other test equipment to avoid an inadvertent live connection. Before the instrument is packed away, any device under test is fully discharged and the tester's own output is confirmed at zero, since HV testers can retain a stored charge on internal capacitors after a test is complete. Leads are coiled loosely to avoid stressing the high-voltage insulation, and safety accessories such as interlocks, warning barriers, and grounding leads are stored and inventoried alongside the instrument so a complete safety setup is available for the next test. Calibration and safety interlock function are checked on the schedule set by the manufacturer, and any lead or probe showing insulation damage is taken out of service immediately rather than used until the next scheduled inspection.

Function

How Does It Work?

An HV tester generates a high voltage (AC or DC) and applies it between the conductor(s) under test and ground (or between conductors). The tester monitors the leakage current that flows through the insulation. The test is typically performed for a specified duration (e.g., 1 minute) at a specified test voltage (e.g., 2× rated voltage + 1000V). If the leakage current exceeds a preset threshold, the tester indicates a failure (breakdown). Key operating principles include:

  • Voltage Generation: A step-up transformer or voltage multiplier circuit generates the required high voltage from a low-voltage supply.
  • Current Monitoring: The tester measures the leakage current and compares it to the allowable limit (typically 5-10 mA for most AGL equipment).
  • Ramp-Up Control: Most testers apply the voltage gradually to avoid transient stresses and allow for accurate measurement of insulation properties.
  • Automatic Discharge: After the test, the equipment automatically discharges the capacitive charge stored in the cable or device under test.
  • Fail/Pass Indication: Visual and audible alarms indicate whether the test passed or failed, with detailed data logging for traceability.

AC hipot tests are common for cables, while DC hipot tests are often used for transformers and high-capacitance loads. Some advanced testers also perform partial discharge measurements to detect microscopic insulation defects.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Safety, not the voltage generator, is what drives the internal architecture of a hipot tester. The MCU commands a controlled ramp on the HV transformer or multiplier stage rather than switching full voltage on directly, both to protect the device under test from transient stress and so the current-sense circuit can watch the rising leakage current for an early sign of impending breakdown. A fast comparator continuously checks the sensed current against a pre-set trip threshold on every cycle of the ramp and, independently of the main control loop, can cut the high-voltage output within milliseconds if the threshold is exceeded — this trip path is typically hardware-latched rather than firmware-only, so a locked-up MCU cannot leave the output energised. Once the test voltage is reached and the dwell timer completes (or a trip occurs), the MCU switches from generation to a controlled discharge path that bleeds down the capacitive energy stored in the cable or device under test through a bleed resistor, and only releases the "test complete" interlock once the discharge circuit confirms the output has decayed to a safe residual voltage. The pass/fail decision and all measured values are then latched to the display and, on logging-capable models, written to memory with a timestamp.

The trip path runs in hardware, in parallel with the MCU's normal ramp/dwell control — a design that guarantees the output can be cut even if the main controller stalls mid-test.
Operating Principle

Dielectric Withstand Test Sequence

Every hipot test runs the same ramp / hold / monitor sequence regardless of outcome. Both the pass and fail paths end in an automatic discharge of the capacitive charge stored in the device under test, so the equipment is left safe to disconnect either way.
Construction

Physical Construction

The tester's high-voltage generation stage, comprising a step-up transformer or voltage multiplier, is housed in a heavily insulated, high-dielectric-strength enclosure separated from the low-voltage control and display section by an internal barrier to protect the operator from the generated test voltage. Output terminals are recessed behind interlocked, high-voltage-rated shrouds, and a physical safety interlock disables the high-voltage output the moment an access panel or test compartment is opened. An emergency-stop button and a visible, illuminated high-voltage-present indicator are mounted prominently on the control panel, with the enclosure itself constructed from flame-retardant, impact-resistant material rated to contain internal arcing. Portable units are built into a rugged case with wheels or a carry handle and a compartment for high-voltage test leads and safety grounding equipment, while bench units use a rack- or trolley-mount chassis with forced-air ventilation to dissipate heat from sustained high-voltage testing.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Test Voltage Selection

Applying a test voltage higher than the equipment's rated insulation level can cause catastrophic failure. Always refer to the manufacturer's specifications or relevant standards (e.g., 2× rated voltage + 1000V for cables).

Leakage Current from Surface Contamination

Dust, moisture, or dirt on the surface of insulators or cable terminations can cause leakage currents that falsely indicate failure. Ensure test surfaces are clean and dry, and use guard terminals to eliminate surface leakage effects.

Insufficient Discharge After DC Testing

DC high-voltage tests leave a capacitive charge on the cable. Failing to discharge adequately can result in electric shock. Always use the tester's automatic discharge function and verify zero voltage before handling.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Test Trips Out Immediately on Insulation That Should Be Healthy
Likely Cause
Test voltage set higher than the equipment's rated insulation level, or leakage current from surface contamination (dust, moisture, dirt) rather than a genuine insulation fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the selected test voltage against the manufacturer's specification or the relevant standard (e.g., 2× rated voltage + 1000 V for cables); inspect and clean insulator or termination surfaces before retesting.
Corrective Action
Correct the test voltage setting if it was too high, clean and dry all test surfaces, and use guard terminals to exclude surface leakage before re-running the test.
Inconsistent Readings Between Repeat Tests
Likely Cause
Surface contamination introducing variable leakage paths, or ambient humidity/temperature changes between test runs affecting insulation resistance.
Diagnostic Steps
Check test surfaces for visible dust, moisture, or contamination; note ambient temperature and humidity at each test and compare against prior records; confirm guard terminal connections are secure if fitted.
Corrective Action
Clean test surfaces thoroughly, retest under stable ambient conditions where possible, and record environmental conditions alongside each result so trends aren't misread as equipment degradation.
Residual Voltage or Shock Risk After a DC Test
Likely Cause
The capacitive charge left on the cable or equipment after a DC high-voltage test was not fully discharged.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the tester's automatic discharge function actually completed before disconnecting; independently verify zero voltage at the test point with a rated voltage detector before handling.
Corrective Action
Always run the full discharge cycle and confirm zero voltage with an independent detector before touching test leads or terminations; do not rely solely on the tester's internal indication.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Treat as a Lethal Hazard
Electrical Safety

An HV/hipot tester deliberately generates dangerous voltages; only trained, authorised personnel should operate it, and the test area must be barricaded and clear of unauthorised personnel before energising.

Establish a Clear Test Area
Personnel Safety

Post a second person as a safety watch where required, use warning barriers or signage, and confirm no one is in contact with the device under test before applying voltage.

Discharge and Ground After Every Test
Electrical Safety

Always discharge and short the device under test to ground after a test, particularly after a DC hipot test, since cable and equipment capacitance can retain a dangerous charge well after the voltage source is removed.

Never Touch the DUT During or Immediately After Test
Personnel Safety

Do not touch test leads, terminals, or the device under test while voltage is applied or before confirming discharge, even if the display indicates the test has ended.

PPE for High-Voltage Testing
Personnel Safety

Use insulated gloves and tools rated for the test voltage, and follow the instrument's interlock and ramp-up/ramp-down procedures rather than bypassing them for speed.

Avoid Testing in Wet or Contaminated Conditions
Operational

Do not perform HV testing in rain, high humidity, or on visibly contaminated insulation surfaces, since surface leakage can produce a false failure or an unsafe flashover.

Restore Isolation Before Leaving Live
Compliance

Remove all test connections and confirm the circuit is restored to its normal, safe configuration before re-energising it for service.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Cable Temperature and Humidity Bias the Result on the Day
Field Technique

Insulation resistance and dielectric withstand readings on buried cable are meaningfully affected by soil temperature and moisture on the day of the test, so a cable that passes comfortably in dry summer conditions can show a markedly different result after weeks of rain. Record ambient conditions alongside every HV test result so later trend comparisons account for this rather than mistaking weather variation for genuine degradation.

A Pass at Test Voltage Isn't a Margin Guarantee
Testing

Cable or transformer insulation that just barely passes a proof test at the specified voltage may have very little remaining margin, and a straight pass/fail result on its own hides how close to the edge the insulation actually is. Where the test method allows, record the actual breakdown or leakage current trend rather than only the pass/fail outcome.

Trend Test Results Year Over Year on the Same Cable Run
Diagnostics

A single HV test result tells you whether a cable passed today; a sequence of results on the same run over several maintenance cycles tells you whether it's degrading, and how fast. Insulation that's trending steadily downward toward the threshold, even while still passing, deserves closer attention than a result that's stable year to year.

Never Assume a Discharged Cable Stays Discharged
Electrical Safety

Long cable runs and cables with significant capacitance can regain a dangerous residual charge after being discharged, particularly if the discharge step was rushed. Re-verify zero charge immediately before handling conductors, even on a cable that was properly discharged minutes earlier.

Verify the Tester's Own Calibration Before a Compliance Campaign
Calibration

Committing to a full-airfield HV test campaign on an instrument whose own calibration is close to expiring risks having to repeat significant portions of the work if a later calibration check shows the instrument had drifted. Confirm calibration currency with margin before scheduling large testing programmes, not just before individual tests.

A Marginal Pass After Splicing Work Warrants a Retest, Not a Sign-Off
Quality

A repaired splice that passes an HV test but noticeably closer to the threshold than the surrounding healthy cable often indicates workmanship that will degrade faster than the rest of the run. Treat a marginal post-repair result as a prompt to inspect the splice technique, not simply a pass to close out the work order.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about HV testers (hipot testers) in AGL maintenance: purpose, use, safety, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is an HV tester?

An HV (high voltage) tester, also called a dielectric strength or hipot tester, applies a high AC or DC voltage to a device under test to check the integrity of its insulation. In AGL it is used on cables, transformers and fixtures.

Q2When are HV tests done on AGL equipment?

During factory acceptance testing, installation commissioning (especially for underground cables), preventive maintenance to detect insulation ageing or moisture ingress, and fault diagnosis when insulation breakdown causes earth faults or circuit failures.

Q3How does an HV tester work?

A step-up transformer or voltage multiplier generates the test voltage, applied gradually between the conductor and ground or between conductors. The tester measures leakage current and compares it with an allowable limit, typically 5 to 10 mA for most AGL equipment.

Q4Why is it important to select the correct test voltage?

Applying a test voltage higher than the equipment's rated insulation level can itself damage the insulation. Always use the voltage specified for the equipment and the test type.

Q5Why must cables be discharged after DC high-voltage testing?

DC tests leave a capacitive charge on the cable, which can be dangerous to personnel. The tester's automatic discharge function should fully dissipate the charge before anyone touches the cable.

Q6Which standards apply to HV testers?

The main references are IEC 61010-1 (safety of test and measurement equipment), IEC 61180 (high-voltage test techniques for low-voltage equipment), IEEE 400 (field testing of shielded power cable systems) and EN 50191 (safety of high-voltage test equipment).

Q7How often should an HV tester be maintained?

Annual calibration against a traceable high-voltage standard, and quarterly checks of the discharge function and of the test leads and guard terminals.

Q8What are the common problems when using an HV tester?

Incorrect test voltage selection, leakage current from surface contamination such as dust or moisture on insulators, and insufficient discharge after DC testing.
