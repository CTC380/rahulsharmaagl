O-Ring

A circular elastomeric sealing ring with a round cross-section, used to create a watertight and airtight seal between mating components in AGL fixtures. Essential for maintaining IP67/IP68 ingress protection at threaded joints, cable entries, and between housing sections.

Overview

What Is It?

An O-Ring is a torus-shaped (doughnut-shaped) seal with a circular cross-section, made from an elastomeric material such as silicone, EPDM, nitrile (NBR), fluorosilicone (FVMQ), or Viton® (FKM). In AGL fixtures, O-rings are used to seal threaded joints (e.g., between the top cover and housing), cable gland entries, and between mating housing sections. When compressed between two surfaces, the O-ring deforms to fill the gap, creating a positive seal against moisture, dust, and contaminants. O-rings are a simple, reliable, and cost-effective sealing solution, widely used in AGL fixtures due to their excellent resistance to compression set, temperature extremes, and chemical exposure.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The O-ring's purpose is to preserve the ingress protection rating — typically IP67 or IP68 — that keeps an AGL fixture's electrical components dry and functioning throughout its service life. Airfield lighting circuits operate in standing water, de-icing fluid, temperature swings, and constant vibration, and a single failed seal at a housing joint, cable gland, or connector can let moisture reach a live connection, causing corrosion, a short circuit, or an unplanned outage on a runway that must stay lit. Because the O-ring's seal depends on maintaining a specific compression against the mating surfaces, its role is not just to fill a gap once during assembly but to keep sealing reliably despite repeated thermal expansion, contraction, and vibration over years of outdoor service — which is why material selection and correct groove dimensions matter as much as the O-ring itself.

Application

Where Is It Used?

O-rings are used extensively throughout AGL fixtures and associated equipment:

  • Fixture Housing Seals: Between the top cover and housing in runway, taxiway, and approach lights.
  • Lens and Prism Seals: To seal the optical window or prism assembly against moisture ingress.
  • Cable Gland Seals: To seal cable entry points on the fixture base or junction boxes.
  • Connector Seals: Inside L-823 connectors to prevent water ingress at cable terminations.
  • Isolating Transformer Seals: To seal the transformer housing and cable entries.
  • CCR and Control Panel Seals: To seal cabinet doors and cable entry points.
  • Valve and Vent Seals: To seal pressure equalisation valves and moisture vents.
Placement

Installation & Location

The O-ring seats into a machined groove sized so it's compressed by roughly 15–30% of its cross-section once the joint is closed, providing the squeeze needed to seal without over-stretching or pinching the ring. A light film of compatible silicone grease is applied before fitting, both to ease assembly and to help the ring seal on the first closure, and it's checked for cuts, flat spots, or twists in the groove before the joint is closed, since a damaged or twisted ring will leak regardless of how well the rest of the joint is torqued. Like the gasket, it's treated as a routine consumable and replaced, not reused, every time the joint it seals is opened for maintenance.

Function

How Does It Work?

An O-ring works by being compressed between two surfaces, creating a barrier that prevents the passage of fluids or gases. The principle is based on the elastomer's ability to deform under pressure and return to its original shape when the pressure is released:

  • Compression: When the mating surfaces are tightened, the O-ring is squeezed, deforming from its round cross-section into a flattened oval shape. This deformation creates a contact pressure that seals the gap.
  • Gland Design: The O-ring is typically seated in a rectangular groove (gland) that provides a controlled amount of compression (typically 15–30% of the O-ring's cross-sectional diameter).
  • Pressure Activation: In dynamic applications, system pressure can further energise the O-ring, enhancing the seal.
  • Self-Sealing: The elastomeric memory of the material ensures that the O-ring maintains contact pressure even with minor variations in surface finish or thermal expansion.

Proper O-ring performance depends on correct material selection (compatible with operating temperatures and fluids), accurate groove dimensions, and adequate compression force. Over-compression can cause stress damage, while under-compression can lead to leakage.

Construction

Physical Construction

O-rings are extruded and vulcanised (or injection-moulded, for smaller sizes) as a continuous elastomer torus with a controlled circular cross-section, produced to a standard size series so replacements can be sourced generically rather than only from the fixture manufacturer. The ring seats in a machined groove — square or half-round in cross-section — sized so that when the joint is closed, the O-ring is compressed to roughly 15–30% of its free cross-sectional diameter, enough to guarantee a positive seal without over-stressing the material into a permanent compression set. Groove dimensions also allow a small amount of clearance (gland fill) so the compressed rubber has somewhere to migrate rather than being fully confined, which would risk splitting the ring on reassembly. Material choice (silicone, EPDM, nitrile, fluorosilicone, or Viton) is matched to the specific joint's temperature range and chemical exposure, since a ring correctly sized but made from the wrong compound can still fail early through hardening or swelling.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Compression Set (Permanent Deformation)

Over time, O-rings can lose their elasticity due to thermal cycling and compression set, resulting in a reduction of sealing force. This can lead to moisture ingress. Regular inspection and replacement of O-rings during lamp change or maintenance is essential.

UV & Ozone Degradation

O-rings exposed to direct sunlight and ozone can become brittle and crack. Use of UV-stabilised materials (silicone, EPDM, FVMQ) is essential for long-term durability in outdoor airfield environments.

Chemical Attack from De-icing Fluids

De-icing fluids (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol) and jet fuel can cause swelling or degradation of some elastomers. Always use O-ring materials that are compatible with the chemicals used at the airfield. Viton® and fluorosilicone offer excellent chemical resistance.

Improper Installation (Twisting, Pinching, Over-Torque)

If the O-ring is twisted, pinched, or over-compressed during installation, it can be damaged and fail to seal properly. Use proper lubrication (compatible with the O-ring material) during assembly, and follow the manufacturer's torque specifications.

Incorrect Sizing or Material Selection

Using an O-ring of the wrong size (ID or CS) or incompatible material can lead to leakage or premature failure. Always refer to the fixture's parts list for the correct O-ring part number and material.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Moisture Ingress at a Sealed Joint
Likely Cause
O-ring has taken a permanent compression set and lost sealing force, or was twisted, pinched, or over-compressed during a previous reassembly.
Diagnostic Steps
Remove the joint and inspect the O-ring for flattening, twist marks, or visible gaps in the seal groove; check groove dimensions against the O-ring's rated cross-section.
Corrective Action
Replace with a new O-ring of the correct part number, lubricate with a compatible lubricant, and seat it evenly in the groove before torquing to the manufacturer's specified value.
O-Ring Brittle or Cracked
Likely Cause
UV and ozone degradation of a non-UV-stabilised elastomer exposed outdoors over an extended period.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect for surface cracking or brittleness when flexed; confirm the O-ring material matches the specified UV-stabilised type (silicone, EPDM, FVMQ).
Corrective Action
Replace with the correct UV-stabilised material as specified for the fixture; correct the part record if the wrong material was previously fitted.
O-Ring Swollen or Softened
Likely Cause
Chemical attack from de-icing fluid or fuel exposure on an elastomer material not rated for that chemical environment.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect for swelling, tackiness, or softening compared to an unused O-ring; identify the likely chemical exposure source at the installation location.
Corrective Action
Replace with a chemically resistant material such as Viton or fluorosilicone appropriate to the site's chemical exposure.
Seal Fails Shortly After Reassembly
Likely Cause
Wrong O-ring size or material was fitted, or the correct O-ring was damaged by twisting, pinching, or over-torquing during installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the fitted O-ring's part number and dimensions (ID and cross-section) against the fixture's official parts list; inspect for installation damage.
Corrective Action
Fit the correct part number, lubricate appropriately, and follow the manufacturer's torque specification exactly to avoid over-compression on reassembly.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Correct Size & Material
Workmanship

Fit an O-ring matching the specified part number, cross-section, and elastomer type for the application; a substitute size or material is a common cause of an early re-seal failure.

Careful Installation
Workmanship

Avoid twisting, pinching, or over-stretching the O-ring during fitting, and use a light film of compatible lubricant where specified to seat it evenly in the groove without damage.

Do Not Over-Torque the Joint
Workmanship

Tighten the mating fasteners to the specified torque; over-torquing can pinch or extrude the O-ring out of its groove, defeating the seal it is meant to provide.

Chemical Compatibility
Operational

Confirm the O-ring's elastomer is rated for any de-icing fluid, fuel, or cleaning solvent it may contact in service; an incompatible chemical will swell or soften the material and compromise the seal.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep the removed O-ring and any replacement stock accounted for during handling, and dispose of degraded seals appropriately to prevent foreign object debris on or near the movement area.

Verify the Seal Before Closing Out
Compliance

Check for even seating and no visible pinching or extrusion once the joint is closed, since a poorly seated O-ring can fail shortly after reassembly.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return an enclosure to service with a cracked, flattened, or incorrectly sized O-ring; replace it rather than reuse a compressed or aged seal.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Petroleum-Based Lubricants Can Attack the Wrong Elastomer
Workmanship

Using a lubricant compatible with one O-ring material on a different elastomer type can cause swelling or softening that isn't apparent until the seal fails later; confirm lubricant compatibility with the specific elastomer, not just that a lubricant is specified.

Compression Set Is a Time-and-Temperature Effect, Not Just Wear
Durability

An O-ring can lose sealing force purely from being compressed in place over years, even with zero mate/unmate cycles and no visible damage; replace O-rings on a service interval rather than only when a leak or visible defect prompts it.

Elastomer Rating Needs to Match Actual, Not Nominal, Operating Temperature
Durability

A fixture's internal temperature near a high-drive LED or halogen source can run well above ambient; specify O-ring material rated for the actual internal temperature the seal will see in service, not just the published ambient rating of the fixture.

Cross-Section Mismatches Can Still Appear to Fit
Workmanship

An O-ring with a similar outer diameter but different cross-section thickness can be forced into a groove and appear seated, while providing inadequate or excessive compression; verify cross-section against the specified part number, not just that it physically fits the groove.

Extrusion Marks Point to Over-Torque, Not a Bad O-Ring
Troubleshooting

A thin sliver of extruded material at the seam of a failed O-ring usually indicates the joint was over-torqued or the gap wasn't within tolerance, rather than a defective seal; check joint tolerance and fastener torque before assuming the replacement O-ring will fare any better.

Shelf-Stored O-Rings Age Even Unused
Operational

Elastomer seals can degrade in storage from heat, ozone, or UV exposure well before installation; rotate stock and avoid drawing replacement O-rings from old, improperly stored batches even if they look new.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL O-rings: materials, compression, standards, maintenance and seal failures.

Q1What is an O-ring and what is it made of in AGL fixtures?

An O-ring is a torus-shaped elastomer seal with a circular cross-section. In AGL equipment it is typically silicone (VMQ), EPDM, nitrile (NBR), fluorosilicone (FVMQ) or Viton (FKM), and it seals threaded joints, cable gland entries and mating housing sections.

Q2Why are O-rings important in airfield lighting?

They preserve the IP67 or IP68 rating that keeps a fixture's electrical parts dry. AGL circuits operate in standing water, de-icing fluid, temperature swings and vibration, and one failed seal at a housing joint, cable gland or connector can let moisture reach a live connection.

Q3Where are O-rings used in AGL equipment?

Between the top cover and housing on runway, taxiway and approach lights, in lens and prism seals, in cable gland seals at fixture bases and junction boxes, and inside L-823 connectors to keep water out of the mated pair.

Q4How much should an O-ring be compressed?

The groove is sized so the ring is compressed by roughly 15-30% of its cross-section once the joint is closed. Under-compression leaves a leak path, and over-compression stresses the elastomer and leads to damage or compression set.

Q5Which O-ring material should be used?

Choose the compound to match the joint's temperature range and chemical exposure. Silicone handles about -55°C to +200°C and FKM about -30°C to +150°C. Silicone and FVMQ have excellent UV and ozone resistance, EPDM is good, and NBR is poor without additives.

Q6What sizes and hardness are typical?

Typical AGL cross-sections are 1.0-6.0 mm, and hardness ranges from Shore A 50 to 90 depending on the application. Rings are made to standard size series so replacements can be sourced generically.

Q7Which standards apply to AGL O-rings?

IEC 60529 (IP code), ISO 3601 (O-ring inside diameters, cross-sections, tolerances and designation), ASTM D2000 (rubber products classification) and FAA AC 150/5345-46 for the environmental sealing requirements of AGL fixtures.

Q8What lubricant should be used when fitting an O-ring?

A light film of compatible silicone grease eases assembly and helps the ring seal on first closure. A lubricant that suits one elastomer can swell or soften another, so confirm compatibility with the specific O-ring material.

Q9How do O-rings fail and how often should they be checked?

Common failures are compression set (permanent flattening), UV and ozone cracking, and swelling or softening from de-icing fluid or solvents. Inspect annually and replace O-rings during lamp changes or other maintenance that opens the joint.

Q10What should I do if moisture gets into a sealed joint?

Open the joint and check the O-ring for flattening, twist marks or gaps, and check the groove dimensions against the ring's rated cross-section. Replace it with the correct size and material, seating it without twisting or pinching.

Q11Can any O-ring of similar size be substituted?

No. A substitute size or elastomer is a common cause of early seal failure, so fit a ring that matches the specified part number, cross-section and compound for that joint.
