Marker Beacon

A ground-based radio navigation aid that provides distance‑to‑runway information to aircraft on an Instrument Landing System (ILS) approach. Marker beacons transmit vertical fan‑shaped signals at fixed distances from the runway threshold, enabling pilots to identify key positions (outer, middle, and inner markers) along the final approach path.

Overview

What Is It?

A Marker Beacon is a ground‑based VHF radio transmitter that radiates a narrow, fan‑shaped signal vertically upward. It is used in conjunction with the Instrument Landing System (ILS) to provide distance‑fix information to pilots during the final approach. The system typically consists of three marker beacons located at specific distances from the runway threshold:

  • Outer Marker (OM): Located approximately 4–7 nautical miles (7.4–13 km) from the threshold, at or near the glide slope intercept altitude. It identifies the initial approach fix and the final approach segment. The signal is modulated at 400 Hz and produces a continuous series of dashes (— — —) in the cockpit.
  • Middle Marker (MM): Located approximately 1.1 km (0.7 miles) from the threshold. It identifies the decision height point for CAT I approaches. The signal is modulated at 1300 Hz and produces alternating dots and dashes (· — · —).
  • Inner Marker (IM): Located approximately 300 metres (1,000 ft) from the threshold. It identifies the decision height point for CAT II/III approaches. The signal is modulated at 3000 Hz and produces a continuous series of dots (· · · ·).

Each marker beacon transmits a 75 MHz carrier, which is received by the aircraft’s marker beacon receiver, triggering both an aural tone and a visual light (blue, amber, white) in the cockpit to inform the pilot of the aircraft’s position along the approach.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The marker beacon's purpose is to give the pilot a positive, unambiguous confirmation of specific distances along the final approach, independent of the localizer and glide slope signals that provide continuous lateral and vertical guidance. Because each of the three beacons uses a different modulation tone and produces a distinct aural and visual cue in the cockpit, a pilot flying the approach gets a series of confirmed checkpoints, initial approach fix, then decision height for CAT I, then decision height for CAT II/III, without needing to interpret a continuously varying deviation indicator. This distance-fix role is largely what DME now provides on most modern approaches, but where marker beacons remain installed they still serve the same underlying purpose of marking a fixed point along the approach path with a signal the pilot cannot mistake for anything else.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Marker beacons are installed on precision approach runways that support ILS operations. They are used for:

  • CAT I Precision Approaches: The outer and middle markers provide critical distance and decision height references.
  • CAT II and CAT III Precision Approaches: The middle and inner markers provide enhanced distance and decision height references for low‑visibility operations.
  • Military Airfields: Supporting military ILS approaches and training.
  • Non‑ILS Approaches: In some cases, marker beacons are also used for localiser‑only or VOR approaches as a distance‑fix aid.
Placement

Installation & Location

Each marker sits directly on the extended runway centreline, at the fixed distance from the threshold its role requires — the outer marker roughly 4-7 nm out, the middle marker around 3,500 ft from the threshold, and the inner marker (where fitted) just short of it. The antenna radiates a narrow fan-shaped pattern straight upward, so the site must be clear of overhead obstructions and levelled to keep the beam correctly shaped; a small equipment hut at the base houses the transmitter, monitoring electronics, and standby power supply. Because each marker's exact position defines the distance and decision-height cue it gives the pilot, siting is tied to a precise survey referenced to the runway threshold, with the location fixed on the published approach chart.

Function

How Does It Work?

The marker beacon system operates on a simple but reliable principle:

  • Signal Transmission: Each marker beacon transmitter generates a 75 MHz carrier signal that is modulated with a specific audio frequency (400 Hz, 1300 Hz, or 3000 Hz). The signal is radiated vertically upward using a directional antenna that creates a narrow, fan‑shaped pattern (approximately 20° to 40° wide).
  • Aircraft Reception: As the aircraft passes over the beacon, the aircraft’s marker beacon receiver detects the 75 MHz signal, demodulates the audio tone, and triggers both an aural tone and a visual indication in the cockpit.
  • Visual Indication: The cockpit marker beacon display typically includes three coloured lights: blue (outer), amber (middle), and white (inner). The appropriate light flashes in sync with the received audio tone.
  • Distance Fix: The known distances of the markers from the runway threshold provide the pilot with a precise distance‑fix, which is used to confirm the aircraft’s position along the final approach and to verify the decision height.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The marker beacon is one of the simplest transmitting facilities on the airfield and carries a correspondingly thin control loop. The transmitter runs continuously at fixed power and frequency (75 MHz) with a fixed keying pattern for its identity (outer, middle or inner), so there is no dynamic command input to regulate during normal operation. An internal monitor checks transmitted power and modulation depth against tolerance; if either falls out of tolerance, the unit reports a fault — and on more advanced installations may shut down — so that a controller or maintenance system is alerted rather than an aircraft continuing to receive a degraded or false marker indication.

Construction

Physical Construction

The transmitter and its monitoring electronics are housed in a weatherproof equipment shelter or rack cabinet near the antenna site, built with dual-redundant, hot-standby transmitter modules so a fault in the active unit triggers an automatic changeover without interrupting the signal. The antenna itself is a fixed, vertically polarised array — typically a set of dipole or Alford-loop elements arranged to produce the directional, fan-shaped radiation pattern that defines the beacon's coverage — mounted on a low, frangible support structure set well clear of the approach or runway centreline so it poses no obstruction hazard. Cabling runs from the shelter to the antenna in a weatherproof feed line, and the whole installation is bonded to a local earthing system for lightning protection. Power comes from the mains supply backed by an uninterruptible supply and standby generator, and a continuous self-monitoring circuit compares transmitted signal parameters against tolerance limits, raising an alarm to the control tower or maintenance system the moment the beacon drifts out of specification.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Antenna Pattern Distortion

Vegetation growth, buildings, or terrain changes near the marker beacon site can distort the fan‑shaped antenna pattern, causing uneven coverage or signal nulls. Regular site surveys and pattern checks are recommended.

Modulation Depth Drift

Over time, the modulation depth of the marker beacon signal can drift due to component ageing or environmental factors, reducing the reliability of the aural and visual indications. Periodic calibration and flight inspection are essential.

Power Amplifier Failure

The transmitter power amplifiers are subject to thermal stress and can fail, especially in remote or harsh environments. Regular maintenance and the use of hot‑standby systems are critical to ensure continuous service.

Interference from Adjacent Transmitters

Co‑channel or adjacent‑channel interference from other 75 MHz transmitters can cause false marker indications or missed detection. Proper frequency planning and site coordination are required.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Uneven Coverage or Signal Nulls in the Fan Pattern
Likely Cause
Vegetation growth, new construction, or terrain changes near the site are distorting the fan-shaped antenna pattern.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct a site survey to identify new obstructions since the last check; compare the current radiation pattern against the baseline flight-inspection record.
Corrective Action
Clear vegetation or obstructions from the antenna's field, and schedule a flight inspection to confirm the pattern is restored.
Weak or Unreliable Aural/Visual Indications
Likely Cause
Modulation depth has drifted due to component ageing or environmental factors.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure modulation depth against the required specification; review calibration history for a drift trend.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the transmitter to restore correct modulation depth, and confirm compliance with a follow-up flight inspection.
Loss of Service or Reduced Output Power
Likely Cause
Thermal stress has degraded or failed the transmitter power amplifier.
Diagnostic Steps
Check transmitter output power against the rated specification; review amplifier temperature and fault logs; confirm hot-standby transfer, if fitted, operates correctly.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed power amplifier, and verify the hot-standby system is armed and ready to take over automatically.
False Marker Indications or Missed Detections
Likely Cause
Co-channel or adjacent-channel interference from another 75 MHz transmitter in the area.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a frequency survey around 75 MHz to identify the interfering source; review recent frequency coordination records for nearby sites.
Corrective Action
Coordinate with the interfering site's operator to resolve the conflict, or adjust site parameters within the allowable tolerance, then re-verify with a flight inspection.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Antenna or RF Work
RF / Electrical Safety

Shut down and lock out the transmitter before working on the antenna, feedline, or ground-plane, and treat the antenna as a live RF source whenever the unit is powered.

Lightning & Static Discharge
Electrical Safety

Marker beacon masts and ground planes are lightning-exposed structures; avoid working on the antenna during thunderstorm activity and bond/discharge the mast before handling it.

Off-Airfield Site Access
Operational

Outer and middle marker sites are often located outside the airport perimeter on separate parcels of land; confirm site access arrangements, gate codes, and any landowner or livestock considerations before dispatching a crew.

NOTAM for Approach Fix Outage
Operational

An out-of-service marker beacon removes a distance-fix cue used during ILS approaches; raise a NOTAM before taking the transmitter down and confirm restoration before cancelling it.

Fall Protection for Mast Work
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate fall-arrest equipment and follow site-specific procedures whenever work requires accessing an elevated antenna mast or support structure.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and confirm the equipment shelter, mast, and ground-plane are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting or re-energising the system.

Verify Frequency & Modulation Before Restoration
Compliance

Confirm the 75 MHz carrier and correct modulation tone/keying for the specific marker (OM, MM, or IM) before returning the beacon to service, since an incorrect signature could mislead cockpit indications.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Antenna VSWR Drift Precedes Hard Failure
RF

A rising standing wave ratio at the antenna feed is often the first sign of connector corrosion or a cracked radome, well before the transmitter faults or field strength drops enough to notice on approach; log VSWR at each visit rather than only checking on fault reports.

Ground-Plane Bonding Degrades Invisibly
Electrical

Corrosion at ground-plane bonding straps can quietly raise a site's effective ground resistance over years, changing the antenna's radiation pattern without any visible sign at the mast; include a bonding resistance check in the periodic maintenance cycle, not just a visual inspection.

Isolated Mast Sites See Faster Weathering
Environmental

Outer and middle marker sites are often unstaffed and away from the main terminal apron, so ice, vegetation encroachment, and animal damage to cabling can go unnoticed for longer than airfield-core equipment; weight inspection intervals accordingly.

Verify Modulation Tone, Not Just Carrier Presence
Compliance

A beacon transmitting on frequency but with a degraded or incorrect modulation tone or keying can still show "on" on basic monitoring while providing an ambiguous or wrong cockpit indication; confirm tone and keying pattern specifically, not just carrier power.

Standby Power Transfer Needs Field Verification
Electrical

Where the site relies on standby power for continuity during outages, verify the actual transfer under load during a scheduled test rather than trusting a monitoring panel's "standby ready" indication alone.

Confirm Site Access Before Dispatch, Every Time
Operational

Because outer and middle marker sites can sit on land the airport doesn't directly control, access arrangements, gate codes, or land use can change between visits without airfield-side notice; reconfirm access ahead of dispatch rather than assuming last visit's arrangement still holds.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about marker beacons: types, frequencies, positions, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a marker beacon?

A marker beacon is a ground-based VHF radio transmitter that radiates a narrow, fan-shaped signal vertically upward. Used with the Instrument Landing System (ILS), it gives pilots distance-fix information along the final approach.

Q2What are the three types of marker beacon?

The outer marker sits roughly 4 to 7 nautical miles from the threshold, the middle marker about 1.1 km (0.7 miles) from the threshold, and the inner marker about 300 m (1,000 ft) from it. The middle marker marks the CAT I decision height and the inner marker the CAT II/III decision height.

Q3What frequency and tones do marker beacons use?

All marker beacons transmit on a 75 MHz carrier. The outer marker is modulated at 400 Hz (continuous dashes), the middle at 1300 Hz (alternating dots and dashes) and the inner at 3000 Hz (continuous dots).

Q4What do the cockpit marker beacon lights mean?

The receiver gives an aural tone and a light: blue for the outer marker, amber for the middle marker and white for the inner marker. The light flashes in sync with the received tone.

Q5How does a marker beacon work?

The transmitter radiates a modulated 75 MHz signal upward through a directional antenna. As the aircraft passes overhead, its receiver demodulates the tone and triggers the cockpit indication, confirming position along the approach.

Q6Which standards apply to marker beacons?

The main references are ICAO Annex 10, FAA Order JO 6770.2B (maintenance of 75 MHz fan marker facilities), 14 CFR 171.207 and RTCA DO-143 for airborne receivers. Always verify the current edition and your regulator's requirements.

Q7How often should a marker beacon be maintained?

Typical intervals are an annual antenna pattern and site survey, semi-annual modulation depth calibration, annual flight inspection coordination, and quarterly power amplifier checks and standby transmitter switchover tests. Your regulator's programme takes precedence.

Q8What are the common faults on marker beacons?

Common problems are antenna pattern distortion from vegetation or new structures, modulation depth drift, power amplifier failure and interference from adjacent 75 MHz transmitters. Site surveys, calibration and hot-standby transmitters address these.
