Light-Unit Count & Coverage Comparison

PAPI 4-UNIT VS APAPI 2-UNIT

This comparison isolates one specific variable: unit count, and what it buys you. A four-unit PAPI row gives five graduated visual indications; drop to APAPI's two units and that falls to three. Same optical principle, same single-row layout, same on-path logic — just a direct trade between installation size and vertical-position resolution.

02 · The Core Relationship

Unit Count Drives Indication Count

This is the single mathematical relationship underlying the whole comparison: each light unit added to a single-row PAPI-family indicator adds one more principal indication state. A 4-unit row can show anywhere from 4 White down to 4 Red, one unit flipping from white to red at a time, giving 5 distinct combinations. A 2-unit row can only run from 2 White to 2 Red, giving 3 distinct combinations.

Rule of thumb: for an N-unit row read as a simple white/red count, the number of principal indications is N + 1. PAPI (N=4) → 5 indications. APAPI (N=2) → 3 indications.

03 · Definitions

PAPI 4-Unit & APAPI 2-Unit

PAPI (4-Unit)

The standard configuration: four light units in a horizontal row, each set to a slightly different elevation transition angle, giving the pilot the fullest graduation of vertical-position information available in the PAPI family. In FAA terms this is the Type L-880 system.

APAPI (2-Unit)

The abbreviated configuration: the same single-row, elevation-angle principle, reduced to two light units. Less equipment and a simpler read for the pilot, at the cost of coarser vertical discrimination. In FAA terms this is the Type L-881 system.

04 · Visual Indication Logic

Reading Each Row

PAPI — 4 Units

5 principal indications
4 White — too high
3 White + 1 Red — slightly high
2 White + 2 Red — on path
1 White + 3 Red — slightly low
4 Red — too low

APAPI — 2 Units

3 principal indications
2 White — above approach slope
1 White + 1 Red — on approach slope
2 Red — below approach slope

Notice what's missing on the APAPI side: the "slightly high" (3W+1R) and "slightly low" (1W+3R) intermediate states simply don't exist with only two units. That gap is the entire practical difference between the two configurations.

Reading the row: red builds up from the unit nearest the runway. On slope, the runway-side unit(s) show red and the outer unit(s) show white, on both PAPI and APAPI.

05 · Coverage & Physical Footprint

Ground Coverage of Each Installation

PAPI 4-Unit

Four units spaced along the row occupy a longer installation footprint, with proportionally more foundation, cabling, and isolation-transformer positions to plan for along that span.

APAPI 2-Unit

Two units span a single spacing interval instead of three, so for the same unit spacing the wing bar is only about one-third as long — reducing the footprint, cable run, and number of foundation/transformer positions needed for the same basic function.

Neither system provides wider lateral coverage or a broader field of view than the other by virtue of unit count — the reduction is in vertical discrimination and installed footprint, not in the angular coverage seen by an approaching aircraft.

06 · Typical Application

When Each Configuration Is Used

A full 4-unit PAPI is the standard configuration used for the majority of runways where a visual approach-slope indicator is provided, giving pilots the finer five-state graduation described above. APAPI's abbreviated 2-unit configuration is typically considered where a full PAPI installation is not warranted by the applicable design criteria for that particular runway or aerodrome — for example, at smaller aerodromes or on runways used by smaller aircraft categories, where the coarser three-state indication is judged adequate.

Always confirm against the current governing standard. The specific runway characteristics, code number and traffic category that justify choosing APAPI over a full PAPI are set out in ICAO Annex 14 / Doc 9157 and the applicable national regulations — this page is a conceptual comparison, not a substitute for that design criteria.

07 · Optical Principle

Fewer Transition Zones

Both systems work on the same underlying optical idea: each unit is set to a slightly different elevation transition angle, so a unit shows white above its transition angle and red below it. With four units, there are four separately-set transition angles spread across the approach path, producing intermediate combinations as the aircraft descends. With only two units, there are just two transition angles, so the aircraft passes straight from "both white" to "one white, one red" to "both red" with nothing in between.

In the usual ICAO arrangement the four PAPI units step 20′ apart, giving an on-slope band of about 20′ (settings roughly θ ± 10′), whereas APAPI's two settings are 30′ apart (θ ± 15′). That is what "lower vertical discrimination" means in numbers: the APAPI on-slope band is about half again as wide. Confirm the exact unit settings against the current Annex 14 figure for the project.

10 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: APAPI is just a cheaper, lower-quality PAPI
Fact

No. APAPI uses the same optical principle and fixture quality standards as PAPI — it simply uses fewer units, which reduces indication resolution and footprint, not build quality.

Myth: 2 white + 2 red and 1 white + 1 red mean different things
Fact

No. Both are the respective system's on-approach-slope indication — the meaning is the same, only the unit count producing it differs.

Myth: Fewer units means alignment matters less
Fact

No. With only two units on an APAPI, each one carries more weight in the overall indication — alignment accuracy remains just as critical as on a 4-unit PAPI.

Myth: A 4-unit PAPI and 2-unit APAPI can be freely swapped on any runway
Fact

No. Which configuration applies is governed by the applicable design standard for that specific runway and aerodrome — it is not simply a matter of preference or cost.

11 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI 4-Unit vs APAPI 2-Unit

Engineering Verdict

This comparison comes down to one clean trade-off: unit count against indication resolution. Every unit added to a single-row PAPI-family indicator buys one more principal indication state. Four units get you five states and a larger installation; two units get you three states, half the fixture count and a much shorter wing bar.

Neither configuration is "better" in absolute terms — the correct choice is whichever level of resolution and footprint the applicable design criteria call for on a given runway.

PAPI 4-Unit5 states, finer graduation, larger footprint, higher maintenance load.
APAPI 2-Unit3 states, coarser graduation, smaller footprint, lower maintenance load.

Don't design from unit count alone — the choice between a full PAPI and an abbreviated APAPI installation must follow the current governing standard for the specific runway, not this page.

Quick Answer

PAPI 4 Unit System vs PAPI 2 Unit System

The PAPI 4 unit system uses four light units in a single wing bar beside the runway. The pilot sees a different mix of red and white across the four units: all white is too high, three white and one red is slightly high, two of each is on the slope, one white and three red is slightly low, and all red is too low.

The PAPI 2 unit system (APAPI) uses two units, so it shows fewer steps: two white is above the slope, one of each is on slope, and two red is below it. It is a simpler and cheaper system, normally chosen for smaller aerodromes or runways with lighter traffic, as allowed by the applicable standard.

12 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

How many indications does a 4-unit PAPI give compared with a 2-unit APAPI?

A 4-unit PAPI gives 5 principal indications — 4 White, 3 White+1 Red, 2 White+2 Red, 1 White+3 Red, and 4 Red. A 2-unit APAPI gives 3 — 2 White, 1 White+1 Red, and 2 Red.

Why does reducing units from 4 to 2 lose two indication states, not one?

Because each unit removed also removes one transition zone. Going from 4 units to 2 removes both of the intermediate "slightly high" and "slightly low" states, leaving only high, on-path, and low.

Does APAPI cover a smaller area on the ground than PAPI?

APAPI's installed footprint along the row is smaller because it has half as many units, but this reflects a reduction in equipment and vertical-position resolution — not a change in the lateral field of view seen by an approaching aircraft.

Is alignment less critical on APAPI because it has fewer units?

No. With only two units, each one has a larger share of the overall indication, so correct elevation and azimuth alignment remains just as important as on a 4-unit PAPI.

Can any runway choose between a full PAPI and an abbreviated APAPI freely?

No. The applicable configuration is determined by the current governing design standard for that runway and aerodrome, not by preference — always verify against ICAO Annex 14 / Doc 9157 and the relevant national regulations for the specific project.

13 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — configuration, siting and photometric requirements for PAPI and APAPI installations.

ICAO Doc 9157 — Aerodrome Design Manual, Part 4

Guidance on visual aids design, including when an abbreviated PAPI configuration may be applicable for a given runway.

FAA AC 150/5345-28H — PAPI Systems

Equipment specification covering Type L-880 (four identical light units) and Type L-881 (two identical light units) to the same fixture requirements. Siting and installation details, including unit spacing, were relocated out of this equipment spec into FAA AC 150/5340-30 from the 2011 edition onward — confirm the exact spacing figure against the current edition of that AC rather than this one.

FAA AC 150/5340-30 (current: 5340-30J)

Design and installation details for airport visual aids — PAPI siting and installation, which was moved here from the equipment specification.

DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — the Indian regulatory requirement adopting the ICAO Annex 14 visual approach slope indicator provisions; also verify AAI and project-specific specifications.

Final Engineering Note

Do not select between a 4-unit PAPI and 2-unit APAPI from a generic web comparison. The final configuration must always be developed from the current governing standard, approved airport design and project-specific requirements.

Fewer units is not a lesser system — it's a deliberate trade of vertical-position resolution and installation footprint for simplicity, applied where the governing design criteria consider it adequate.
