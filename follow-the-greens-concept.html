“Follow the Greens” Concept AGL System Explained: Green Taxiway Centreline Lighting, Control & Airport Operations

How selectable green taxiway centreline segments, red stop bars, lighting control and ATC procedure combine into a dynamic visual route for pilots, and what it takes to design, test and maintain one.

Start Learning Taxiway Lighting Systems
Overview

What Is the “Follow the Greens” Concept?

“Follow the Greens” is an advanced airport surface-movement guidance concept in which selectable green taxiway centreline light segments give pilots a visual indication of the cleared taxi route. Instead of relying only on a full set of continuously lit centreline lights plus verbal turn-by-turn instructions, the airport lighting control system illuminates the appropriate green segments along the assigned route.

The illuminated green path acts as a dynamic visual route across the taxiway network. It is a visual guidance aid tied to an approved operational procedure. It is not permission to taxi.

Operating rules differ between airports. Treat each airport’s published AIP entry and approved procedure as the authority for how its system works.

Why It Exists

Why Was “Follow the Greens” Developed?

As airports grow and taxiway networks become more complex, ground movement is harder to manage with static signs, markings and verbal instructions alone. In low visibility or at night the number of cues becomes harder to interpret.

An aircraft may need to navigate

Multiple taxiway intersectionsParallel taxiwaysRunway crossingsRapid-exit taxiwaysHolding positionsComplex apron areasSeveral aircraft routes at once

A dynamic green-light route adds a visual layer that says: “This is the route currently selected for you.” It is most valuable where the airport has an integrated surface-movement control system.

Operation

How Follow the Greens Works

01

Aircraft requests or receives taxi clearance

The pilot communicates with ATC, and ATC establishes the intended route and clearance limit.

02

ATC selects the route

The controller or surface-movement control system selects the relevant taxi route.

03

Green centreline segments illuminate

The centreline lighting system activates the appropriate sections of green taxiway centreline lights.

04

Pilot follows the green path

The pilot uses the lights for direction while keeping watch on markings, signs, the clearance, stop bars, other traffic and ATC instructions.

05

Previously used segments are extinguished

As the aircraft progresses, some implementations switch off lighting behind it and light new sections ahead.

06

Stop bars control crossing points

At protected holding positions red stop bars can stay on until ATC authorizes the aircraft to proceed.

ATC

Phraseology and Why It Does Not Replace ATC

Phraseology

Where an airport has an approved procedure, “FOLLOW THE GREENS” becomes part of the taxi instruction, normally together with a clearance limit. Published procedures give worked examples of route instructions that include both.

Guidance vs clearance

The lights provide directional guidance. ATC provides operational clearance and control. The pilot stays responsible for the clearance, wingtip clearance and other hazards.

Dynamic lighting is guidance, not an autonomous replacement for operational control.

Green centreline lighting

A physical lighting characteristic.

Follow the Greens

An operational and control concept that uses selected green centreline lighting to give dynamic route guidance.

The two terms are not interchangeable. An airport can have green taxiway centreline lights without a Follow the Greens procedure.

Stop Bars

Green Guidance, Red Stop Bars and Interlocking

Green = follow the route

The selected green path shows where to go.

Red stop bar = stop

At protected runway entries or holding positions the stop bar is a stronger control indication than continuing along a green route. Pilots do not proceed past an illuminated red stop bar, or when there are no green lights ahead after being told to follow the greens. Exact rules come from the airport’s approved procedure.

Typical interlocking logic

Route selected

Command accepted

Green guidance active

Segments lit

Approaching holding point

Position known

Stop bar status checked

Interlock

ATC authorization

Controller action

Stop bar off, route continues

As approved

A route command should never simply switch lights on without considering protected crossings. The exact interlocking logic must be developed and validated as part of the airport’s approved system design.

Architecture

Follow the Greens System Architecture

Representative architecture. An advanced system may add: aircraft position → surface surveillance → route control logic.

The field lighting is still ordinary AGL electrical infrastructure. Where a series circuit is used: CCR → primary AGL cable → isolating transformer → light. The control system chooses which segments or circuits are active, while the CCR keeps the electrical conditions the circuit needs. A Follow the Greens project therefore covers lighting, electrical, control, communication, surveillance and operational procedures, not software alone.

Control Systems

ALCMS, Surveillance, A-SMGCS and the Controller Interface

A conventional ALCMS monitors

  • CCR status
  • Lamp circuits
  • Alarms
  • Lighting switching
  • Brightness

A Follow the Greens system may also manage

  • Route objects and taxiway segments
  • Route conflicts
  • Stop-bar states
  • Position-based activation and extinguishing
  • Controller commands and route progress
  • Event history

Ground surveillance

Aircraft position can feed route logic: aircraft position → route logic → lighting command → green segment selection. The lighting then responds to the aircraft’s progress rather than being only a static layer. Not every implementation needs this.

Place in A-SMGCS

Follow the Greens can be one layer of an A-SMGCS architecture: surveillance detects where traffic is, routing and control set the approved route, lighting shows it, and ATC monitors compliance. Automation varies widely between airports.

Controller HMI

Poor HMI design can undermine a capable system. The controller should quickly see: which route is active, which aircraft it belongs to, where the aircraft is, which stop bar is active and whether any part of the route is unavailable. Useful displays include the ground map, active aircraft, selected route, green-light and stop-bar status, clearance limit, and fault, communication and surveillance status.

Design

Segmentation, Intersections, LED Fittings, Cabling and Civil Works

Network segmentation

The centreline network is divided into logical segments, for example Segment 01 → 02 → 03 → 04. Each segment ties to a location, circuit, light fittings, a control address, a route function and status monitoring. This builds a digital representation of the physical airfield.

Complex intersections

With conventional lighting several visual paths may appear at once. Dynamic route selection can light only the intended route. Route lighting must be designed so that conflicting routes are never displayed in a confusing way.

LED centreline fittings

LED fittings suit dynamic architectures because they can offer fast, controllable switching and monitoring features, depending on the equipment. Select on complete project requirements, not on the nominal LED technology.

Cabling and circuits

Dynamic routing may need more carefully engineered segmentation than a conventional circuit. Study circuit loading, cable topology, transformer allocation, isolation, redundancy, fault propagation and maintainability.

Civil works

Inset fixture installation, ducting, cable trenches, chambers, transformer housings, junction boxes, foundations, drainage and pavement reinstatement all still apply. A sophisticated control system cannot make up for badly positioned field equipment.

Operations

Low Visibility, Pilot Responsibilities and Signs

Low visibility

Fog, reduced RVR, complex geometry and simultaneous movements make ground movement harder. The system is an added layer of routing and guidance, not simply brighter taxiway lights.

Pilot responsibilities

Keep a careful lookout and remain responsible for wingtip clearance. Do not continue if there are no green lights ahead or a red stop bar is lit. Do not go beyond the verbal clearance limit without ATC approval.

Signs and markings

Green lights tell the pilot “follow this route”. Signs help answer “where am I?”. Both functions stay valuable, and published procedures advise using signs and markings alongside the lights.

Airport safety context

A dynamic taxiway guidance system is part of the airport's overall surface safety architecture. It should work coherently with ATC, runway holding positions, stop bars, taxiway signs, pavement markings, runway guard lights, A-SMGCS where applicable, low-visibility procedures, vehicle movement control and emergency procedures. The green route should never be considered independently of the rest of the airfield environment.

Failure Modes

Follow the Greens Failure Modes

Click a failure to see what to investigate.

No green lights ahead

Do not assume the route is still active. Check:

  • Published stopping and ATC-query provisions
  • Route cancelled or timed out
  • Segment or circuit failure
  • Control or communication fault
  • Surveillance data lost

Red stop bar remains ON

The aircraft does not proceed unless the approved procedure covers it. Check:

  • ATC authorization status
  • Interlock state
  • Stop-bar control fault
  • Communication with field controller

Wrong route illuminated

A potentially serious control issue needing immediate investigation:

  • Route logic or data error
  • Wrong segment addressing
  • Operator selection error
  • Conflicting route requests

Multiple green paths

Ambiguity is a safety issue, not a minor lighting fault:

  • Overlapping route segments
  • Late extinguishing behind aircraft
  • Two routes requested together
  • Pilot stops and seeks confirmation

Communication failure

The field lighting may need to revert to a defined safe state:

  • Network or switch failure
  • Field controller offline
  • Fallback state defined and tested

Surveillance failure

The system needs an approved response to loss of position data:

  • Sensor or data-link loss
  • Track loss or false track
  • Fallback to manual route control

CCR failure

Affected lighting segments may become unavailable:

  • CCR trip or open circuit
  • Circuit loading problem
  • Segment-to-circuit mapping
  • Standby or alternative circuit

Control system failure

Operational procedures should define the fallback method:

  • Server or processor fault
  • Single point of failure
  • Revert to verbal taxi instructions
  • Event logging for investigation

Fail-safe design questions

1

What if a light fails?

Define the effect on the route indication.

2

What if a stop bar fails?

Define the safe state and the pilot indication.

3

What if communications fail?

Define the safe state of the field lighting.

4

What if surveillance is lost?

Define the approved response.

5

What if two routes are requested?

Define priority and conflict handling.

6

What if the controller cancels a route?

Define what the pilot sees.

7

What does the pilot see in degradation?

Define the indication during partial failure.

8

Is redundancy adequate?

Cover power, processors, links, switches, field controllers and surveillance inputs.

Redundancy matters because a failure here can affect more than one light. It can affect the route-generation function. The level of redundancy should follow the operational role and airport requirements.

Testing

Testing and Commissioning

A complete programme tests the system in layers. The aim is to learn what the system does when something goes wrong, not only when everything works.

01

Field equipment test

Centreline light and stop-bar operation, fixture orientation, physical condition and electrical connections.

02

Electrical test

Cable condition, insulation, transformers, CCR operation, circuit loading and earthing.

03

Control test

Individual segment commands, route activation, extinguishing, status feedback and alarms.

04

Interlocking test

Stop-bar logic, holding-position protection, conflicting routes, route cancellation and fail-safe behaviour.

05

Surveillance integration test

Where applicable: aircraft position, route tracking, automatic activation and extinguishing, and position-data failure behaviour.

06

Operational test

Approved operating scenarios with ATC and airport stakeholders.

07

Photometric test

Verify the optical performance of the installed lighting.

08

Failure mode test

Deliberately test communication, field-light, control-panel, surveillance, power and stop-bar failures and route conflicts.

India

Follow the Greens in India

India already has extensive green taxiway centreline lighting. Current AAI eAIP material documents inset bidirectional green centreline lights at Cochin, and unidirectional green lights on rapid-exit taxiways at Hyderabad. The engineering question is therefore not “can green centreline lights be installed?” It is whether the rest of the system can support dynamic routing.

Questions for an Indian airport to answer

1

Can the AGL network be segmented?

Existing circuits may be too coarse.

2

Can the ALCMS control individual routes?

Check what the current system can command.

3

Can stop bars be interlocked?

Review the control architecture.

4

Is reliable surveillance available?

Aircraft and vehicle position data.

5

Can the procedure be approved?

Agreement with ATC and the relevant authorities.

6

Can the whole system be tested and maintained?

Plan for the full life cycle.

Existing airports (retrofit)

A retrofit can be significantly more complex than a new installation.

  • Old or shared AGL circuits
  • Legacy CCRs and limited segmentation
  • Existing stop bars and mixed LED / conventional fittings
  • Legacy control panels and incomplete monitoring
  • Outdated cable layouts

New airports (greenfield)

Greenfield offers more flexibility and is generally easier than adding routing logic to a legacy network.

  • Digital taxiway map with AGL segmentation
  • ALCMS, surveillance and stop-bar interlocking designed together
  • CCR allocation, circuit topology and communication redundancy
  • Power supply, failure modes, manual fallback, maintenance access

Assessment

Benefits and Limitations

Benefits (when properly designed and approved)

  • Improved route visibility
  • Reduced route ambiguity
  • Better support for complex taxiway layouts
  • Low-visibility support
  • Better integration with ATC
  • Active route distinguished from inactive routes
  • Lighting becomes an operational tool, not only infrastructure

Limitations

  • Cannot replace ATC clearance, pilot lookout, signs, markings, surveillance or approved procedures
  • Adds dependencies: control, communications, surveillance, field lighting, power and procedures
  • A failure in any critical part can affect the guidance function
  • Changi’s procedure, for example, keeps verbal clearance limits and normal positional awareness
Actual benefits depend heavily on system design, airport procedures, traffic characteristics and operational implementation.

Final Engineering Perspective

Traditional AGL lights the taxiway. Follow the Greens uses controlled green centreline lighting to show the selected route. Treat it as an integrated lighting, control and operational-safety project, not a green-light replacement.

FAQ

Follow the Greens FAQ

Q1What does “Follow the Greens” mean at an airport?

It is an operational surface-guidance concept in which selected green taxiway centreline light segments are illuminated to give pilots visual guidance along an ATC-cleared taxi route.

Q2Are Follow the Greens and green taxiway centreline lights the same thing?

No. Green centreline lights are the physical visual aid. Follow the Greens is an operational and control concept that dynamically selects and uses those lights.

Q3Does Follow the Greens replace ATC instructions?

No. It works with ATC clearance and an approved procedure. Published airport procedures keep clearance limits and other ATC controls.

Q4Does Follow the Greens work only in low visibility?

Not necessarily. Dubai’s published procedure says its system can provide guidance during, but not only during, low visibility. Singapore Changi’s procedure applies to night operations and periods of low visibility.

Q5What colour are the route lights?

The route indication uses green taxiway centreline lights.

Q6What happens at a runway holding position?

The route generally ends at the protected holding position, where red stop-bar lighting and ATC authorization form part of the control concept. Exact logic depends on the airport’s approved system.

Q7Does the system need aircraft surveillance?

Not in every implementation. Advanced implementations can use aircraft position information to manage the illuminated route automatically, as Dubai’s published system does with ground-surveillance data.

Q8Is Follow the Greens available in India?

India has extensive green taxiway centreline lighting, documented in AAI eAIP material for airports including Cochin and Hyderabad. Whether a particular airport runs a full Follow the Greens procedure needs separate confirmation from its published aeronautical information and approved operational documents.

Q9Does Follow the Greens need ALCMS?

A dynamic implementation needs a control architecture that can select and monitor the required lighting segments. The platform may be an ALCMS, an ILCMS or an integrated surface-movement lighting-control architecture.

Q10Does Follow the Greens require photometric testing?

The underlying taxiway lighting still needs optical performance verification. Dynamic control does not remove the need for AGL testing, inspection and photometry.

Q11What should a pilot do if there are no green lights ahead?

Do not continue taxiing on the assumption the route is still active. Published procedures tell pilots to stop and query ATC.

Q12What if several green segments are visible?

Treat it as ambiguity. Changi’s published procedure tells pilots to stop and seek confirmation before taxiing further.

Guidance Depends on Correct Routing Data
Routing

Check route definitions and segment configuration, as errors can lead aircraft along the wrong path.

Stop Bars Remain the Control Layer
Stop Bars

Green guidance does not replace clearance. Confirm that red stop bars and procedures interlock correctly.

Test the Whole Chain
Integration

Verify the link from control to lighting segments to what the pilot sees, including degraded modes.

Check Visibility in Low Visibility Conditions
Visibility

Confirm that the system is usable by flight crews at the intended visibility and spacing.

Plan Failure Responses
Failure Modes

Decide in advance what happens when segments fail or data is lost, and brief staff.

Train Controllers and Crews Together
Training

The concept relies on shared understanding. Include phraseology and procedures in training.
