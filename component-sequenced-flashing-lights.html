Sequenced Flashing Lights

A dynamic visual guidance system that produces a "rolling" or "chasing" light effect toward the runway threshold. An integral component of precision and medium‑intensity approach lighting systems (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSF, MALSR, Calvert II), sequenced flashing lights provide pilots with critical directional cues, enhancing depth perception and glide‑slope awareness during final approach, particularly in low visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL) — also known as Runway Alignment Indicator Lights (RAIL) in some configurations — are a series of high‑intensity lights installed along the approach lighting system that flash in rapid sequence from the outer end toward the runway threshold. This creates a distinctive "rolling" or "chasing" effect that provides pilots with dynamic directional cues during final approach. The SFL is a critical component of many precision and medium‑intensity approach lighting systems, including ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSF, MALSR, and the Calvert II system.

The flashing sequence helps pilots maintain the correct approach direction, enhances depth perception, and provides a clear visual indication of the glide‑slope path. In low visibility conditions (fog, rain, snow), the SFL's dynamic nature makes it significantly more visible and informative than steady‑burning lights alone.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The sequenced "rolling" flash exists to encode direction into the light pattern itself, something a row of simultaneously flashing or steady-burning lights cannot do: because each light fires in order from the outer end toward the threshold, the pilot's eye is drawn along the intended flight path in the same direction the aircraft should be traveling, reinforcing correct approach direction and depth perception in a way static lighting cannot replicate. This directional cueing becomes most valuable exactly when visual references are most degraded, in fog, rain, or snow, where the dynamic, moving nature of the sequence is far easier for the eye to pick out and interpret than a static reference at the same distance and intensity. Its purpose is therefore concentrated on reinforcing correct alignment and directional sense during the approach's most visually demanding conditions, which is why it appears across nearly every major precision and medium-intensity system rather than being reserved for a single configuration.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Sequenced flashing lights are installed as part of approach lighting systems at airports that support precision and enhanced non‑precision approaches. They are typically used in:

  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems (Category I, II, and III precision approach runways)
  • MALSF and MALSR systems (medium‑intensity approach lighting with sequenced flashing)
  • Calvert II systems (enhanced British approach lighting configuration)
  • Precision approach runways requiring enhanced visual guidance for low‑visibility operations
  • Major international airports with CAT II/III capability
  • Military airbases requiring all‑weather approach capability
Placement

Installation & Location

SFL units are installed on the outer section of the approach lighting pier line, from the far end of the system in toward the threshold, sharing the same frangible poles and pier foundations as the centreline barrettes they overlay. Each flasher head mounts atop its pole alongside (or integrated with) the steady-burning barrette fixture, with the SFL fed from a dedicated sequencing control unit rather than the CCR series circuit, requiring its own control cable run back to the approach lighting control cabinet in addition to the series-circuit cabling. Pier setout follows the same extended-centreline survey as the rest of the approach lighting system, and on approaches over water or uneven terrain the SFL poles are carried on the same raised platform or bridge structure as the adjacent centreline piers.

Function

How Does It Work?

Sequenced flashing lights operate through a precisely timed sequence of flashes that create a dynamic visual effect:

  • Sequence Logic: A control unit triggers each light in the SFL row in rapid succession, starting from the outer‑most light and progressing toward the threshold. Per FAA Order JO 6850.2 (Visual Guidance Lighting Systems), the sequence repeats at a fixed rate of twice per second (120 cycles per minute) — this is a defined rate, not a variable range.
  • Rolling Effect: The progressive flashing creates a "rolling" or "chasing" effect that appears to move toward the runway, providing pilots with a clear directional cue for the approach path.
  • Depth Perception: The dynamic nature of the SFL enhances depth perception, helping pilots judge their distance from the runway and maintain the correct glide slope.
  • Directional Guidance: The sequence direction (outer to threshold) indicates the correct approach direction, helping pilots maintain proper orientation, especially in low visibility where external visual references are limited.
  • Intensity Control: SFL units are typically controlled by a dedicated CCR with multiple intensity steps (usually 5‑step), allowing ATC to adjust brightness for visibility conditions. Higher intensities are used in fog and low visibility.
  • Integration: The SFL works in conjunction with steady‑burning barrettes, centreline lights, and crossbars to provide a comprehensive visual guidance system.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The rolling effect depends on staggering each flash tube's firing by a fraction of a second relative to the next, a timing precision that a centralised ALCMS link cannot reliably guarantee over the distance and cable run of an approach lighting pier line. For that reason, SFL runs on its own dedicated sequence controller, physically local to the pier line, which stores the firing order and inter-flash delay and drives the tubes directly rather than being stepped through by ALCMS in real time. The ALCMS's role is to set the mode the sequence controller runs in — on, off, or (where fitted) a selectable flash rate — and to select the brightness step, which is still applied through the conventional CCR loop feeding each flasher head's isolating transformer. The two layers stay separate in operation: the CCR/brightness layer can be adjusted for visibility conditions without touching the sequence timing, and a flash-tube failure is typically flagged by the sequence controller itself and reported back to the ALCMS as a fault, rather than being detected by the CCR loop.

Construction

Physical Construction

Sequenced Flashing Light heads are built around a high-intensity xenon strobe tube or, in newer installations, a high-output LED cluster, housed in a rugged die-cast aluminium enclosure designed to withstand repeated high-energy discharges and the associated heat cycling. The lens is a hardened, UV-stabilised polycarbonate or tempered glass dome, gasket-sealed to the housing to keep the fixture watertight, with a narrow, forward-directed optical system that concentrates the flash toward the approach path rather than dispersing it broadly. Elevated units mount on a frangible coupling atop an L-867 base can, matching the mounting standard used elsewhere along the approach lighting system so the SFL row integrates structurally with the sequenced or steady-burning light poles either side of it. The strobe capacitor bank and trigger electronics are housed in a separate weatherproof enclosure at the base of the pole or in an adjacent equipment case, connected to the lamp head by heavy-gauge cable rated for the high peak currents of a xenon discharge; this separation keeps the failure-prone electronic components accessible for maintenance without disturbing the elevated optical head. Internal wiring terminates at sealed L-823 connectors linking to a dedicated SFL controller or shared CCR circuit, and all fasteners and brackets are corrosion-resistant given the fixture's typically exposed siting along the extended approach lighting corridor.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

SFL units are exposed to environmental pollutants, bird droppings, and de‑icing chemicals. Since they rely on high‑intensity output for visibility in low conditions, regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is essential to maintain the required light output.

Sequence Synchronisation Drift

Over time, SFL controllers can drift, causing the flashing sequence to become irregular or out of sync. This can disrupt the rolling effect and confuse pilots. Regular calibration and testing of the SFL control system is critical.

Individual Light Failures

A single failed SFL unit can break the sequence, creating a "gap" in the rolling effect. Regular inspection and prompt replacement of faulty units are essential to maintain system integrity.

Power Supply and CCR Issues

SFL systems draw significant power and require precise current regulation. Voltage fluctuations, CCR faults, or cable damage can cause inconsistent brightness, sequence errors, or total failure. Regular power quality monitoring and cable testing are recommended.

Misalignment of Elevated Fixtures

Elevated SFL units can be knocked out of alignment by wind, wildlife, or maintenance vehicles. Regular alignment checks using a theodolite or laser system are essential to maintain the correct beam direction toward approaching aircraft.

LED Driver Failure

In LED‑based SFL units, driver failure can cause individual lights to go out or dim. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers are essential to maintain the flashing sequence.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Unit Dark or Not Flashing
Likely Cause
Failed lamp/capacitor bank in a xenon unit, or a failed LED driver in an LED-based unit; alternatively an open connection to the sequencer.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the sequencer's fault log for the affected position; test the trigger signal at the unit; verify capacitor charge voltage or driver output as applicable.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp, capacitor, or LED driver, and confirm the unit fires correctly and back in sequence before re-energising the row.
Entire Row Dark or Sequence Stopped
Likely Cause
Master sequencer/control unit fault, loss of the trigger reference signal, or an upstream power supply failure feeding the whole SFL string.
Diagnostic Steps
Check sequencer status and fault indications; confirm incoming power and trigger reference; test the master-to-slave signal chain unit by unit.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the faulted sequencer/control module, restore power feed, and re-verify the full sequence runs correctly end to end.
Sequence Irregular or Rolling Effect Out of Sync
Likely Cause
Timing drift in the sequencer's controller, or a degraded signal cable causing delayed triggering at one or more units along the row.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the flash pattern against the specified sequence timing; check controller calibration and firmware settings; test signal continuity and timing to each unit.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the sequencer/control system, and repair or replace the faulted signal cable causing the delayed trigger.
CCR/Power Supply Trips or Fluctuating Brightness
Likely Cause
Voltage fluctuation on the supply feed, a CCR fault, or cable damage causing inconsistent current delivery to the SFL string.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor incoming supply voltage and CCR output under load; megger the feeder cable; check for loose or corroded connections along the run.
Corrective Action
Correct the power quality issue at source, repair or replace the faulted cable, or replace the CCR if it fails to hold regulation.
Reduced Intensity Across One or More Units
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from environmental pollutants or bird droppings, or an aging lamp/LED array losing output.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against the required output; inspect and clean the lens; compare output against a known-good unit.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens or replace the degraded lamp/LED array, and re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Misaligned (Elevated Units)
Likely Cause
Wind loading, wildlife interference, or contact from a maintenance vehicle has knocked the elevated unit out of its intended beam direction.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture aim using a theodolite or laser alignment tool against the design beam direction; inspect the mounting structure for damage.
Corrective Action
Re-aim and re-secure the fixture, repairing or replacing any damaged mounting hardware, then re-verify beam direction.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Xenon Flash Tube / Capacitor Bank Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated high-energy discharge cycles wearing the flash tube electrodes, or capacitor dielectric ageing that reduces stored charge over the unit's service life.
Effect
Single unit fires dimly, intermittently, or not at all, breaking the rolling ball-of-light effect that draws a pilot's eye down the approach.
Detection Method
Sequencer fault log review for the affected position, and capacitor charge-voltage or flash-energy verification at the unit.
LED Driver Failure (LED-Based Units)
High Severity
Root Cause
Driver board component failure from thermal stress, or a firmware/trigger-interface fault on LED-based SFL units.
Effect
Single unit fails to flash in sequence or drops output intensity, leaving a gap in the flashing row.
Detection Method
Driver output and trigger-signal verification at the unit, and comparison against a known-good unit in the row.
Master Sequencer / Control Unit Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal control-board failure, loss of the timing reference, or a firmware fault in the master sequencer driving the row.
Effect
Entire row goes dark or the flash sequence stops altogether, since every unit depends on the master trigger signal.
Detection Method
Sequencer status and fault-indication check, and verification of incoming power and the trigger reference signal.
Trigger / Signal Cable Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage, corroded splice connections, or insulation breakdown along the signal run between the sequencer and individual units.
Effect
Delayed or missing triggering at one or more units, producing an irregular or out-of-sync rolling effect.
Detection Method
Signal continuity and timing tests to each unit, and inspection of splice connections along the run.
Power Supply Fluctuation / CCR Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Voltage fluctuation on the incoming supply feed, a CCR/power-supply fault, or feeder cable damage causing inconsistent current delivery to the SFL string.
Effect
Fluctuating brightness across the row or the supply tripping out, taking the entire SFL system offline.
Detection Method
Monitoring of incoming supply voltage and CCR output under load, and megger testing of the feeder cable.
Lens Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Environmental pollutants, bird droppings, or general weathering building up on the lens surface.
Effect
Reduced flash intensity from one or more units, weakening the visual cue at longer range.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity check against required output, and visual inspection during routine cleaning.
Elevated Mounting / Structure Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wind loading, wildlife interference, or contact from a maintenance vehicle acting on the elevated unit and its mounting structure over time.
Effect
Unit knocked out of its intended beam direction, or the mounting hardware loosens, reducing the effectiveness of the flash toward the runway.
Detection Method
Fixture aim check using a theodolite or laser alignment tool, and visual inspection of the mounting structure for damage.
Inter-Unit Timing Drift (Controller Calibration)
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Calibration drift in the sequencer's timing circuit, or accumulated clock error across a long row of units.
Effect
The rolling ball-of-light effect appears irregular or loses its intended directional flow along the approach.
Detection Method
Observation of the flash pattern against the specified sequence timing, and controller calibration verification.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Separate Sequencing Control Circuit
Electrical Safety

SFL units are fed from a dedicated sequencing control unit rather than the CCR series loop; isolate and lock out at the sequencing control unit as well as the series circuit before climbing the pole or handling connectors, since one being dead does not guarantee the other is.

Flash-Tube Capacitor Discharge Hazard
Electrical Safety

The flasher power supply stores energy in high-voltage capacitors that can remain charged after the unit is switched off; follow the manufacturer's discharge procedure and confirm capacitors are bled down before opening the control unit or handling a flash head.

Elevated Pier Work
Personnel Safety

SFL heads mount atop the same poles as the centreline barrettes they overlay; use fall protection appropriate to the pole height, and follow the pier structure's own access procedure where it is a raised platform over water.

High-Intensity Flash Exposure
Personnel Safety

SFL units are designed for high conspicuity from a distance; avoid direct close-range viewing of an energised flash head, and warn nearby personnel before re-energising a unit under test.

Sequence Timing Verification
Compliance

A single unit firing individually is not enough; after any repair, confirm it fires in correct order and timing within the full rolling sequence, since a unit that is bright but out of sequence can be as misleading to the pilot as one that is dark.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return the system to service with an unverified sequence; confirm the full chase pattern and intensity before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Sequence Timing Drift Undermines the Rolling Effect
Configuration

The perceived 'rolling ball' effect of SFL depends on tight timing between adjacent units; a controller or local module that drifts even slightly out of sequence degrades the depth-perception cue well before any single unit looks obviously faulty, so verify sequence timing as a whole rather than checking that each unit fires.

Trigger Transformer Wear Precedes Tube Failure
Electrical

As with other xenon-based flashing systems, the high-voltage trigger transformer that ignites each flash tube typically degrades before the tube does; an SFL unit that flashes weakly or intermittently is more often a trigger circuit issue than a tube nearing end of life.

Isolate a Sequencing Fault to Controller, Feeder, or Unit
Troubleshooting

A single unit that fires out of sequence can be caused by a fault at that unit, its local feeder, or the master controller's timing output; check the controller's own sequence output first, since chasing fixture wiring on a controller-side fault wastes a maintenance window.

Intensity Uniformity Matters as Much as Timing
Photometric

SFL/RAIL units at the far end of a long approach lighting run can read visibly dimmer than those closer to the threshold even when each is individually within spec, undercutting the sequence's directional cue; check relative intensity across the whole run, not just each unit against its own tolerance.

Coordinate Night Intensity Steps With Nearby Communities
Operational

High-intensity sequenced flashing near the extended centreline is a common source of light-nuisance complaints from residents under the approach path; where certification allows a lower night-time intensity step, coordinate its use with air traffic control rather than leaving the system at maximum intensity by default.

Track Cumulative Flash Cycles for Capacitor Replacement
Maintenance

The capacitor bank behind each flash tube has a service life that tracks flash count and ambient temperature rather than calendar time; log cumulative cycles per unit and schedule capacitor replacement proactively instead of waiting for an in-service failure during low-visibility operations.

Q10How do I troubleshoot common Sequenced Flashing Lights faults?

Common symptoms covered are: Single Unit Dark or Not Flashing; Entire Row Dark or Sequence Stopped; Sequence Irregular or Rolling Effect Out of Sync; CCR/Power Supply Trips or Fluctuating Brightness; Reduced Intensity Across One or More Units; Fixture Misaligned (Elevated Units). For example, for "Single Unit Dark or Not Flashing": Likely Cause: Failed lamp/capacitor bank in a xenon unit, or a failed LED driver in an LED-based unit; alternatively an open connection to the sequencer. Diagnostic Steps: Check the sequencer's fault log for the affected position; test the trigger signal at the unit; verify capacitor charge voltage or driver output as applicable. Corrective Action: Replace the failed lamp, capacitor, or LED driver, and confirm the unit fires correctly and back in sequence before re-energising the row.
