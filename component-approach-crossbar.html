Approach Crossbar

A row of high‑intensity lights installed perpendicular to the approach centreline at fixed distances from the runway threshold. Approach crossbars provide pilots with critical distance and lateral guidance cues during final approach, helping maintain accurate glide‑slope tracking and centreline alignment in low‑visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

An Approach Crossbar is a row of high‑intensity lights installed perpendicular to the approach centreline, typically consisting of 3 to 5 lights spaced evenly across the approach path. These crossbars are placed at fixed distances from the runway threshold (e.g., 150 m, 300 m, 450 m, 600 m, etc.) as part of the approach lighting system (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSF, Calvert, etc.). They provide pilots with a series of bright visual "bars" that help maintain precise lateral alignment, judge distance‑to‑go, and monitor glide‑slope position during the final approach segment.

Approach crossbars are a fundamental component of both precision and medium‑intensity approach lighting systems. They work in conjunction with centreline lights, barrettes, side row bars, and sequenced flashing lights (SFL) to create a comprehensive visual reference for the pilot, particularly critical during low‑visibility (CAT I/II/III) operations where accurate glide‑path and centreline tracking are essential.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The approach crossbar's purpose is to give a pilot a repeated, unmistakable reference for both lateral alignment and distance-to-go during the final approach, at exactly the phase of flight where precise positional awareness matters most and visual cues are otherwise sparse. Because each crossbar spans the approach path at a known distance from the threshold, a pilot can read their lateral position from whether the bar appears symmetrical, and their progress along the approach from which crossbars are visible and how far apart they appear — a form of guidance that becomes essential, not just helpful, in the low-visibility CAT I/II/III conditions the approach lighting system exists to support.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Approach crossbars are deployed on precision and non‑precision approach runways and are used in:

  • CAT I precision approach runways (ALSF‑1) – providing essential distance and lateral guidance for approaches with 200 ft decision height.
  • CAT II and III precision approach runways (ALSF‑2) – critical for low‑visibility operations with decision heights as low as 100 ft or less.
  • Medium‑intensity approach lighting systems (MALSF, MALSR) – providing enhanced visual cues for non‑precision runways with higher traffic volumes.
  • Calvert I and II systems – a distinctive approach lighting configuration using crossbars as a key visual element.
  • Runways with low‑visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) – ensuring pilots have continuous distance and alignment cues.
  • Major international airports – where high traffic volumes and adverse weather demand reliable approach guidance.
  • Military airfields – requiring all‑weather precision approach capability.
Placement

Installation & Location

Crossbars are mounted on the approach lighting structures at their designated stations along the extended runway centreline, set perpendicular to the approach path at the widths and distances specified for the particular ALS configuration (ALSF-1, ALSF-2, MALSF, MALSR, or Calvert). Each crossbar sits on its own frangible support at the height called for by the system design, with the array of individual light units on the bar levelled and aimed during commissioning so the crossbar reads correctly from the cockpit. Cabling is run out from the approach light junction boxes along the structure to each unit, and the crossbar's position is tied into the overall approach lighting survey so its distance from the threshold and offset from the runway centreline extension match the design drawing exactly.

Function

How Does It Work?

Approach crossbars function as a series of visual reference points along the approach path:

  • Configuration: Each crossbar is a row of 3‑5 lights, typically spaced 3–5 m apart, installed perpendicular to the approach centreline. Multiple crossbars are placed at specific distances from the threshold (e.g., 150 m, 300 m, 450 m, 600 m, etc.) to provide a regular visual pattern along the approach path.
  • Lighting: The lights are high‑intensity white, steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by the CCR (5‑step). In some systems, crossbars may flash in sequence to create a "running" effect that guides the pilot toward the runway.
  • Distance Reference: As the aircraft descends, the pilot sees the crossbars as horizontal lines. The spacing between crossbars provides distance‑to‑go information, helping the pilot judge approach progress and descent rate.
  • Lateral Guidance: If the aircraft is off‑centreline, the crossbars will appear asymmetrical, indicating the need for lateral correction. The perpendicular orientation of the crossbars provides a clear reference for maintaining centreline alignment.
  • Glide‑Slope Monitoring: By observing the spacing and position of crossbars relative to the aircraft, pilots can assess whether they are on the correct glide slope — appearing too close together may indicate being too high or too low.
  • Integration: Approach crossbars are integrated with centreline lights, barrettes, side row bars, and sequenced flashing lights (SFL) to form a comprehensive visual approach system. They are typically positioned at regular intervals along the approach lighting structure.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Approach crossbars are not individually addressable — they sit on a dedicated approach lighting series circuit, kept separate from the runway edge and centreline loops so the ALS can be intensity-stepped on its own schedule. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), driven by prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or pilot-controlled lighting at smaller fields, selects the required brightness step and passes it to the approach lighting CCR, which sets the entire loop to that fixed current (typically 5-step, up to 6.6A). Every crossbar station on that loop — along with the barrettes, side rows, and any sequenced flashers sharing the circuit — burns at the identical step simultaneously; there is no mechanism to brighten or dim one crossbar independently of the rest of the approach lighting array. Fault detection is loop-based: the CCR watches loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise flags an open-circuit condition — a burnt-out lamp, a failed isolating transformer, or a sheared frangible support beneath one of the crossbar's individual units — as a single approach-lighting-loop fault. The ALCMS reports the fault to the loop, not to a specific crossbar station, so a field walk of the structure is what actually locates the failed unit.

ALCMS / ATC / PCL → Approach Lighting CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Crossbar Station (per fixture) → loop-level feedback

Construction

Physical Construction

Each light in a crossbar is a self-contained high-intensity fixture — an LED array or 45W/65W/100W halogen lamp behind a precision optical prism, housed in a die-cast aluminium body that is anodised or powder-coated against jet fuel, de-icing fluid, and UV exposure. Elevated crossbars mount each fixture on a frangible coupling and pole seated in an L-867 base can, so an individual light shears cleanly off on impact rather than transmitting the load along the bar; flush-mounted crossbars instead seat each fixture in an L-868 base can rated for aircraft wheel loading. What makes a crossbar a single component rather than a set of unrelated fixtures is the shared civil layout: all 3–5 lights are set to a common width and spacing, wired back to individual isolating transformers of matched wattage, and aligned so their beams present as one continuous bar rather than a scatter of independent points. Gasket-sealed top caps and O-ring seals on each fixture give the assembly its IP67/IP68 rating, and sealed L-823 splice connectors carry the series circuit between fixtures and back to the primary cable.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Precise Alignment and Spacing

Approach crossbars must be installed with exact alignment perpendicular to the centreline and consistent spacing between successive crossbars. Any misalignment or deviation in spacing can create confusing visual cues for pilots. Regular surveys using theodolites or laser alignment tools are essential, especially after pavement resurfacing or maintenance.

Lens Contamination and Light Output

Crossbar lights are exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits, and de‑icing fluids. Contamination can reduce light output and affect the clarity of the visual pattern. Routine cleaning and photometric checks are critical, especially before low‑visibility seasons.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated crossbar lights are subject to wind loading and thermal cycling, which can fatigue the frangible couplings. Regular inspection and replacement per manufacturer schedules ensure the lights remain safe and properly positioned.

Flashing Synchronisation Failure

In systems where crossbars flash in sequence, timing errors can occur due to control circuit issues. This can disrupt the "running" effect and confuse pilots. Periodic testing of the flashing sequence and synchronisation is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Visual Pattern Appears Skewed or Confusing to Pilots
Likely Cause
Crossbar alignment perpendicular to the centreline, or the spacing between successive crossbars, has drifted from design specification — often after pavement resurfacing or maintenance nearby.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct a survey using a theodolite or laser alignment tool, checking crossbar alignment and inter-crossbar spacing against the design drawing.
Corrective Action
Re-align and re-position crossbars found out of tolerance, and re-survey after any nearby pavement work going forward.
Reduced Light Output or Unclear Visual Pattern
Likely Cause
Jet blast, rubber deposits, or de-icing fluids have contaminated the crossbar lenses.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect lenses for film or contamination; take a photometric reading and compare against specification, especially ahead of low-visibility season.
Corrective Action
Clean lenses per the routine maintenance schedule, prioritising this before low-visibility periods, and re-verify photometric output.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Wind loading and thermal cycling have fatigued the frangible coupling on an elevated crossbar light.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically inspect the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against tolerance.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling per the manufacturer's schedule, and re-level and re-aim the fixture.
"Running" Flash Effect Disrupted or Out of Sequence
Likely Cause
A control circuit issue has introduced timing errors into the crossbar flashing sequence.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the flashing sequence and synchronisation across all crossbars in the system, checking each unit's timing against the control specification.
Corrective Action
Repair or recalibrate the faulted control circuit component, then re-verify the full sequence before returning the system to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Individual Light Unit Failure Within the Crossbar
Medium Severity
Root Cause
LED array or transformer failure at a single unit on the crossbar, from thermal cycling or moisture-driven insulation breakdown.
Effect
One unit in the crossbar goes dark; the crossbar shape remains partly readable but with reduced conspicuity at the widened station it marks.
Detection Method
Test activation of the crossbar with a visual count of illuminated units, and loop-resistance testing to isolate the failed unit.
Crossbar Level / Aim Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
One or more units on the crossbar shifting out of the level and aim set during commissioning, from structure settlement or handling during maintenance.
Effect
The crossbar no longer reads correctly from the cockpit as the distinct widened bar it's designed to be, weakening the specific ALSF-1/ALSF-2/Calvert station cue it provides.
Detection Method
Level and aim check of each unit on the crossbar against the commissioning survey during scheduled inspection.
Frangible Support Fatigue / Impact Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading fatiguing the crossbar's frangible support, or a strike given its location directly in the approach path.
Effect
Crossbar leans, misaligns, or partially separates from its structure, distorting or removing the widened-station cue entirely.
Detection Method
Post-strike inspection procedure, and coupling condition check during scheduled maintenance.
Feed Cable / Junction Box Failure to the Whole Crossbar
High Severity
Root Cause
Fault in the cable run out from the approach light junction box feeding the entire crossbar, or a junction box seal failure allowing moisture ingress at the shared feed point.
Effect
Loss of the entire crossbar rather than a single unit, since all units on the bar share this feed, removing the station cue completely.
Detection Method
Continuity and insulation testing of the crossbar's dedicated feed run back to its junction box.
Series Circuit Insulation Breakdown Along the Structure Run
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cable insulation degradation from age, moisture, or handling damage along the run from the junction box up the approach light structure to the crossbar.
Effect
Intermittent operation or a partial ground fault on the circuit segment serving the crossbar.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit segment during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Crossbar circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR, then verify zero energy at the crossbar before opening any fixture or connector.

Elevated Tower & Off-Runway Access
Personnel Safety

Crossbars span the full width of the light bar structure, often on elevated frangible towers over terrain that may be uneven or off the paved movement area; use fall protection when climbing towers and assess ground conditions before positioning vehicles or ladders.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling or tower base with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the structure must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

A crossbar failure or outage can affect published CAT I/II/III minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any crossbar light is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

FOD Control Along the Approach
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every crossbar worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work on a crossbar circuit, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Uniformity Across the Bar
Compliance

A crossbar's guidance value depends on all lights across the row reading as a single uniform bar; verify aim and intensity are matched across every light in the crossbar, not just the unit that was serviced.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a crossbar light out of aim tolerance or at reduced intensity; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Crossbar Alignment Is Judged Laterally, Not Just Per-Fixture
Verification

Each fixture on a crossbar can individually test within photometric spec while the bar as a whole reads unevenly if fixture aim or mounting height varies slightly across its width. Check the crossbar's lateral uniformity as a unit, not only fixture-by-fixture compliance.

Crossbar Distance From Threshold Should Be Re-Surveyed After Structural Work
Design

The crossbar's designed distance from the threshold is a key part of the approach lighting geometry pilots use for distance cueing. After any structural repair, foundation work, or mast replacement, re-survey the crossbar's position rather than assuming reinstallation returned it to the exact original location.

Wind Loading Across a Full-Width Crossbar Structure Compounds
Structural

A crossbar spans multiple fixtures across a structure wider than a single mast; wind loading on the combined structure is not simply the sum of individual fixture loads and should be assessed as a system, particularly for the central support points.

Uniformity Across a Bar Depends on Matched Fixture Age, Not Just Matched Spec
Maintenance

Replacing one fixture on a crossbar with new stock next to older, slightly lumen-depreciated neighbours can create a visible brightness step across the bar even though the new fixture is correctly specified. Consider replacing crossbar fixtures as a matched set where lumen depreciation has diverged significantly.

Snow and Ice Loading Concentrates on the Horizontal Structure
Seasonal

The horizontal structure of a crossbar accumulates snow and ice differently than a single vertical mast, adding load in a location the original point-fixture design basis may not have anticipated. Confirm structural design accounts for accumulation specific to the crossbar geometry in icing-prone climates.

Verify Crossbar Position Against the Full Approach Layout, Not Just Local Tolerance
Verification

A crossbar can be internally well-aligned and still sit slightly off its designed station along the approach if the original survey reference point has shifted or was mismeasured. Periodically re-verify crossbar position against the full approach lighting layout, not only against its own local alignment.
