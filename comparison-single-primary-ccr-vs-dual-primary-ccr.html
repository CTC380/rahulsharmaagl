CCR & Electrical Systems Comparison

Single-Primary CCR VS Dual-Primary CCR

A single-primary CCR is fed from one primary electrical input, while a dual-primary CCR has two independent primary inputs for greater power-supply redundancy. The distinction is entirely about the input side of the regulator — not the number of lighting circuits it feeds.

02 · Definitions

What Is a Single-Primary & Dual-Primary CCR?

Single-Primary CCR

A single-primary CCR receives its input from one primary power source. Under normal operation this single source feeds the constant current regulator, which in turn energises the series airfield lighting circuit. If that primary input is lost, the CCR cannot continue operating from another primary source unless a separate upstream transfer or emergency arrangement is provided elsewhere in the electrical design.

Dual-Primary CCR

A dual-primary CCR has two independent primary inputs. The two sources provide input redundancy: if the active primary source becomes unavailable, the system can transfer to the alternate source according to the CCR's own transfer/control architecture, so the constant-current output — and the lighting it drives — is better protected against a single power failure.

03 · Key Difference

One Way In vs Two Ways In

The core distinction between the two configurations comes down to how many independent paths of primary power can reach the regulator:

Single-Primary

One primary source → lower redundancy

Dual-Primary

Two primary sources → higher redundancy

Easy memory: Single = one way in. Dual = two ways in.

There is one primary supply path into a single-primary regulator. A dual-primary regulator can draw from either Source A or Source B, as provided by its transfer/control architecture — the CCR itself still produces a single constant-current output to the lighting circuit either way.

05 · What "Primary" Actually Means

Primary Input vs Regulated Output

It's easy to conflate "primary" with the CCR's regulated output, but the two are separate functions of the same device:

The single/dual terminology refers strictly to the number of primary input sources feeding the regulator — not to the number of lighting circuits or output loops it energises.

07 · Reliability

Why Redundancy Matters at the Input Side

The main engineering advantage of dual-primary equipment is redundancy at the input-power level, not at the output. With a single primary source, that source is a single point of dependence for the whole CCR:

Single-primary risk: Source A → CCR → Lighting. One primary source is a single point of dependence for the entire regulator.

With dual-primary architecture, the loss of one source does not necessarily mean loss of CCR operation, because the regulator can draw from whichever source is available:

Dual-primary benefit: Source A and Source B both feed the CCR. Losing either source alone does not necessarily interrupt CCR operation or the lighting it supplies.

08 · Not the Same as Two CCRs

Dual-Primary CCR vs Two Separate CCRs

This distinction matters for design reviews and is a common point of confusion:

Architecture What It Means
Dual-primary CCRTwo independent primary sources feed one CCR, which produces one regulated output circuit
Two separate CCRsSource A feeds CCR 1 → Circuit 1, and Source B feeds CCR 2 → Circuit 2 — two fully independent regulator/circuit pairs

These are different redundancy architectures. Dual-primary means two primary inputs to one CCR — it is not the same as duplicating the CCR and circuit themselves.

09 · Not the Same as Dual Secondary Circuits

Input Side vs Output Side

A CCR can be built with either one or two primary inputs, independent of how many regulated output circuits it has:

  • One primary input, one regulated output circuit
  • Two primary inputs, one regulated output circuit

The words "single-primary" and "dual-primary" describe the input side of the CCR only:

11 · Operational Benefit

Continuity of Supply for Critical Lighting

For critical airfield lighting, continuity of supply can be extremely important. Under a dual-primary arrangement, one source is typically active while the other stands by:

Normal operation: Source A (active) → CCR → Lighting, while Source B remains on standby as the alternate. If Source A becomes unavailable, the system transfers to Source B to keep the CCR — and the lighting circuit — in service.

13 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: "Dual-primary" means two separate CCRs
Fact

Dual-primary describes two primary inputs feeding one CCR, which is a different redundancy architecture from installing two independent CCRs on two circuits.

Myth: "Primary" refers to the CCR's output side
Fact

Primary refers to the AC power input side of the CCR. The regulated constant-current output to the lighting circuit is a separate function entirely.

Myth: A dual-primary CCR guarantees uninterrupted lighting during any failure
Fact

Continuity during a source failure is subject to the specific CCR's transfer characteristics and the overall system design — it is improved, not absolute.

Myth: A single-primary CCR can never have backup power
Fact

A single-primary CCR can still be backed up, but only through a separate, independent upstream transfer or emergency power arrangement — the redundancy isn't built into the CCR's own input architecture.

14 · Engineering Selection

Choosing Between Single-Primary and Dual-Primary CCR

When selecting between single-primary and dual-primary CCR architecture, consider:

  • Criticality of the lighting circuit served
  • Applicable ICAO/national continuity-of-supply requirements
  • Availability of independent primary power sources on site
  • Existing electrical infrastructure and switchgear
  • Budget for equipment and installation
  • Maintenance capability and spares availability
  • Airport-specific specifications and approved design
  • Overall power-resilience strategy for the airfield
15 · Engineering Workflow

From Requirement to Commissioning

Determine continuity-of-supply requirement → assess available primary sources → select single- or dual-primary CCR architecture → confirm transfer/control design (if dual) → size CCR to circuit load → design primary & secondary cabling → install and interconnect sources → electrical testing → transfer/failover testing (if dual) → commissioning.

16 · Engineering Verdict

Which Architecture Applies Where?

Engineering Verdict

Single-primary and dual-primary CCR describe how many independent primary power inputs feed the regulator — the difference is entirely on the input side, not the regulated output. A dual-primary CCR provides input-power redundancy that a single-primary CCR cannot provide on its own.

The final selection should always be based on the applicable continuity-of-supply requirement, criticality of the circuit, available site infrastructure and approved airport electrical design — not on a generic comparison alone.

Single-PrimarySimplicity, lower cost, lower input redundancy.
Dual-PrimaryHigher cost and complexity, in exchange for input-power redundancy.

17 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest difference between a single-primary and dual-primary CCR?

The number of independent primary power inputs feeding the CCR — one for single-primary, two for dual-primary — which determines the level of input-power redundancy available.

Does "dual-primary" mean two separate CCRs?

No. Dual-primary means two primary inputs feeding a single CCR. Two separate CCRs, each fed by its own source and driving its own circuit, is a different redundancy architecture entirely.

Does "primary" refer to the CCR's output?

No. Primary refers to the AC power input side of the CCR. The regulated constant-current output that feeds the airfield lighting circuit is a separate function.

Will a dual-primary CCR always keep the lighting on during a power failure?

Not automatically. Continuity during a source failure depends on the specific CCR's transfer characteristics and the overall system design, though it is generally more resilient than a single-primary arrangement.

Can a single-primary CCR still have backup power?

Yes, but only through a separate, independent upstream transfer or emergency power arrangement provided outside the CCR itself — the redundancy is not built into a single-primary CCR's own input.

Is a dual-primary CCR always the better choice?

Not necessarily. It offers higher input-power redundancy but at greater cost, equipment complexity and maintenance burden, so selection should follow the actual continuity-of-supply requirement for the installation.
