Threshold Wing Bar Light

A supplementary visual aid installed on precision approach runways to enhance threshold visibility. These green lights are arranged in two rows perpendicular to the runway centreline, extending outward from the runway edges to define the lateral limits of the threshold.

Overview

What Is It?

A Threshold Wing Bar Light is a green light fixture installed on precision approach runways (CAT II and III) to supplement the threshold lights. These lights are arranged in two rows, one on each side of the runway, extending outward from the runway edge lights. The wing bar lights project a uni-directional beam towards the approaching aircraft, providing a clear visual indication of the threshold location, particularly in low visibility conditions. They are also known as threshold bar lights or threshold wing lights.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Wing bar lights exist to widen and reinforce the threshold signal on precision approach runways, where a narrow row of threshold lights alone may not stand out strongly enough against background lighting or a low-visibility approach environment. By extending outward from the runway edge lights on both sides, the wing bars make the threshold location visually larger and harder to miss from a distance, giving pilots an earlier and more certain confirmation of exactly where the landing surface begins. Their purpose is concentrated specifically on CAT II/III operations, where the margin for misjudging the threshold position during a low-visibility landing is smallest and the value of an unmistakable, wide visual reference is highest.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on precision approach runways (CAT II and CAT III) where enhanced threshold visibility is required. Each wing bar extends outward from the line of the runway edge lights, with the innermost light of the bar in line with the edge lights and the bar reaching at least 10 metres (33 feet) further out. Per ICAO Annex 14, each wing bar is formed by at least five lights on each side, typically spaced at roughly 2–3 metre intervals to span that minimum 10 m extent. Wing bar lights are used in conjunction with threshold lights and approach lighting systems to provide comprehensive visual guidance during low visibility approaches.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed in two groups, one group on each side of the runway, each formed by at least five lights per ICAO Annex 14, with the innermost light of each bar in line with the runway edge lights and the bar extending at least 10 m further outward, spaced at roughly 2–3 m intervals to form a compact bar rather than a continuous line. Each light mounts on an L-868 base can recessed flush with the pavement or shoulder, sealed against surface water, with cabling run in pavement ducting or shoulder trenching back to the threshold circuit's isolating transformer, typically sharing a feed with the adjacent threshold lights. The unidirectional beam is angled to project toward the approach direction only, and setting-out is tied to the threshold survey mark and the runway edge light line so the wing bars sit at the correct offset and remain square to the runway centreline. Where the wing bar is added to an existing threshold installation, the new base cans are positioned to keep clear of the runway edge light circuit trenching already in place.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each wing bar light is powered from the series circuit via an isolating transformer. The light projects a uni-directional beam towards the approaching aircraft using a precision optical system with a reflector and prism. The green colour is achieved through a green filter or dichroic coating, matching the threshold lights. The intensity is controlled by the CCR's current steps, with higher intensities used during low visibility operations. The lights are typically elevated or flush-mounted, depending on the runway configuration.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Wing bar lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit that also feeds the associated threshold lights. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or (at smaller aerodromes) pilot-controlled lighting input. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to a single fixed current. Because the wing bars typically share the threshold circuit rather than run on a dedicated loop of their own, every wing bar light steps up and down in intensity in lock-step with the threshold lights at that runway end — there is no way to command the wing bars to a different brightness independently. Fault detection remains loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing.

ALCMS → CCR → Threshold Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Wing Bar Fixture (with loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is a compact die-cast aluminium housing finished for corrosion resistance, glazed with a green lens or filter over an LED array or halogen lamp behind a unidirectional optical system angled to project only toward the approach direction. The lens is gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring or silicone seal for an IP67/IP68 rating; elevated units bolt to a frangible coupling seated on an L-867 base can, while flush-mounted units seat directly into an L-868 base can recessed into the shoulder or pavement. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors sealed for direct burial, which mate to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer shared with the adjacent threshold circuit. Stainless-steel fasteners are used throughout, and because wing bar units are grouped tightly in bars of at least five lights per ICAO Annex 14, housings and base cans are dimensioned to permit the close 2–3 m spacing without cable congestion in the shared trench.

Installation Requirements

Threshold Offset & Base Can Setout
Civil

Set out each L-868 base can with the innermost light in line with the runway edge lights and the bar extending at least 10 m outward per the threshold survey mark, holding the two-group, minimum-five-light, ~2–3 m spacing pattern per side before excavation.

Drainage Provision
Civil

Provide a weep hole or drainage path at each base can so surface water cannot pool around the transformer and cable entry in the flush/shoulder-mounted position.

Duct & Cable Route to Threshold Circuit
Electrical

Run pavement ducting or shoulder trenching back to the threshold circuit's isolating transformer feed, keeping clear of the adjacent runway edge light circuit trenching where the wing bar is added to an existing installation.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal each primary connector per manufacturer procedure, keeping the splice fully submersible-rated before backfill or reinstatement of the base can.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the 45W/65W isolating transformer feeding each fixture matches the shared threshold circuit's rated current step before connection.

Uni-Directional Beam Aim
Mechanical

Orient each fixture's uni-directional optic strictly toward the approach direction, squaring the group to the runway centreline off the threshold survey mark.

Base Can / Mounting Fixing
Mechanical

Torque base can or elevated-mount fixing bolts to the specified value, confirming a frangible fitting shears as designed under lateral load without binding.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record each base can's offset and spacing within its group, cable route, connector seal, and torque values as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Green Filter Degradation

The green filters in wing bar lights are susceptible to UV degradation and thermal stress. Over time, the colour can fade or shift toward yellow, reducing the effectiveness of the visual cue. Regular photometric testing and filter replacement is essential.

Lens Contamination

Wing bar lights are exposed to rubber deposits from aircraft, tyre debris, and de-icing fluids. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and colour fidelity.

Misalignment from Pavement Settlement

Pavement settlement can cause wing bar lights to tilt, altering the beam pattern and reducing visibility on approach. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Wing Bar Light Dark, Rest of Bar and Threshold Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED array/lamp in that fixture, or an open isolating transformer if the wing bar element has its own dedicated secondary rather than sharing one with the threshold lights.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the L-823 connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED array/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the connectors before backfill.
Entire Wing Bar and Threshold Row Dark Together
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the shared threshold circuit loop, since wing bars typically feed from the same series circuit as the adjacent threshold lights rather than a dedicated loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output; sectionalise and megger cable runs between the threshold row and the wing bar groups to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a wing bar fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or partial ground fault — the tight 1.5–3 m spacing means several fixtures share a congested trench where a nicked cable is easy to miss.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full threshold/wing bar circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Green Colour Faded Toward Yellow
Likely Cause
UV-driven degradation or thermal stress in the green filter/dichroic coating — a chromaticity shift, not LED wear, is the usual cause on wing bar fixtures.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric chromaticity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect the filter for visible fading or thermal marks; rule out lens contamination as a simpler first check.
Corrective Action
Replace the green filter or lens assembly; re-verify chromaticity with a follow-up photometric reading before returning the fixture to service.
Reduced Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from rubber deposits and de-icing fluid residue, an aging LED array, or the CCR running on a lower brightness step than the operational requirement.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the lens; confirm the CCR is set to the correct intensity step for prevailing conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lens, replace a degraded LED array, or correct the CCR step setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling has begun to fatigue or partially shear (elevated units), or the base can has settled due to pavement/shoulder movement near the threshold.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically check the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against tolerance; inspect surrounding pavement/turf for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling if fatigued, re-level and re-aim the fixture, and address any base can or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Array / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single wing bar fixture goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the bar and the adjacent threshold row continue to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the shared threshold/wing bar circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time — more likely here given the number of connectors packed into the tight wing bar trench run.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades insulation resistance across the shared circuit.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from a nicked run in the congested wing bar trench, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire shared threshold/wing bar loop.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Green Filter Fade / Chromaticity Shift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure and thermal cycling breaking down the filter's dye or dichroic coating, accelerated by heat from the source behind it.
Effect
Chromaticity drifts toward yellow and falls outside the ICAO/FAA colour tolerance box even though the fixture still lights normally.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric and chromaticity testing, and visual comparison of the filter against a known-good reference unit during inspection.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic loading from mower or snowplough contact near the threshold shoulder, material fatigue over the service interval, or corrosion at the coupling threads.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or separates entirely from its base, taking the light out of tolerance or out of service.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim/level checks against tolerance.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on healthy circuits.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Base Can / Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure allowing water to pool and erode the fill around the base can near the threshold shoulder.
Effect
Fixture tilts out of aim tolerance, cable connections come under mechanical stress, and water can pool around the base can.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of the base can and surrounding pavement or turf, and a check of drainage paths during annual maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Wing bar lights typically share a feed with the adjacent threshold lights on a constant-current series circuit; de-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the base can before opening it or handling connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

The wing bars sit at the threshold on a precision approach runway; any work here requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM where fixtures will be dark, with personnel and vehicles kept clear of the runway strip.

Precision Approach Photometric Criticality
Compliance

Because wing bars supplement CAT II/III threshold guidance, confirm beam angle and intensity are restored to the design aiming diagram before closing out; a degraded bar here has an outsised effect on approach guidance in low-visibility conditions.

Base Can Seal Integrity
Mechanical

Reseal the base can lid and cable gland fully watertight after any work, especially where the wing bar sits on the shoulder rather than the paved runway edge.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the shared threshold feed.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture dark, misaligned, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; verify beam orientation and photometric conformance before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Wing Symmetry Matters as Much as Individual Output
Photometric

Wing bar lights work as a matched pair extending outward from each side of the threshold; a brightness mismatch between the two wings undercuts the width cue they exist to provide even if each individual fixture is within its own tolerance, so check side-to-side symmetry as a specific inspection item.

Confirm Uni-Directional Aim Toward the Approach
Installation

Wing bar lights are uni-directional and must present their beam toward the approaching aircraft, not toward the runway interior or an oblique angle that reduces effective range; verify aim after installation or any pavement work in the vicinity, since a fixture can be knocked slightly out of alignment without visible damage.

Verify Wing Bar Extent After Any Threshold Change
Installation

The published lateral extent of the wing bar lighting is tied to the specific CAT II/III configuration and threshold position in use; resurvey wing bar layout after any threshold displacement or category upgrade, since the correct extent for the new configuration may differ from what's currently installed.

LED Retrofit Needs Both Wings Matched, Not Just Each Fixture
Retrofit

An LED retrofit that individually passes photometric testing on each fixture can still introduce a visible left-right asymmetry if the two wings are retrofitted at different times with different LED batches; retrofit and requalify both wings as a matched set where practical.

Threshold-Adjacent Fixtures See Concentrated De-Icing Runoff
Durability

Wing bar lights near the threshold often sit in a zone with concentrated de-icing fluid application and runoff; specify glycol-rated seals for these fixtures and inspect them ahead of the general edge-light rotation given the added chemical exposure.

Trend Loop Insulation on the Threshold Feeder
Troubleshooting

Wing bar circuits often share a feeder with other threshold-area lighting; trend insulation resistance on that specific feeder across maintenance cycles, since a slow-developing fault here can present as inconsistent wing bar intensity well before an outright failure.
