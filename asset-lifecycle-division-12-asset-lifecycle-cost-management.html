LCC-01 Lifecycle Cost Fundamentals
Purpose

Lifecycle Cost Management evaluates the financial consequences of an asset throughout its useful life. The objective is to understand the total economic burden of the asset, not just its purchase price.

Lifecycle Stages
PLANNINGACQUISITIONINSTALLATIONCOMMISSIONINGOPERATIONINSPECTIONTESTINGMAINTENANCEFAILURE/REPAIRREHABILITATION/UPGRADEREPLACEMENTDISPOSAL
Lifecycle Cost Should Consider
  • Initial investment
  • Operating expenditure
  • Maintenance
  • Energy
  • Failures
  • Downtime
  • Spares
  • Testing
  • Rehabilitation
  • Replacement
  • Disposal
Principle

The lowest acquisition cost is not necessarily the lowest lifecycle cost.

LCC-02 Acquisition Cost
Purpose

Acquisition cost is the initial cost of obtaining the asset.

May Include
  • Equipment purchase
  • Manufacturer cost
  • Supplier cost
  • Freight
  • Insurance
  • Import/customs costs where applicable
  • Packaging
  • Associated accessories
  • Initial spare parts
  • Licensing where applicable
AGL Example

For a CCR, acquisition cost may include the CCR, required accessories, control interface, initial spares and delivery — rather than simply the quoted equipment price.

LCC-03 Installation Cost
Purpose

Installation cost represents the resources required to place the asset into service.

May Include
  • Civil works
  • Foundation/base can
  • Cabling
  • Cable termination
  • Transformer installation
  • Electrical connections
  • Earthing
  • Labour
  • Equipment/access
  • Airside coordination
  • Temporary works
  • Testing support
Important

Two products with identical purchase prices may have very different installation costs.

Principle

Product comparison should consider installed cost, not only equipment price.

LCC-04 Commissioning Cost
Purpose

Commissioning cost includes the resources required to verify and establish the asset as an operational system.

May Include
  • Testing
  • Inspection
  • Photometry
  • Electrical testing
  • Functional testing
  • Calibration
  • Specialist engineers
  • Manufacturer support
  • Documentation
  • Defect correction
  • Retesting
Downstream Link

This should connect directly with Division 07 — Commissioning & Baseline Asset Performance.

LCC-05 Energy Cost
Purpose

Energy cost may become a significant portion of lifecycle expenditure for continuously or frequently operated AGL systems.

Consider
  • Rated power
  • Actual operating power
  • Operating hours
  • Brightness levels
  • Energy efficiency
  • Energy price
  • Expected operating life
  • System configuration
Basic Concept
LIFETIME ENERGY COST = ENERGY CONSUMPTION × OPERATING EXPOSURE × ENERGY RATE

Actual calculations should use the organization's approved assumptions.

Important

An asset with a higher acquisition cost may have a lower total lifecycle cost if its energy consumption is substantially lower.

LCC-06 Maintenance Cost
Purpose

Maintenance cost includes resources required to preserve asset performance.

Consider
  • Preventive maintenance
  • Predictive maintenance
  • Condition monitoring
  • Corrective maintenance
  • Labour
  • Contractor support
  • Test equipment
  • Access equipment
  • Consumables
  • Documentation
  • Planned shutdown/closure costs
Lifecycle Comparison

Asset A: Low purchase cost, high maintenance requirement. Asset B: Higher purchase cost, lower maintenance requirement. The correct decision requires lifecycle comparison.

LCC-07 Spare-Parts Cost
Purpose

Spare-parts cost should be included in lifecycle cost analysis.

Consider
  • Initial spare inventory
  • Routine replacement parts
  • Emergency spares
  • Long-lead items
  • Obsolete parts
  • Minimum stock requirements
  • Storage
  • Expired/unused stock
AGL Examples
  • Isolation transformers
  • Connectors
  • Fixtures/components
  • CCR components
  • Sign components
  • PAPI components
  • Control-system components
Important

A low-cost asset with expensive or unavailable spare parts can create a high lifecycle cost.

LCC-08 Failure Cost
Purpose

Failure cost represents the financial impact associated with asset failure.

May Include
  • Spare part
  • Labour
  • Emergency response
  • Contractor
  • Testing
  • Investigation
  • Repair
  • Replacement
  • Secondary damage
  • Additional inspection
Important

Failure cost should also consider operational consequences.

LCC-09 Downtime Cost
Purpose

Downtime cost can be much greater than the physical repair cost.

Consider
  • Loss of asset availability
  • Operational restrictions
  • Runway/taxiway restrictions
  • Operational delays
  • Temporary arrangements
  • Emergency response
  • Additional manpower
  • Airside coordination
  • Closure-related costs
Important Principle
FAILURE COST = REPAIR COST + CONSEQUENCE OF FAILURE

Therefore, a low-value component failure may create a much larger lifecycle impact if it causes significant operational disruption.

LCC-10 Inspection Cost
Purpose

Inspection costs should be included where they represent meaningful lifecycle resources.

May Include
  • Technician labour
  • Engineer inspection
  • Airside access
  • Inspection equipment
  • Vehicle/access equipment
  • Documentation
  • Specialist inspection
Lifecycle Optimization

The objective is not to minimize inspection frequency blindly. Instead, optimize inspection effort according to asset risk and value of information.

LCC-12 Rehabilitation Cost
Purpose

Rehabilitation restores or improves an existing asset without necessarily replacing the complete system.

Examples

Upgrade costs arise when an existing asset is modified to achieve improved capability.

LCC-14 Replacement Cost
Purpose

Replacement cost includes the complete cost of removing an existing asset and installing the replacement.

Consider
  • New equipment
  • Removal
  • Disposal
  • Civil works
  • Cabling
  • Installation
  • Testing
  • Commissioning
  • Documentation
  • Training
  • Initial spares
  • Operational disruption
Important

Replacement should not be triggered solely by age. The decision should consider condition, reliability, RUL, criticality, lifecycle cost, obsolescence, availability of spares, technology and future requirements.

LCC-15 Disposal Cost

End-of-life disposal should be included in lifecycle planning.

May Include
  • Removal

  • Disposal

  • Environmental handling
  • Decommissioning
  • Site restoration
  • Documentation
  • Data/system removal
Important

Where assets contain materials subject to specific environmental or disposal requirements, applicable regulations should be considered.

LCC-16 Total Cost of Ownership
Purpose

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) represents the broader economic cost of owning and operating an asset. The exact organizational definition should be standardized.

TCO = ACQUISITION + INSTALLATION + COMMISSIONING + OPERATION + MAINTENANCE + SPARES + FAILURE + TESTING + REHABILITATION + UPGRADE + REPLACEMENT/END-OF-LIFE
Why TCO Matters

A procurement decision based only on purchase price can produce the wrong lifecycle decision. Instead compare purchase, operation, maintenance, failure, energy and end-of-life cost.

LCC-17 Net Lifecycle Cost
Purpose

Net lifecycle cost considers the timing of costs over the asset's life. For longer-term evaluations, organizations may use an approved discounting methodology to express future expenditures in present-value terms.

Conceptual Timeline
YEAR 0 — ACQUISITIONYEARS 1–5 — ENERGY + MAINTENANCEYEARS 6–10 — MAINTENANCE + REHABILITATIONYEARS 11–15 — HIGHER MAINTENANCE + REPLACEMENTEND OF LIFE — DISPOSAL
Result

The result provides a more meaningful comparison between alternatives with different cost profiles.

LCC-18 Cost Comparison
Purpose

Cost comparison should be used during:

  • Product selection
  • Procurement
  • Design
  • Maintenance strategy selection
  • Rehabilitation
  • Upgrade
  • Replacement
Example — Product A vs. Product B

Product A: Lower acquisition, lower installation, higher energy, higher maintenance, higher spares, higher failure cost, shorter expected life, higher end-of-life cost.

Product B: Higher acquisition, higher installation, lower energy, lower maintenance, lower spares, lower failure cost, longer expected life, lower end-of-life cost.

The lifecycle cost of each must be calculated — this is much more useful than simply comparing supplier quotations.

Purpose

Lifecycle-cost forecasting estimates future expenditure requirements.

Forecast Inputs

  • Asset population
  • Condition
  • RUL
  • Failure history
  • Maintenance history
  • Energy trends
  • Spare requirements
  • Expected replacement date
  • Inflation assumptions

This supports long-term financial planning.

LCC-20 Lifecycle-Cost Optimization
Purpose

Lifecycle-cost optimization means finding the option that provides the required performance and risk level at the most appropriate total lifecycle cost.

Optimize Across
  • Acquisition
  • Energy
  • Maintenance
  • Reliability
  • Spares
  • Downtime
  • Rehabilitation
  • Replacement
Important

The cheapest option is not necessarily the optimum. The optimum option is the one that provides the required performance, safety, reliability and lifecycle value within acceptable risk.

LCC-21 Budget Planning
Purpose

Lifecycle cost information should feed the airport's financial planning process.

Budget Categories

Operational expenditure: Maintenance, inspection, testing, energy, spares, minor repairs.

Capital expenditure: New equipment, major rehabilitation, upgrades, replacement, system modernization.

Lifecycle Planning Can Identify
  • Future maintenance peaks
  • Major replacement years
  • Critical-spare requirements
  • Rehabilitation requirements
  • Technology upgrades
  • Capital funding requirements
LCC-22 Capital-versus-Maintenance Decision
Purpose

One of the most important decisions in asset lifecycle management: should we continue maintaining the existing asset, or invest in rehabilitation/replacement?

Compare

Option A — Continue Maintenance: Annual maintenance, failure risk, energy cost, spare availability, reliability, RUL, downtime.

Option B — Rehabilitation: Rehabilitation cost, improved performance, extended life, maintenance reduction.

Option C — Replacement: Capital cost, installation, commissioning, energy, maintenance, reliability, expected life, future support.

Decision Factors
  • Health
  • Criticality
  • RUL
  • Reliability
  • Obsolescence
  • Lifecycle cost
  • Operational risk
  • Regulatory requirements
  • Future airport requirements

Division 12 — Core Principle

Every major AGL asset decision should consider the cost of the entire lifecycle — not simply the price paid to acquire the equipment.

The complete decision chain: Acquisition Cost → Installation Cost → Commissioning Cost → Energy Cost → Maintenance Cost → Spare Cost → Inspection & Testing Cost → Failure Cost → Downtime Cost → Rehabilitation/Upgrade Cost → Replacement Cost → Disposal Cost → Total Cost of Ownership → Net Lifecycle Cost → Technical + Risk + Performance Assessment → Lifecycle Investment Decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Asset Lifecycle Cost Management in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 12 — Asset Lifecycle Cost Management in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 12 is the financial-intelligence layer that converts technical information from Divisions 01-11 into a defensible basis for procurement, maintenance, rehabilitation, upgrade and replacement decisions. It covers 22 topics on lifecycle cost fundamentals, acquisition, installation, commissioning, energy, maintenance, spare-parts, failure, downtime, inspection, testing, rehabilitation, upgrade, replacement and disposal cost, TCO, net lifecycle cost, cost comparison, forecasting, optimization, budget planning and capital-versus-maintenance decisions.

Q2What is lifecycle cost management for AGL assets?

It evaluates the financial consequences of an asset throughout its useful life, so decisions are not based only on the initial purchase price. It looks at initial investment, operating expenditure, maintenance, energy, failures and downtime.

Q3Which cost categories are included in AGL lifecycle cost?

Division 12 covers acquisition, installation, commissioning, energy, maintenance, spare parts, failure, downtime, inspection, testing, rehabilitation, upgrade, replacement and disposal costs.

Q4Why is downtime cost important?

Downtime cost can be much greater than the physical repair cost. It includes loss of asset availability, operational restrictions, runway or taxiway restrictions and operational delays.

Q5What is the difference between Total Cost of Ownership and Net Lifecycle Cost?

Total Cost of Ownership is the broader economic cost of owning and operating an asset, and its exact organizational definition should be standardized. Net lifecycle cost also considers the timing of costs, and for longer-term evaluations an approved discounting methodology may be used.

Q6Where should cost comparison be used?

Cost comparison should be used during product selection, procurement, design, maintenance strategy selection, rehabilitation and upgrade decisions.

Q7How do forecasting and budget planning use lifecycle cost data?

Lifecycle-cost forecasting estimates future spending using asset age, population, condition, RUL, and failure and maintenance history. Lifecycle cost information then feeds financial planning, highlighting future maintenance peaks, major replacement years, critical-spare needs and rehabilitation requirements.

Q8How does lifecycle cost inform the capital-versus-maintenance decision?

This is described as one of the most important decisions in asset lifecycle management: whether to keep maintaining the existing asset or invest in rehabilitation or replacement. The decision draws on health, criticality, RUL, reliability, obsolescence and lifecycle cost.
