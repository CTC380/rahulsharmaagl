Isolation Switch

A manually operated electrical switching device used in Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) circuits to provide safe isolation of sections of the lighting loop for maintenance, fault finding, and emergency disconnection. Isolation switches ensure the safety of personnel working on AGL systems by physically breaking the circuit and providing a visible air gap.

Overview

What Is It?

An Isolation Switch (also known as an isolating switch or disconnect switch) is a manually operated device that provides electrical isolation of a section of an AGL series circuit. Unlike circuit breakers, which are designed for automatic fault protection, isolation switches are intended for deliberate disconnection to allow safe maintenance, repair, or modification of the lighting system. They provide a visible air gap when open, ensuring that the circuit is de‑energized and cannot be re‑energized unintentionally. Isolation switches are typically installed at strategic points in the AGL loop, such as at the entry to a runway or taxiway section, allowing maintenance crews to isolate a specific area without affecting the rest of the airfield lighting.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The isolation switch's purpose is to give maintenance crews a way to positively and visibly de-energise a section of an AGL circuit before they work on it, which is a fundamentally different job from the automatic fault-clearing that circuit breakers perform. Its visible air gap exists specifically so that a technician can see with their own eyes that a circuit is open, rather than trusting an indicator light or a breaker's internal state — a distinction that matters because it is what makes lock-out/tag-out procedures effective and prevents someone else from unknowingly re-energising a circuit while work is in progress. By allowing a single runway or taxiway section to be isolated without shutting down the rest of the airfield's lighting, it also lets routine maintenance happen without unnecessarily disrupting other operations.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Isolation switches are used throughout the AGL infrastructure:

  • In CCR Rooms: Between the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) output and the series circuit feeder cables, allowing the CCR to be isolated for maintenance.
  • At Lighting Circuit Branch Points: Where the main series loop splits into sections for different runways, taxiways, or aprons.
  • At Airfield Boundaries: To isolate entire lighting circuits for security or emergency purposes.
  • At Maintenance Access Points: Located in manholes or pits where technicians need to isolate a section for lamp replacement, cable repair, or transformer testing.
  • In Substations and Distribution Boards: As part of the primary distribution system for AGL power.
Placement

Installation & Location

Isolation switches are mounted at the points in the network where a section needs to be taken out of service independently of the rest — CCR output panels, lighting circuit branch points, substation and distribution board incomers, and maintenance access pits or manholes along the series loop. Panel-mounted units are fixed so the operating handle is accessible from outside the enclosure without exposing live parts, while field-installed units in pits or manholes are housed in a sealed, labelled enclosure that keeps the visible-break mechanism protected from moisture and debris. Location is chosen so each switch clearly isolates a defined, labelled section of circuit, letting a technician work on that section, a fixture, or a transformer without de-energising the whole airfield.

Function

How Does It Work?

An isolation switch operates on a simple but essential principle:

  • Manual Operation: The switch is operated by a handle or rotary mechanism that moves the contacts to the open or closed position. The mechanism is designed to provide a positive action, ensuring the contacts are fully engaged or fully disengaged.
  • Visible Break: When open, the switch provides a visible air gap between the contacts, giving a clear visual indication that the circuit is disconnected. This is a key safety feature, as it prevents accidental re‑energization and provides lock‑out/tag‑out capability.
  • Load‑Make/Load‑Break Capability: Isolation switches used in AGL circuits are typically designed to handle the series circuit current (up to 6.6A or 20A) and can be operated under load, though for safety they are often opened under no‑load conditions.
  • Interlocking: Some isolation switches are interlocked with circuit breakers or other switches to prevent operation under unsafe conditions, such as opening under heavy fault current.
  • Mounting: They are housed in weatherproof enclosures (IP65 or higher) for outdoor installation, or in indoor panels for CCR rooms and substations.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

An isolation switch carries no automatic control logic of its own — its position is set manually and it does not respond to current, voltage, or a remote command in normal operation. Architecturally it sits inline in the power path, typically between a breaker and the downstream equipment it protects, giving maintenance staff a visible, lockable break point independent of the breaker's internal contacts. Where isolators are interlocked with an upstream breaker or with each other (a common arrangement in CCR rooms), the interlock is a mechanical or key-exchange permissive rather than an electronic control signal: it physically prevents the isolator from being opened or closed in a sequence that could leave a circuit unsafely live or de-energised. Some switches used in larger installations add auxiliary contacts that report open/closed status to a local panel or the ALCMS, but this is a status indication rather than a control function — the switch state is always changed by hand.

Construction

Physical Construction

The switch mechanism sits inside a moulded enclosure — weatherproof glass-reinforced polyester or polycarbonate for outdoor installations, sheet steel for indoor panel use — with an external operating handle mechanically linked to the internal contacts so the blade position is visible or padlockable from outside the enclosure. Silver-alloy contacts are used at the moving blade and fixed jaw to resist arcing and pitting over repeated operations, and the air-break design relies on physical separation of the contacts, rather than a sealed medium, to establish a visible isolation gap that maintenance staff can confirm by eye before working on the downstream circuit. Cable entries at the base or side of the enclosure accept glanded conduit or direct-buried cable, and the enclosure includes a hinged, gasketed door or cover secured by captive screws to maintain its IP rating in outdoor service. A mechanical interlock or padlocking facility on the handle allows the switch to be locked in the open position during maintenance, satisfying lock-out/tag-out requirements for the series circuit it isolates.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Contact Corrosion

Exposure to moisture, salt spray, or chemical contaminants can cause contact corrosion, leading to high resistance, heating, and eventual failure. Regular inspection and cleaning, or the use of sealed enclosures and corrosion‑resistant contacts, are essential.

Mechanical Jamming

The operating mechanism can jam due to dirt, rust, or wear, preventing the switch from opening or closing fully. Periodic lubrication and exercise of the switch are recommended.

Inadequate Load‑Break Capability

If the isolation switch is not rated for load‑break, opening it under load can cause arcing and damage. Ensure proper procedure (de‑energize circuit first) or use load‑break‑rated switches where required.

Enclosure Water Ingress

Outdoor enclosures can allow water ingress through worn gaskets or cable glands, leading to internal corrosion and short circuits. Regular seal checks and maintenance of gland integrity are critical.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Switch Feels Stiff or Won't Fully Open/Close
Likely Cause
Mechanical jamming from dirt, rust, or wear in the operating mechanism.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise the circuit and operate the switch manually to feel for binding points; inspect the mechanism for corrosion, debris, or worn linkage.
Corrective Action
Clean and lubricate the operating mechanism, and exercise the switch through several cycles to confirm smooth operation.
Switch Runs Hot or Shows Discoloration at Contacts
Likely Cause
Contact corrosion increasing resistance at the contact points, generating heat under load.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise and inspect contacts for pitting, discoloration, or corrosion; measure contact resistance across the closed switch.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace corroded contacts, and consider sealing the enclosure better or using corrosion-resistant contacts if the issue recurs.
Arcing or Damage After Opening the Switch
Likely Cause
The switch was opened under load without adequate load-break rating, or the correct de-energising procedure wasn't followed.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect contacts and housing for arc damage; confirm the switch's load-break rating against how it was actually operated.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged contacts or the switch if arc damage is significant, and reinforce the de-energise-before-operating procedure or upgrade to a load-break-rated switch.
Internal Corrosion or Moisture Inside the Enclosure
Likely Cause
Water ingress through worn gaskets or cable glands on an outdoor enclosure.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect gaskets, seals, and cable glands for wear or gaps; check for visible moisture or corrosion inside the enclosure.
Corrective Action
Replace worn gaskets and glands, dry out the enclosure, and re-verify the seal before re-energising.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Confirm De-Energised Before Opening
Electrical Safety

Do not rely on the switch's visible air gap alone; verify zero energy on both sides of an opened isolation switch with a calibrated tester before working on the isolated section.

Lock Out and Tag the Switch
Electrical Safety

Apply a lock and tag to the switch in the open position for the duration of the work, so it cannot be closed unintentionally by another party while the section is being worked on.

Confirm the Correct Section Is Isolated
Operational

Positively identify and label which circuit section a given switch isolates before relying on it, since operating the wrong switch can leave the intended work area live while de-energising an unrelated section.

Inspect the Visible Break Before Use
Personnel Safety

Visually confirm the air gap is fully open and the contacts are clean and undamaged before treating the switch as a safe point of isolation; a pitted or misaligned contact can fail to provide full isolation.

PPE for Switching Operations
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when operating a switch under load or near a live circuit, and stand clear of the switch body during operation in case of an internal arc.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After work on the isolated section, megger test it before closing the switch, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy equipment on the same circuit.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools and fasteners accounted for at all times when the switch enclosure is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a switch unlabelled, an enclosure unsealed, or a lock-out device still in place after work is complete and verified safe; confirm the switch is correctly labelled and closed before closing out the work order.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify the Off Position Doesn't Just Look Open
Electrical Safety

A switch blade or contact that visually appears open can still be carrying voltage if a mechanism has partially failed or a contact has welded under fault current. Always confirm isolation with a voltage test at the load side, not just by the switch handle position.

Contact Pitting Precedes a Thermal Failure
Diagnostics

A switch that operates fine mechanically but shows discolouration or pitting on the contacts is often close to a thermal failure under load, even if it tests fine with no current flowing. Inspect contact condition at every scheduled maintenance, not just when a fault is already reported.

Enclosure Seal Failure Shows Up as Corrosion First
Durability

Early moisture ingress into an outdoor isolation switch enclosure usually shows up as light corrosion on fasteners or the enclosure interior well before it affects switch operation. Treat visible corrosion as a seal-integrity warning and inspect gaskets proactively rather than waiting for a functional fault.

Mislabelled Switches Are a Recurring Root Cause
Process

Incorrect or outdated circuit labelling on an isolation switch is a common contributor to near-miss incidents during lock-out/tag-out, especially after circuit rework. Verify the label matches the as-built circuit at every maintenance visit, not only when a new switch is installed.

Undersized Switches Age Faster Under Inrush
Design

An isolation switch rated close to the circuit's steady-state current but not comfortably above its inrush or fault current tends to show accelerated contact wear. Confirm the switch rating against actual circuit inrush characteristics, not just nameplate load current, when specifying a replacement.

Track Operating Cycles, Not Just Calendar Time
Maintenance

A switch operated frequently as part of routine maintenance wears differently than one left untouched for years. Where practical, log operating cycle counts alongside calendar-based inspection intervals so mechanical wear-out is caught on its own timeline, not just the standard schedule.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL isolation switches: safe isolation, ratings, lock-out, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is an AGL isolation switch?

An isolation switch, also called an isolating or disconnect switch, is a manually operated device that isolates a section of an AGL series circuit. Unlike circuit breakers, which trip automatically on faults, it is used to deliberately isolate a circuit for maintenance.

Q2Why is an isolation switch needed?

It lets maintenance crews positively and visibly de-energise a circuit section before working on it. That is different from the automatic fault clearing of a breaker.

Q3Where are isolation switches used?

Between the CCR output and the series circuit feeder cables in CCR rooms, and at lighting circuit branch points throughout the AGL infrastructure.

Q4How does an isolation switch work?

A handle or rotary mechanism moves the contacts to open or closed with a positive action, so the open position gives a reliable isolation gap.

Q5What are the typical ratings?

6.6 A or 20 A on the series-circuit side and 16-63 A on primary feeder or distribution-board isolators, up to 1000 V AC, short-circuit withstand up to 10 kA for 1 second, and 1- to 4-pole versions with silver-alloy contacts.

Q6Can an isolation switch be locked out?

Yes. The handle is padlockable in the open position to satisfy lock-out/tag-out requirements, and the visible isolation gap should be confirmed before downstream work.

Q7What protection should be upstream of an isolation switch?

An MCB, MCCB or fuse sized to coordinate with the switch's short-circuit rating, because the isolator itself is not a fault-clearing device.

Q8Which standards apply?

IEC 60947-3 (switches, disconnectors and fuse-disconnectors), IEEE C37.20 and NFPA 70 (NEC) for isolation and disconnecting means.

Q9How often should an isolation switch be maintained?

Annually: inspect contacts for corrosion or pitting, cycle the mechanism, and check enclosure seals. Confirm the load-break rating still suits the connected load at installation and on review.

Q10Why does an isolation switch feel stiff?

Usually mechanical jamming from dirt, rust or wear in the mechanism. De-energise the circuit and operate the switch manually to find binding points.
