Beacon Controller

The intelligent control unit that manages the operation of airport, heliport, and obstruction beacons. Provides precise control over rotation speed, flash patterns, intensity levels, and synchronization between multiple beacons, ensuring compliance with ICAO and FAA requirements for visual identification signals.

Overview

What Is It?

A Beacon Controller is an electronic control unit that regulates the operation of rotating or flashing beacon systems used for airport, heliport, and obstruction identification. It provides precise management of beacon parameters including rotation speed (for rotating beacons), flash pattern and rate, colour sequence timing, and intensity levels. Advanced controllers can manage multiple beacons simultaneously, ensuring synchronized operation across a wind farm, a group of towers, or multiple airport terminals. The controller receives input from photocells, timers, or remote control systems (e.g., ALCMS) and adjusts beacon operation accordingly. It also includes diagnostic features to monitor beacon status, detect faults, and report alarms. Modern beacon controllers use microprocessor‑based technology with programmable settings, allowing flexibility to meet various regulatory requirements and site‑specific needs.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The beacon controller exists because a beacon's identification signal is only useful if its timing, colour sequence, and intensity are correct and consistent — a beacon rotating too fast, too slow, or flashing an incorrect pattern could confuse rather than inform a pilot, undermining the very identification function the beacon exists to provide. By centralizing photocell input, timing, and remote-control interfacing (such as ALCMS) into one governing unit, the controller ensures every beacon under its management stays within its regulatory flash pattern and switches day/night modes automatically without manual intervention. Its diagnostic and fault-reporting capability serves a related purpose: because a beacon failure can go unnoticed for hours in the field, especially at unmanned sites, the controller's ability to detect and report faults closes the gap between a beacon failing and someone knowing about it.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Used in all applications where beacons require controlled operation: airport identification beacons, heliport beacons, obstruction warning beacons on wind turbines, communication masts, transmission towers, chimneys, and tall buildings. Installed in control rooms, equipment shelters, or near the beacon itself. Used for both single‑beacon installations and multi‑beacon systems requiring synchronization (e.g., wind farms with GPS‑synchronized beacons). Also used in portable and temporary beacon systems where a compact controller is needed.

Placement

Installation & Location

The controller is housed indoors or in a weatherproof equipment shelter, sited as close as practical to the beacon(s) it manages to keep signal and control cabling runs short, typically in the base of the tower or mast, a dedicated equipment room, or a wind farm's central control building. It is wall- or rack-mounted with clear access for maintenance, wired to mains or standby power, to the photocell (or remote day/night signal), and to the beacon head(s) via control and monitoring cable. For multi-beacon or wind-farm installations, the controller is connected into a GPS-synchronization or network link so that all beacons under its management flash in coordinated unison. Surge protection and isolation are fitted at the cable entry points, since the controller sits between exposed outdoor cabling and the site's low-voltage control electronics.

Function

How Does It Work?

The beacon controller operates by receiving input signals from various sources (photocell, timer, manual switch, or remote command) and generating the appropriate output to drive the beacon(s). For rotating beacons, it controls the motor speed via a variable frequency drive or phase control, ensuring the correct rotation rate (typically 12–15 RPM, producing 24–30 flashes per minute). For flashing beacons, it generates precise timing pulses for the flash pattern and rate (typically 20–40 flashes per minute). The controller also manages colour sequencing (e.g., alternating green/white) by controlling the lamps or LEDs. Intensity levels can be adjusted using PWM or current control, with multiple preset levels for day, twilight, and night operations. Advanced controllers include GPS receivers for time‑synchronized flashing across multiple units, fault detection (lamp failure, motor overload, loss of sync), and communication interfaces (RS‑485, Ethernet, fiber optic) for remote monitoring and control via ALCMS. The controller is housed in a weatherproof enclosure with surge protection and power conditioning.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The beacon controller is the single decision-making node in the system — everything upstream feeds it a command, and everything downstream only does what the controller tells it. Photocell, timer, remote ALCMS command, or GPS sync tell the controller when to run and at which preset intensity; the controller decides everything from there. It issues two independent outputs from the same internal clock: a speed command to the drive motor (variable frequency drive or phase control) and a timing/colour command to the lamp or LED circuit, so rotation and flash colour stay synchronized even though the two paths are driven separately. Where several beacons must flash in step — co-located units, or beacons visible together on the same approach — a GPS receiver disciplines the controller's internal clock so each unit's timing tracks a common reference instead of drifting on its own. The controller also owns fault detection: it monitors motor current, lamp circuit continuity, and loss of rotational sync, and reports any anomaly as an alarm over its communication interface (RS-485, Ethernet, or fibre) to the ALCMS — the controller flags faults, it does not resolve them locally.

Construction

Physical Construction

The controller is housed in a corrosion-resistant, weatherproof cabinet (typically powder-coated steel or aluminium) mounted at the base of the beacon tower or within an equipment shelter, gasket-sealed to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating. Internally, the cabinet is organised around a DIN-rail-mounted power stage (variable frequency drive or PWM driver), a logic/control board handling flash-rate and colour-sequence programming, and a terminal block distributing motor, lamp, and communications wiring, all laid out with segregated high- and low-voltage sections for safety and EMI control. Surge suppression and isolation devices protect the electronics from lightning-induced transients given the beacon's exposed mounting, and a fused disconnect or circuit breaker provides local isolation for maintenance. Cable entries use sealed glands rated for continuous outdoor exposure, and the enclosure includes a heater or breather to manage internal condensation. External communication ports (RS-485, Ethernet, or fiber) and a GPS antenna connection are brought out through weatherproof connectors on the cabinet exterior.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Power Supply Instability

Beacon controllers are sensitive to voltage fluctuations and surges. Using a dedicated power supply with surge protection and voltage regulation is essential. Regular inspection of the power input and surge protection devices is recommended.

GPS Signal Loss (Synchronized Systems)

Controllers that rely on GPS for synchronization can lose signal due to antenna issues or atmospheric conditions. This can cause beacons to fall out of sync. Regular GPS antenna inspection and use of a backup timing source (e.g., internal oscillator) is recommended.

Communication Failure

Controllers that communicate with a central ALCMS can experience communication failures due to cable damage or network issues. Regular testing of communication links and use of redundant communication paths is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Controller Not Powering Up
Likely Cause
Loss of incoming mains power, a tripped breaker or blown internal fuse, or a failed surge protection device left in an open-circuit state after a lightning event.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming supply voltage and breaker status; inspect and test the surge protection device for continuity; check internal fuses against the rated value.
Corrective Action
Restore mains power or reset the breaker, replace a failed surge protection device, and replace blown fuses with correctly rated parts; confirm stable power-up before returning to service.
Beacon Flash Rate Incorrect or Inconsistent
Likely Cause
Voltage fluctuation at the controller's input, an out-of-calibration internal timing circuit, or a downstream drive motor/mechanical fault being misread as a controller issue.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor incoming supply voltage for sags or surges over a full duty cycle; verify controller output timing against its rated setpoint; confirm the drive motor itself is rotating at the correct speed independent of the controller signal.
Corrective Action
Stabilise the incoming supply with voltage regulation, recalibrate or replace the controller's timing circuit, or address the drive motor fault per its own procedure.
Beacon Out of Sync with Adjacent Units
Likely Cause
Loss of GPS signal to the synchronization receiver, a degraded GPS antenna or feeder cable, or drift in the internal backup oscillator during an extended GPS outage.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the GPS receiver's lock/signal-quality indicator; inspect the antenna, mounting, and feeder cable for damage or obstruction; compare the controller's timestamp against the backup oscillator's expected drift rate.
Corrective Action
Clear antenna obstructions or replace a damaged antenna/feeder, reacquire GPS lock, and recalibrate or replace the internal oscillator if drift exceeds tolerance during backup operation.
Controller Not Reporting to ALCMS
Likely Cause
Damaged communication cable, a failed network interface card in the controller, or an addressing/configuration mismatch after a firmware update or field swap.
Diagnostic Steps
Test continuity and signal quality on the communication link back to the ALCMS head end; check the controller's network interface status and error logs; verify the unit's network address and configuration against ALCMS records.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged communication cable, replace a failed network interface card, or correct the addressing/configuration; confirm the controller re-establishes reporting to the ALCMS before closing out.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Surge Protection Device Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced or switching surge exceeds the SPD's rating, leaving it open-circuit or degraded after clamping the event.
Effect
Controller fails to power up at all, or repeated downstream electronic faults follow storm activity.
Detection Method
Continuity testing of the SPD, incoming supply voltage checks, and internal fuse inspection against rated values.
Timing Circuit Calibration Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Component ageing or thermal stress causes the internal timing circuit to drift out of calibration, or incoming supply voltage fluctuates beyond its regulation range.
Effect
Beacon flash rate becomes incorrect or inconsistent, independent of the drive motor's own condition.
Detection Method
Monitoring of incoming supply voltage over a full duty cycle, and verification of controller output timing against its rated setpoint.
GPS Synchronisation Loss
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Loss of GPS signal to the synchronisation receiver, a degraded antenna or feeder cable, or oscillator drift during an extended GPS outage.
Effect
Beacon falls out of sync with adjacent units, which can create a confusing or non-compliant flash pattern across a beacon array.
Detection Method
Checks of the GPS receiver's lock/signal-quality indicator, antenna and feeder inspection, and comparison of controller timestamp against expected backup-oscillator drift.
Network Interface / Communication Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Damaged communication cable, a failed network interface card, or an addressing/configuration mismatch after a firmware update or field swap.
Effect
Controller stops reporting status to the ALCMS, leaving the site without remote visibility of beacon health.
Detection Method
Continuity and signal-quality testing on the communication link, network interface status/error-log review, and verification of network address and configuration.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Isolate and lock out the incoming supply before opening the controller enclosure, and verify zero energy at the terminal block; the output stage can remain live even when the beacon itself is dark.

ESD Precautions
Component Handling

Use an anti-static wrist strap and mat when handling the microprocessor board or replacing components, since electrostatic discharge can damage the control circuitry without any visible sign.

Surge / Lightning Exposure
Electrical Safety

Treat the controller as a lightning-exposed device on tower or mast installations; confirm surge protection devices are intact and avoid servicing during an active electrical storm.

Coordinate Before Disabling
Operational

Notify the relevant authority (ATC, ALCMS operator, or wind-farm control centre) before taking the controller offline, since this silences every beacon it manages, not just one fixture.

Configuration Backup
Operational

Record or export the existing programmable settings (flash pattern, intensity steps, synchronisation offsets) before any firmware update or board replacement so the site-specific configuration can be restored.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for live-side checks and confirm the enclosure is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting supply.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify every managed beacon returns to its correct pattern, intensity, and synchronisation before closing out the work and cancelling any associated outage notice.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Lost GPS Sync Doesn't Always Look Like a Fault
Diagnostics

A controller running on its internal clock after losing GPS lock can keep flashing at a plausible rate for hours, so a drifting sync is easy to miss until beacons across a farm or airfield visibly fall out of step. Check GPS antenna and lock status as a routine item, not only when synchronisation has already visibly failed.

Segregate High/Low Voltage at Every Re-Termination
Electrical

Cabinets that pass their original segregation check can drift out of compliance after field modifications add a communications or sensor cable through the same gland or duct as motor/lamp power. Re-verify high/low voltage segregation whenever wiring is added or re-terminated, not only at initial commissioning.

Read the Fault Log Before You Reset It
Troubleshooting

Clearing the controller's fault log to get a clean status reading throws away the intermittent-fault history that's often the only clue to a nuisance trip's root cause. Pull and archive the log before any reset or firmware update, especially when chasing an issue that hasn't been reproduced on demand.

Surge Damage Accumulates Before It Causes an Outage
Reliability

A controller can survive several transients with a degraded surge-protection stage before the next one takes it down completely, so a unit that has "always worked" after nearby lightning activity isn't necessarily undamaged. Include surge-device condition checks in scheduled maintenance rather than waiting for a hard failure to prompt inspection.

Confirm ALCMS Reporting Matches Physical Reality
Commissioning

A controller can report a healthy status to the ALCMS while the beacon it drives is actually degraded, if the fault relay logic doesn't cover the specific failure mode occurring in the field. Physically verify beacon output against the remote status display periodically, rather than trusting the ALCMS feed as a complete picture of fixture health.

Test the Backup Transfer, Not Just the Battery Voltage
Testing

A backup battery reading full voltage on a meter can still fail to actually pick up the load if the transfer relay or charging circuit has degraded. Run a genuine mains-loss transfer test at scheduled intervals rather than relying on standby voltage alone as proof the backup path works.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about beacon controllers: what they control, inputs, synchronisation, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a beacon controller?

A beacon controller is an electronic control unit that regulates rotating or flashing beacons used for airports, heliports and obstruction identification. It manages rotation speed, flash pattern and rate, colour sequence timing and intensity levels, and includes diagnostics to monitor the beacon.

Q2What does a beacon controller control?

For rotating beacons it holds rotation at 12–15 RPM (24–30 flashes per minute) through a variable frequency drive or phase control. For flashing beacons it generates the timing pulses for the flash pattern, typically 20–40 flashes per minute. It also sequences colours, such as alternating green and white, and switches intensity steps.

Q3What inputs does a beacon controller use?

It takes day/night input from a photocell, timer or astronomical clock, plus manual switches or remote commands from a system such as ALCMS. Communication interfaces include RS-485, Ethernet, fibre optic and GPS input.

Q4Where is a beacon controller installed?

It is housed indoors or in a weatherproof shelter, as close as practical to the beacon it manages. Typical locations are the base of the tower or mast, a dedicated equipment room, or a wind farm's central control building, wall- or rack-mounted with clear maintenance access.

Q5How do beacon controllers synchronise multiple beacons?

Advanced controllers can manage several beacons at once, for example across a wind farm or a group of towers. GPS input keeps flashes synchronised, and an internal oscillator acts as the backup timing source if the GPS signal is lost.

Q6What supply and outputs does a beacon controller need?

It typically runs on 230 V AC ±10% at 50/60 Hz, or a 24 V DC variant, with MCB/MCCB protection. The output is a variable frequency drive for the motor or PWM for an LED array. Surge protective devices on the supply and communication lines are recommended because panels are often exposed.

Q7Which standards apply to beacon controllers?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 for beacon operation and control, FAA AC 150/5345-12 for the beacon controller specification, and IEEE C62.41 for surge voltages in low-voltage AC circuits. Always verify the current edition and your project's approved requirements.

Q8What faults does a beacon controller detect?

It detects lamp failure, motor overload and loss of synchronisation, and flags rotation or flash timing drift beyond tolerance as a discrete fault. These are logged so they can be reviewed before they cause a beacon outage.

Q9How often should a beacon controller be maintained?

Typical intervals are monthly for fault log review, semi-annual for GPS antenna and sync verification, communication link tests and intensity step verification, and annual for power supply and surge protection checks and rotation and flash rate calibration. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is a beacon flash rate incorrect or inconsistent?

Likely causes are voltage fluctuation at the controller input, an out-of-calibration timing circuit, or a downstream drive motor or mechanical fault that is being misread as a controller problem. Monitor the incoming supply for sags and surges and check the motor and bearings before recalibrating the controller.
