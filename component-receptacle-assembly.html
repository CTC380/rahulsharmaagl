Receptacle Assembly

The complete equipment‑mounted receptacle (female) half of the L‑823 connector system, comprising the connector body with external threads, contact sockets, mounting flange, and sealing gasket. Pre‑assembled and ready for panel or bulkhead installation, it provides a secure, waterproof interface for cable‑mounted plugs.

Overview

What Is It?

The Receptacle Assembly is the complete, ready‑to‑install equipment‑mounted half of the L‑823 two‑pin waterproof connector system used in airfield ground lighting (AGL) primary series circuits. It is a factory‑assembled unit that includes all components required for panel or bulkhead mounting: the connector body with external threads, two gold‑ or tin‑plated brass socket contacts, a mounting flange with screw holes, an elastomeric sealing gasket, and internal O‑ring seals.

The receptacle assembly is designed to be mounted on isolating transformers, junction boxes, CCR output panels, and other inline equipment. Once installed and mated with the corresponding plug assembly (cable‑mounted), it forms an IP68 submersible, low‑resistance connection that withstands the harsh airfield environment, including moisture, temperature extremes, and mechanical vibration.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The receptacle assembly's purpose is to give the fixed, equipment-mounted side of a primary circuit connection a mating point that stays watertight through years of maintenance disconnects and reconnects, not just at the moment of installation. Because it lives permanently on the transformer or junction box while the plug side is disturbed each time a fixture is serviced, its internal seals and socket contacts have to tolerate repeated mating cycles without the connection degrading, which is why it is supplied as a factory-assembled unit rather than built up from separate parts on site. Getting this half of the connection right removes one variable from every future maintenance visit to that piece of equipment.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The receptacle assembly is used throughout the airfield wherever primary circuit terminations are required on equipment, including:

  • Isolating Transformer Inputs: The primary side of every isolating transformer is fitted with a receptacle assembly that accepts the plug assembly from the series cable.
  • CCR Output Panels: The output terminals of Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) are often equipped with receptacle assemblies for field cable connection.
  • Junction Boxes and Splice Enclosures: Junction boxes use receptacle assemblies to provide plug‑in access points for incoming and outgoing primary cables.
  • Test Points and Disconnect Panels: Receptacle assemblies are used on test panels and disconnect boxes to facilitate circuit isolation and testing.
  • Transition and Distribution Boxes: At underground‑to‑above‑ground transitions, receptacle assemblies provide a standardized interface for cable connections.

The receptacle assembly is available in panel‑mount (with a flange and gasket for bulkhead installation) and cable‑mount (with a pigtail) configurations. It is rated for continuous operation at 6.6A or 20A primary current.

Placement

Installation & Location

The receptacle assembly is mounted directly onto the equipment it serves — an isolating transformer housing, junction box wall, or CCR output panel — using its flange and gasket to create a sealed, bulkhead-style penetration through the enclosure wall. Mounting screws are tightened evenly to compress the gasket fully, since an unevenly seated flange is a common path for moisture to bypass the seal even when the connector itself is intact. Internal wiring from the receptacle's socket contacts back to the equipment's terminals is completed before the enclosure is closed up, and the assembly is positioned on the enclosure so the mating plug can be connected and disconnected without obstruction from nearby equipment or cable runs. Because the receptacle stays in place for the working life of the equipment, its mounting location is chosen to keep the mating face accessible for the many connect/disconnect cycles it will see over that time.

Function

How Does It Work?

The receptacle assembly provides a secure, low‑resistance electrical interface for the primary circuit while enabling rapid connection and disconnection to cable‑mounted plugs. Its functional components work together as follows:

  • Socket Contacts: Two machined brass sockets (female) with gold or tin plating provide the electrical interface. The sockets are designed to receive the plug's pin contacts with consistent contact force, ensuring low‑resistance, gas‑tight connections. The sockets are typically crimped or soldered to the equipment's internal wiring.
  • Mounting Flange: A flange with screw holes allows the receptacle to be securely mounted to the equipment panel or bulkhead. The flange is designed to accommodate a sealing gasket that prevents moisture ingress between the connector and the mounting surface.
  • External Threads: The connector body features external threads that accept the plug's coupling ring. When tightened to the recommended torque (3.5–5.5 N·m), the ring compresses the internal O‑rings, forming a watertight seal and locking the connection against vibration and accidental disconnection.
  • Elastomeric Seals: Multiple O‑rings (silicone or EPDM) provide IP68 sealing when the connector is fully mated. The flange gasket provides additional sealing at the mounting interface.
  • Insulating Insert: A high‑dielectric insert (typically glass‑filled nylon or similar) holds the socket contacts in position and provides electrical insulation between them and the connector shell.

When properly installed with the recommended torque, the receptacle assembly provides a connection that can remain submerged in water for extended periods without degradation. Its low contact resistance ensures minimal voltage drop and heating, critical for maintaining the performance of the constant‑current series circuit.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The receptacle assembly is the equipment-mounted half of an inline connection in the series circuit loop, receiving a mating plug and carrying the loop's full commanded current through its socket contacts into the fixture's internal wiring. It is not individually addressable — the ALCMS and CCR regulate the loop as a single unit and have no direct visibility into any receptacle on its own. When properly mated and torqued, the joint is electrically transparent to the control system. Over time, socket wear, a compromised flange gasket, or a compressed O-ring raises contact resistance at that point, and because the CCR holds loop current constant rather than voltage, that added resistance shows up as increased loop voltage at the commanded current step. A receptacle that no longer seals or seats correctly is reported by the CCR as an open-circuit or ground-fault loop condition, indistinguishable at the control level from any other break, and confirming the receptacle as the cause still requires physical inspection of that mounting point in the field.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Receptacle Assembly (mated to plug) → Fixture Wiring (with loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

As the equipment‑mounted half of the connector pair, the receptacle assembly is built around a moulded or cast body carrying two gold‑ or tin‑plated brass socket contacts, with a mounting flange moulded or bolted to the shell so the unit can be fixed to a transformer case, junction box wall, or panel with standard screws. An elastomeric sealing gasket sits between the flange and the mounting surface to prevent water tracking in behind the receptacle, while a separate set of internal O‑rings seals the mating face where the plug's coupling ring threads on. Internal wiring from the sockets is terminated to the equipment's own internal conductors during manufacture or panel assembly, so the receptacle presents only its external threaded face and socket contacts to the field technician making the connection. Because it is fixed in place rather than field‑terminated, the receptacle is built to withstand many mate/unmate cycles from the plug side without wear affecting the seal or contact resistance.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Socket Contact Cleaning
Annual

Clean socket contacts with contact cleaner and reapply dielectric grease to prevent corrosion-driven resistance increase.

Contact Spring Tension Check
Annual

Check socket contact spring tension and replace receptacles with reduced retention force, which otherwise leads to overheating and intermittent connections.

O-Ring & Gasket Inspection
Annual

Check O-rings and flange gaskets for hardening or cracking and replace on schedule, applying silicone grease to extend life.

External Thread Inspection
Annual

Inspect external threads for cross-threading damage and keep protective caps on when the receptacle is not mated.

Mounting Hardware Torque Check
Annual

Re-torque mounting screws and confirm lock washers or thread-locking compound are in place, since vibration can loosen hardware and compromise the flange seal.

Contact Alignment Check
Annual

Check socket contacts for bending or damage from improper plug insertion, and replace receptacles with damaged contacts.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Socket Contacts

Despite plating, sockets can corrode in saltwater or chemically aggressive environments, increasing contact resistance. Regular inspection and cleaning with contact cleaner, along with application of dielectric grease, are recommended.

Loss of Contact Spring Tension

Over time and with repeated mating cycles, the socket contacts can lose spring tension, reducing contact force and increasing resistance. This can cause overheating and intermittent connections. Replace receptacles showing reduced contact retention.

O‑Ring and Gasket Degradation

O‑rings and flange gaskets can harden, crack, or lose elasticity due to UV exposure, temperature cycles, or chemical attack. Replace seals periodically and use silicone grease to prolong their life.

Cross‑Threading of External Threads

Cross‑threading of the connector's external threads can make mating with the plug coupling ring difficult or impossible. Protect threads with caps when not in use and inspect before mating.

Moisture Ingress via Mounting Interface

If the flange gasket is damaged or missing, moisture can enter the equipment housing through the mounting interface. Ensure the gasket is in good condition and the mounting screws are properly torqued.

Damaged or Bent Socket Contacts

Improper insertion of the plug can bend or damage the socket contacts. Use alignment guides and insert the plug straight to avoid damage. Replace receptacles with damaged contacts.

Loose Mounting Hardware

Vibration can loosen the mounting screws, compromising the seal and mechanical stability. Use lock washers or thread‑locking compound and periodically check torque.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

High Resistance or Intermittent Contact at the Socket
Likely Cause
Corroded socket contacts, despite plating, from saltwater or chemically aggressive environments, increasing contact resistance.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure contact resistance across the mated connection; visually inspect sockets for corrosion, pitting, or discolouration.
Corrective Action
Clean corroded contacts with contact cleaner and apply dielectric grease, or replace the receptacle if corrosion has pitted the plating.
Overheating or Intermittent Connection After Repeated Mating
Likely Cause
The socket contacts have lost spring tension over time and repeated mating cycles, reducing contact force and increasing resistance.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect contacts for visibly reduced spring tension or looseness when a plug is mated; measure contact resistance and compare against acceptance value.
Corrective Action
Replace the receptacle if reduced contact retention is confirmed — spring tension cannot be restored in the field.
Water Ingress at the Receptacle
Likely Cause
O-ring or flange gasket hardening, cracking, or loss of elasticity from UV exposure, temperature cycling, or chemical attack.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the O-ring and flange gasket for hardening, cracking, or flattening; check for moisture inside the receptacle body once opened.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded seals with new ones lubricated with silicone grease, and re-verify the seal before returning to service.
Plug Will Not Mate or Seat Fully
Likely Cause
Cross-threading of the receptacle's external threads is preventing proper engagement with the plug's coupling ring.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the external threads for visible cross-threading or deformation before forcing another mating attempt.
Corrective Action
Replace the receptacle if threads are damaged; protect threads with a cap when the receptacle is not in use.
Moisture Ingress via the Mounting Interface
Likely Cause
The flange gasket between the receptacle and equipment housing is damaged or missing, or the mounting screws were not properly torqued.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the flange gasket for damage or gaps; check mounting screw torque against specification.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged flange gasket and re-torque mounting screws to specification, then re-verify the seal is intact.
Fixture or Equipment Dark Due to Damaged Socket Contacts
Likely Cause
Improper insertion of the plug, without alignment, has bent or damaged the socket contacts.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the socket contacts visually for bending or damage; test continuity through the mated connection.
Corrective Action
Replace the receptacle if contacts are bent or damaged, and use alignment guides for straight insertion on re-installation.
Receptacle Loose or Seal Compromised
Likely Cause
Vibration has loosened the mounting hardware over time, compromising both the mechanical stability and the seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Check mounting screw torque against specification; look for visible movement or a broken seal around the mounting flange.
Corrective Action
Re-torque mounting hardware to specification using lock washers or thread-locking compound, and re-verify the seal.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Mating or Unmating
Electrical Safety

Never insert or remove a plug from a receptacle on a live series circuit; de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy before mating or servicing the connection.

Aligned Insertion
Workmanship

Align the plug squarely with the receptacle before inserting; forcing a misaligned plug bends or damages the socket contacts and can leave the circuit open at that point.

Mounting Hardware Torque
Workmanship

Torque the receptacle's mounting screws to specification; loosened hardware compromises both the mechanical mounting and the flange seal against moisture.

Seal Inspection
Workmanship

Inspect the O-ring and flange gasket for hardening or cracking before mating or remounting; a compromised seal invites water ingress through either the mating face or the mounting interface.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Check contact condition and continuity through the mated connection before restoring power; a marginal connection found after re-energisation can trip the CCR or leave the equipment dark.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on the receptacle, and confirm bonding/grounding is intact per the electrical design before reconnecting.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a receptacle loose, with damaged contacts, or with a suspect seal; verify the connection and confirm mounting integrity before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Socket Contact Damage From a Forced Mating Isn't Always Visible
Troubleshooting

A receptacle mated with a misaligned plug can have its socket contacts bent or splayed slightly without obvious external damage, creating an intermittent or high-resistance connection that only shows up under load; inspect contact alignment specifically after any report of a forced or difficult mating.

Mounting Hardware Torque Affects the Seal, Not Just the Fixing
Mechanical

Under-torqued mounting screws on a flange-mounted receptacle compromise the flange gasket's seal even if the receptacle feels mechanically secure by hand; torque to specification rather than "snug," since the seal depends on even, correct clamping force.

Flange Gasket and Mating-Face O-Ring Age at Different Rates
Durability

The static flange gasket and the dynamic mating-face O-ring on a receptacle see different stresses and can degrade on different timelines; inspect both independently at service rather than assuming one's condition tells you about the other.

Verify Continuity Through the Mated Pair, Not Just at the Receptacle Alone
Electrical

Testing a receptacle's contacts in isolation can miss a fault that only appears once a specific plug is mated to it; verify continuity with the actual mating plug in place before closing out work.

Cross-Threaded or Forced Mounting Damages Threads Invisibly
Workmanship

Forcing a receptacle into a slightly misaligned mounting hole can strip or gall threads that still appear to hold the fastener; a receptacle that has ever been force-mounted is worth a thread inspection at the next service rather than assumed sound.

Log Recurring Faults by Receptacle Location, Not Just by Fixture
Compliance

Because receptacle-side faults often trace back to a specific mounting or environmental issue at that location rather than the hardware itself, tracking faults by physical location can reveal a site-specific cause a component-by-component log would miss.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the receptacle assembly: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a receptacle assembly?

The receptacle assembly is the complete, ready-to-install equipment-mounted half of the L-823 two-pin waterproof connector system used in AGL primary series circuits. It is factory assembled with a threaded connector body, two gold- or tin-plated brass socket contacts, a mounting flange, a sealing gasket and internal O-rings.

Q2What is the difference between a receptacle assembly and a plug assembly?

The receptacle is the equipment-mounted half with socket (female) contacts, mounted on items such as isolating transformers, junction boxes and CCR output panels. The plug is the cable-mounted half that mates with it. Mated, they form an IP68 submersible, low-resistance connection.

Q3Where are receptacle assemblies used?

Wherever primary circuit terminations are needed on equipment, including the primary side of isolating transformers, CCR output panels and junction boxes.

Q4What are the typical ratings?

Rated continuous current is 6.6 A or 20 A depending on variant, at up to 5 kV transient and 600 V continuous. Initial contact resistance is 0.5 mΩ or less per contact pair, protection is IP68 when mated, and gold-plated contacts are typically rated for at least 500 mating cycles.

Q5What insulation resistance and torque apply?

Mated insulation resistance should be at least 50 MΩ, measured connector-to-ground with a megger. The recommended coupling ring torque is 3.5 N·m to 5.5 N·m.

Q6What materials are used?

Contacts are machined brass with gold or tin plating. The shell is brass, bronze or UV-stabilised polyamide, the flange is brass or stainless steel, and seals are silicone or EPDM O-rings and gaskets. The operating range is -40°C to +85°C.

Q7Which standards apply to receptacle assemblies?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-26E (L-823 plug and receptacle connectors, where L-823 is the equipment type designation rather than the AC number), FAA AC 150/5345-53 (equipment certification) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a receptacle assembly be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover socket contact cleaning, contact spring tension, O-ring and gasket condition, external threads, mounting hardware torque and contact alignment. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9How does a faulty receptacle show up on the circuit?

Poor mating or moisture ingress shows up as a CCR ground fault or open circuit. Symptoms at the receptacle include high resistance or intermittent contact, water ingress, a plug that will not seat, or equipment that is dark due to damaged socket contacts.

Q10What are the common problems with receptacle assemblies?

Common problems are corrosion of socket contacts, loss of contact spring tension, O-ring and gasket degradation, cross-threading of external threads, moisture ingress through the mounting interface, bent socket contacts and loose mounting hardware.
