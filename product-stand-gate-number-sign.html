Product Overview

Stand / Gate Number Sign

What It Is

Stand and gate number signs give clear visual identification of aircraft parking positions, terminal gates and designated apron locations, helping pilots, ground handlers, airline staff, maintenance teams and operations staff identify the correct position.

They are configured around the apron layout, stand numbering system, character size, viewing distance, illumination, mounting and environmental conditions, as non-illuminated, retroreflective or illuminated (LED) signs.

Five Principal Components

  • Legend — stand number, gate number, letter or identifier
  • Sign face — high-contrast identification surface
  • Illumination — optional internal or external lighting
  • Support structure — posts, brackets, frame
  • Electrical interface — where illuminated

Designed for weather, engine airflow, vehicle movement, ground-service activity and frequent cleaning.

FAQ

Stand / Gate Number Sign — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a stand or gate number sign?

Stand and gate number signs give clear visual identification of aircraft parking positions, terminal gates and designated apron locations, helping pilots, ground handlers, airline staff, maintenance teams and operations staff identify the correct position. They can be non-illuminated, retroreflective or illuminated (LED), and have five principal components: the legend, sign face, optional illumination, support structure and, where illuminated, the electrical interface.

Where are stand and gate signs used?

They are used at aircraft parking and contact stands, terminal gates (passenger boarding positions), remote stands and apron positions, cargo and maintenance stands, general aviation parking areas, and turnaround, stand guidance and positioning areas. Coordinate them with the airport's approved stand numbering and apron management plan.

Does ICAO set the legend and colours for stand and gate signs?

ICAO Annex 14 addresses aircraft stand identification mainly through apron markings. For stand and gate signs, the airport's approved signage plan sets the legend format, colours, character height and mounting height. The legend must match the approved stand numbering system exactly, and should be confirmed before manufacture to avoid inconsistencies with operational databases, drawings, pavement markings and terminal information systems.

How are character size and colour chosen?

Character size depends on aircraft cockpit and vehicle-operator viewing distance, stand geometry and sign location, apron lighting, character contrast, required recognition time and airport requirements. A sign for aircraft crews may need a different configuration from a small board for ground-service personnel. Colour is selected according to the sign function and airport signage plan, the applicable standard, day and night visibility, the illumination method and surrounding apron lighting, with clear legend-to-background contrast and consistent colour and typography across the stand-sign system.

When should a stand sign be illuminated, and what does LED offer?

Illuminated signs help during night operations and reduced visibility, poor weather, high apron-lighting environments and turnaround operations after dark, with sufficient contrast and no excessive glare. LEDs offer long service life, efficient operation, consistent output, rapid switching, reduced maintenance and compatibility with modern control systems. Evaluate the completed sign as an assembly, since dark areas reduce readability and LED placement, diffusion, sign depth and viewing angle all affect uniformity.

What electrical arrangements do illuminated stand signs need?

Consider the supply voltage, AC/DC configuration, local switching or central control, dedicated sign circuits and power supply location, together with surge protection, earthing and bonding, cable entry and maintenance isolation. The arrangement depends on the existing apron infrastructure and project requirements.

How are stand signs mounted, and must they be frangible?

Mounting options include single- or two-post mounting, ground-mounted frames, bracket-mounted signs, terminal-building mounting, fixed apron structures and project-specific support assemblies. Review clearance from wings, engines, wingtips, tail surfaces, jet bridges, ground-support equipment, service vehicles and passenger boarding routes, and establish the final location and height from the approved apron layout. Where a sign is in an area of aircraft or vehicle movement, evaluate the enclosure, posts, base, fasteners, breakaway interfaces, foundation and cable routing against airport safety and frangibility requirements.

What conditions are stand signs designed for?

They are designed for weather, engine airflow, vehicle movement, ground-service activity and frequent cleaning, including rain, dust, UV, temperature variation, humidity, wind, cleaning chemicals, fuel and fluid exposure, vibration and corrosive environments. Coastal or corrosive sites may need additional corrosion-resistant materials or finishes.

How do stand signs coordinate with apron markings and guidance lights?

Coordinate with stand identification and centreline markings, lead-in and lead-out lines, aircraft stop markings, apron taxiway markings, apron stand guidance lights, docking guidance systems and direction signs. Position signs so they do not interfere with stand guidance lighting or visual positioning equipment, so the combined system presents a logical sequence from apron route to stand entry, stand identification and aircraft positioning.

What is checked at commissioning, and how are stand signs maintained?

Commissioning covers the correct identifier, position and orientation, character visibility and sign-face condition, illumination operation and uniformity, mounting stability, electrical integrity and cable condition, and aircraft and ground-service clearance, with a night-time inspection recommended for illuminated installations. Routine maintenance covers cleaning the sign face, inspecting lettering, checking illumination, LED modules and power supplies, inspecting electrical connections and cable entries, and checking mounting hardware, supports, corrosion and physical damage.

What are common problems with stand and gate signs?

Poor night-time visibility can come from LED failure, power-supply problems, a dirty face, degraded optics, excessive surrounding light or an incorrect illumination level. An incorrect stand number points to an outdated legend, to be checked against the latest approved layout and stand-management documentation. Poor sign position can result from aircraft parking geometry, ground-support equipment, jet bridges, vehicles, temporary structures or apron layout changes. Physical damage and corrosion are other typical issues, and orientation matters where a stand number must be read from a specific aircraft approach direction.

What information is needed to request or replace a stand sign?

Provide the airport or project name, stand or gate identifier, quantity, sign location, viewing direction, character size, approximate dimensions, illumination requirement, electrical supply, mounting arrangement, foundation information, aircraft-clearance and frangibility requirements, environmental conditions, applicable airport standard and required documentation. For replacement or retrofit, existing installations can be assessed for current stand numbering, sign dimensions, mounting structures, electrical supply, illumination condition, foundation condition, clearance and compatibility with updated airport signage, with replacement signs designed around existing infrastructure where practical.

Which standards apply to stand and gate signs?

Potential references include ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I (apron markings and signs) with Doc 9157 Part 4, applicable FAA airport signage and visual-aid requirements, national civil aviation authority requirements, airport-specific apron design standards, tender specifications and approved signage drawings, and apron layout and aircraft stand drawings. Verify the legend, colour, character size, illumination, mounting height, location and frangibility against the current applicable standard before manufacture and installation.
