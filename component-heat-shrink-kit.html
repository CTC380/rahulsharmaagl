Heat Shrink Kit

A specialised kit containing a selection of heat‑shrink tubing and accessories used for insulating, sealing, and protecting cable joints, terminations, and splices in AGL primary and secondary circuits. Designed for field installation, it provides reliable moisture protection and electrical insulation in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Heat Shrink Kit is a collection of heat‑shrinkable tubing and associated components specifically selected for use in airfield ground lighting (AGL) cable jointing, termination, and repair applications. The kit typically includes a range of tubing diameters and colours, as well as adhesive liners, end caps, and sometimes a heat gun (or instructions for its use).

Heat‑shrink tubing is a polymer tube that shrinks radially (and often longitudinally) when heated, forming a tight, protective layer over cables, joints, and connectors. Dual‑wall tubing with an internal adhesive liner provides a watertight seal, making it ideal for buried and submerged AGL installations. The kit is designed to meet the demanding performance requirements of airfield environments, including resistance to moisture, temperature extremes, UV exposure, and mechanical stress.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

A heat shrink kit exists to restore, at a joint or termination, the same level of insulation and moisture protection the cable had straight from the factory, since a splice is only as reliable as its weakest point of coverage. The dual-wall design with an internal adhesive liner is what makes this possible below ground: as the outer wall shrinks and the liner melts and flows around the conductor and any surface irregularities, the result is a continuous seal rather than a wrap that simply sits on top of the joint. Without that seal, moisture ingress at a buried termination is one of the more common and hardest-to-diagnose causes of intermittent circuit faults on an AGL system, since the fault can appear and disappear with soil moisture conditions long after installation.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The heat shrink kit is used across the airfield for a variety of cable and connector applications, including:

  • Cable Splicing: Insulating and sealing joints in primary and secondary AGL cables, both underground and in pits.
  • Connector Termination: Providing stress relief and moisture sealing at the cable entry points of L‑823 connectors and other terminations.
  • Fixture Wiring: Protecting and insulating connections inside light fixtures, junction boxes, and signs.
  • Repair and Maintenance: Quick, reliable insulation repair of damaged cable insulation in the field.
  • Colour Coding: Using different colour tubing to identify cable phases, circuits, or function (e.g., primary vs. secondary).

The kit is suitable for use with both copper and aluminium conductors, and the tubing is compatible with common cable insulations such as XLPE, PVC, and EPR. It is designed for installation by trained personnel using a standard heat gun or gas torch with a suitable diffuser.

Placement

Installation & Location

The kit is applied at the location of a cable splice or termination — a below-grade joint pit, a handhole, a junction box, or inside a light fixture base — with the tubing selected to suit the diameter of the completed splice before any of it is heated. Work is generally carried out in dry conditions or with the joint temporarily shielded from moisture, since the adhesive liner needs a clean, dry surface to bond and seal properly. The tubing is positioned to fully overlap the joint and a reasonable length of the original cable insulation on each side, then shrunk evenly from the centre outward using a heat gun or gas torch, so the adhesive flows around the joint's irregular profile rather than leaving voids. Once shrunk, the joint is generally allowed to cool before being placed in its final position — in a splice enclosure, buried trench, or junction box — so the sealed section isn't flexed or stressed while the adhesive is still warm and pliable.

Function

How Does It Work?

Heat‑shrink tubing works by applying heat to cause the polymer material to shrink and conform tightly to the underlying cable or joint. The kit provides the necessary components and facilitates the following functions:

  • Electrical Insulation: The tubing provides a high‑dielectric‑strength insulating layer over conductors, joints, and terminations, preventing short circuits and leakage.
  • Moisture Sealing: Dual‑wall tubing with an internal hot‑melt adhesive liner melts and flows during heating, creating a watertight seal that prevents moisture ingress—critical for buried and pit installations.
  • Mechanical Protection: The shrunk tubing provides a tough, abrasion‑resistant layer that protects against mechanical damage, vibration, and environmental stress.
  • Strain Relief: The tight fit of the tubing provides strain relief at cable entries, preventing conductor fatigue and breakage.
  • Colour Identification: Coloured tubing allows for easy identification of circuit phase, function, or cable type, aiding in maintenance and troubleshooting.

The typical installation process involves selecting the correct tubing size (pre‑shrink diameter must be larger than the cable, and the final diameter must be smaller), sliding it over the cable or joint, applying heat evenly with a heat gun until the tubing shrinks and the adhesive flows, and allowing it to cool to form a permanent seal.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Heat-shrink tubing carries no current at all — it is insulation, not conductor — so it has no direct place in the ALCMS/CCR control chain and no way to signal its own condition to the series circuit. Its function is to protect the loop's insulation resistance at the joint or termination it covers, and it is that insulation resistance, not the tubing itself, that the wider system depends on. A failed or poorly formed shrink seal does not present as a loop current or voltage anomaly on the CCR in the way a broken conductor would; instead it slowly admits moisture into the joint beneath it, which degrades insulation resistance over time and can eventually manifest as a nuisance earth-fault trip or increased leakage current, well after the original installation. Because the CCR's real-time loop monitoring is built around current and voltage rather than insulation resistance, a compromised heat-shrink seal is normally only caught through scheduled offline insulation resistance testing (megger testing) of the loop, not through anything the ALCMS reports during normal operation.

Construction

Physical Construction

The core component of the kit, the heat‑shrink tubing itself, is manufactured as a dual‑wall extrusion: an outer wall of radiation cross‑linked polyolefin (or PVDF/elastomeric compound for higher‑temperature duty) that is expanded to several times its finished diameter during manufacture and "remembers" its smaller original size once reheated, and an inner wall of thermoplastic adhesive that remains solid at ambient temperature but melts and flows once the outer wall shrinks around it. When a heat gun or torch is applied on installation, the outer wall contracts radially (and to a lesser extent longitudinally) to conform tightly to the cable, connector, or joint underneath, while the melted inner adhesive is forced into any surface irregularities and bonds to the substrate, producing a continuous watertight seal rather than a simple mechanical wrap. The kit is supplied as a range of tubing diameters and pre‑cut lengths to match different cable and joint sizes, together with matching heat‑shrink end caps for sealing spare cores, and is packaged with basic application instructions since correct, even heating is what determines whether the finished seal performs to specification.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Tubing Size Selection

Using tubing with an insufficient shrink ratio or diameter will not provide a tight seal and may crack. Always select tubing with a pre‑shrink diameter that comfortably fits over the cable or joint, and a final diameter that is smaller than the cable after shrinking.

Uneven Heating

Heating one area too much or too little can cause incomplete shrinkage, charring, or even melting of the tubing. Use a heat gun with a diffuser and move it evenly around the tubing, rotating the cable if possible.

Insufficient Adhesive Flow

For dual‑wall tubing, if the adhesive does not fully melt and flow, the seal will not be watertight. Ensure the tubing is heated to the manufacturer's recommended temperature for the adhesive to flow (visible as a bead at the ends).

Contamination of Surface

Dirt, oil, or moisture on the cable can prevent the tubing from adhering properly, especially with adhesive‑lined tubing. Clean the cable with isopropyl alcohol and allow it to dry before applying the tubing.

Mechanical Damage During Installation

Sharp edges on connectors or cable lugs can cut or puncture the tubing during installation. Deburr sharp edges and use protective layers (e.g., tape) over sharp points before applying heat‑shrink.

Using a Flame Instead of a Heat Gun

Open flames (e.g., from a gas torch) can easily overheat the tubing, causing charring, weakening, or burning. Always use a heat gun with temperature control or a torch with a large diffuser, and keep the heat source moving.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Tubing Splits, Cracks, or Fails to Fully Recover
Likely Cause
Tubing with an insufficient shrink ratio or an oversized pre-shrink diameter was selected for the cable or joint, leaving the material over-stressed as it shrinks.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure the cable/joint diameter and compare against the tubing's rated pre-shrink and recovered diameters on the product datasheet; inspect the failed section for stress cracking.
Corrective Action
Remove and replace with correctly sized tubing that comfortably fits the joint before shrinking and recovers smaller than the cable OD; re-apply heat evenly per the manufacturer's procedure.
Charring, Scorching, or Melting of the Tubing Surface
Likely Cause
Uneven or excessive heat application — often from holding a heat gun or torch too close in one spot, or using an open flame instead of a controlled heat source.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the scorched area for discolouration or thinning; review the technician's heating technique and confirm the heat source and diffuser being used.
Corrective Action
Cut away and replace the damaged tubing section, and re-apply using a heat gun with a diffuser (or a torch with a large diffuser tip), keeping the heat source moving evenly around the joint.
No Adhesive Bead Visible at Tubing Ends After Shrinking
Likely Cause
The dual-wall tubing was not heated to the manufacturer's specified temperature long enough for the internal adhesive liner to fully melt and flow.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect both ends of the shrunk tubing for the expected adhesive bead; if absent, check the heat gun's temperature setting and the dwell time used against the manufacturer's instructions.
Corrective Action
Reheat the joint to the correct temperature until adhesive flow is visible at both ends, or remove and reapply a new length of tubing if the liner has already been damaged.
Water Ingress or Poor Adhesion Found at a Sealed Joint
Likely Cause
Dirt, oil, or moisture was present on the cable surface at the time of installation, preventing the adhesive-lined tubing from bonding properly.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the joint for gaps or lifting at the tubing-to-cable interface; check installation records for surface preparation steps taken.
Corrective Action
Cut back and remove the compromised tubing, clean the cable thoroughly with isopropyl alcohol, allow it to fully dry, and reapply a new heat-shrink kit.
Tubing Punctured or Torn During or Shortly After Installation
Likely Cause
A sharp edge on a connector, cable lug, or crimp was not deburred or protected before the tubing was applied, cutting into the material.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the suspected sharp edge and the tear pattern on the tubing to confirm the damage point.
Corrective Action
Deburr or file the sharp edge, wrap it with a protective layer (e.g., tape) if it cannot be fully smoothed, and reapply a new length of heat-shrink tubing.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Heat Source Handling
Personnel Safety

Use a controlled heat gun or torch rather than an open flame where the manufacturer does not call for one, keep the flame or air stream moving to avoid scorching skin or nearby cable, and allow the tubing to cool before handling.

De-Energise Before Shrinking Over a Live Joint
Electrical Safety

Confirm the cable or joint underneath is de-energised and locked out before applying heat; never attempt to shrink tubing onto an energised series-circuit connection.

Fire & Ventilation
Operational

Keep the heat source clear of dry vegetation, fuel, or other flammable material, and work in a ventilated area since heated tubing can give off fumes.

Correct Sizing & Deburred Edges
Workmanship

Select tubing sized to the manufacturer's rated pre-shrink and recovered diameters for the joint, and deburr or pad any sharp edge on a connector or lug before applying the tubing to prevent puncture.

Surface Preparation
Workmanship

Clean and dry the cable surface before applying the tubing, since dirt, oil, or moisture will prevent the adhesive liner from bonding and sealing properly.

Inspect Before Closing Out
Electrical Safety

Check both tubing ends for a visible adhesive bead and confirm full, even shrinkage with no scorching or gaps before backfilling or re-energising the circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a joint with incomplete tubing coverage, visible cracking, or an unsealed end; reapply a fresh length of tubing rather than patching a damaged section.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Uneven Heating Leaves Invisible Weak Points
Workmanship

A tubing section shrunk too quickly from one side can trap air pockets or leave the adhesive liner unevenly melted on the far side, defects that aren't visible once the tubing has fully recovered; work the heat source evenly around the full circumference.

Undersized Tubing Looks Fine Until It Doesn't
Workmanship

Tubing sized too close to the connector's outer diameter can fully recover with little adhesive squeeze-out, giving the appearance of a good shrink while leaving too thin an adhesive layer to seal reliably long-term.

Multi-Layer Builds Need Overlap, Not Just Coverage
Design

Where a joint needs more than one length of tubing to build up insulation thickness, each layer should overlap the previous one's shrunk edge; butting layers edge-to-edge instead of overlapping creates a seam that's a moisture entry point.

A Cracked Adhesive Bead Predicts Failure Before Water Gets In
Troubleshooting

Hairline cracking in the visible adhesive bead at a tubing end is often the earliest external sign of an aging or improperly applied seal; treat a cracked bead found on inspection as a reason to redo the joint, not just note it for next time.

Wind Affects Torch Work More Than Heat Guns
Operational

An open flame torch used outdoors in wind can apply heat unevenly or blow out mid-shrink, leaving a partially recovered section that looks complete from a distance; use a heat gun with a controlled air stream where wind conditions are marginal, or shield the work area.

Record the Batch or Lot of Tubing Used at Each Joint
Compliance

Heat-shrink materials can vary slightly in adhesive performance between manufacturing lots; logging which batch was used at each joint helps trace a cluster of early failures back to a specific lot rather than assuming a workmanship issue.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about heat shrink kits: how they work, installation, specifications, standards and common mistakes.

Q1What is an AGL heat shrink kit?

A heat shrink kit is a collection of heat-shrinkable tubing and accessories for insulating, sealing and protecting cable joints, terminations and splices in AGL primary and secondary circuits. It typically includes a range of tubing sizes and colours, adhesive liners and end caps.

Q2How does heat-shrink tubing work?

Heating makes the polymer tube shrink radially and conform tightly to the cable or joint. Dual-wall tubing has an internal hot-melt adhesive liner that melts and flows during heating, forming a watertight seal that cools into a permanent layer.

Q3What does heat shrink provide on an AGL joint?

Electrical insulation, moisture sealing, mechanical protection against abrasion and vibration, strain relief at cable entries, and colour identification of circuits, phases or cable type.

Q4Where is a heat shrink kit used in AGL?

For insulating and sealing joints in primary and secondary cables (underground and in pits), stress relief and sealing at L-823 connector cable entries, fixture and junction box wiring, insulation repair, and colour coding. It works with copper or aluminium conductors and XLPE, PVC and EPR insulations.

Q5How do I install heat-shrink tubing correctly?

Select tubing whose pre-shrink diameter is larger than the cable and whose final diameter is smaller. Clean and dry the surface, slide the tubing into position, heat evenly with a heat gun until it shrinks and the adhesive flows, then let it cool. Check for a visible adhesive bead at both ends.

Q6What are typical heat shrink kit specifications?

Material is cross-linked polyolefin, PVDF or elastomeric, with shrink ratios of 2:1, 3:1 or 4:1. Polyolefin operates continuously from -55°C to +135°C, shrinks at 100 to 130°C, and has dielectric strength of at least 15 kV/mm. Installed protection with adhesive liner is IP68.

Q7What insulation resistance should a sealed joint achieve?

At least 50 MΩ on a megger test across the sealed joint. Tubing is suitable for primary circuits up to 5 kV transient and 600 V continuous. A seal failure shows up as a CCR ground fault at the joint location.

Q8Which standards apply to heat shrink tubing?

The main references are SAE AS23053 (military heat-shrinkable insulation sleeving), UL 224, IEC 60684 (insulating sleeving) and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program). Always verify the current edition.

Q9How often should heat shrink seals be checked?

At installation, verify the adhesive bead and full, even shrinkage. Annually, re-inspect accessible tubing for cracking, UV degradation or mechanical damage, megger-test the sealed joint on the regular schedule, and check stored stock for shelf-life expiry.

Q10What are the common heat shrink mistakes?

Wrong tubing size, uneven heating (causing incomplete shrinkage or charring), insufficient adhesive flow, contaminated cable surfaces, sharp connector edges puncturing the tubing, and using an open flame instead of a temperature-controlled heat gun.
