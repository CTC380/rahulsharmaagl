CCR & Power Distribution

The heart of the AGL electrical system — Constant Current Regulators convert utility power into regulated series-circuit current, while distribution cabinets, switchgear and monitoring systems deliver and protect that power across the airfield.

Overview

What Is It?

Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) are the specialised power supplies that feed AGL series circuits. They take mains AC voltage (typically 415V or 230V) and convert it to a regulated, high-voltage, constant-current output — most commonly 6.6A for standard AGL circuits, or 20A for high-current loops. The CCR maintains this current regardless of load variations, fixture count or cable length, which is the fundamental requirement of a series circuit.

Power distribution encompasses the switchgear, distribution panels, changeover systems and monitoring equipment that sit between the utility supply and the CCRs — ensuring that power is available, selectable, protected and monitored across the entire AGL system.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

CCRs exist because the series-circuit architecture underlying most AGL loops depends on a specific electrical behaviour that ordinary mains supply can't provide on its own: a constant current that stays fixed regardless of how many fixtures are on the loop, how long the cable run is, or how the load shifts as fixtures fail or are added. Without that regulation, every fixture on a loop would receive an inconsistent, load-dependent brightness rather than the precise, repeatable intensity steps pilots and controllers rely on for visual guidance in every visibility condition.

Power distribution around the CCR exists to make sure that regulated current is never at the mercy of a single point of failure in the incoming supply. Changeover systems and monitored switchgear let the airfield fall back to standby power automatically if the primary utility feed is lost, since a lighting outage during an approach or departure is not a delay that can be tolerated the way an outage in an ordinary building might be. Together, the CCR and its supporting distribution infrastructure are what turn an unpredictable utility feed into the dependable, precisely controlled current every fixture on the airfield depends on.

Application

Where Is It Used?

CCRs and distribution equipment are typically housed in a dedicated AGL equipment room or shelter, often at the airfield's electrical centre:

  • CCR Units: Individual regulators for each series circuit — one per runway edge circuit, approach circuit, taxiway circuit, stop-bar circuit, etc.
  • Distribution Panels: Main and sub-distribution boards that feed the CCRs from the utility supply, with circuit breakers and protection devices.
  • Changeover Systems: Automatic or manual changeover switches that allow a CCR to feed alternate circuits in case of failure, or to swap between primary and standby power sources.
  • Monitoring & Control: Remote terminal units (RTUs), supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) interfaces, and local control panels that allow operators to select intensity steps, switch circuits, and monitor circuit health.
  • Standby Power: Diesel generators or UPS systems that maintain AGL operation during mains power failure, connected through the distribution switchgear.
Placement

Installation & Location

CCRs and their associated distribution panels are installed in a dedicated, weatherproof equipment room or shelter positioned close to the circuits they feed — often at or near the airfield's electrical centre — to keep the high-voltage series cable runs to each circuit as short as practical. Each CCR is mounted on its own base or rack within the room with adequate clearance for ventilation and maintenance access, wired on the input side to the distribution panel's protected circuit breakers and on the output side to the series circuit cable leaving the building through a sealed cable entry. Changeover switchgear and standby power connections (generator or UPS) are installed so that a loss of the primary utility feed transfers the CCRs to backup power automatically, with the transfer wiring tested at commissioning to confirm the changeover completes within the time the airfield's operating category allows. Monitoring and control wiring back to the ALCMS or local RTU is run separately from the high-current power cabling to avoid interference, and the room itself is fitted with earthing and surge protection appropriate to a facility that sits at the point where utility power is converted into the high-voltage current feeding every fixture on the airfield.

Function

How Does It Work?

A CCR is essentially a variable transformer (or a solid-state equivalent) with closed-loop current regulation. It senses the actual output current and adjusts the voltage automatically to maintain the setpoint — typically 6.6A RMS, with selectable steps corresponding to different intensity levels (e.g. 2.8A for low intensity, 6.6A for high intensity).

Key functional aspects include:

  • Constant-Current Regulation: The CCR compensates for load impedance changes — whether a fixture fails or a connector develops a high-resistance joint — by adjusting voltage to keep current constant.
  • Selectable Intensity Steps: The air traffic controller or AGL operator selects an intensity step (typically 5 or more steps) which the CCR translates into a corresponding output current level. All fixtures on that circuit brighten or dim together.
  • Fault Protection: The CCR monitors for open-circuit, short-circuit, earth leakage and other fault conditions, tripping and alarming if it detects a hazardous condition.
  • Voltage Compliance: The CCR is designed to deliver up to several thousand volts (typically up to 5kV or more) across the series circuit to overcome cable impedance and maintain current, particularly on long circuits with many fixtures.
  • Remote Control: Modern CCRs are monitored and controlled via a remote control system, allowing the operator to change intensity steps, switch circuits on/off, and read fault status from the control tower or maintenance office.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The CCR's own architecture is a closed-loop feedback controller sitting between the utility supply and the series circuit. Incoming AC power is conditioned and fed through a regulation stage — a variable-ratio transformer or a solid-state converter — that sets the output current; a current sensor on the output continuously measures the actual series-loop current and feeds that reading back to the regulation stage, which adjusts its output voltage to hold the current at the commanded setpoint regardless of how the load impedance changes downstream. The commanded setpoint itself, along with on/off and step selection, is normally driven remotely by the ALCMS rather than set locally, and the CCR reports its own current, voltage and fault status back to that same remote system, making it the executing node in the airfield's wider control hierarchy rather than a self-contained one.

Utility AC → Regulation Stage → Current Sensing Feedback → Series Circuit Output → ALCMS Interface

Power Architecture

From Utility Supply to Runway Fixture

Power path
Standby / control path

Utility mains and standby power (generator or UPS) both terminate at an automatic changeover switch, which guarantees the distribution panel always has a live source. From there, protected feeders reach the CCR, which converts the supply into a regulated constant-current output for the series circuit — so every fixture on the loop sees the same current regardless of position. Remote control / SCADA runs alongside the power path, letting the tower select intensity steps and read circuit health without altering the circuit topology itself.

Construction

Physical Construction

A CCR is built around a power transformer or solid-state inverter core, mounted within a floor-standing or wall-mounted steel cabinet sized to the unit's power rating, with the high-voltage output winding and its associated insulation kept physically separated from the input and control sections to maintain the isolation the series circuit design depends on. Cooling is provided either by natural convection through louvred vents in the cabinet, or by forced-air fans for higher-power units, with the cabinet's internal layout arranged so heat-generating components have clear airflow paths. The control and protection electronics, including current regulation circuitry, fault detection, and any remote communication interface, are mounted on a separate panel or sub-enclosure within the cabinet, wired to the power section through protected cable runs and circuit breakers. The cabinet itself is constructed from corrosion-resistant sheet steel, powder-coated or galvanised, with a lockable door for restricted access and gasket seals around door edges and cable entries to achieve the enclosure's rated ingress protection, since CCRs are typically installed in equipment rooms or outdoor cabinets exposed to airfield weather conditions.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Cooling System Failure

CCRs generate significant heat, particularly magnetic (transformer-based) designs. Fan failure or blocked air filters can cause over-temperature trips, knocking a circuit offline.

Control Board / Logic Failure

Modern solid-state CCRs rely on microprocessor-based control boards that can fail due to power surges, ageing or environmental conditions, requiring board-level replacement.

Contact Wear on Tap-Changers

Older magnetic CCRs use tap-changing mechanisms to select intensity steps. Mechanical wear on the tap contacts is a well-known maintenance item that can cause erratic output or arcing.

Earth Leakage Detection

CCRs are designed to trip on earth leakage (current returning through the ground rather than the intended circuit). Nuisance tripping due to cable insulation breakdown is a common diagnostic challenge.

Remote Control Communication Loss

Loss of communication between the control tower and the CCR room can leave operators unable to change intensity or switch circuits, requiring local override.

Distribution Panel Coordination

Incorrectly coordinated protection devices (circuit breakers, fuses) can lead to unnecessary trips or, worse, a failure to trip when needed, causing equipment damage.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

CCR Fails to Start / No Output
Likely Cause
Upstream breaker tripped or not closed, blown control fuse, a failed start command from the ALCMS/RTU, or an internal control-board fault holding the unit in a lockout state.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm incoming supply voltage at the distribution panel breaker feeding the CCR; check control fuses and the local/remote selector position; review the CCR's fault log and control-board status LEDs for a lockout or alarm code.
Corrective Action
Reset the breaker or replace the control fuse if the supply checks out; clear the lockout via the local panel once the underlying fault is resolved; escalate to board-level replacement if the control board itself is faulty.
CCR Trips on Over-Temperature
Likely Cause
Blocked or dirty air filters, a failed cooling fan, or the CCR being run at a duty cycle or intensity step beyond what its cooling system was designed for.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and clean intake/exhaust filters; confirm the cooling fan runs and moves adequate air; check ambient equipment-room temperature and ventilation; log the internal temperature reading against the manufacturer's trip threshold.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace filters, repair or replace the fan, and improve room ventilation if ambient temperature is contributing; allow the unit to cool fully before resetting and re-energising.
CCR Trips on Earth Leakage / Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Insulation breakdown in the series-circuit cable or a downstream fixture allowing leakage current to return through the ground rather than the loop, or a nuisance trip from a marginal leakage-detection setpoint.
Diagnostic Steps
Isolate the CCR and megger the outgoing series circuit to locate the low-resistance segment; sectionalise the loop to narrow down the faulted cable run or fixture; verify the earth-leakage relay's setpoint against commissioning records.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted cable section, connector or fixture and confirm insulation resistance is restored above the acceptable minimum; recalibrate the leakage relay only if the setpoint itself is confirmed to be out of tolerance.
Output Current Unstable or Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Worn or arcing tap-changer contacts on an older magnetic CCR, a drifting current-sensing feedback loop, or a failing regulation stage struggling to compensate for load changes on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor output current over a full duty cycle for hunting or step changes; inspect tap-changer contacts for pitting or carbon build-up; verify the current-sensing transformer/shunt and its feedback wiring to the regulation stage.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace worn tap-changer contacts, re-terminate feedback wiring, or replace the current sensor; escalate to regulation-stage repair or unit replacement if instability persists after these checks.
CCR Will Not Respond to Remote Intensity / Step Commands
Likely Cause
Loss of communication between the tower/ALCMS and the RTU, the CCR left in local control mode, or a fault in the RTU-to-CCR control wiring.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the RTU's communication link status and confirm the CCR's local/remote selector is set to remote; test step commands directly at the RTU output terminals to isolate whether the fault is in the link or the control wiring.
Corrective Action
Restore the communication link or replace the RTU if faulty, correct the local/remote selector position, or repair the control wiring between the RTU and the CCR; confirm all intensity steps respond correctly before returning to service.
Automatic Changeover Fails to Transfer to Standby Power
Likely Cause
Failed changeover contactor or controller, a standby generator/UPS that fails to start or reach stable output, or a control-wiring fault preventing the transfer signal from reaching the changeover switch.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a manual test transfer to verify the changeover mechanism operates and times correctly; confirm the standby source starts and reaches rated voltage/frequency within the required window; check control wiring and sensing relays between the utility feed and the changeover controller.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted contactor/controller, service the standby generator/UPS per its maintenance schedule, and re-terminate any faulted control wiring; re-verify transfer time meets the airfield's operating category requirement before sign-off.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

CCR Fails to Start
High Severity
Root Cause
A blown input fuse, a failed internal power supply component, or a control logic fault that prevents the regulator from initiating output.
Effect
No output at all from the CCR, taking the entire series circuit it feeds out of service until restored.
Detection Method
Input voltage verification at the CCR, fault log review, and inspection of internal fuses and the power supply stage.
Overtemperature Protection Trip
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Blocked or fouled cooling fins, a failed cooling fan, or operation in an enclosure with inadequate ventilation for the ambient conditions.
Effect
CCR trips on overtemperature, interrupting the circuit until it cools and is reset, and can recur under sustained high load.
Detection Method
Internal temperature monitoring against the trip threshold, and inspection of cooling fans/fins during scheduled maintenance.
Earth Leakage / Ground Fault Trip
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable insulation breakdown, water ingress at a connector or splice, or a fixture-level fault feeding current to ground.
Effect
CCR trips on earth leakage or ground fault protection, and the fault will recur on re-energising until the underlying leak is located and repaired.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing to pinpoint the fault location.
Regulation Instability
High Severity
Root Cause
Output filter capacitor degradation, a degraded regulation component, or an incoming supply disturbance outside the unit's specified tolerance.
Effect
Output current unstable or fails to regulate, risking under- or over-driven fixtures across the circuit.
Detection Method
Output current and ripple measurement against tolerance, and correlation with logged incoming supply anomalies.
Communication / Control Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
CCR will not respond to remote intensity or step commands, forcing local manual control until communication is restored.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the ALCMS, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Automatic Changeover Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
A worn transfer contactor, a failed generator interface, or a control logic fault in the transfer sequence.
Effect
Automatic changeover fails to transfer to standby power during a utility outage, leaving the circuit unsupported.
Detection Method
Scheduled functional transfer test under load, and inspection of the transfer switch and generator interface during maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Lock Out Before Internal Access
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out both the incoming mains supply and the series-circuit output before opening a CCR or distribution panel; the output side can carry several kV even though the input is low voltage.

Arc Flash & Switchgear Precautions
Personnel Safety

Follow the site's arc-flash and switching procedures, use appropriate PPE, and avoid opening load-break devices under fault conditions when working on distribution switchgear.

Verify Both Sides of a Changeover
Electrical Safety

Before working on an automatic transfer or changeover system, confirm which source is live and lock out both the primary and standby feeds; a changeover system can be back-fed from either side.

Coordinate Circuit Outages
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations and raise a NOTAM before taking a CCR or feeder out of service, since darkening a circuit affects live runway or taxiway lighting.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger the series loop after any CCR, cabling, or distribution work before restoring power, since a residual fault at start-up can trip protection or damage fixtures.

Allow Cool-Down Before Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow CCR heatsinks, chokes, and transformer components to cool before handling after operation; regulator components can retain significant heat.

Verify Regulation Before Handover
Compliance

Confirm the CCR holds correct output current across all brightness steps and that monitoring/communication to the ALCMS is restored before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Output Accuracy at Low Steps Deserves as Much Attention as Full Brightness
Design

A CCR that regulates accurately at 100% output can still drift out of tolerance at lower brightness steps, which is exactly where pilots most need a consistent, calibrated visual reference during low-visibility approaches. Verify regulation across the full step range during commissioning and periodic testing, not just at maximum output.

An Untested Changeover Path Is an Unverified Assumption
Reliability

A dual-CCR or changeover arrangement that has never actually been forced to switch under load can fail to change over correctly when a real fault occurs, even though it "should" work on paper. Periodically exercise the changeover under controlled conditions rather than relying on it having worked correctly since commissioning.

Arc Flash Energy Scales With Upstream Fault Current, Not Panel Size
Electrical Safety

The arc flash hazard at a distribution panel depends on the available fault current from upstream, which can be substantial even at a modest-looking distribution board. Work from the panel's actual arc flash study rather than assuming a smaller panel means lower incident energy.

A Slowly Rising Loop Current Reading Points Upstream, Not to the Fixtures
Troubleshooting

When several fixtures on a loop all show a gradual output drift in the same direction, the CCR's own current regulation is a more likely cause than a coincidental fault at multiple fixtures simultaneously. Check CCR calibration before dispatching a crew to inspect fixtures individually when a loop-wide trend appears.

Thermal Cycling in the Cabinet Ages Components Faster Than Rated Life Suggests
Durability

Repeated heating and cooling from daily operation and seasonal swings accelerates component aging inside a poorly ventilated distribution cabinet beyond what a component's rated life under steady conditions would suggest. Confirm cabinet ventilation and cooling are performing as designed as part of routine inspection, not just at initial installation.

Coordinate Outages Around the Circuits They Actually Affect
Operational

A single CCR often feeds a specific runway or taxiway system in its entirety, so an outage for maintenance can have a much broader operational impact than the physical size of the equipment being worked on suggests. Confirm exactly which lighting systems go dark before scheduling the work, not just which panel is being opened.
