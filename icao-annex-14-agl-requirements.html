01 · Core Principle

Annex 14 Is a Framework, Not a Parts List

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I, Aerodromes — Aerodrome Design and Operations, establishes Standards and Recommended Practices covering aerodrome physical characteristics, obstacle limitation, visual aids and much of the operational and safety framework that governs an airport.

For Airfield Ground Lighting, Annex 14 should not be treated simply as a list of lamps. It provides the regulatory framework within which an aerodrome's runway lighting, taxiway lighting, approach lighting, PAPI, stop bars, runway guard lights, signs, markings, obstacle lighting, visual guidance systems, electrical systems, emergency systems and operational procedures are designed and operated.

02 · Definition

What Is ICAO Annex 14?

ICAO Annex 14 is the ICAO Annex dedicated to aerodromes. It establishes Standards and Recommended Practices for aerodrome design and operations.

Volume I addresses aerodromes, while Volume II addresses heliports. For AGL engineering, Volume I is the principal Annex 14 reference.

03 · Relevance

Why Annex 14 Matters to AGL

AGL is part of the aerodrome's visual guidance infrastructure. Annex 14 provisions interact with a wide range of aerodrome factors — therefore an AGL engineer should never select a lighting system independently of the aerodrome design.

06 · SARPs

Standards & Recommended Practices

ICAO uses the term Standards and Recommended Practices, commonly abbreviated as SARPs.

A Standard represents a provision considered necessary for the safety or regularity of international air navigation, to which Contracting States are expected to conform. A Recommended Practice provides guidance that ICAO considers desirable. The engineer must understand which type of provision is being applied.

Do not treat every Annex 14 paragraph as if it has exactly the same regulatory status. For compliance documentation, distinguish:

StandardRecommended PracticeNoteGuidance MaterialNational ImplementationAirport-Specific Requirement
This distinction is important when preparing compliance statements — a Standard and a Recommended Practice do not carry the same weight. A State that cannot comply with a Standard must notify the difference to ICAO under Article 38 of the Chicago Convention.
07 · State Responsibility

ICAO establishes international SARPs, but implementation occurs through States and their national aviation systems. Therefore: ICAO Requirement ≠ Automatically the Entire National Requirement. 11 · Taxiway Lighting

Taxiway Lighting, Stop Bars & Runway Guard Lights

Taxiway lighting supports aircraft movement on taxiways. Depending on the applicable configuration, this may include taxiway centreline lights, taxiway edge lights, rapid exit taxiway indicator lights, intermediate holding position lights, stop bars and runway guard lights.

Taxiway Centreline Lighting

Visual guidance along the intended aircraft route — must correspond with geometry, markings, intersections and holding positions.

Taxiway Edge Lighting

Visual identification of taxiway edges — location, spacing, colour, intensity and photometric performance.

Runway Guard Lights

Conspicuous visual warning of the runway environment, integrated with holding position markings, signs, stop bars and ATC procedures.

Stop Bars

Highly visible indication associated with a runway holding position — more than a fixture installation.

Taxiway Intersection Lighting

Coordination of centreline, edge lighting, signs, markings, holding positions and guard lights for unambiguous routing.

Taxiway Guidance Signs

Location, direction and mandatory-instruction information, matched to actual taxiway geometry.

A stop bar is an operational control element as well as a visual element. Engineering should evaluate control logic, switching, interlocking, monitoring, fail-safe philosophy, the ATC interface and emergency conditions.

Taxiway Lighting ICAO Requirements

For taxiway lighting, ICAO Annex 14 sets out where each type of light is required, its colour, its spacing and its photometric performance. In general, taxiway centreline lights are green, taxiway edge lights are blue, stop bars are red and intermediate holding position lights are yellow. Always confirm the colour, spacing and intensity against the current edition of Annex 14 and the national regulation, not from memory.

13 · Photometry

AGL Photometry vs Electrical Testing

Photometry verifies whether the installed lighting equipment produces the required optical performance. Depending on the system, testing may involve intensity, colour, beam alignment, coverage, uniformity and other specified characteristics. The applicable measurement method must correspond to the equipment and requirement.

Electrical testing and photometry are different disciplines. A fixture can pass an electrical test and still fail photometry.

18 · Obstacle, SMGCS & Low Visibility

Obstruction Lighting, Surface Movement Guidance & Low-Visibility Operations

Annex 14 also addresses obstacle-related visual identification, and AGL contributes to Surface Movement Guidance and Control as well as runway incursion prevention through a layered safety architecture.

Obstruction Lighting

Considered based on obstacle characteristics, location, height, aerodrome environment and applicable obstacle requirements.

Surface Movement Guidance

Combines markings, signs, taxiway lights, stop bars, runway guard lights, ALCMS, surveillance, ATC and procedures.

Increases dependence on lighting, signs, markings, stop bars, centreline guidance, control, monitoring and backup power.

Human Factors

Visibility, contrast, colour, position, consistency, glare and complexity — the objective is safe human interpretation, not just technical compliance.

Maintenance should be based on: manufacturer instructions, airport maintenance procedures, applicable ICAO requirements, national requirements, equipment condition and operational importance — not on calendar intervals alone.

Measurement equipment used for compliance testing — photometric equipment, illuminance meters, electrical test equipment, current measurement equipment, alignment equipment — should have valid calibration, and calibration records should be included in the test documentation.

21 · ICAO vs FAA vs Indian Standards

ICAO Compliance vs FAA vs Indian Standards

ICAO vs FAA

ICAO and FAA documents are not interchangeable. For an ICAO-based airport, ICAO + National Requirements + Airport Specification should form the primary compliance basis. FAA guidance may be a useful equipment or engineering reference where specifically applicable, but should not automatically replace the governing ICAO/national framework.

ICAO vs IS Standards

Indian projects may also specify Indian Standards for electrical equipment, AGL components, transformers, cables and connectors.

FAA Runway Lighting Standards

FAA runway lighting standards are published in FAA advisory circulars, which cover the equipment specifications and the design and installation of the lighting. They are a separate framework from Annex 14, so they are used as an equipment or engineering reference only where the project specification names them.

FAA Taxiway Lighting Standards

The same applies to FAA taxiway lighting standards. They cover taxiway edge and centreline lighting, but they do not automatically replace the ICAO and national requirements for an ICAO-based airport. See the ICAO vs FAA standards comparison and the Standards Companion.

26 · Safety Management & NOTAMs

Safety Significance & the NOTAM Interface

AGL failures should be evaluated according to their operational significance — a single runway light failure, multiple centreline-light failures, a stop-bar failure, PAPI failure, approach-lighting failure or secondary-power failure each carry a different operational response depending on the system, aerodrome configuration and applicable procedures.

An engineering fault may have operational consequences. The engineer should identify the technical condition, determine affected equipment, record the actual field condition, notify the responsible airport operational function, and support the authorized aeronautical-information process.

Engineering personnel should not independently issue an operational notice unless authorized to do so.

28 · Final Principle

The Final Engineering Principle

ICAO Annex 14 compliance is not achieved by writing "ICAO Compliant." It is achieved by demonstrating the complete chain: Applicable Requirement → Correct Design → Suitable Equipment → Correct Installation → Verified Electrical Performance → Verified Optical Performance → Verified Functional Performance → Operational Verification → Objective Documentation.

For an AGL engineer, the most important principle is: never ask only, "Is this AGL equipment ICAO compliant?" Ask, "Which ICAO requirement applies, how has it been implemented in the approved design, and what objective evidence proves that the installed system satisfies it?"

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is ICAO Annex 14?

ICAO Annex 14 is the ICAO Annex dedicated to aerodromes, establishing Standards and Recommended Practices for aerodrome design and operations. Volume I addresses aerodromes and Volume II addresses heliports; for AGL engineering, Volume I is the principal reference.

Q2Is Annex 14 a list of approved lights or equipment?

No. It is a framework, not a parts list or equipment datasheet. It provides the regulatory framework within which runway, taxiway and approach lighting, PAPI, stop bars, runway guard lights, signs, markings, obstacle lighting, electrical and emergency systems are designed and operated. Manufacturer datasheets, project specifications and approved design each define their own part of the picture.

Q3What is the current Annex 14 baseline according to this page?

The page reports that the ICAO Store lists Annex 14, Volume I, 9th Edition, July 2022, including Amendment 18, along with Corrigendum 2 dated 18 March 2026 and Corrigendum 3 dated 8 June 2026. For compliance work, verify the complete current edition, amendments and applicable corrigenda rather than relying on a single number.

Q4What are the key dates for Annex 14 Amendment 18?

According to the page, Amendment 18 was adopted 28 March 2025 and became effective 4 August 2025, with aerodrome design, visual aids and apron management provisions applicable from 27 November 2025. Provisions on ground handling apply from 26 November 2026, and the new obstacle limitation surfaces from 21 November 2030.

Q5Which visual-aid changes does Amendment 18 include?

ICAO's summary lists runway distance remaining signs (RDRS), harmonized CAT II lighting requirements, main beam average intensity of lights, threshold marking on paved runways, closed-runway lighting and unserviceability signs. Several lighting provisions previously keyed to an RVR below 350 m are re-keyed to 300 m. Confirm wording, clause numbers and national differences against the consolidated current text.

Q6What is the difference between a Standard and a Recommended Practice?

A Standard is a provision considered necessary for the safety or regularity of international air navigation, to which Contracting States are expected to conform. A Recommended Practice is guidance ICAO considers desirable. They do not carry the same weight, so compliance documentation should distinguish them, along with notes, guidance material, national implementation and airport-specific requirements.

Q7Does an ICAO requirement automatically become the national requirement?

No. ICAO establishes SARPs but implementation occurs through States. For an Indian airport project the engineer must also consider DGCA and AAI requirements, national regulations, airport specifications, the approved aerodrome design and project contract requirements. Where a State has notified a difference from an ICAO Standard, the national requirement governs, and the page suggests checking published differences such as AIP GEN 1.7 for India.

Q8What must a State do if it cannot comply with an ICAO Standard?

A State that cannot comply with a Standard must notify the difference to ICAO under Article 38 of the Chicago Convention.

Q9What aerodrome factors affect AGL design under Annex 14?

Before finalizing the lighting arrangement, establish the runway type, approach type, aircraft operating characteristics, aerodrome reference code, visibility and operational requirements, and instrument or non-instrument operation. An AGL engineer should never select a lighting system independently of the aerodrome design.

Q10What does Annex 14 say about runway lighting?

Annex 14 establishes requirements for runway visual aids according to the applicable runway and operational characteristics, covering elements such as edge, centreline and threshold lighting. Exact values should always be taken from the current applicable Annex 14 provision and the approved design.

Q11

What does ICAO Annex 14 say about runway lighting?

Annex 14 Volume I specifies the runway lights required for each runway type and approach category, with their colours, arrangement and intensity. The State's adopted regulation and approved design decide the details.

Q12

What does ICAO Annex 14 say about taxiway lighting?

It specifies taxiway edge, centerline and stop bar provisions, with colours and guidance functions, and expects them to support safe surface movement in low visibility.

Q13

What does ICAO Annex 14 say about PAPI?

It defines the PAPI as the visual approach slope system, with its light units, siting, aiming-angle approach, tolerances and photometric characteristics, with a smaller 2-unit form for some aerodromes.

Q14

What is ICAO Doc 9157?

ICAO Doc 9157 is the Aerodrome Design Manual, a multi-part guidance series supporting Annex 14. Part 4 covers visual aids and Part 5 covers electrical systems. It is guidance, not a Standard.
