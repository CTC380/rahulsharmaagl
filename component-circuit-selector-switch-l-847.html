Circuit Selector Switch (L-847)

A manually or remotely operated switching device that routes the output of one or more Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) to multiple AGL series circuits. Enables flexible allocation of lighting loads, redundancy management, and maintenance isolation without de-energizing the entire lighting system.

Overview

What Is It?

A Circuit Selector Switch (L‑847) is a specialised switching device used in AGL power distribution systems to direct the output of a Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to one of several series lighting circuits (e.g., runway edge, centreline, taxiway, approach). It provides the flexibility to share a single CCR among multiple lighting loads, reducing the number of regulators needed and enabling backup or redundancy schemes.

The L‑847 designation comes from FAA Advisory Circular 150/5345‑5 (currently Revision B), which specifies the performance and construction requirements for circuit selector switches used in airfield lighting — a separate AC from the one covering the CCR itself (AC 150/5345‑10, the L‑828 Constant Current Regulator spec). The switch is typically installed in the equipment shelter between the CCR output and the individual circuit feeders. It can be operated locally via a manual handle or remotely via a motor‑driven actuator controlled from the ALCMS or a control panel. The switch ensures that only one circuit is energised at a time, preventing conflicts and overloading, and it provides clear visual indication of which circuit is selected.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The L-847 switch exists to let a single CCR serve more than one lighting circuit, avoiding the cost and equipment-shelter space of dedicating a separate regulator to every runway, taxiway, or approach circuit at airfields where usage patterns don't require them to run simultaneously. Its purpose extends to redundancy as well as economy: if a CCR normally assigned to one circuit fails, the selector switch can redirect a working spare regulator to take over that circuit's load, providing a backup path without requiring a fully duplicated CCR for every single circuit in the system. Ensuring only one circuit is energised at any given time is itself a core part of the switch's purpose, since accidentally connecting a CCR's output to two circuits simultaneously would create an overload and current-sharing conflict the regulator isn't designed to handle.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Circuit Selector Switches are installed in airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters, or AGL power centres. They are used in various applications to optimise CCR utilisation and enhance system flexibility:

  • Sharing a CCR Among Multiple Circuits: A single CCR can be used to power different lighting circuits at different times (e.g., runway edge lights during take‑off and taxiway lights during ground movement).
  • Redundancy and Backup: In a redundant configuration, a standby CCR can be switched to any circuit if the primary CCR fails, ensuring continuity of critical lighting.
  • Maintenance Isolation: The switch allows an individual circuit to be isolated for maintenance without affecting other circuits powered by the same CCR.
  • Load Shedding: Non‑critical circuits can be deselected during generator operation to prioritise runway and approach lighting.
  • Integration with ALCMS: Motorised selector switches can be remotely controlled from the ALCMS, enabling automated circuit selection based on operational needs or fault conditions.
Placement

Installation & Location

The switch is installed in the equipment shelter or electrical room between the CCR output terminals and the individual circuit feeders it serves, mounted on a panel or rack within reach of the CCRs it's associated with so the interconnecting cabling stays short and manageable. Each circuit feeder wired to the switch is clearly labelled at the point of termination, since a mislabelled position is a direct route to energising the wrong circuit, a mistake the switch's own interlocking is meant to prevent between circuits but can't catch if the wiring itself was connected incorrectly at installation. Where a motor-driven actuator is fitted, its control cabling is run back to the ALCMS or a local control panel alongside other AGL control wiring, kept identifiable so a remote circuit-selection command can be traced to the correct switch in an equipment room serving several CCRs and circuits. The switch's position indicator, whether a mechanical handle position or an electronic status readout, is mounted so it remains clearly visible to anyone working in the equipment room, giving an immediate visual check of which circuit is currently selected before any related maintenance work begins.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Circuit Selector Switch consists of a multi‑position rotary switch or a set of interlocked contactors that route the CCR output to one of several load circuits. The switch has a common input (from the CCR) and multiple output terminals (each connected to a separate series circuit). A mechanical or electrical interlock prevents more than one output from being connected at a time.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Manual Operation: A front‑panel rotary handle with clear position indicators allows the operator to select the desired circuit. The handle is typically spring‑loaded to ensure positive engagement.
  • Remote Operation: Motor‑driven versions incorporate an electric actuator (often with position feedback) that can be controlled via dry contacts, Modbus, or other protocols from the ALCMS or a remote control panel.
  • Position Indication: The switch provides local visual indication (pointer on the handle) and remote position status via auxiliary contacts or a communication interface.
  • Interlocking: Electrical and mechanical interlocks prevent simultaneous connection of multiple circuits, protecting the CCR from overload and avoiding damage to the switch.
  • Load Break Capability: The switch is designed to make and break the series circuit under load (up to the rated current, typically 6.6A or higher) without causing arcing or damage, complying with relevant switching standards.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The selector switch sits between a single CCR and multiple candidate loops, so it is itself a control point rather than a passive component — ALCMS or an operator commands a position change, and the switch's own interlock (mechanical or electrical) is what prevents that command from ever connecting two circuits to one CCR simultaneously, protecting the regulator from paralleling loops of different impedance. Because only one circuit is powered at a time, any fault the CCR reports (voltage rise, no output) is unambiguously attributable to whichever circuit is currently selected — the switch's own position feedback is what lets ALCMS know which physical circuit a CCR-level alarm actually belongs to.

Construction

Physical Construction

The switch is built around a set of heavy-duty contactors or a motor-driven multi-position switch mechanism, sized to carry the full series-circuit current (commonly up to 20A) without significant contact resistance, since even a small added resistance in a constant-current loop shows up as an unwanted voltage rise the CCR has to compensate for. Contacts and busbars are housed in a metal cabinet with clear internal segregation between the different circuit paths being switched, preventing cross-connection between loops that must never be energised together. Where switching is motorised, a geared actuator drives the mechanism between positions under control-cable command, with mechanical position-indication contacts wired back to the ALCMS so the selected circuit routing can be confirmed remotely rather than only by physical inspection. The cabinet includes cable gland plates for the CCR input and each of the output circuit feeds, with a separate low-voltage compartment for the control and position-feedback wiring.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Contact Pitting and Wear

Repeated switching under load causes contact erosion. Over time, pitting increases contact resistance, leading to overheating and voltage drop. Regular inspection and replacement of contacts when worn is essential.

Mechanical Interlock Failure

The mechanical interlock mechanism can jam or break, potentially allowing two circuits to be connected simultaneously. This can cause the CCR to overload or damage the switch. Periodic mechanical inspection and lubrication are recommended.

Motor Actuator Malfunction

In motorised versions, the actuator motor, gearbox, or limit switches can fail, preventing remote operation or causing mis‑positioning. Regular testing and backup manual operation capability are critical.

Position Indication Errors

Auxiliary contacts or position sensors may fail or become misaligned, leading to incorrect status reporting to the ALCMS. This can cause confusion or unintended switching. Verify position feedback during routine maintenance.

Improper Sequencing

Switching from one circuit to another without de‑energising the CCR can cause arcing and contact damage. Proper control logic should ensure the CCR is turned off or its output is reduced before the switch is operated (unless designed for load break). Follow manufacturer guidance for safe operation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Switch Overheating or Showing Voltage Drop Across Contacts
Likely Cause
Contact pitting and wear from repeated switching under load, increasing contact resistance.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure voltage drop across the contacts under load, visually inspect contact faces for pitting or burning, and check switching history against the rated duty cycle.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace worn/pitted contacts, and schedule replacement before resistance rises further.
Two Circuits Appear Energised Simultaneously, or Switch Jams
Likely Cause
Mechanical interlock mechanism jammed, worn, or broken.
Diagnostic Steps
Manually operate the switch to check interlock action, inspect the interlock mechanism for jamming, wear, or breakage, and check lubrication condition.
Corrective Action
Free and lubricate the interlock, or repair/replace the mechanism if broken; verify only one circuit can be selected before returning to service.
Motorised Switch Fails to Operate Remotely or Mis-Positions
Likely Cause
Actuator motor, gearbox, or limit switch fault in the motorised version.
Diagnostic Steps
Test remote operation and observe actuator response, check motor supply voltage, inspect the gearbox for mechanical binding, and verify limit switch operation and adjustment.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted motor, gearbox, or limit switch, and confirm manual backup operation is available while repairs are completed.
Incorrect Position Reported to the ALCMS
Likely Cause
Auxiliary contacts or position sensors have failed or become misaligned.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify actual switch position against the position feedback reported to the ALCMS, inspect auxiliary contacts/sensors for misalignment or damage.
Corrective Action
Realign or replace the auxiliary contacts/position sensor, and re-verify correct position feedback before returning to service.
Arcing or Contact Damage During Switching
Likely Cause
Improper sequencing — the switch operated while the CCR is still energised, instead of being de-energised or reduced first.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the control logic/sequence used at the time of switching, inspect contacts for fresh arc damage, and confirm whether the switch is rated for load-break operation.
Corrective Action
Correct the control logic to de-energise or reduce the CCR output before switching (unless the unit is rated for load-break), and replace any arc-damaged contacts.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Contact Pitting and Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Repeated switching under load causing normal contact wear over the rated duty cycle.
Effect
Rising contact resistance leads to overheating or voltage drop across the switch, degrading circuit performance.
Detection Method
Measuring voltage drop across the contacts under load, and visual inspection of contact faces for pitting or burning.
Interlock Mechanism Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical interlock jammed, worn, or broken, often from lack of lubrication over time.
Effect
Two circuits can appear energised simultaneously, or the switch jams, defeating the interlock's safety purpose.
Detection Method
Manually operating the switch to check interlock action, and inspecting the mechanism for jamming, wear, or breakage.
Motorised Actuator Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Actuator motor, gearbox, or limit switch fault developing on the motorised version of the switch.
Effect
Switch fails to operate remotely or mis-positions, requiring manual backup operation until repaired.
Detection Method
Testing remote operation and observing actuator response, and checking motor supply voltage and limit switch adjustment.
Position Feedback Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Auxiliary contacts or position sensors failing or becoming misaligned over time.
Effect
Incorrect switch position is reported to the ALCMS, undermining confidence in the system's status displays.
Detection Method
Verifying actual switch position against the position feedback reported to the ALCMS, and inspecting sensors for misalignment or damage.

High Severity
Root Cause
Improper sequencing where the switch is operated while the CCR is still energised, instead of being de-energised or reduced first.
Effect
Arcing damages the contacts each time it occurs, shortening the switch's service life and risking a failed circuit selection.
Detection Method
Reviewing the control logic/sequence used at switching time, and inspecting contacts for fresh arc damage.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Before Internal Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR and all series circuits routed through the switch before working inside it, and verify zero energy before touching internal contacts.

Verify Interlock Before Manual Operation
Electrical Safety

Confirm the mechanical interlock is intact and functioning before manually cycling the switch; a jammed or worn interlock can leave two circuits appearing energised simultaneously or the switch itself jammed mid-position.

Confirm Selected Circuit Before Energising
Operational

Physically verify which series circuit the switch is routed to before commanding the CCR on, rather than relying on remote status alone, since sending regulated current to the wrong circuit misdirects lighting guidance on the airfield.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any switch operation that changes which circuit a CCR serves requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM for the circuit(s) that will be dark or off-regulation during the transfer.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any contact or wiring work, megger test the affected circuit paths before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the newly connected loop.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on switch contacts, and confirm the enclosure is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting a circuit.

Motorised Actuator Verification
Verification

On motorised versions, confirm the actuator drives fully to each commanded position and the limit switches register correctly, since a fault here can leave the switch mis-positioned without an obvious external sign.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the switch is in its intended position, the interlock is fully functional, and the correct circuit is energised before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
The Make-Before-Break vs Break-Before-Make Behaviour Needs to Be Known, Not Assumed
Design

Whether an L-847 switch briefly connects two circuits together during transfer or briefly de-energises both matters for how it interacts with downstream protection and with any interlocked equipment; confirm the specific switch's transfer behaviour against the system design intent rather than assuming a generic selector switch behaves one particular way.

Verify Visual Position Indication Matches Actual Contact Position
Verification

A mechanical position indicator can become misaligned relative to the actual internal contact position after years of operation or a maintenance intervention, showing a circuit as selected when it isn't actually energised through that path; periodically verify the indicator against an independent electrical check rather than trusting the pointer alone.

Motor-Driven Actuators Need Their Own Maintenance Attention
Maintenance

Where the switch is remotely operated via a motor-driven actuator, the actuator's own mechanical condition — gearing, limit switches, coupling to the switch shaft — needs periodic attention distinct from the switch contacts themselves; an actuator fault can leave the switch stuck mid-travel even though the switch mechanism itself is in good condition.

Never Switch a Loaded Series Circuit Without Confirming It's the Right Type of Switch for the Job
Safety

Not every circuit selector switch is rated for load-break operation; some designs require the CCR to be de-energised or the circuit to be otherwise unloaded before switching. Confirm the specific switch's load-break rating before operating it under load, since attempting to switch a loaded circuit on a non-load-break-rated unit can damage the switch or create an arcing hazard.

Exercise Switches on Circuits That Rarely Get Reassigned
Reliability

A selector switch dedicated to a backup or rarely-used routing option can sit in one position for years without being exercised, allowing contacts on the unused positions to develop resistance or a mechanism to stiffen; periodically cycle through all positions as a planned maintenance activity rather than only operating the switch when an actual reroute is needed.

Document the As-Left Position After Every Manual Operation
Documentation

A circuit selector switch operated manually during an emergency or maintenance action and then left in a non-standard position without clear documentation can cause confusion for the next person working on the system; record and communicate the as-left switch position any time it's manually changed from its normal operating configuration.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the L-847 circuit selector switch: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an L-847 circuit selector switch?

An L-847 circuit selector switch is a switching device that directs the output of a constant current regulator (CCR) to one of several series lighting circuits, such as runway edge, centreline, taxiway or approach. It lets one CCR be shared among multiple loads and supports backup and redundancy schemes.

Q2Where does the L-847 designation come from?

From FAA Advisory Circular 150/5345-5 (currently Revision B), the specification for circuit selector switches, 5,000 volt, 6.6 A/20 A. It is a separate AC from the one covering the CCR itself (AC 150/5345-10, the L-828 regulator specification).

Q3Where is the switch installed?

Typically in the equipment shelter between the CCR output and the individual circuit feeders, in airfield electrical rooms and AGL power centres.

Q4How does the switch work?

It is a multi-position rotary switch or a set of interlocked contactors with one common input from the CCR and several outputs, each feeding a separate series circuit. A mechanical or electrical interlock prevents more than one output from being connected at a time.

Q5Can it be operated remotely?

Yes. Manual versions use a front-panel rotary handle with position indicators. Motor-driven versions use an electric actuator for remote operation, with 2 to 4 auxiliary contacts giving position feedback to the CCR or ALCMS.

Q6What are the typical ratings?

Rated current 6.6 A to 20 A and voltage up to 5 kV (the CCR open-circuit voltage), with 2 to 6 positions (commonly 3 or 4). It is rated to make and break the full series-circuit load without excessive arcing per AC 150/5345-5.

Q7Does the selector switch protect the circuit?

No. It has no internal protection and is not itself a protective device, as protection is provided upstream by the CCR.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-5 (L-847), IEC 60947, BS EN 60947-3 and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should the switch be inspected?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for interlock function test and position feedback verification, and annual for contact resistance and wear, actuator and handle operation, insulation resistance, terminal tightness, enclosure and seal inspection and lubrication. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with L-847 switches?

Common problems are contact pitting and wear, mechanical interlock failure, motor actuator malfunction, position indication errors and improper sequencing. Two circuits appearing energised at once, a jammed switch, overheating or arcing during switching all need prompt attention.
