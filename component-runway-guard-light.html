Runway Guard Light

A critical safety lighting system that provides visual warning to pilots and vehicle operators approaching an active runway. Runway guard lights are a standalone ICAO Annex 14 / FAA-mandated fixture, flashing continuously whenever the associated lighting circuit is energised, and are independent of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system — helping to prevent runway incursions and enhance airfield safety.

Overview

What Is It?

A Runway Guard Light (RGL), commonly called a "wig-wag," is a visual warning system installed at runway holding positions to alert pilots and vehicle operators that they are approaching an active runway. Unlike the automated, occupancy-driven Runway Status Light (RWSL) system, an RGL is a standalone fixture that flashes continuously — typically alternating amber/yellow, either as a pair of elevated units either side of the taxiway (Configuration A) or a row of in-pavement units across the taxiway (Configuration B) — for as long as the associated circuit is switched on, independent of whether the runway is actually occupied. It serves as a constant, unmissable reminder of the holding position, not a dynamic go/no-go signal.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The runway guard light exists as the last visual checkpoint before a pilot or vehicle operator physically crosses onto an active runway, placed precisely at the holding position where a moment's inattention has the most serious consequence. Its flashing amber pattern is deliberately distinct from steady-burning taxiway and runway lighting, drawing attention specifically because a flashing signal is harder to overlook than a static one, especially in a high-workload taxi phase where attention may be divided. Because the fixture flashes continuously rather than only in response to a detected conflict, its purpose is best understood as a constant, unambiguous marker of where the runway environment begins, reinforcing — rather than replacing — a pilot's own judgement and any ATC clearance to enter or cross. At airports where RWSL is also installed, RGL is a complementary but functionally separate layer, not a component driven by the same occupancy logic.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at runway holding positions on taxiways, typically at the intersection where a taxiway meets the runway. Runway guard lights are used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where enhanced safety is required
  • Busy airports with high traffic volumes where runway incursion risk is elevated
  • Runway crossings where taxiways intersect active runways
  • Intersecting runways where crossing traffic needs to be managed
Placement

Installation & Location

A pair of Runway Guard Light fixtures (the elevated "wig-wag" configuration) is installed on either side of the taxiway at the runway holding position, mounted on frangible stems set back from the pavement edge so the alternating flashing beams are visible to a pilot or vehicle operator approaching the holding point; where an in-pavement configuration is used instead, a row of flush units is set across the taxiway following the same layout as Runway Entrance Lights. Each fixture is fed by cable run in a trench or duct back to a control/flasher unit that drives the alternating flash pattern, typically switched on with the taxiway edge lighting circuit (for night or low-visibility operation, or continuously per local operating policy) rather than by any occupancy sensor. The control unit and any associated transformer are housed in an enclosure clear of the taxiway shoulder, with setting-out tied to the holding position marking so the fixtures sit at the same reference line as the hold line.

Function

How Does It Work?

Runway guard lights are typically powered from the series circuit via isolating transformers, with a dedicated flasher interface producing the alternating amber/yellow wig-wag pattern. Unlike RWSL fixtures, RGL activation is not driven by surveillance data or runway occupancy: the lights flash continuously at a fixed rate whenever the circuit is energised, on a schedule set by airport operating procedure (commonly linked to darkness/low-visibility operation, or run 24/7 at busier fields). This constant flashing is what makes the fixture effective as an always-present reminder of the holding position, rather than a conditional warning.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Runway guard lights are wired onto the airfield's constant-current series circuit like other elevated and in-pavement AGL fixtures, each fed through its own isolating transformer, but their flash behaviour is not set by the CCR's brightness steps alone. A dedicated flasher/switching interface taps the same loop current and converts it into a rapid alternating (A/B) flash pattern between the two lamps of a Configuration A pair, or in sequence across the fixtures of a Configuration B row. The command to energise that interface comes from the ALCMS on a schedule tied to airport operating procedure — commonly switched with the taxiway lighting circuit for darkness or low-visibility operation, or left continuously on — and is entirely independent of any runway-occupancy surveillance system such as RWSL; on fields where RWSL is also present, the two operate side by side with no shared activation logic. Once energised, the fixture flashes continuously until the ALCMS commands it off. Fault supervision therefore centres on the flasher interface and the series circuit itself: a flasher stuck in one state, a lamp that fails to alternate in step, or a loss of series current is reported as an RGL fault requiring field follow-up.

ALCMS (On/Off Schedule) → Flasher / Switching Interface → Series Circuit → Isolating Transformer → Fixture Pair (alternating A/B)

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is typically an inset (flush) unit seating into an L-868 base can recessed into the taxiway pavement at the runway-holding position, with the top surface load-rated for aircraft wheel loading; elevated (wig-wag) configurations instead mount two paired units on frangible fittings either side of the taxiway. The housing is a die-cast aluminium alloy body, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance against de-icing fluid, fuel spillage, and UV exposure. The amber LED array sits under a high-strength polycarbonate or borosilicate glass lens, gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating against water and dust ingress. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors, which mate to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer housed in an adjacent base can, with a separate control link carrying the ALCMS on/off command to the flasher interface. Stainless-steel fasteners are used throughout, and the base can assembly includes a drainage provision to prevent water pooling around the transformer and cable entry points.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

Runway guard lights are exposed to rubber deposits, tyre debris, and de-icing fluids. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and visibility.

LED Driver Failure

LED drivers in runway guard lights can fail due to thermal stress or power surges. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers is essential to maintain system reliability.

ALCMS Interface Fault

Runway guard lights rely on the ALCMS on/off command reaching the flasher interface. A wiring fault or interface failure can leave the lights stuck off when they are scheduled to be on, or stuck on when they should be off. Regular interface checks and prompt fault reporting are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Lights Fail to Flash When Runway Is Occupied
Likely Cause
Loss of the ALCMS on/off command reaching the flasher interface, or a flasher unit stuck in the off state despite a valid ON command.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the ALCMS interface status for this fixture group; verify the flasher/switching interface is receiving the ON command; confirm loop voltage is present at the transformer secondary with a manual test activation.
Corrective Action
Restore or re-route the ALCMS control cabling, or replace the flasher interface if it fails to respond to a manual activation test; log the fault to maintenance for follow-up.
One Lamp of a Wig-Wag Pair Dark or Out of Step
Likely Cause
Failed LED module or isolating transformer on the affected unit, or a flasher interface no longer alternating the A/B pattern correctly between the two fixtures.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the pair through a full flash cycle to confirm which side is dark or mistimed; check series loop continuity through the affected fixture's transformer; verify the flasher output alternates cleanly between the A and B legs.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED module or transformer on the affected fixture; if both units are healthy but out of step, replace the flasher/switching interface.
Lights Remain Flashing After Runway Is Clear
Likely Cause
Flasher interface stuck in the on state, or the ALCMS command not being cleared/updated when the schedule calls for the circuit to be off.
Diagnostic Steps
Cross-check the ALCMS's current commanded state for this fixture group against the actual scheduled state; issue a manual extinguish command to the flasher interface and observe response; inspect the flasher for a hardware latch fault.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the stuck flasher interface; if the ALCMS command itself is stale, escalate to ALCMS maintenance to clear or restart the affected control channel.
Erratic or Irregular Flash Rate
Likely Cause
Ageing or thermally stressed LED driver, or a marginal flasher interface no longer holding the specified 45–50 flashes-per-minute-per-lamp rate under load.
Diagnostic Steps
Time the flash rate against the specified range during an activation; check driver output and case temperature for signs of thermal stress; substitute a known-good flasher interface to isolate the fault.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded LED driver or flasher interface, then re-verify the flash rate falls within the specified range before returning the fixture to service.
Reduced Conspicuity / Hazy Lens
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits, de-icing fluid residue, or fuel-spill film built up on the lens, or a hazed/eroded lens on an inset unit exposed to constant wheel traffic.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for contamination or surface degradation during a scheduled activation check; run a photometric check if haze is suspected beyond surface contamination.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush; replace the lens or fixture if erosion or haze persists after cleaning and photometric output remains below threshold.
Series Circuit Fault Traced to Guard Light Position
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at the base can or L-823 connectors has degraded insulation resistance, or pavement/subgrade settlement around an inset unit has damaged cabling.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the circuit segment covering the guard light position; inspect the base can, gaskets, and connector seals for water ingress; check surrounding pavement for settlement or cracking that may have stressed the cable run.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected connector/transformer, renew seals, and confirm insulation resistance is restored; address pavement settlement to prevent recurrence before re-energising the circuit.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Driver Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling stress inside the driver housing from repeated activation, a power surge transient, or an aging driver drifting off its regulated output as it nears end-of-life.
Effect
One or both lamps of the wig-wag pair go dark or flash at reduced intensity, weakening the fixture's conspicuity to approaching flight crews.
Detection Method
Scheduled activation check with a visual or photometric intensity comparison between the paired lamps, and driver case-temperature inspection during maintenance.
Flasher / Switching Interface Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Relay or solid-state switching component wear from continuous duty cycling, a firmware fault, or a failed activation-command interpreter in the interface.
Effect
Fixture fails to flash on a valid ALCMS ON command, remains flashing after being commanded off, or the A/B wig-wag pattern loses its alternating sequence.
Detection Method
Manual activation test against the ALCMS command, flash-rate timing check, and inspection of the interface's fault/status indicators.
ALCMS Control Link Loss
High Severity
Root Cause
Control cable damage, a failed interface card, or an interrupted command feed between the ALCMS and the local flasher interface.
Effect
Guard light fails to switch on when scheduled, or continues flashing on a stale command after being scheduled off, reducing confidence in the fixture's state.
Detection Method
ALCMS interface status monitoring at the control system, and correlation of local fixture behaviour against the commanded schedule during a test activation.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop that darkens the affected wig-wag unit or, if the open extends to the loop itself, takes down the entire circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Wig-Wag Synchronisation Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Mismatched timing settings between the paired flasher outputs, or one unit's LED driver responding more slowly than its pair after partial degradation.
Effect
The two lamps of the pair flash out of the required alternating sequence, weakening the attention-getting wig-wag effect.
Detection Method
Visual timing observation through several flash cycles during an activation check, or channel comparison of the two flasher outputs for phase drift.
Lens Contamination / Optical Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits, de-icing fluid residue, and fuel-spill film accumulating on the lens, compounded by UV exposure and abrasive erosion on inset units exposed to wheel traffic.
Effect
Reduced conspicuity and light output that can fall below the level needed for the fixture to be reliably seen from the cockpit.
Detection Method
Visual inspection during scheduled cleaning cycles, and a photometric intensity check where haze or erosion is suspected beyond surface contamination.
L-823 Connector / Base Can Moisture Ingress
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or pavement/subgrade settlement around an inset unit stressing the cable run.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance across the guard light's series segment.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit segment, and visual inspection of connectors and base can seals during maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Runway guard lights sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling connectors.

Surveillance Interlock Dependency
Operational

The alternating flash is switched by the ALCMS on a fixed operating schedule, not by any surveillance feed; any work on the fixture or control unit must be followed by a check confirming the flasher still correctly responds to the ALCMS on/off command before returning the system to service.

Holding Position Work Coordination
Operational

These fixtures act as the final visual barrier before entering the runway environment; work here requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM where lights will be dark, with the holding position clearly marked by other means in the interim.

Frangibility Where Elevated
Mechanical

Where the elevated "wig-wag" configuration is used, never substitute the frangible stem with a rigid fitting or over-torque it; it must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft or ground vehicle.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the pair.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return the fixture to service with an unverified surveillance interlock; confirm the alternating flash pattern, activation logic, and photometric conformance before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify Command Timing, Not Just State
ALCMS Integration

A guard light that correctly alternates when commanded can still create confusion if the ALCMS-command-to-activation latency has drifted; benchmark end-to-end activation timing after any controller firmware update, not just a pass/fail flash check.

LED Alternation Can Outrun the Spec Envelope
Retrofit

LED drivers can switch faster than the incandescent lamps the alternating flash rate was originally tuned for, so a retrofit that hits the numeric flash-rate spec can still read as a distracting strobe to pilots; validate the retrofit against observed visual character, not the datasheet number alone.

Frangible Stem Fatigue in the Wig-Wag Configuration
Mechanical

The elevated wig-wag mounting puts continuous wind and jet-blast loading on the frangible stem that a flush-mounted fixture never sees. Treat these stems as a wear item on the overhaul cycle, since cumulative fatigue can lower the shear point well before any visible damage appears.

Nuisance Dark Pairs Often Trace to the Loop, Not the Fixture
Troubleshooting

When a guard light pair goes dark intermittently, the fixture itself is rarely the first thing to check; a slow-drifting series loop connection or an isolating transformer nearing end of life produces the same symptom and is far more common at holding-position locations with heavy vehicle traffic nearby.

Keep Pair Brightness Matched Across the Loop
Design

Where several guard light pairs share one CCR loop, uneven cable runs or a mix of lamp ages between pairs can make one holding position visibly dimmer than another even though each fixture individually tests in tolerance; balance loop impedance and lamp condition at the design and overhaul stage, not after a pilot report.

Base Can Seals Take the Brunt at Holding Positions
Durability

Holding positions collect standing water and de-icing fluid runoff more than most other AGL locations on the field. Specify base can and connector seals rated for that exposure and check gasket condition every overhaul rather than only when a fault is already reported.

Corrective Action: Restore or re-route the ALCMS control cabling, or replace the flasher interface if it fails to respond to a manual activation test; log the fault to maintenance for follow-up.
