Plug Assembly

The complete cable‑terminated plug (male) half of the L‑823 connector system, comprising the connector body, contact pins, coupling ring, and cable gland. Pre‑assembled and ready for field termination to primary series cables, it provides a reliable, waterproof, and low‑resistance connection to equipment‑mounted receptacles.

Overview

What Is It?

The Plug Assembly is the complete, ready‑to‑terminate cable‑mounted half of the L‑823 two‑pin waterproof connector system used in airfield ground lighting (AGL) primary series circuits. It is a factory‑assembled unit that includes all components required for field termination: the connector body with external threads, two gold‑ or tin‑plated brass contact pins, a knurled coupling ring, internal O‑ring seals, and a compression‑type cable gland for the primary cable entry.

The plug assembly is designed to be field‑terminated to the primary series cable (typically 6mm² to 16mm²) using standard crimping or soldering techniques. Once assembled and mated with the corresponding receptacle assembly (secondary connector), it forms an IP68 submersible, low‑resistance connection that withstands the harsh airfield environment, including moisture, temperature extremes, and mechanical vibration.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The plug assembly's purpose is to let the cable side of a primary circuit connection be terminated reliably under field conditions, in a trench or equipment pit rather than a controlled workshop, while still achieving the same IP68 seal integrity as a factory joint. Supplying it as a complete kit, body, pins, coupling ring, seals, and gland together, matters because a watertight connector depends on every one of those elements being correctly matched and assembled; substituting a mismatched gland or omitting an O-ring at termination is a common way for a connection that looks correct to fail months later once submerged. Once mated to its receptacle, the plug assembly becomes the point where the technician's field work directly determines the long-term reliability of that circuit connection.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The plug assembly is used throughout the airfield wherever primary circuit cable terminations are required, including:

  • Field Cable Ends: Terminating the ends of primary series cables at isolating transformers, junction boxes, and distribution points.
  • CCR Output Cables: Terminating the output cables from Constant Current Regulators (CCRs) for connection to the field distribution panel.
  • Test and Disconnect Points: Providing a plug‑in interface for circuit isolation during maintenance and testing.
  • Underground to Above‑Ground Transitions: Terminating cables at transition boxes where underground cables meet above‑ground equipment.
  • Mobile and Temporary Installations: Enabling rapid deployment and reconfiguration of AGL systems in temporary airfield setups.

The plug assembly is available in straight and right‑angle configurations, with cable gland sizes to accommodate various primary cable gauges. It is rated for continuous operation at 6.6A or 20A primary current.

Placement

Installation & Location

The plug assembly is terminated onto the cable end at the location where a primary circuit connection is required, typically inside an equipment pit, handhole, or trench section adjacent to an isolating transformer or junction box, since field termination is exactly what the assembly is designed for. The cable is fed through the compression gland before the conductors are crimped or soldered to the contact pins, and the gland is then tightened to grip the cable jacket and seal the entry point — a step that is as important to the finished seal as the pin termination itself. Once assembled, the plug is mated to its corresponding receptacle with the coupling ring threaded fully home, and the completed connection is left with enough cable slack to sit naturally within the pit rather than under tension. Where the assembly serves a test or disconnect point, it is positioned so it remains accessible at finished grade rather than buried under permanent fill.

Function

How Does It Work?

The plug assembly provides a secure, low‑resistance electrical termination for the primary cable while enabling rapid connection and disconnection to equipment‑mounted receptacles. Its functional components work together as follows:

  • Contact Pins: Two machined brass pins (male) with gold or tin plating provide the electrical interface. The pins are crimped or soldered to the primary cable conductors, creating a gas‑tight, low‑resistance joint. The pins are designed to mate with the receptacle's socket contacts with consistent contact force.
  • Cable Gland: A compression‑type gland secures the primary cable and provides strain relief. When tightened, the gland compresses an elastomeric seal around the cable jacket, preventing moisture ingress and providing mechanical retention.
  • Coupling Ring: A large, knurled ring on the connector body threads onto the receptacle's external threads. When tightened to the recommended torque (3.5–5.5 N·m), it compresses the internal O‑rings, forming a watertight seal and locking the connection against vibration and accidental disconnection.
  • O‑Ring Seals: Multiple O‑rings (silicone or EPDM) provide IP68 sealing when the connector is fully mated. The seals are compressed by the coupling ring to form a watertight barrier.
  • Insulating Insert: A high‑dielectric insert (typically glass‑filled nylon or similar) holds the contact pins in position and provides electrical insulation between them and the connector shell.

When properly assembled and torqued, the plug assembly provides a connection that can remain submerged in water for extended periods without degradation. Its low contact resistance ensures minimal voltage drop and heating, critical for maintaining the performance of the constant‑current series circuit.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The plug assembly is the cable-side half of an inline connection in the series circuit loop, mating with a receptacle to carry the loop's full commanded current across its pin contacts. It has no addressability of its own — the ALCMS and CCR regulate the loop end-to-end and have no direct visibility into any individual plug/receptacle pair. When fully mated and torqued to specification, the connection is electrically transparent to the control system. As the connection ages, pin wear, an under-torqued coupling ring, or a compressed O-ring past its service life raises contact resistance, and because the CCR holds the loop current constant rather than voltage, that added resistance shows up as increased loop voltage at the commanded current step. A plug that backs out, cracks, or lets moisture bridge the pins is reported by the CCR as an open-circuit or ground-fault condition on the loop — indistinguishable at the control level from any other break — and pinpointing it to that specific connection still requires physical inspection of each mated pair on the affected section.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Plug Assembly (mated to receptacle) → Loop continues (with loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The plug assembly is supplied as a complete, factory‑built unit rather than loose parts: a moulded connector body carries the two gold‑ or tin‑plated brass pins at its mating face, backed by internal O‑ring seals that compress against the receptacle once the knurled coupling ring is threaded down, and a compression‑type cable gland at the rear where the primary cable enters. Field termination consists of stripping the cable conductors, crimping or soldering them to the rear of each pin, and closing the cable gland around the cable jacket to seal the entry point before the assembly is mated to its receptacle. The body material — typically a UV‑stabilised, glass‑filled thermoplastic — and the multiple O‑ring seal points at the pin face, the shell‑to‑body joint, and the cable gland are what allow the finished, mated connection to hold an IP68 rating even under continuous submersion in a flooded duct or pit.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Contact Pins

Despite plating, pins can corrode in saltwater or chemically aggressive environments, increasing contact resistance. Regular inspection and cleaning with contact cleaner, along with application of dielectric grease, are recommended.

Improper Cable Termination

Poorly stripped or crimped cable terminations can cause high resistance and overheating. Use the proper stripping and crimping tools, and ensure the cable conductors are fully inserted into the termination points. Follow the manufacturer's recommended crimp dimensions.

O‑Ring Degradation

O‑rings can harden, crack, or lose elasticity over time due to UV exposure, temperature cycles, or chemical attack. Replace O‑rings periodically and use silicone grease to prolong their life.

Incomplete Mating & Loose Coupling

If the coupling ring is not fully tightened, the connection may be loose, leading to intermittent contact, arcing, and overheating. Always tighten to the recommended torque and verify that the connection is secure.

Damaged Threads on Coupling Ring

Cross‑threading or impact can damage the coupling ring threads, making proper mating difficult or impossible. Protect threads with caps when not in use and inspect before mating. Replace damaged assemblies.

Incorrect Cable Gland Selection

Using a gland that does not match the cable diameter will fail to seal properly, allowing water ingress. Always select the correct gland size for the cable being used. The gland should be tightened to the manufacturer's recommended torque.

Over‑Tightening of Cable Gland

Excessive torque on the cable gland can damage the cable insulation or crush the conductor, leading to a high‑resistance connection. Use a torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Elevated Contact Resistance at the Plug Pins
Likely Cause
Corrosion has developed on the plated contact pins despite their protective coating, typically from saltwater or chemically aggressive site conditions.
Diagnostic Steps
Disconnect the assembly and inspect pins for discolouration or pitting; measure contact resistance across the mated connection.
Corrective Action
Clean the pins with approved contact cleaner and apply dielectric grease before re-mating; replace the plug assembly if pitting is found on the contact surface.
High Resistance or Overheating Traced to the Cable Termination
Likely Cause
The cable was poorly stripped or crimped, or conductors were not fully inserted into the termination points during assembly.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the crimp for correct conductor insertion depth and crimp geometry; measure resistance across the termination under load.
Corrective Action
Re-strip and re-crimp using the proper tools and manufacturer-recommended crimp dimensions, ensuring full conductor insertion before re-testing.
Water Ingress Inside the Plug Body
Likely Cause
The O-ring has hardened, cracked, or lost elasticity from UV exposure, temperature cycling, or chemical attack, compromising the seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Disassemble and visually inspect the O-ring for hardness, cracking, or flattening; check for moisture or condensation inside the plug body.
Corrective Action
Replace the O-ring with the correct size and material, lubricate with silicone grease, and re-mate and re-seal the assembly before returning it to service.
Intermittent Contact, Arcing, or Overheating at the Coupling
Likely Cause
The coupling ring was not tightened to the specified torque, leaving the mated assembly loose under vibration or thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Check coupling ring tightness against the manufacturer's torque specification; inspect contacts for arc marks or heat discolouration.
Corrective Action
Disassemble, inspect the contacts for damage, and re-mate the assembly, tightening the coupling ring to the correct torque with a torque wrench.
Plug and Receptacle Will Not Mate Properly
Likely Cause
Cross-threading or impact damage has deformed the coupling ring threads, or debris is lodged in the threaded interface.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coupling ring threads for galling, stripping, or deformation, and check for debris; attempt to mate a known-good half to isolate the fault.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged plug assembly, and protect unused connectors with dust caps to prevent thread damage and debris ingress going forward.
Water Ingress Traced to the Cable Gland
Likely Cause
An incorrectly sized cable gland was fitted for the cable diameter, or the gland was not tightened to the specified torque, leaving an incomplete seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the gland size against the cable outer diameter and confirm it was tightened to the manufacturer's specified torque; inspect for gaps around the cable jacket.
Corrective Action
Fit the correctly sized gland and tighten to the specified torque; if the cable insulation shows crushing from prior over-tightening, replace the affected cable section.
High-Resistance Connection Linked to Cable Gland Over-Tightening
Likely Cause
Excessive torque was applied to the cable gland, damaging the cable insulation or crushing the conductor beneath it.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the cable jacket and conductor at the gland entry point for crushing or deformation; measure resistance through that section of cable.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged cable section and gland, and re-tighten using a torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Contact Pin Corrosion
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion on the plated contact pins despite their protective coating, typically from saltwater or chemically aggressive site conditions.
Effect
Elevated contact resistance at the plug pins that can lead to overheating under load.
Detection Method
Disconnection and inspection of pins for discolouration or pitting, and a contact-resistance measurement across the mated connection.
Poor Cable Termination Crimp
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The cable poorly stripped or crimped, or conductors not fully inserted into the termination points during assembly.
Effect
High resistance or overheating traced to the cable termination inside the plug body.
Detection Method
Inspection of crimp geometry and conductor insertion depth, and a resistance measurement across the termination under load.
O-Ring Seal Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
The O-ring hardening, cracking, or losing elasticity from UV exposure, temperature cycling, or chemical attack.
Effect
Water ingress inside the plug body, risking corrosion and eventual electrical failure.
Detection Method
Disassembly and visual inspection of the O-ring for hardness, cracking, or flattening, and a check for moisture inside the plug body.
Under-Torqued Coupling Ring
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The coupling ring not tightened to the specified torque, leaving the mated assembly loose under vibration or thermal cycling.
Effect
Intermittent contact, arcing, or overheating at the coupling.
Detection Method
Coupling ring torque check against specification, and inspection for arc marks or heat discolouration on the contacts.
Cross-Threaded Coupling Ring
Low Severity
Root Cause
Cross-threading or impact damage deforming the coupling ring threads, or debris lodged in the threaded interface.
Effect
The plug and receptacle will not mate properly, or will not fully seat.
Detection Method
Inspection of coupling ring threads for galling, stripping, or deformation, and a check for debris, confirmed by mating a known-good half.
Cable Gland Sizing or Torque Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
An incorrectly sized cable gland for the cable diameter, or a gland not tightened to the specified torque.
Effect
Water ingress traced to the cable gland, or — where over-tightened — a high-resistance connection from a crushed conductor.
Detection Method
Gland size and torque check against specification, and inspection of the cable jacket and conductor at the gland entry for crushing or gaps.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Mating or Unmating
Electrical Safety

Never connect or disconnect a plug assembly on a live series circuit; de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy at the pins before separating a mated pair.

Correct Coupling Ring Torque
Workmanship

Tighten the coupling ring to the manufacturer's specified torque; under-torquing leaves the mated assembly loose under vibration, while cross-threading or over-torquing can damage the coupling threads.

O-Ring & Gland Inspection
Workmanship

Inspect the O-ring for hardness or cracking and confirm the cable gland matches the actual cable diameter and torque before mating; a compromised seal here is a leading cause of water ingress inside the plug body.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Check contact condition, termination crimp, coupling torque, and seal integrity before restoring power; a marginal connection found after re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Confined Space
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working inside a base can, and follow confined-space precautions where the can is deep or poorly ventilated.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the plug is exposed, and secure or remove loose material before closing the base can.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a plug assembly unmated, under-torqued, or with a suspect seal; verify the connection and reseal the base can before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A 'Working' Plug Can Still Be Marginal Under Load
Troubleshooting

A plug assembly can pass a continuity check at zero or low load yet develop resistance heating once carrying full series-circuit current; where nuisance CCR trips recur at one location, check contact resistance under actual load current, not just continuity.

O-Ring Compression Set Happens Even Without Visible Damage
Durability

An O-ring can lose its sealing force through compression set after repeated mate/unmate cycles or years in place, without showing cracking or visible wear; replace it on schedule at overhaul rather than only when it looks obviously degraded.

Coupling Ring Torque Affects Contact Pressure, Not Just Mechanical Security
Electrical

Under-torquing the coupling ring doesn't just risk the assembly working loose under vibration, it can leave contact pressure at the pins below spec even while the plug appears fully mated.

Strain Relief Failures Show Up as Intermittent Faults, Not Hard Failures
Workmanship

A plug assembly with inadequate strain relief on the cable entry can develop an intermittent connection from repeated flexing long before it fails completely; an intermittent fault reported at a fixture is worth checking at the plug's strain relief, not just the contacts.

Confirm Gland Torque Independently of Coupling Ring Torque
Workmanship

Technicians sometimes assume a correctly torqued coupling ring also means the cable gland is sealed; the gland must be checked and torqued to its own specification, since the two seals are independent.

Log Which Base Cans Have Had Recent Plug Work
Compliance

Because plug assembly faults commonly reappear at the same location shortly after a repair, keeping a simple log of recent connector work by base can location helps a follow-up crew recognize a recurring problem rather than treating each callout as unrelated.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about plug assemblies for L-823 connectors: termination, ratings, standards, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is a plug assembly?

A plug assembly is the complete, ready-to-terminate cable-mounted half of the L-823 two-pin waterproof connector system used on AGL primary series circuits. It is a factory-assembled kit including the connector body, gold- or tin-plated pins, coupling ring, seals and cable gland.

Q2Why is it supplied as a complete kit?

So the cable side of a primary connection can be terminated reliably in a trench or equipment pit rather than a workshop, while still achieving the same IP68 seal integrity as a factory joint.

Q3Where are plug assemblies used?

At field cable ends where primary series cables terminate at isolating transformers, junction boxes and distribution points, and on CCR output cables.

Q4What are the typical ratings of a plug assembly?

6.6 A or 20 A continuous depending on variant, up to 5 kV transient and 600 V continuous, cable compatibility of 6-16 mm² (AWG 10-6), IP68 when mated, at least 500 mating cycles, and -40°C to +85°C operating temperature.

Q5How is a plug assembly terminated to the cable?

By crimp or solder termination, with pins matched to the conductor size. The termination must carry the rated current without derating, and initial contact resistance is typically 0.5 milliohm or less per contact pair.

Q6What torque and insulation resistance apply?

The coupling ring is typically tightened to 3.5-5.5 N·m, and the mated connection should show at least 50 megohms insulation resistance when megger tested to ground.

Q7Which standards apply to plug assemblies?

FAA AC 150/5345-26 (Specification for L-823 Plug and Receptacle, Cable Connectors) and ICAO Annex 14 for general AGL circuit component requirements.

Q8How does a plug assembly relate to the receptacle?

The plug assembly is the cable-mounted half, and it mates with a receptacle half, for example on an isolating transformer, to complete the L-823 connection.

Q9How often should plug assemblies be maintained?

Annually: clean the contact pins and reapply dielectric grease, verify cable terminations, inspect and replace O-rings, check coupling ring threads, confirm gland torque, and verify mating and coupling torque. Keep protective caps on when the plug is disconnected.

Q10Why is contact resistance high at the plug pins?

Corrosion on the plated pins, typically from saltwater or aggressive chemical exposure, raises resistance. Disconnect and inspect the pins for discolouration or pitting and measure contact resistance across the pair.
