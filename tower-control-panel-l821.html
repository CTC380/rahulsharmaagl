What is a tower control panel (L-821)?

The Fact Tower control point

L-821 is the FAA designation for a panel for the control of airport lighting. It is the point where the operator turns lighting systems on and off and sets their brightness: a panel of switches, push buttons or a touchscreen, normally installed in the airport traffic control tower. Its switches operate regulators, beacons, obstruction lights, lighted wind indicators, apron lights, emergency generators and the main lighting systems, and set the intensity of those that have steps. At an airport without a tower, the panel goes somewhere convenient, such as a hangar, an office or the electrical vault.

The panel does not power the lights. It sends control signals over control wiring, directly or through relays, to the regulators, and it shows lamps for what has been selected. Whether those lamps show only the selection or the actual state of the field depends on the design. FAA AC 150/5345-3G groups panels by Type, Class, Style and Mode, all printed on the nameplate; two modes are special, a land and hold short panel and a stop bar panel, and the stop bar panel is a dedicated panel, separate from the main one. A panel that disagrees with the airfield, through a dead lamp, a switch that no longer operates its relay or a regulator left in Local, misleads the controller, so lamp tests and checks against the field matter.

L-821 panel

A dedicated panel built to AC 150/5345-3, with a switch, button or touch area for each lighting system. Often the main control point at a smaller airport.

ALCMS tower HMI (L-890)

A screen connected through servers and field controllers, with access levels, status and alarms (Entries 6 and 7). Whether an L-821 panel is kept alongside it is a design decision.

  1. Decide. The controller chooses what the traffic and visibility need, following the airport’s procedures.
  2. Select. Operate the switch or button for the lighting system, or touch its area on a touchscreen.
  3. Set the brightness. Choose the intensity step where the system has steps. A push-button panel brings a system on at its lowest step and returns it there when switched off.
  4. Read the lamps. The lamps and backlighting show what was selected; the lamp test proves the lamps themselves work.
  5. Check the field. Confirm that the lights respond, on the monitoring display or by what the controller can see.
  6. Report. Tell maintenance about any lamp, switch or light that does not respond, and record it.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is a tower control panel (L-821)?

L-821 is the FAA designation for a panel for the control of airport lighting. It is where the operator switches lighting systems on and off and sets their brightness, normally in the airport traffic control tower, using switches, push buttons or a touchscreen. It is built to FAA AC 150/5345-3.

Q2What do Type, Class, Style and Mode mean on an L-821 panel?

Under AC 150/5345-3G, Type is I conventional, II facsimile (a layout of the airfield on the panel) or III mimic or PLC-based. Class is F flush mounted, S surface mounted or W wall or box mounted. Style is 1 unlighted, 2 backlighted or 3 touchscreen. Mode is 1 main panel, 2 land and hold short or 3 stop bar panel. The nameplate shows all four.

Q3How is an L-821 panel different from an ALCMS tower HMI?

An L-821 panel is a dedicated control panel with a switch, button or touch area for each lighting system. An ALCMS (L-890) tower HMI is a screen connected through servers and field controllers, with access levels, status and alarms. A Type III L-821 panel is PLC-based with a touchscreen, so the two can look alike. Which one an airport uses, and whether it keeps both, is a design and procedure decision.

Q4What is a stop bar control panel?

It is a Mode 3 L-821 panel. It must be a dedicated panel in the tower, separate from the main lighting control panel, with an ON/OFF push button for each stop bar it controls, a diagram of the airport, status lights and a red ALERT button. A master switch on the main panel, used for low-visibility operations, activates it. The details depend on the airport's low-visibility plan and air traffic control.

Q5What goes wrong with a tower control panel?

Dead lamps, a switch that no longer operates its relay, a regulator left in Local so that the panel is ignored, labels that no longer match the airfield after a change, and lamps that show what was selected rather than what the lights are doing. Use the lamp test, compare the panel with the field status, and report faults to maintenance.
