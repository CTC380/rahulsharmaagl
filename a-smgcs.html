Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control (A-SMGCS)

How surveillance, identification, routing, guidance and safety support combine into one surface-management system, and how it connects to airfield ground lighting, stop bars and Follow the Greens.

Start Learning Follow the Greens
Overview

What Is A-SMGCS?

Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control System (A-SMGCS) is an integrated airport surface-management system that improves the safety, efficiency and situational awareness of aircraft and vehicle movements on the aerodrome surface. Conventional arrangements depend heavily on visual observation, radio, signs, markings and separate lighting systems. A-SMGCS combines surveillance, identification, routing, guidance and safety support in one operational environment.

EUROCONTROL describes it as a system that improves airport throughput while keeping the required level of safety. Its basic surveillance service gives position, identification and tracking of mobiles, and further services can add airport safety support, routing and guidance.

Need

Why Is A-SMGCS Required?

Busy periods at large airports can involve hundreds of surface movements. In good visibility controllers and pilots can keep strong visual awareness. In reduced visibility that becomes much harder. A-SMGCS gives controllers an electronic picture of surface traffic and supports safety functions that visual observation alone cannot provide.

      Surveillance

      Multilateration, Radar, ADS-B and Identification

      Multilateration (MLAT)

      A network of receiving stations fixes the position of a cooperating aircraft or vehicle from signals received at several locations. A Mode-S equipped aircraft can also give identification that is tied to the track: transponder → ground sensors → position calculation → identification → ATC display.

      Surface movement radar (SMR)

      Detects surface traffic and gives a radar-derived picture of the movement area. It is one input to A-SMGCS, not the same thing as A-SMGCS.

      ADS-B

      Where aircraft equipment and the airport system support it, ADS-B can add position, identification, altitude, ground speed and other broadcast data.

      Cameras and other sensors

      Can support visual verification, vehicle tracking, stand and runway monitoring, intrusion detection and safety alerts. The goal is an accurate, operationally useful surface picture, not just more data.

      Identification

      Surveillance tells the system where a target is. Identification says what it is: call sign, flight ID or transponder identity for aircraft, and a vehicle transponder, assigned ID, operational database or tracking system for vehicles. An alert for “Aircraft AIC123” is far more useful than one for “Unknown Target 57”.

      Safety Support

      Runway Monitoring, Conflict Detection and Conformance

      Runway incursion monitoring

      Flags unauthorized or conflicting runway occupancy, such as aircraft + runway + aircraft or aircraft + runway + vehicle. An extra safety layer beyond controller observation.

      Conflict detection

      Converging aircraft, conflicting taxi clearances, runway occupancy conflicts, unauthorized movement, route deviation and entry to restricted areas.

      Conformance monitoring

      Compares the expected route with the actual one. Wrong taxiway, unexpected stop or deviation can raise an alert under the configured operational rules.

      Routing

      Routing: Static and Dynamic

      Routing determines the intended surface trajectory. It can consider origin, destination, runway, taxiway configuration, closures, restricted areas, aircraft characteristics, operational constraints and active routes.

      Static route

      A pre-defined route is selected from known movement paths.

      Dynamic route

      The system generates a route from the current airport situation. More useful with taxiway closures, heavy traffic, restricted crossings, temporary work zones or aircraft that need alternative routes.

      Dynamic routing capability depends on the implementation.
      Guidance and AGL

      Where A-SMGCS Meets Airfield Ground Lighting

      Guidance turns the authorized route into something the pilot can follow. EUROCONTROL identifies guidance functions that can include automated switching of taxiway centreline lights, stop bars and AVDGS. That creates the link A-SMGCS → AGL control → taxiway lighting.

      Control chain

      Follow the Greens

      One important example of A-SMGCS guidance interfacing with AGL: ATC clearance → A-SMGCS routing → AGL control → green centreline lights → pilot follows the route. At more advanced sites aircraft position progresses the lighting route automatically. It should be treated as part of a larger surface-management architecture, not just green lights.

      Stop bars and runway guard lights

      Stop bars protect runway holding positions, selected intersections and crossings. A-SMGCS may control their status under approved logic, so route, green centreline, stop bar and clearance must stay consistent. Contradictory lighting states must be prevented by the control architecture and procedure. Runway guard lights add another layer alongside signs, markings and ATC instructions.

      Taxiway lighting as active guidance

      The network may include green centreline, blue edge, stop bars, runway guard lights and controlled intersections. A-SMGCS can decide which segments are active, which is very different from switching a whole taxiway circuit on.

      Interface boundary

      A-SMGCS manages and supports surface movement. ALCMS manages and monitors airfield lighting. They can be integrated, and the engineering boundary between CNS/ATM and AGL must be clearly defined.

      Operations

      Low Visibility, CAT II/III, Capacity, Vehicles and Human Factors

      Low visibility

      Fog, reduced RVR, night operations, high traffic and complex taxi routes make an electronic surface picture valuable because it depends less on direct visual observation.

      CAT II / CAT III

      Low-visibility operations need runway and taxiway lighting, stop bars, guard lights, signage, surveillance, communication, ATC procedures and surface movement control together. A-SMGCS can be a significant part, but it is not the sole system responsible. Capability depends on the full set of facilities, procedures, equipment and approvals.

      Capacity

      A better surface picture helps controllers see traffic distribution, occupied taxiways and route conflicts, reduce uncertainty and handle traffic more efficiently. EUROCONTROL lists improved throughput as a benefit alongside safety.

      Vehicles

      Follow-me, maintenance, fire and rescue, operations, airside engineering and service vehicles can be tracked. Vehicle identification may need transponders, ID devices, fleet databases, position tracking and communications. A small airport needs a far simpler system than a large hub.

      Human factors and the controller display

      Technology does not automatically improve safety. The HMI must show identity, position, track, route, alerts, runway occupancy, system status and degraded conditions clearly. A typical display holds the airport map, runway and taxiway geometry, aircraft and vehicle symbols, call signs, tracks, stop bars, alerts and route information. Poor display design adds controller workload.

      The change in thinking

      “Where do I think the aircraft is?” becomes “Where does the surveillance system show it is?” And “Which route did it intend to follow?” becomes “Which route is assigned, and is it conforming?”

      Failure Modes

      A-SMGCS Failure Modes

      Click a failure to see what to examine.

      Surveillance failure

      What happens when a sensor is unavailable?

      • Single sensor loss
      • Coverage gap
      • Degraded-mode procedure defined

      Track and identification failure

      Track lost, or identity cannot be matched:

      • Lost or false track
      • Unknown target in the display
      • Alert references an unidentified target

      Communication failure

      Field equipment cannot receive commands:

      • Network or switch fault
      • Field controller offline
      • Defined safe state of the lighting

      AGL failure

      Taxiway guidance lighting is unavailable:

      • Segment or circuit fault
      • CCR fault
      • System must identify it and apply the prescribed response

      Routing failure

      Route generation becomes unavailable:

      • Routing function down
      • Database error
      • Fallback to controller routing

      Server failure

      A processing node fails:

      • Single point of failure
      • Failover behaviour
      • Data synchronization

      Network failure

      Link between subsystems is interrupted:

      • Redundant path available?
      • Latency or loss of integrity
      • Time synchronization

      ATC workstation failure

      The controller loses the primary display:

      • Backup display or position
      • Alert path
      • Degraded-mode procedure

      Address these through architecture, redundancy, degraded-mode procedures and operational testing. The question is not “is the system redundant?” but “what functionality remains after each defined failure?”. Redundancy may cover servers, networks, sensor processing, communications, power, databases and controller workstations.

      Communication network

      • Sensors → processing → ATC → ALCMS → field controllers
      • Latency and availability
      • Redundancy and fault detection
      • Time synchronization and data integrity
      • Network segmentation
      • Matters most when A-SMGCS directly controls field visual aids

      Cybersecurity

      • Network segmentation
      • User authentication and privileged access
      • Secure configuration and logging
      • Software updates
      • Incident response, backup and recovery
      • Integrated with the airport’s wider IT/OT security architecture

      Photometry

      A-SMGCS and Photometry

      A-SMGCS may control the lights, but it does not remove the need to verify their physical and optical performance. The AGL still needs electrical, functional and photometric testing, aiming and colour verification and maintenance inspection. For dynamically controlled centreline lighting, also verify that the correct segment responds to the correct command.

      Does the system command the correct light?

      A control and integration question, answered by functional and integrated testing.

      Does that light give the required optical performance?

      A photometry question, answered by measurement. Photometry does not validate the routing logic.

      Asset database

      The digital airport model holds runway coordinates, taxiway geometry, holding positions, stands, AGL assets, route segments, stop bars, restricted areas and surveillance coverage. It must stay synchronized with the physical airport. A taxiway change completed on site but not in the database can create serious operational problems.

      Expansion

      A new runway, taxiway, apron, rapid exit, remote stand or terminal apron means reviewing A-SMGCS: survey → database update → surveillance coverage → route update → AGL update → control logic → testing. It is an ongoing asset, not a one-time project.

      A-SMGCS vs Surface Movement Radar

      SMR is primarily a surveillance technology. A-SMGCS is an integrated system that can add identification, safety support, routing and guidance. A radar is one input, not a synonym.

      A-SMGCS vs ALCMS

      A-SMGCS manages and supports surface movement. ALCMS manages and monitors airfield lighting. Example: A-SMGCS route → ALCMS guidance command → green centreline lights.

      A-SMGCS is an evolution of the overall surface movement management concept.

      Digital Airport

      Operational Database, Digital Airport and Future Automation

      Operational database

      Linking A-SMGCS to flight plans, stand allocation, runway in use, taxiway status, closures, aircraft information and relevant NOTAM-related data keeps routing based on a current picture of the airport.

      Digital airport

      A-SMGCS is part of a digitally represented airport surface in which movements and infrastructure are continuously reflected in operational systems, connecting aircraft, vehicles, surveillance, ATC, AGL, stand systems and data networks.

      The long-term direction includes automated surveillance, predictive alerts, dynamic routing, intelligent lighting control, vehicle tracking, better conformance monitoring and decision-support tools. Introduce automation according to the airport’s operational requirements, safety case, system performance and regulatory framework.

      FAQ

      A-SMGCS FAQ

      Q1What is A-SMGCS?

      An advanced airport surface-management system that integrates surveillance and may provide safety-support, routing and guidance functions for aircraft and vehicles on the aerodrome surface.

      Q2What are the main functions of A-SMGCS?

      The commonly described service set is surveillance, airport safety support, routing and guidance.

      Q3Is A-SMGCS the same as Surface Movement Radar?

      No. Surface Movement Radar is a surveillance technology. A-SMGCS is an integrated system that can use radar alongside other surveillance sources and provide additional services.

      Q4What is the role of multilateration?

      Multilateration can provide surface position and identification information from cooperating aircraft or vehicles and forms part of the A-SMGCS surveillance layer.

      Q5Does A-SMGCS control taxiway lights?

      It can. In an appropriate implementation the guidance service can interface with lighting-control systems to automate taxiway centreline and stop-bar switching.

      Q6What is the relationship between A-SMGCS and Follow the Greens?

      Follow the Greens is a surface-guidance concept in which selected green taxiway centreline segments show an authorized route. It can be implemented as part of the A-SMGCS guidance function.

      Q7Does A-SMGCS replace ATC?

      No. It is a decision-support and surface-management system that supports controllers and approved operational procedures. It does not replace human ATC responsibilities.

      Q8Is A-SMGCS used in India?

      Yes. AAI lists ASMGCS as part of its airport surveillance infrastructure, and AAI aeronautical information publications document A-SMGCS based on Mode-S multilateration at several major airports. Confirm the current status for a specific airport.

      Q9Is A-SMGCS useful for low visibility?

      Yes. AAI describes ASMGCS as supporting surface movement control and monitoring in low visibility. It is one part of the facilities, procedures and approvals that make up low-visibility capability.

      Q10Does A-SMGCS require ALCMS?

      Not every implementation has the same guidance architecture, but an airport using dynamic AGL guidance can require an interface between A-SMGCS and ALCMS/ILCMS.

      Q11Does A-SMGCS require green taxiway centreline lights?

      No. Green centreline lighting is one possible guidance mechanism. A-SMGCS is a broader surveillance, safety, routing and guidance system.

      Q12Does A-SMGCS require photometric testing?

      Where A-SMGCS controls visual guidance lighting, the underlying AGL still needs electrical, functional and optical testing. Photometry verifies the physical lighting performance. It does not validate the routing logic.

      Field Insight

      Engineering & Expert Notes

      RS
      By Rahul Sharma
      Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
      A Clean Track on the Screen Is Not a Verified Light Response
      Integration

      A-SMGCS may show a vehicle on the correct taxiway while the stop bar or guidance segment it should trigger has not changed state; test the surveillance-to-lighting handshake end to end, not each subsystem in isolation.

      Treat Time Synchronisation as a Safety Parameter
      Architecture

      Multilateration, radar and ADS-B fusion depend on a common time base; a drifting reference can degrade position accuracy before any alarm appears, so monitor and log sync status as carefully as sensor health.

      Ghost and Dropped Tracks Cluster Where the Airfield Geometry Is Hardest
      Field Practice

      Multipath and shadowing tend to show up near large buildings, aprons and hangars. Log where false or lost tracks recur and review sensor placement before blaming the software.

      Prove the Fallback When the Surveillance Layer Is Lost
      Failure Modes

      Confirm what the lighting and procedures do when surveillance degrades: controllers should know which guidance reverts to manual or procedural control and how that state is displayed to them.

      Commission With Real Movements, Not Only Test Targets
      Commissioning

      Injected test targets prove logic, but only controlled vehicle and aircraft runs across runway crossings and intersections expose coverage gaps, latency and mis-association in the real environment.

      Keep Configuration Baselines Under Change Control
      Maintenance

      Zone boundaries, routing tables and alert thresholds are safety settings. Record who changed them and why, and re-verify the affected functions after any change, update or sensor replacement.
