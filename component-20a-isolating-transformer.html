20A Isolating Transformer

A high‑current isolating transformer designed for 20A AGL series circuits — typically used in specialised, high‑power airfield installations. With a primary rating of 20A, this transformer delivers significantly more power than the standard 6.6A variants, catering to demanding military airfields, high‑intensity approach lighting systems, and large‑scale obstruction lighting configurations.

Overview

What Is It?

The 20A Isolating Transformer is a heavy‑duty, single‑phase transformer engineered for use in 20A constant‑current AGL series circuits. Unlike the industry‑standard 6.6A transformers, the 20A variant is deployed in specialised installations where the lighting load exceeds the capacity of conventional 6.6A loops — such as high‑intensity approach lighting systems (e.g., ALSF‑2 with extended bars), large obstruction lighting arrays on very tall structures, and military airfields with exceptionally high lighting power requirements.

The transformer provides the same galvanic isolation, fault tolerance, and fixture‑level control as its 6.6A counterparts, but is built with substantially heavier gauge windings, a larger core, and enhanced thermal management to handle the elevated primary current and associated power transfer. It is typically deployed in specialised pits or enclosures with dedicated ventilation or cooling provisions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The 20A isolating transformer exists because some lighting loads — extended approach lighting bars, large obstruction arrays on very tall structures, or heavy-duty military installations — simply draw more power than a standard 6.6A loop can practically deliver. Rather than forcing these high-demand systems onto multiple parallel 6.6A circuits with all the added complexity that implies, a 20A primary lets a single loop carry the higher current these fixtures need.

Handling that higher current safely is what drives the heavier windings, larger core, and enhanced cooling: a transformer under-built for its primary current risks overheating or premature insulation failure, which would undermine the very isolation and fault-tolerance the transformer exists to provide in the first place.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The 20A isolating transformer is reserved for the most demanding AGL applications, including:

  • High‑Intensity Approach Lighting Systems: Extended ALSF‑2 and military approach configurations requiring very high luminous output from barrettes, crossbars, and sequenced flashing lights, where the total loop load exceeds the 6.6A capacity.
  • High‑Power Obstruction Lighting: Extremely tall structures (e.g., >500m communication towers, chimneys) requiring high‑intensity white flashing lights with peak intensities exceeding 200,000 cd, often using 500W or 1,000W lamps.
  • Large Airport Beacons: High‑power rotating beacons with multiple 500W lamps, requiring significant primary current to drive the beacon's illumination and rotation.
  • Military Airfields & Specialised Operations: Military airfields often specify 20A circuits for enhanced reliability, redundancy, and the ability to power high‑intensity tactical lighting systems in combat or extreme weather conditions.
  • Long Runways with High Fixture Density: Very long runways (e.g., >4,000m) with densely spaced lighting may exceed the power capacity of a single 6.6A loop, necessitating a 20A loop.
  • Multi‑Fixture Circuits: Configurations where a single transformer powers multiple high‑intensity fixtures (e.g., five 100W fixtures, ten 50W fixtures, or combinations) requiring total secondary power up to 2,000W.

The transformer is typically installed in large, well‑ventilated handholes or dedicated transformer pits, using specialised primary connectors rated for 20A (often L‑823 or larger variants) and heavy‑gauge secondary cabling (≥ 16mm²) to handle the increased current.

Placement

Installation & Location

Because it carries the higher-current 20A series loop, this transformer is installed in a larger, well-ventilated handhole or dedicated transformer pit rather than a standard small enclosure, to dissipate the greater heat load and accommodate the heavier-gauge cabling. Primary connections use L-823 (or larger) connectors rated for 20A service, and secondary cabling of at least 16mm² is run to the fixture to keep voltage drop and heating within limits. These pits are typically sited on high-fixture-density runways, extended approach lighting systems, or military circuits, and are spaced and sized so maintenance crews can access and test each unit without disturbing the loop cabling.

Function

How Does It Work?

The 20A transformer operates on the same electromagnetic induction principle as other AGL isolating transformers, but with significant design enhancements for high‑current operation. The primary winding is connected in series with the 20A constant‑current loop. The primary current induces a secondary voltage — typically in the range of 6V to 120V depending on the turns ratio and the secondary power rating — which powers the connected fixture(s).

Specific design features of the 20A variant include:

  • Large‑Gauge Windings: Primary and secondary windings use very thick copper wire to minimise I²R losses and prevent overheating at the elevated primary current (20A vs. 6.6A).
  • High‑Flux Core: The core is significantly larger to handle the high flux density and prevent saturation at 20A, using high‑grade grain‑oriented silicon steel laminations.
  • Enhanced Thermal Management: The encapsulation compound is formulated with high thermal conductivity, and the transformer housing often includes cooling fins or a larger surface area to dissipate heat more effectively.
  • Optimised Turns Ratio: Designed to deliver the required secondary voltage at 20A primary current, with custom voltages available for specific fixture types (e.g., 30V for 600W, 60V for 1,200W).
  • Robust Short‑Circuit Protection: The transformer's leakage impedance limits secondary fault currents, but short‑circuit conditions at 20A are far more severe than at 6.6A, requiring immediate attention and protection.
  • High Dielectric Strength: With greater power transfer, the insulation system is rated for higher voltage stress, typically exceeding 3kV primary‑secondary.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The 20A transformer occupies the same position in the control chain as any 6.6A-loop transformer, just on a different-capacity circuit: an Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or pilot-controlled lighting input, and the CCR regulates the whole 20A loop to the corresponding fixed current. The transformer has no addressable identity of its own — it converts whatever current the loop is carrying at that moment into the voltage its fixture or fixture group needs, and cannot be commanded independently of the rest of the loop. Because a 20A circuit is reserved for the highest-load installations, a loop fault detected by the CCR's voltage monitoring can potentially affect a large extended approach lighting array, a very tall obstruction light, or a long high-density runway section in one event; the choice between a 6.6A and 20A loop for a given installation, and what that implies for the scale of a single fault, is covered in the loop current selection logic below.

Construction

Physical Construction

The 20A-rated transformer is a substantially larger and heavier assembly than the 6.6A range, reflecting the heavier-gauge conductor its primary winding needs to carry the higher loop current: even at an equivalent wattage, the winding and core are not interchangeable with a 6.6A unit. Construction otherwise follows the same principle as the rest of the range — windings around a laminated core, fully encapsulated in a moulded shell that is vacuum-impregnated and back-filled with epoxy or polyurethane resin, which is what gives the unit its IP68 rating directly from the potting. Complete units run from roughly 25 to 50 kg depending on the power option (500W to 2,000W), so mechanical handling and lifting provision at the base can are planned for during installation. Insulation is specified to Class H, dielectric strength is higher than the 6.6A range at 4kV primary-secondary, and leads terminate in heavier-gauge sealed splice connectors sized for the higher fault current a 20A loop can present.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Extreme Heat Generation & Thermal Management

20A transformers generate substantially more heat than 6.6A units — often 3 to 4 times higher. In poorly ventilated pits, heat accumulation can lead to rapid insulation degradation, core saturation, and premature failure. Use large pits with excellent drainage and airflow, avoid burial in water‑logged soils, and consider forced ventilation or cooling systems for high‑power installations.

Primary Connector Overheating & Contact Resistance

At 20A primary current, the power dissipated in primary connectors is substantial (P = I²R). Even a small increase in contact resistance (e.g., corrosion or loose connections) can cause localised overheating, connector melting, and fire risk. Use only high‑quality L‑823 or high‑current connectors, apply anti‑corrosion compound, and perform torque checks as part of routine maintenance.

Secondary Cable Sizing & Voltage Drop

The secondary current at 20A primary can be extremely high (e.g., 2,000W/100V = 20A). Ensure secondary cables are adequately sized (≥ 16mm² or 6 AWG) to prevent voltage drop and overheating, especially over longer cable runs. Use proper cable glands and maintain insulation integrity.

Dimming Compatibility with CCR

20A circuits often use 3‑step or custom dimming profiles (e.g., 6.6A, 13.3A, 20A) rather than the standard 5‑step (2.8A to 6.6A). At lower current steps, the transformer operates at reduced flux density, which can lead to increased harmonic distortion, audible noise, and interference with sensitive electronics. Verify compatibility with the CCR's dimming profile and waveform quality.

Parallel Fixture Wiring & Load Sharing

When using a 20A transformer to power multiple fixtures in parallel, ensure the total load does not exceed the transformer's rated power, that the wiring configuration maintains proper current sharing, and that individual fixture connections are secure. Use a distribution junction box with appropriate protection.

High Inrush Current & CCR Tripping

The larger core and heavier windings result in a significantly higher inrush current during energisation (often 5‑10 times the nominal current for a few cycles). This can cause nuisance tripping of circuit breakers or the CCR. Consider using soft‑start features, selecting appropriate protective devices, or coordinating with the CCR manufacturer for proper sizing.

Weight, Handling & Installation

At 25‑50 kg, the 20A transformer is extremely heavy and requires mechanical lifting aids for installation and maintenance. Ensure proper handling procedures, lifting equipment, and safety protocols are in place to prevent injury or damage to the transformer during installation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

CCR/Breaker Trips on Energisation (Inrush)
Likely Cause
The large core and heavy windings of a 20A transformer draw a high inrush current for the first few cycles at energisation — often 5–10 times nominal — which nuisance-trips protective devices.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR or breaker's trip log for an instantaneous overcurrent event at energisation rather than a sustained fault; confirm protective device settings and coordination against the transformer manufacturer's inrush data.
Corrective Action
Coordinate protective device sizing and any soft-start feature with the CCR/transformer manufacturer; adjust trip settings if they are undersized for the documented inrush profile.
Overheating in the Transformer Pit
Likely Cause
20A transformers generate substantially more heat than 6.6A units (often 3–4×), and poor pit drainage or ventilation prevents that heat from dissipating.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the pit for standing water or restricted airflow; if accessible, check the transformer casing temperature against manufacturer limits during operation.
Corrective Action
Improve pit drainage, enlarge the pit, or add forced ventilation/cooling for high-power installations; do not bury the unit in water-logged soil.
Primary Connector Overheating or Melting
Likely Cause
At 20A primary current, even a small increase in connector contact resistance dissipates significant power (P = I²R), causing localised overheating.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect L-823 or high-current connectors for discolouration, melting, or pitting; check torque and contact surface condition.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged connectors with high-quality, high-current-rated types, apply anti-corrosion compound, and re-torque to specification; add connector torque checks to the routine maintenance schedule.
Audible Noise or Harmonic Distortion at Low Dimming Steps
Likely Cause
At reduced current steps (e.g., 6.6A of a 6.6A/13.3A/20A profile), the transformer operates at reduced flux density, which can increase harmonic distortion and cause audible hum.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the CCR's dimming profile matches the transformer's design steps; check for audible noise correlating with specific brightness steps.
Corrective Action
Verify compatibility with the CCR manufacturer's dimming profile and waveform quality, and adjust the step configuration if mismatch is confirmed.
Secondary Voltage Drop on Long Cable Runs
Likely Cause
Undersized secondary cable for the high secondary current (which can reach 20A) causes excessive voltage drop, especially over longer runs.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure secondary voltage at the transformer and again at the fixture under load to quantify the drop; check secondary cable gauge against the run length.
Corrective Action
Upgrade secondary cable to an adequately sized conductor (≥ 16mm² / 6 AWG or as specified) to bring voltage drop within tolerance.
Transformer Damaged or Dropped During Installation
Likely Cause
At 25–50 kg, mishandling without proper lifting equipment can damage the core, windings, or encapsulation before or during installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect for visible physical damage, cracked encapsulation, or dented casing before energising; megger-test before backfill if damage is suspected.
Corrective Action
Replace a damaged unit rather than installing it, and use appropriate mechanical lifting aids and handling procedures for future installations.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Primary Winding Open Circuit
High Severity
Root Cause
Wire fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown internal fuse-wire link from a lightning-induced transient.
Effect
Opens the series loop, darkening every fixture downstream of the transformer on that circuit.
Detection Method
CCR loop-continuity/open-circuit indication, and megger or continuity testing at the transformer primary.
Insulation Breakdown from Moisture Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or degraded encapsulation, a failed cable gland or seal, or prolonged submersion in a flooded handhole or pit.
Effect
Reduced insulation resistance leading to a partial or full ground fault that can trip the CCR.
Detection Method
Megger testing against the ≥2 MΩ minimum, and visual inspection of the encapsulation and cable entries.
Secondary Winding Short or Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal insulation failure between secondary turns, often accelerated by sustained overtemperature operation.
Effect
Loss of secondary voltage regulation, fixture dimming or failure, and possible primary overcurrent.
Detection Method
Secondary voltage measurement under load, compared against the transformer's rated output.
Overload / Core Saturation from Rating Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A CCR step or connected fixture load exceeding the transformer's rated primary current or secondary power.
Effect
Increased core losses, overheating, and accelerated insulation ageing.
Detection Method
Verification of the CCR step current against the transformer's rating plate, and thermal inspection where accessible.
L-823 Connector / Termination Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating during installation, gasket failure allowing moisture in, or galvanic corrosion building up at the connector pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent contact, flicker, or a high-resistance joint that generates localised heat.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at scheduled maintenance, and continuity or resistance checks across the joint.
Encapsulation Cracking / Case Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Freeze-thaw cycling, mechanical impact during installation or maintenance, or age-related degradation of the potting compound.
Effect
Exposes the winding to moisture, accelerating insulation breakdown.
Detection Method
Visual inspection during handhole or pit access, and a drop in insulation resistance on megger testing.
Thermal Degradation from Sustained Overcurrent
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated or prolonged operation above rated current, often from an incorrectly set CCR step or a transformer undersized for the installed load.
Effect
Progressive insulation embrittlement that shortens service life and raises the risk of an eventual winding failure.
Detection Method
Rating verification against actual loop current, and insulation resistance trending across successive maintenance cycles.
Output Voltage Drift / Regulation Loss
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual winding degradation or core ageing that shifts the transformer's turns-ratio performance away from its original specification.
Effect
Fixture brightness drifts slightly from the intended intensity step without an outright fault.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity survey and secondary voltage measurement compared against commissioning records.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The isolating transformer sits on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the transformer before opening a handhole, pit, or connector housing.

Confirm Correct Transformer in Circuit
Operational

On multi-transformer series circuits, positively identify the correct unit before isolating work, since opening the wrong handhole can leave live conductors exposed while the intended fixture stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the primary and secondary windings against the minimum insulation resistance before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy transformers on the loop.

Rating Verification Before Replacement
Compliance

Confirm the replacement transformer's rated primary current and secondary power match the CCR step and connected fixture before installing it; an incorrectly rated unit will saturate, overheat, or under-drive the fixture.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the transformer case and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

FOD Control at the Handhole
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when a handhole or pit is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the cover, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Hot Surface Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the transformer case to cool before handling after an extended active period, since sustained loop current can leave the encapsulation and connectors noticeably warm to the touch.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a transformer with a degraded insulation reading, a loose connection, or a mis-seated cover after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and secure closure before closing out the work order.

Never Cross-Stock With 6.6A Units
Compliance

A 20A-rated transformer must never end up in a 6.6A circuit's spares bin or vice versa. Keep 20A stock physically separated and clearly labelled given the consequence of a mix-up.

Confirm the Whole Circuit Is Actually 20A Before Any Component Swap
Electrical

Verify the CCR and every other transformer sharing that loop are genuinely rated for 20A before treating a replacement as routine — one wrong-rated component defeats the purpose of the higher-current design.

Specialised Circuits Warrant Specialised Documentation
Maintenance

Because 20A installations are less common and often tied to a specific high-power or military application, keep circuit-specific documentation — loop diagram, rated components list — readily available rather than assuming standard 6.6A references apply.

Higher Primary Current Changes the Thermal Picture at the Handhole
Inspection

A 20A primary circuit generates more heat in cabling and connections than a 6.6A one at equivalent duty. Factor that into handhole thermal inspection expectations rather than applying 6.6A-based assumptions.

Confirm the Applicable Insulation Minimum Before Accepting a Reading
Inspection

Confirm Personnel Are Specifically Trained on This Circuit Type
Operational

Because 20A systems are less common, don't assume every technician qualified on standard 6.6A AGL work is automatically familiar with this circuit's specific procedures. Verify training before assigning the work.

Typical maintenance tasks and intervals are: Thermal Imaging Survey (annual); Primary Connector Torque & Corrosion Check (annual); Insulation Resistance Test (annual); Secondary Cable Sizing & Voltage Drop Check (annual); CCR Dimming Compatibility Test (semi-annual); Pit Ventilation & Drainage Inspection (annual); Inrush & Protective Device Check (annual). Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9How do I troubleshoot common 20A Isolating Transformer faults?

Common symptoms covered are: CCR/Breaker Trips on Energisation (Inrush); Overheating in the Transformer Pit; Primary Connector Overheating or Melting; Audible Noise or Harmonic Distortion at Low Dimming Steps; Secondary Voltage Drop on Long Cable Runs; Transformer Damaged or Dropped During Installation. For example, for "CCR/Breaker Trips on Energisation (Inrush)": Likely Cause: The large core and heavy windings of a 20A transformer draw a high inrush current for the first few cycles at energisation — often 5–10 times nominal — which nuisance-trips protective devices. Diagnostic Steps: Check the CCR or breaker's trip log for an instantaneous overcurrent event at energisation rather than a sustained fault; confirm protective device settings and coordination against the transformer manufacturer's inrush data. Corrective Action: Coordinate protective device sizing and any soft-start feature with the CCR/transformer manufacturer; adjust trip settings if they are undersized for the documented inrush profile.
