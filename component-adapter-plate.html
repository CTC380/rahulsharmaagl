Adapter Plate

A versatile mounting plate that enables the installation of AGL fixtures, signs, or equipment onto existing base plates, base cans, or structures with different bolt patterns or mounting configurations. Designed to bridge compatibility gaps, simplify retrofits, and reduce installation costs while maintaining structural integrity and precise alignment.

Overview

What Is It?

An Adapter Plate (also known as a conversion plate, mounting adapter, or interface plate) is a precision‑machined steel or aluminium plate that provides a mechanical interface between AGL equipment (fixtures, signs, sensors) and existing mounting structures (base plates, base cans, poles) that have different bolt patterns, diameters, or mounting configurations. It allows new equipment to be installed on legacy infrastructure without the need for costly and disruptive pavement modifications.

Key characteristics of adapter plates include:

  • Dual Bolt Patterns: The plate features two sets of mounting holes: one set matches the existing base (base plate or base can), and the other set matches the new fixture's mounting pattern.
  • Precision Machining: The plate is machined to tight tolerances to ensure accurate alignment of the fixture's optical axis and correct beam orientation.
  • High Strength: Made from high‑grade steel (S235JR or S355JR) or aluminium, designed to withstand the same loads as the original mounting structure.
  • Corrosion Protection: Hot‑dip galvanised, powder‑coated, or stainless steel construction ensures long‑term durability in the harsh airfield environment.

Adapter plates are used in the following scenarios:

  • Fixture Replacement: When replacing an old AGL fixture with a new model that has a different bolt pattern or mounting height.
  • Retrofit Upgrades: When upgrading from halogen to LED fixtures, or from one manufacturer's fixture to another.
  • Compatibility Bridging: When installing new equipment (signs, sensors, junction boxes) onto existing foundations.
  • Height Adjustment: Some adapter plates also provide a small height increase (10‑50mm) to bring the fixture to the correct elevation.

The adapter plate is typically installed between the existing base plate (or base can top flange) and the new fixture's base plate. It is bolted to the existing structure using the original anchor bolts, and the new fixture is bolted to the adapter plate using new bolts. This provides a secure, level, and accurately aligned mounting solution without modifying the pavement.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The adapter plate exists to protect the airfield's investment in its lighting circuits and pavement while enabling technology upgrades and manufacturer changes. Rather than requiring an old base plate, base can, or foundation to be demolished and re-cast whenever a fixture with a different bolt pattern is fitted — work that would take a runway or taxiway out of service and disturb the surrounding pavement — the adapter plate lets the new fixture bolt straight onto the existing infrastructure. Its function is therefore twofold: it preserves structural continuity, carrying the same design loads (weight, wind, impact, vibration) as the original mounting, and it preserves optical accuracy, since precise hole location and orientation marking ensure the new fixture's beam is still aimed correctly. In this way the adapter plate supports lifecycle upgrades — most commonly halogen-to-LED retrofits — without compromising either the mechanical integrity or the photometric performance the airfield depends on.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Adapter plates are used throughout the airfield in retrofit and upgrade projects, including:

  • Runway Lighting Retrofits: Installing new LED runway edge lights, centreline lights, or threshold lights onto existing base plates with different bolt patterns.
  • Taxiway Lighting Upgrades: Replacing old taxiway edge lights or centreline lights with new fixtures that have different mounting dimensions.
  • Approach Lighting Conversions: Retrofitting approach lighting systems with new fixtures while reusing existing foundations.
  • Signage Replacement: Installing new airfield signs (mandatory instruction, direction, information) onto existing sign bases.
  • Obstruction Lighting Upgrades: Replacing old obstruction lights with new LED units on existing poles or structures.
  • Sensor Mounting: Installing new weather sensors, RVR sensors, or other instrumentation on existing mounting structures.
  • Manufacturer Changes: Converting from one equipment manufacturer's products to another when bolt patterns differ.

The adapter plate is designed to match the specific bolt patterns of both the existing infrastructure and the new equipment. It is available in various sizes (150mm to 400mm diameter or square) and thicknesses (10mm to 25mm) to accommodate different load requirements and fixture weights.

Placement

Installation & Location

The plate is bolted directly onto the existing base can or foundation, using the original bolt circle, with the new fixture's mounting holes drilled or pre-machined into the plate's upper face to the new equipment's pattern. A gasket is fitted on both faces, below the plate against the base can and above it against the fixture, so the swap doesn't introduce a new leak path into an otherwise sealed foundation. Fitting is checked for flatness and full contact across the plate before final torqueing, since any rocking under wheel or foot load will fatigue the bolts over time, and the plate's thickness is chosen to keep the new fixture at the correct beam or optical height once stacked on top of the original foundation.

Function

How Does It Work?

The adapter plate functions as a mechanical interface that translates between two different mounting systems. Its design ensures that the new fixture is securely and accurately mounted on the existing foundation:

  • Bolt Pattern Translation: The plate has two sets of holes. The lower set matches the existing base plate or base can's bolt pattern (e.g., 4‑hole on a 150mm PCD, M12 bolts). The upper set matches the new fixture's bolt pattern (e.g., 6‑hole on a 200mm PCD, M16 bolts). This allows the new fixture to be bolted securely to the existing foundation.
  • Load Transfer: The plate transfers all loads (vertical weight of the fixture, wind loads, vibration, impact) from the new fixture down through the existing base plate and into the pavement. The plate is designed to be at least as strong as the original mounting structure.
  • Alignment: The plate is machined with precise hole locations and is marked with orientation indicators to ensure that the fixture is correctly aligned. This is critical for maintaining the required beam direction and photometric performance.
  • Height Compensation: Some adapter plates are designed with an integrated height offset to compensate for differences in mounting height between the old and new fixtures, ensuring that the fixture is at the correct elevation above the pavement.

The installation process typically involves:

  • Cleaning: The existing base plate or base can flange is cleaned and inspected for damage.
  • Adapter Placement: The adapter plate is positioned on the existing base, aligning the lower bolt holes with the existing anchor bolts.
  • Levelling: The adapter plate is levelled using shims or a levelling compound to ensure the top surface is perfectly horizontal.
  • Securing: The adapter plate is bolted to the existing base using the original anchor bolts (or new bolts if the originals are damaged).
  • Fixture Mounting: The new fixture is placed on the adapter plate, aligned, and secured with new bolts through the upper bolt holes.
  • Alignment Check: The fixture's orientation is checked using a digital level or alignment jig to ensure the beam is correctly aimed.

The adapter plate is designed to provide a service life of 20+ years, with regular inspection for corrosion, bolt tightness, and damage.

System Architecture

Existing Base Plate / Base Can (Original Bolt Pattern — e.g. 4-Hole, 150mm PCD, M12)

Weight, wind, vibration, and impact loads all pass straight down through this stack into the pavement — the plate must be at least as strong as the original mounting structure it bridges.

Construction

Physical Construction

The plate is CNC-machined from steel or aluminium billet or plate stock to hold flatness and bolt-hole tolerances that keep both mating flanges square once torqued together, since any warp or misalignment translates directly into fixture tilt. Steel plates are typically hot-dip galvanised or zinc-plated, and aluminium plates anodised, to resist corrosion in the buried or semi-buried environment of a base can. Two bolt-hole patterns are machined into the same plate — one matching the legacy base plate, base can, or pole, the other matching the new fixture's mounting flange — with a gasket or O-ring groove on one or both faces so the stack still meets the fixture's IP rating once assembled. Stainless-steel fasteners, torqued to the fixture manufacturer's spec, complete the joint on both sides of the plate.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Bolt Pattern Verification
Pre-Installation

Confirm the plate's hole pattern, bolt circle diameter, and thread size match both the new fixture and the existing base plate or can before offering it up.

Existing Surface Preparation
Mechanical

Clean and inspect the legacy base plate, base can, or pole mounting surface for corrosion or damage before the adapter plate is offered up.

Gasket/O-Ring Seating
Mechanical

Fit the gasket or O-ring into the retaining groove on one or both faces before assembly, so the completed stack meets the fixture's IP rating.

Levelling & Shimming
Mechanical

Set the plate flush and level on the mounting surface using a spirit or laser level, shimming as required to remove any gap before final bolting.

Galvanic Isolation Fitting
Mechanical

Fit isolating washers or coatings between dissimilar metals at the joint, preventing galvanic corrosion over the assembly's service life.

Stainless Fastener Torquing
Mechanical

Torque stainless-steel fasteners on both sides of the plate to the fixture manufacturer's spec, in the correct sequence, to seat the gasket evenly.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record bolt torque values, shim thickness, and gasket condition as-built before the fixture on the adapter plate is energised.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Bolt Pattern Mismatch

If the existing bolt pattern is not accurately measured, the adapter plate may not fit. Always verify the existing bolt pattern, hole diameter, and PCD (pitch circle diameter) before ordering the adapter plate. Use a bolt‑pattern gauge or accurate measurement tools.

Corrosion at Interface

Moisture and de‑icing fluids can become trapped between the adapter plate and the existing base, causing corrosion. Apply anti‑seize compound or a corrosion‑inhibiting coating between the mating surfaces, and use stainless steel bolts.

Inadequate Bolt Length

The existing anchor bolts may not be long enough to secure both the adapter plate and the fixture. Use longer bolts or bolt extensions. Ensure that there is sufficient thread engagement (at least 1.5 times the bolt diameter).

Misalignment of Fixture Beam

If the adapter plate is not oriented correctly, the fixture's beam will be misaimed. Mark the orientation (e.g., "forward", "towards runway") on the adapter plate and the existing base. Use an alignment jig to verify the beam direction after installation.

Insufficient Plate Thickness

Using a plate that is too thin for the fixture weight or wind loads can result in bending or deformation, causing misalignment or failure. Select a plate with adequate thickness and strength for the specific application.

Galvanic Corrosion with Dissimilar Metals

If the adapter plate and the existing base plate are made of dissimilar metals (e.g., galvanised steel adapter on an aluminium base), galvanic corrosion can occur. Use isolation gaskets, anti‑seize compound, or ensure both components are of the same material.

Inadequate Levelling

If the adapter plate is not level, the fixture will be tilted, affecting the beam pattern. Use shims and a precision level during installation. Grout under the plate if necessary to ensure full support.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fixture Beam Misaimed After Installation
Likely Cause
Adapter plate installed in the wrong orientation, or the plate itself is not sitting flat/level on the existing base.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the orientation marking against the required beam direction (e.g. "towards runway"); verify plate levelness with a precision level; confirm with an alignment jig.
Corrective Action
Remove and reinstall the plate in the correct orientation, re-shim or re-grout to correct level, and re-verify aim with the alignment jig before re-torquing fasteners.
Plate Won't Seat Flush / Bolt Holes Don't Line Up
Likely Cause
Bolt pattern, hole diameter, or PCD mismatch between the adapter plate and the existing base, often from a measurement error before ordering.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-measure the existing base's bolt pattern, hole diameter, and PCD with a bolt-pattern gauge; compare against the plate's as-manufactured dimensions.
Corrective Action
Order a correctly dimensioned replacement plate; do not force-fit or elongate holes in the field, as this can compromise load capacity and alignment.
Corrosion or Staining at the Mating Interface
Likely Cause
Trapped moisture or de-icing fluid between the plate and base, often worsened by galvanic action if the plate and base are dissimilar metals.
Diagnostic Steps
Remove the fixture and inspect the mating surfaces for pitting, white/red corrosion products, or staining; check whether the plate and base materials are compatible.
Corrective Action
Clean and treat the interface, apply anti-seize compound or a corrosion-inhibiting coating, fit isolation gaskets if metals are dissimilar, and use stainless steel bolts on reassembly.
Plate Shows Bending or Deformation
Likely Cause
Plate thickness undersized for the fixture weight or local wind loading, allowing flex over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and dimensionally check the plate for warping against a straightedge; confirm the specified plate thickness matches the fixture's weight and site wind-load rating.
Corrective Action
Replace with a plate of adequate thickness and strength rated for the application; re-verify level and beam aim after replacement.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Incorrect Installation Orientation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Orientation marking overlooked or unclear during installation, or the required beam direction was not communicated to the installer.
Effect
Fixture beam is misaimed after installation despite the plate itself being correctly seated and torqued.
Detection Method
Alignment jig check against the reference orientation, and inspection of the plate's orientation marking during commissioning.
Bolt Pattern / PCD Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Measurement error before ordering, or an assumption that the existing base matched a standard bolt pattern without verification.
Effect
Plate will not seat flush or its bolt holes do not line up with the existing base, halting installation.
Detection Method
Bolt-pattern gauge measurement of the existing base compared against the plate's as-manufactured dimensions before installation.
Mating Interface Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Trapped moisture or de-icing fluid between the plate and base, often accelerated by galvanic action where the plate and base are dissimilar metals.
Effect
Pitting, staining, or corrosion product builds up at the interface, eventually loosening the joint or degrading load transfer.
Detection Method
Periodic removal and visual inspection of the mating surfaces, and confirmation of plate/base material compatibility.
Plate Bending or Deformation
High Severity
Root Cause
Plate thickness undersized for the fixture's weight or the site's wind loading, allowing repeated flex over time.
Effect
Fixture leans or its aim drifts as the plate continues to flex, eventually risking fatigue cracking of the plate itself.
Detection Method
Dimensional check against a straightedge for warping, and periodic beam-aim verification during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Assume the Equipment Below Is Live
Electrical Safety

Treat the base can, base plate, or pole beneath the adapter plate as energised until the associated circuit has been de-energised, locked out, and verified zero energy, even though the plate itself is a passive mechanical part.

Manual Handling
Personnel Safety

Adapter plates can be heavy for their size, particularly steel variants; use correct lifting technique and avoid working alone on larger plates.

Bonding and Earthing Continuity
Electrical Safety

Where the plate forms part of the equipment's earthing/bonding path, verify continuity across the plate after installation, since a poorly seated or painted-over interface can silently break an earth path that looks mechanically sound.

Correct Torque and Load Rating
Mechanical

Torque mounting fasteners to the manufacturer's specification and confirm the plate's load rating matches the fixture and traffic it will carry; an under-torqued or under-rated plate can work loose or fail under aircraft/vehicle loading.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep fasteners and hardware accounted for at all times during fitting, and confirm nothing is left loose on the pavement before reinstating the fixture.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the adapted mounting is flush, correctly torqued, and bonded before closing out the work and returning the fixture to service.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Match the Plate Material to the Base, Not Just the Bolt Pattern
Material Selection

A plate can share bolt spacing with the base can and still be the wrong choice if its material sets up galvanic corrosion against a dissimilar-metal base; specify plate material and any required isolating gasket together, not the hole pattern alone.

Shim Thickness Adds Up Faster Than It Looks
Installation

Stacking several thin shims to correct fixture height under an adapter plate can quietly push the fastener grip length outside spec; where more than one or two shims are needed, re-check bolt length and thread engagement rather than assuming the original hardware still applies.

Re-Check Torque After the First Thermal Cycle
Mechanical

Freshly installed adapter plates on aluminium or composite bases often lose a measurable amount of fastener torque after their first heat-cool cycle as the interface beds in; a short-interval re-torque check shortly after commissioning catches this before it becomes a loose-fixture complaint.

Paint and Coatings Can Hide a Broken Bond Path
Electrical

A repainted or recoated plate can look perfectly seated while the coating itself has broken electrical continuity across the mating faces; strip contact surfaces back to bare metal at the bonding points rather than relying on a visual fit-up check alone.

Keep a Small Stock of Off-Standard Plates
Field Practice

Older bases and newer fixtures don't always share a common bolt circle; keeping a handful of adapter plates for known legacy-to-current mismatches on the truck avoids a return trip when a straight swap doesn't line up in the field.

Standing Water Under the Plate Is the Usual Corrosion Starting Point
Durability

Most premature corrosion at the plate-to-base interface starts from trapped moisture rather than atmospheric exposure alone; confirm drainage paths and gasket seating at every overhaul, not just when a corrosion complaint is already raised.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the adapter plate: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an adapter plate?

An adapter plate (also called a conversion plate, mounting adapter or interface plate) is a precision-machined steel or aluminium plate that provides a mechanical interface between AGL equipment such as fixtures, signs and sensors and existing mounting structures such as base plates, base cans and poles with different bolt patterns or diameters.

Q2Why use an adapter plate?

It lets new equipment be installed on legacy infrastructure without costly and disruptive pavement modifications, which is why it is common in retrofit and upgrade projects.

Q3How does an adapter plate work?

It has two sets of holes. The lower set matches the existing base bolt pattern (for example 4 holes on a 150 mm PCD with M12 bolts) and the upper set matches the new fixture (for example 6 holes on a 200 mm PCD with M16 bolts). The plate transfers vertical, wind, vibration and impact loads from the fixture down into the existing foundation.

Q4Where are adapter plates used?

In runway lighting retrofits (LED edge, centreline and threshold lights onto existing base plates), taxiway lighting upgrades and approach lighting conversions, wherever new fixtures have different mounting dimensions from the old ones.

Q5What are the typical materials and dimensions?

Structural steel (S235JR or S355JR), hot-dip galvanised to at least 85 µm, or 316L stainless steel, with 6061-T6 aluminium for lightweight applications. Thickness is 10 mm to 25 mm and size 150 mm to 400 mm, with custom sizes available.

Q6Can an adapter plate change the fixture height?

Yes. An optional height offset of 10 mm to 50 mm compensates for fixture height differences.

Q7How flat and how strong is an adapter plate?

Flatness tolerance is 0.5 mm or better over the plate area, and vertical load capacity is typically 10 kN to 100 kN depending on thickness and material. Accurate machining preserves correct beam orientation.

Q8Which standards apply to adapter plates?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-42 (light bases and accessories, covering adapter rings and flange hardware), ICAO Annex 14, EN 10025 (structural steel) and ASTM A123 (galvanised coatings). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should an adapter plate be inspected?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for levelling verification, and annual for mounting bolt torque, interface corrosion and galvanic isolation checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with adapter plates?

Common problems are bolt pattern mismatch, corrosion at the interface, inadequate bolt length, misaligned beams, insufficient plate thickness, galvanic corrosion with dissimilar metals and poor levelling. A plate that will not seat flush or bolt holes that do not line up usually indicates a pattern or orientation error.
