Buried Isolating Transformer

A rugged, direct‑burial isolating transformer engineered for installation directly in the ground, without the need for a concrete pit or handhole. Designed for harsh environments, it features a robust, corrosion‑resistant encapsulation and heavy‑duty cable entries, ensuring reliable performance in wet, chemically aggressive, or high‑traffic airfield areas.

Overview

What Is It?

The Buried Isolating Transformer is a heavy‑duty, fully encapsulated single‑phase transformer designed for direct burial in the ground. Unlike standard isolating transformers that are typically installed in concrete pits or handholes, this variant is engineered to withstand the mechanical loads, moisture, and chemical aggression of direct soil contact. Its robust construction, enhanced corrosion protection, and specialised cable sealing make it ideal for installations where a pit is impractical or where maximum protection against environmental factors is required.

The transformer provides the same essential galvanic isolation between the 6.6A (or 20A) primary series circuit and the secondary fixture circuit, ensuring safety and fault containment. It is available in a range of power ratings (15W to 100W) and secondary voltages, catering to most AGL fixture requirements. The direct‑burial design eliminates the need for additional protective enclosures, reducing installation time and material costs.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The buried isolating transformer exists to let a transformer live directly in the ground — without the added cost, footprint, and installation time of a concrete pit — while still surviving prolonged exposure to soil moisture, chemical content, and mechanical loading from vehicles and aircraft overhead. Its enhanced sealing and corrosion protection are what make this direct-burial approach viable rather than a shortcut that compromises reliability.

This matters because a transformer that fails from water ingress or corrosion doesn't just lose its own function; it opens the series loop and can affect every fixture downstream of it, depending on circuit design. Building the unit to withstand direct soil contact from the outset removes a failure mode that a standard pit-mounted transformer would otherwise depend on the pit itself to prevent.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The buried isolating transformer is specifically suited for installations where:

  • Space is Limited: In congested airfield areas where there is insufficient room for a concrete pit or handhole, direct burial is the only viable option.
  • High Water Table: In regions with a high water table or where flooding is common, the buried transformer's superior sealing prevents water ingress.
  • Chemically Aggressive Soils: In areas with corrosive soils (e.g., coastal regions with salt content, industrial areas with chemical spills), the specialised encapsulation and corrosion‑resistant materials ensure long‑term reliability.
  • High Mechanical Loads: In areas subject to heavy vehicle traffic (e.g., runway turn‑offs, heavy apron areas), the buried transformer can withstand the compressive loads without damage.
  • Expeditionary or Temporary Airfields: Quick installation without the need for concrete works, allowing for rapid deployment and removal.
  • Retrofit Projects: Replacing existing buried transformers or upgrading hard‑wired systems with direct‑burial units.

The transformer is typically buried at a depth of 300‑600mm (12‑24 inches) below the surface, with the primary and secondary cables routed through specially designed sealing glands to prevent moisture ingress. It is suitable for both 6.6A and 20A series circuits.

Placement

Installation & Location

The buried transformer is installed directly in the ground rather than in a handhole or concrete pit, typically at a depth of 300-600mm below the surface, with its encapsulated body and sealing glands providing the moisture and mechanical protection that would otherwise come from a separate enclosure. Primary and secondary cables are routed through the glands and spliced or terminated using waterproof connectors before backfilling, with the unit positioned close enough to the fixture to keep the secondary run short. Because it has no accessible chamber, its exact location is recorded against the runway or taxiway survey so it can be located for testing or replacement without random excavation, and it is set below plough or frost depth in areas subject to those conditions.

Function

How Does It Work?

Electrically, the buried isolating transformer operates identically to standard AGL transformers: the primary winding is connected in series with the constant‑current loop, and the secondary winding delivers the appropriate voltage to the fixture. The key differentiator lies in its mechanical and environmental design:

  • Reinforced Encapsulation: The transformer is encapsulated in a high‑strength, chemical‑resistant epoxy or polyurethane compound that provides mechanical protection and a barrier against moisture, soil acids, and salts.
  • Heavy‑Duty Cable Entries: The primary and secondary cables enter the transformer through specialised compression glands or moulded seals that maintain IP68 integrity even under high external pressure (e.g., water table).
  • Corrosion‑Resistant Terminals: Internal connections are made with tinned copper, stainless steel, or other corrosion‑resistant materials to prevent degradation from any moisture that may penetrate the seals.
  • Robust Core and Windings: The magnetic core and windings are designed to withstand the higher thermal and mechanical stresses associated with direct burial, including potential ground movement and thermal cycling.
  • Grounding Provisions: A dedicated earthing terminal (often an external lug) ensures proper grounding of the transformer casing to protect against lightning and fault currents.

The transformer is compatible with standard CCR dimming profiles and meets all relevant electrical and safety standards. Its direct‑burial design does not compromise electrical performance; efficiency and regulation remain within the same tolerances as conventional transformers.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Burial changes the transformer's mechanical environment, not its place in the control chain. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or pilot-controlled lighting input, and the CCR regulates the whole loop to the corresponding fixed current; the buried transformer's primary winding carries that current exactly as an elevated or handhole-mounted unit would, with no addressable identity of its own and no way to be commanded independently of the loop. This has a practical consequence specific to the buried variant: because the unit has no accessible chamber, a loop fault traced to its general vicinity by CCR voltage monitoring cannot be visually confirmed the way an open handhole lid would allow — locating the specific failed unit relies on the as-built survey record and, where available, sectionalised loop testing from adjacent accessible points, since excavation on suspicion alone is impractical.

Construction

Physical Construction

A buried unit has no accessible chamber or removable cover by design: the windings and core are fully encapsulated in a chemical-resistant epoxy or polyurethane compound, UV-stabilised for the brief period before backfill covers it, and this potting is the only barrier between the winding and the surrounding soil moisture — specified more robustly than the resin used on a handhole-mounted unit, since it is never opened again once installed. Primary and secondary cable entries use compression glands rather than a gasketed lid, maintaining IP68 integrity even under sustained water-table pressure at the specified 300–600mm burial depth. Terminals are tinned copper or stainless steel to resist any moisture that eventually works past the seal over a multi-decade service life, and because there is no cover to open for a visual check, every electrical connection is made and tested before the unit goes into the ground. Weight is kept comparatively low, around 2.0–4.0 kg, since the unit carries no enclosure or mounting hardware — only the potted winding assembly itself.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Damage During Backfilling

Direct‑burial transformers are vulnerable to mechanical damage from rocks, sharp objects, or heavy compaction equipment during backfilling. Careful installation practices, including a protective sand or gravel layer around the transformer, are essential to prevent damage to the encapsulation and cable entries.

Cable Entry Seal Failure

Over time, the sealing glands or moulded entries can degrade due to soil chemicals, UV exposure (if near the surface), or thermal cycling. This can allow moisture ingress, leading to insulation breakdown. Regular insulation resistance testing (≥ 2 MΩ at 500V) is recommended.

Corrosion of External Grounding Lug

The earthing lug is exposed to soil and can corrode, increasing earth resistance. Use stainless steel or tinned copper lugs and apply anti‑corrosion compound. Periodically measure earth resistance to ensure it remains below the required value (typically ≤ 10 Ω).

Thermal Overheating in Saturated Soils

In water‑logged conditions, the thermal conductivity of the soil can be affected, leading to reduced heat dissipation. This can cause the transformer to overheat, especially at higher power ratings. Ensure adequate drainage and consider using higher‑rated transformers (e.g., 100W instead of 65W) to provide a safety margin.

Incorrect Burial Depth

Burial too shallow can expose the transformer to mechanical damage from surface loads or frost heave. Burial too deep can make maintenance and replacement difficult. Follow manufacturer recommendations for depth and marking of the location for future access.

Secondary Cable Chafing

If the secondary cables are not properly protected where they exit the transformer, they can chafe against rocks or backfill material, leading to insulation damage and short circuits. Use protective conduit or wrapping for the exposed cable section.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Downstream Lights Dark, No Visible Damage
Likely Cause
Mechanical damage to the encapsulation or cable entries during backfilling — from rocks, sharp objects, or compaction equipment — has broken the primary or secondary connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the transformer primary; megger the unit; if the fault location is uncertain, excavate carefully to inspect the encapsulation and cable entries for physical damage.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged transformer, reinstalling with a protective sand or gravel bed around the unit to prevent recurrence.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Degraded cable entry seals or moulded entries — from soil chemicals, thermal cycling, or age — have allowed moisture ingress and reduced insulation resistance.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the transformer (target ≥ 2 MΩ at 500V); if accessible, inspect cable entry seals for cracking or degradation.
Corrective Action
Replace the transformer with correctly sealed cable entries; consider a higher-integrity sealing system if repeat failures occur at the same location.
Elevated Earth Resistance at This Location
Likely Cause
The transformer's external grounding lug has corroded due to prolonged soil contact, increasing earth resistance above the acceptable value.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure earth resistance at the transformer's grounding point (target typically ≤ 10 Ω); if accessible, inspect the grounding lug for corrosion.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded lugs with stainless steel or tinned copper and anti-corrosion compound; re-measure earth resistance before backfill.
Overheating in Water-Logged Soil
Likely Cause
Saturated soil around the transformer has reduced thermal conductivity, limiting heat dissipation and allowing the unit to run hotter than in well-drained ground.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the burial location for standing water or poor drainage; if repeated failures occur at this location, compare the transformer's rated power against actual loop demand.
Corrective Action
Improve site drainage, or install a higher-rated transformer (e.g., 100W instead of 65W) to provide a thermal safety margin at this location.
Secondary Cable Damaged Near the Transformer
Likely Cause
Unprotected secondary cable exiting the transformer has chafed against rocks or backfill material, damaging the insulation and creating a short.
Diagnostic Steps
Excavate carefully around the cable exit point and inspect for chafing, cuts, or exposed conductor.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable section, fitting protective conduit or wrapping over the exposed length before backfilling.
Transformer Cannot Be Located for Maintenance
Likely Cause
Burial depth or lack of surface marking has made the unit difficult to find for scheduled testing or replacement.
Diagnostic Steps
Cross-reference as-built drawings or GPS records against the expected location; use a cable/pipe locator if surface marking is absent.
Corrective Action
Re-survey and mark the location once found, and update records; follow manufacturer burial depth guidance for future installations to keep units accessible.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Primary Winding Open Circuit
High Severity
Root Cause
Wire fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown internal fuse-wire link from a lightning-induced transient.
Effect
Opens the series loop, darkening every fixture downstream of the transformer on that circuit.
Detection Method
CCR loop-continuity/open-circuit indication, and megger or continuity testing at the transformer primary.
Insulation Breakdown from Moisture Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or degraded encapsulation, a failed cable gland or seal, or prolonged submersion in a flooded handhole or pit.
Effect
Reduced insulation resistance leading to a partial or full ground fault that can trip the CCR.
Detection Method
Megger testing against the ≥2 MΩ minimum, and visual inspection of the encapsulation and cable entries.
Secondary Winding Short or Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal insulation failure between secondary turns, often accelerated by sustained overtemperature operation.
Effect
Loss of secondary voltage regulation, fixture dimming or failure, and possible primary overcurrent.
Detection Method
Secondary voltage measurement under load, compared against the transformer's rated output.
Overload / Core Saturation from Rating Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A CCR step or connected fixture load exceeding the transformer's rated primary current or secondary power.
Effect
Increased core losses, overheating, and accelerated insulation ageing.
Detection Method
Verification of the CCR step current against the transformer's rating plate, and thermal inspection where accessible.
L-823 Connector / Termination Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating during installation, gasket failure allowing moisture in, or galvanic corrosion building up at the connector pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent contact, flicker, or a high-resistance joint that generates localised heat.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at scheduled maintenance, and continuity or resistance checks across the joint.
Encapsulation Cracking / Case Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Freeze-thaw cycling, mechanical impact during installation or maintenance, or age-related degradation of the potting compound.
Effect
Exposes the winding to moisture, accelerating insulation breakdown.
Detection Method
Visual inspection during handhole or pit access, and a drop in insulation resistance on megger testing.
Thermal Degradation from Sustained Overcurrent
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated or prolonged operation above rated current, often from an incorrectly set CCR step or a transformer undersized for the installed load.
Effect
Progressive insulation embrittlement that shortens service life and raises the risk of an eventual winding failure.
Detection Method
Rating verification against actual loop current, and insulation resistance trending across successive maintenance cycles.
Output Voltage Drift / Regulation Loss
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual winding degradation or core ageing that shifts the transformer's turns-ratio performance away from its original specification.
Effect
Fixture brightness drifts slightly from the intended intensity step without an outright fault.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity survey and secondary voltage measurement compared against commissioning records.
Direct-Burial Casing Breach from Soil/Groundwater Exposure
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosive or waterlogged soil attacking the casing and cable entries over years of direct-burial service, without the added protection a concrete pit provides.
Effect
Accelerated moisture ingress and insulation breakdown well ahead of the unit's rated service life.
Detection Method
Soil/groundwater condition review at the site, and more frequent insulation-resistance testing on known aggressive-soil circuits.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The buried transformer sits on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the transformer before opening the pit or handling cable connectors.

Water Ingress in Flooded Pits
Electrical Safety

Treat any water standing in a handhole or pit as potentially energised until isolation is confirmed and the transformer has been megger tested; do not reach into a flooded pit before power is proven off.

Confined Space & Pit Access
Operational

Check for gas accumulation, unstable fill, or restricted egress before entering a deep handhole or vault, and follow the aerodrome's confined-space and lone-working procedures for below-grade access.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since moisture ingress at a buried joint is a common source of a residual fault that can trip the CCR.

Lid & Cover Manual Handling
Personnel Safety

Use correct lifting technique or lifting tools for heavy pit lids and frames, and never leave a cover partially raised or unsecured over an open pit on the movement area.

FOD Control at the Open Pit
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the pit is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the cover, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the transformer case and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a pit lid unseated, a cable gland unsealed, or an insulation reading below the acceptable minimum after maintenance; verify a compliant, weathertight closure before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
No Pit Means No Visual Check Without Excavation
Inspection

A direct-burial unit can't be opened for a quick visual inspection the way a handhole-mounted transformer can. Rely more heavily on insulation resistance trending and less on visual condition checks for this variant.

Locate and Mark Before Any Nearby Digging
Operational

Because there's no visible handhole cover to warn off excavation crews, keep accurate as-built records and physical locate markers for buried transformers before any nearby trenching or paving work.

Encapsulation Integrity Is the Only Line of Defence
Durability

With no secondary pit or drainage to fall back on, the encapsulation is the sole barrier against groundwater. Treat any encapsulation damage found on a neighbouring unit as a reason to inspect this one too.

Cable Entry Seals Deserve Extra Scrutiny at Installation
Installation

Because there's no accessible pit to catch and drain a failed seal, get the heavy-duty cable entry seating right at installation — a failure here isn't easily caught early the way it might be in a pit installation.

Replacement Means Excavation, Not a Pit Swap
Maintenance

Factor excavation time and pavement or turf reinstatement into any planned replacement of a direct-burial unit — it's a different job scope than swapping a unit in an accessible handhole.

Shorten the Test Interval in Chemically Aggressive Soil
Inspection

Where a buried transformer sits in an area with known aggressive soil chemistry or a high water table, shorten its insulation test interval relative to buried units in more benign ground conditions.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about buried isolating transformers: direct-burial design, ratings, standards, installation depth, testing, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a buried isolating transformer?

It is a heavy-duty, fully encapsulated single-phase transformer designed for direct burial in the ground. It provides the same galvanic isolation as a standard AGL isolating transformer between the 6.6 A (or 20 A) primary series circuit and the secondary fixture circuit, but withstands the mechanical loads, moisture and chemical aggression of direct burial.

Q2How is a buried isolating transformer different from a pit-mounted one?

A pit-mounted unit sits in a concrete pit or handhole, whereas a buried unit has no accessible chamber or cover. Its windings and core are encapsulated in chemical-resistant epoxy or polyurethane, with compression glands or moulded cable entries that maintain IP68 integrity, so it needs no separate pit. Electrically it operates identically to a standard AGL transformer.

Q3When should a buried isolating transformer be used?

It suits congested areas with no room for a pit, high water tables, chemically aggressive soils such as coastal or industrial ground, areas with heavy vehicle loads such as runway turn-offs and aprons, expeditionary or temporary airfields, and retrofit projects.

Q4What are the typical specifications of a buried isolating transformer?

Primary current is 6.6 A nominal (five steps from 2.8 A to 6.6 A), with 20 A versions also available. Rated power options are 15, 25, 45, 65 and 100 W (custom up to 300 W), with secondary voltages of 6.82 V (45 W), 9.85 V (65 W) and 15.15 V (100 W) at rated current. Insulation is Class H (180°C), protection is IP68, and operating temperature is -40°C to +55°C.

Q5How deep should a buried isolating transformer be installed?

Typically 300 to 600 mm (12 to 24 inches) below the surface, close enough to the fixture to keep the secondary run short, and below plough or frost depth where relevant. Backfill and compact in layers with a protective sand or gravel bed around the unit, avoiding sharp aggregate against the encapsulation or glands, and record the exact position against the survey so it can be found later.

Q6Why does an AGL fixture need an isolating transformer?

The transformer isolates the secondary fixture circuit from the primary series loop, which provides safety and fault containment. A transformer that fails from water ingress or corrosion can open the series loop and, depending on circuit design, affect every fixture downstream, which is why the buried version is built to survive direct soil contact.

Q7Which standards apply to buried isolating transformers?

The main references are IEC 61823 (AGL series transformers for 6.6 A series circuits), FAA AC 150/5345-47 (series to series isolation transformers, including direct-burial variants), FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How should a buried isolating transformer be tested at commissioning?

Before burial, megger-test the primary and secondary windings against earth and each other, inspect the encapsulation for voids or cracks, apply a dielectric (hi-pot) withstand test and verify loop continuity through the primary. With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage, then re-megger after backfill and compaction to catch a pinched cable or cracked seal, and record everything against the as-built depth and position.

Q9How often should a buried isolating transformer be tested and maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for insulation resistance tests, encapsulation integrity checks, burial depth and cover verification, moisture ingress tests, cable and connector inspection and thermal imaging where a test point exists, and semi-annual for secondary voltage and load current verification. Testing is done from the nearest accessible pull point because the unit cannot be inspected without excavation. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why are the lights downstream of a buried transformer dark with no visible damage?

The likely cause is mechanical damage to the encapsulation or cable entries during backfilling, from rocks, sharp objects or compaction equipment, breaking the primary or secondary connection. Confirm series loop continuity through the primary and megger the unit, and excavate carefully to inspect if the fault location is uncertain. Replace the damaged transformer and reinstall it with a protective sand or gravel bed.

Q11Why does the CCR trip on a ground fault at a buried transformer?

Degraded cable entry seals, from soil chemicals, thermal cycling or age, can let moisture in and lower insulation resistance. Megger the transformer and confirm insulation resistance clears the required minimum (the specification table lists at least 50 MΩ, and the field notes cite at least 2 MΩ at 500 V for in-service checks). If the seals are degraded, replace the transformer, and consider a higher-integrity sealing system if failures repeat at the same location.

Q12What problems are specific to buried transformer installations?

Common ones are damage during backfilling, cable entry seal failure, corrosion of the external earthing lug (earth resistance typically should stay at or below 10 Ω), overheating in water-logged soil where heat dissipation is reduced, incorrect burial depth, and chafing of the secondary cable. Mitigations include a protective sand or gravel layer, stainless steel or tinned copper lugs with anti-corrosion compound, good drainage or a higher-rated unit for a thermal margin, and protective conduit on exposed secondary cable.

Q13What safety precautions apply when working on a buried transformer?

The transformer sits on a series loop that can carry several kV, so de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy before opening the pit or handling connectors. Treat any water standing in a handhole or pit as potentially energised until isolation is confirmed and the transformer has been megger-tested, follow confined-space procedures for deep pits, and megger-test the loop before re-energising after any work.
