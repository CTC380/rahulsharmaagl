PAPI Light Unit

The individual light fixture that forms the building block of a Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) system. Each unit houses the lamp or LED module, precision optics, and frangible mounting, projecting a sharply defined red/white beam that provides pilots with accurate glide slope guidance. Designed for reliable 24/7 operation in all weather conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Light Unit is a precision optical fixture that serves as the fundamental building block of a PAPI system. Each unit is a self-contained assembly comprising a light source (halogen lamp or LED module), a precision optical system (reflector and prism/lens), a housing with integral frangible mounting, and an electrical interface (isolating transformer connection).

The unit projects a tightly collimated beam with a sharp red-to-white colour transition at a precisely defined angle. When installed as part of a four‑unit PAPI array, the individual units are set to slightly different elevation angles so that the pilot sees a combination of red and white lights that indicates their position relative to the 3° glide slope. Each unit is designed for long service life, low maintenance, and reliable performance in extreme environmental conditions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The PAPI light unit's purpose is to give a pilot instant, unambiguous feedback on whether they are above, on, or below the correct glide slope, encoded entirely in colour rather than requiring any instrument interpretation — a red light means too low, white means too high, and the specific mix of red and white across the four-unit array tells the pilot exactly how far off the 3° path they are. This design exists because glide slope deviation is one of the most consequential and time-critical pieces of information a pilot needs on approach, and a system that can be read correctly in a fraction of a second, without conscious calculation, removes a significant source of workload and error at exactly the phase of flight where both are most dangerous.

Architecture

Anatomy of a PAPI Light Unit

A PAPI light unit consists of several precision-engineered sub-assemblies working together to produce the characteristic red/white beam pattern:

Light Source

Halogen lamp (100W/150W) or high‑intensity LED array with integrated driver. Provides the raw luminous output for the optical system.

Optical Assembly

Precision‑ground reflector and prism/lens that collimate and shape the beam, creating the sharp red/white transition at the designated angle.

Housing & Sealing

Rugged aluminium or composite body with O‑ring seals, pressure equalisation valve, and moisture vent. IP67/IP68 rated for all‑weather operation.

Electrical Interface

Secondary connector (L‑823) for isolating transformer connection, surge protection, and optional status monitoring contacts.

Frangible Mounting

Breakable coupling and frangible pole interface that shears cleanly on impact, minimising aircraft damage while preserving the fixture.

Optical Window

High‑transmission glass or polycarbonate window with anti‑reflective coating, protecting the optical assembly from contamination.

Application

Where Is It Installed?

PAPI light units are installed in arrays at airfields worldwide, on a variety of runway types and operational environments:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT I, II, III) – as part of the primary visual glide slope system, typically on the left side of the runway.
  • Non‑precision runways – providing essential visual guidance for circling and visual approaches.
  • Military airfields – supporting tactical and training operations where accurate glide slope control is critical.
  • Heliports – in a specialised configuration (Helicopter PAPI) with steeper beam angles (4°–6°).
  • Temporary or portable deployments – using battery‑powered or solar‑powered units for rapid setup.
  • Runways with obstacle constraints – where a defined glide path is essential for safe obstacle clearance.
Placement

Installation & Location

Each of the four units is mounted on its own base at a precisely surveyed position and elevation, since the array's glide slope indication depends on the units being co-planar and set at the correct height relative to the runway threshold and touchdown zone. The units sit far enough back from the runway edge to remain clear of wingtip and blast clearances while still falling within the obstacle-limitation surface requirements for the runway category, with a frangible mounting used given the array's position close to the operational runway strip. Each unit's elevation angle is set during commissioning using a digital level or similar precision instrument, calibrated against the runway's specific glide path angle (commonly 3°) and re-verified periodically, since even a small tilt error shifts the visual glide slope the array presents to an approaching pilot. Power and control cabling runs from the array back to its own dedicated regulator circuit, with cable routing and pit layout planned to keep the four units' wiring runs matched so brightness steps switch in unison across the full array.

Function

How Does It Work?

The PAPI light unit operates on a precise optical principle to provide unambiguous glide slope information to the pilot:

  • Beam Projection: The light source emits a broad beam that is collected and collimated by a parabolic reflector. The collimated beam then passes through a precision prism or lens that creates a sharp transition between white light (above the transition angle) and red light (below the transition angle).
  • Colour Transition: The transition angle is set to the desired glide slope (typically 3°). The white sector of the beam is directed above the transition angle, while the red sector is directed below. The transition is sharp (typically less than 3 arc‑minutes) to provide an unambiguous indication.
  • Intensity Control: The unit receives power from the isolating transformer on a constant current series circuit. The intensity is controlled by the CCR, typically offering 3 or 5 brightness steps (day, twilight, night). Some LED units include automatic intensity adjustment based on ambient light.
  • Array Configuration: In a standard four‑unit PAPI, the units are set to slightly different elevation angles so that the pilot sees:
    • All white – well above the glide path.
    • 3 white / 1 red – slightly above.
    • 2 white / 2 red – on the correct glide path.
    • 1 white / 3 red – slightly below.
    • All red – well below.
  • Frangible Protection: The mounting includes a frangible coupling that breaks on impact, allowing the unit to shear away from the pole without causing significant damage to an aircraft that strikes it.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A PAPI light unit's electrical control is identical in principle to the runway edge light's — the ALCMS or control unit selects a brightness step, and the CCR delivers the corresponding current through the unit's isolating transformer — but the four units in the array are never controlled to show different things from each other electrically. What produces the red/white transition pattern the pilot reads is a fixed optical setting, not a control signal: each unit's elevation angle is staggered at installation, so the same shared current produces a white or red indication depending purely on where the pilot's eye falls relative to that unit's own transition angle.

This means the array's glide-slope information comes from four units acting in electrical lock-step but optical independence — the control system only ever adjusts overall brightness together, while it's the mounting-base alignment of each unit that determines whether the pilot sees 2 white/2 red on the correct path or something else.

ALCMS → CCR → Isolating Transformer → 4 Units (shared current, staggered optics)

Operating Principle

Beam Optics & the Four-Unit Array

Every PAPI unit produces the same sharp white/red split internally — what differs between the four units is where that transition angle is aimed. Stacking four slightly offset transition angles turns a single-bit red/white signal into a five-position glide slope readout.
Construction

Physical Construction

The sub-assemblies come together inside a single die-cast aluminium or composite body, machined internally to hold the reflector and light source at a fixed, precisely toleranced optical alignment so the beam's angle and sharp red/white transition are set by the housing geometry itself rather than by field adjustment of the internals. The prism or lens sits at the front behind the optical window, both gasket-sealed to the housing with O-rings to keep the internal optics free of dust and moisture, while a pressure-equalisation valve or moisture vent lets the sealed cavity breathe with temperature changes without drawing in liquid water. At the rear, the electrical interface — the secondary connector and any surge protection or monitoring contacts — passes through its own sealed gland into the housing, and the frangible mounting foot bolts to the underside, positioned so the coupling's designed shear point sits below the optical assembly and protects it from a lateral impact. Fasteners throughout are corrosion-resistant to hold the assembly rigid, since any flex between housing and optics would shift the beam angle away from its calibrated setting.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Beam Misalignment

Even a small angular error in a single PAPI unit shifts the effective glide path. Regular alignment checks using a theodolite or specialised alignment tool are essential, especially after maintenance, lamp replacement, or any ground disturbance. The transition angle must be verified to within ±0.05° (3 arcmin).

Optical Contamination

Dust, rubber deposits, de‑icing fluids, and insect residue on the optical window or prism degrade light output and can blur the transition. Routine cleaning with non‑abrasive materials and photometric testing are required to maintain the sharp red/white transition.

Lamp or LED Degradation

In halogen units, lamp output declines with age and burnout is common. In LED units, individual LEDs can fail or the module can experience colour shift. Regular inspections and timely replacement are necessary to avoid misleading indications.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Wind loading, vibration, and thermal cycling can weaken the frangible coupling over time. Regular inspection for cracks or deformation ensures the unit will break away cleanly on impact, preventing aircraft damage.

Moisture Ingress

Failed O‑rings, damaged seals, or blocked pressure vents can allow moisture to enter the housing, causing fogging, corrosion, or electrical faults. Pressure testing and seal inspection should be part of routine maintenance.

Connector Corrosion

The L‑823 secondary connector contacts can corrode in aggressive environments (marine, industrial). Regular inspection and cleaning of connector pins, and application of dielectric grease, ensure reliable electrical contact.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Glide Path Appears Shifted to Pilots
Likely Cause
Beam misalignment — an angular error in one or more PAPI units.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform an alignment check using a theodolite or alignment tool, and verify the transition angle is within ±0.05° (3 arcmin) of specification.
Corrective Action
Re-align the unit to the correct angle, and re-verify after any maintenance, lamp replacement, or ground disturbance.
Blurred or Gradual Red/White Transition
Likely Cause
Optical contamination — dust, rubber deposits, de-icing fluid, or insect residue on the window or prism.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the optical window/prism for contamination and run a photometric test to confirm transition sharpness.
Corrective Action
Clean the optical window/prism with non-abrasive materials, and re-test photometrically to confirm the transition is restored.
Misleading or Dim Indication from One Unit
Likely Cause
Lamp or LED degradation — halogen output decline/burnout, or LED failure/colour shift.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and test the lamp/LED module and compare colour and output against adjacent units.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded lamp or LED module promptly to avoid a misleading indication.
Unit Found Loose or Detached After Impact
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling fatigue from wind loading, vibration, and thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the frangible coupling for cracks or deformation, and confirm it broke away cleanly if impacted.
Corrective Action
Replace the fatigued or broken frangible coupling on schedule, or immediately after any impact event.
Fogging or Corrosion Inside the Unit Housing
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress from failed O-rings, damaged seals, or a blocked pressure vent.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a pressure test on the housing and inspect O-rings, seals, and the pressure vent for damage or blockage.
Corrective Action
Replace failed seals/O-rings, clear the pressure vent, and dry out the housing before re-sealing.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Multi-Unit Alignment Divergence
High Severity
Root Cause
One or more of the four units drifted in azimuth or elevation independently since the last formal alignment check.
Effect
Glide path appears shifted to pilots, since the array's combined red/white presentation depends on all four units agreeing.
Detection Method
Field verification of azimuth and elevation on all four units relative to each other and to the approved glide path setting.
Colour Transition Filter Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing or contamination of the internal colour-transition optics, softening the boundary between the red and white sectors.
Effect
Blurred or gradual red/white transition instead of a sharp changeover, making the glide path harder to read precisely.
Detection Method
Visual/photometric check of the transition sharpness against the specified tolerance during scheduled maintenance.
Single-Unit Output Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Lamp/LED ageing, lens contamination, or a partial connector fault affecting one unit in the four-unit array.
Effect
Misleading or dim indication from one unit, weakening the overall glide path presentation even if the other three units are normal.
Detection Method
Photometric comparison across all four units to identify the one falling outside tolerance.
Mounting Impact Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
A mower, snowplough, or vehicle strike on the unit, or fatigue failure of the mounting hardware over years of service.
Effect
Unit found loose or detached after impact, taking that unit out of the glide path array until repaired.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for looseness or damage during patrol, and physical security check of the mount during maintenance.
Housing Seal Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set, or a housing seam that no longer seals after repeated thermal cycling.
Effect
Fogging or corrosion inside the unit housing, degrading optical clarity and risking internal electronic damage.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for internal fogging during maintenance, and housing seal integrity check.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the series circuit, then verify zero energy at the unit before opening the housing or handling cable connectors.

NOTAM Before Any Aim Disturbance
Operational

Raise a NOTAM before any work that could disturb the unit's aim or take it out of service; PAPI is a primary glide-slope guidance aid and pilots rely on all four units reading correctly on approach.

Verify Elevation Angle Before Return to Service
Compliance

Recheck the unit's elevation angle with a precision inclinometer against its assigned setting after any mounting, coupling, or housing work; a small angular error shifts the red/white transition point pilots use to judge the glide path.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible mounting with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; it must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for whenever the housing is open, and secure loose material before leaving the site to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected series loop after cable or connector work before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or affect other units in the array.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm correct aim, red/white transition, and photometric output on all four units in the array before closing out the work and cancelling the NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Check the Array as a Set, Not Unit by Unit
Verification

Each of the four units in a PAPI array can pass its own individual elevation and photometric check while the array as a whole still presents an inconsistent transition to an approaching pilot. Always finish a maintenance visit by verifying the units together from a representative approach position, not just each one in isolation at the fixture.

Ground Settlement Near the Array Changes the Reference, Not Just the Fixture
Design

A unit reading correctly against its own inclinometer setting can still present a wrong glide indication if the ground beneath the array has settled or been regraded since the original survey. Where recurring elevation complaints don't correlate with any mechanical fault, check the site survey reference before assuming the unit itself has drifted.

Obstruction Growth Between Units Isn't Always Obvious From the Ground
Operational

Vegetation or nearby equipment that grows or is added over time can partially obstruct one unit's beam in a way that's hard to notice from directly beside the fixture but very apparent from an aircraft on approach. Periodically check the array's sightline from an approach-representative vantage point, not only up close during routine maintenance.

Housing Thermal Cycling Fatigues the Frangible Interface
Reliability

Repeated thermal expansion and contraction of the housing against its frangible coupling over years of service can gradually change the coupling's shear characteristics, independent of any impact history. Include a periodic torque/shear verification in the overhaul cycle rather than only inspecting frangibility after a reported strike.

One Unit Reading Low Can Be a Loop Symptom, Not a Fixture Fault
Troubleshooting

Where a single unit's output reads consistently low but its own components test healthy, check the series loop's impedance and connector condition upstream of that unit before replacing parts, since a degrading connector earlier in the loop can present exactly this symptom at one fixture.

Log Aim/Elevation Readings Over Time, Not Just Pass/Fail
Maintenance

Recording the actual measured elevation at each visit, rather than only whether it passed tolerance, reveals a slow drift trend well before the unit crosses out of spec. That history is far more useful for planning proactive correction than a series of isolated pass results with no comparative baseline.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the PAPI light unit: beam, specifications, standards, maintenance and glide path faults.

Q1What is a PAPI light unit?

The PAPI light unit is the fundamental building block of a PAPI system: a self-contained precision optical fixture with a halogen lamp or LED module, a reflector and prism or lens system, and a frangible mounting.

Q2What does a PAPI light unit tell the pilot?

Whether the aircraft is above, on or below the glide slope, encoded in colour: red means too low and white means too high, without any instrument interpretation.

Q3Where are PAPI light units installed?

In arrays at airfields worldwide, for example on precision approach runways (CAT I, II and III) as the visual glide slope system, typically on the left side of the runway.

Q4How does a PAPI light unit form its beam?

A parabolic reflector collimates the light source's output, and the optics then create a sharp colour transition so the pilot sees red or white depending on the approach angle.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

Up to 25,000 cd white and 12,000 cd red for halogen (20,000 and 10,000 cd for LED), a transition sharpness under 3 arc-minutes, a 3° glide path adjustable from 2.5° to 4°, a beam width of ±10° to ±15° horizontally, and IP67/IP68 protection.

Q6How is a PAPI light unit powered?

From a 6.6 A series circuit (or 20 A for high-intensity variants) through a 65-150 W isolating transformer per unit. Intensity follows a 3-step or 5-step CCR setting, and no field fuse is fitted within the light unit.

Q7What electrical tests apply?

Insulation resistance of at least 50 MΩ at 500 VDC primary-to-secondary and to ground, and a dielectric withstand of at least 2,000 VAC. The fixture body and mounting base are bonded to the earth grid.

Q8Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-28, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (fixture photometric performance), IEC TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief 67 (EB-67D) for LED light sources.

Q9How often should a PAPI light unit be maintained?

Quarterly lens cleaning; semi-annual beam alignment checks; and annual lamp or LED output checks, frangible coupling inspection, and seal and connector inspection.

Q10Why might the glide path appear shifted to pilots?

Beam misalignment, meaning an angular error in one or more units. Check alignment with a theodolite or alignment tool and verify the transition angle is within ±0.05° (3 arc-minutes).
