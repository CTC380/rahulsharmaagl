Runway Status Light (RWSL)

An advanced automated airfield safety system that provides real-time visual indications of runway status to pilots and vehicle operators. The RWSL system uses surveillance data to detect runway occupancy and activates Runway Entrance Lights, Runway Intersection Lights, and Take-Off Hold Lights — all red, in-pavement fixtures — to prevent runway incursions.

Overview

What Is It?

The Runway Status Light (RWSL) system is an automated safety system that provides direct, real-time visual indications of runway status to pilots and vehicle operators. The system uses surveillance data (radar, ADS-B, surface movement radar) to detect the presence and movement of aircraft and vehicles on the runway. When the system determines that a runway is occupied or that a conflict is imminent, it illuminates its own dedicated field elements — steady-burning red, in-pavement Runway Entrance Lights (REL) at taxiway/runway intersections, Runway Intersection Lights (RIL) at runway/runway crossings, and Take-Off Hold Lights (THL) on the runway centreline at the departure position — to alert pilots and vehicle operators to stop or remain clear. RWSL is a distinct system from Runway Guard Lights (RGL), the separate elevated amber flashing fixtures at holding positions that are switched independently, typically by ATC or local control logic rather than by RWSL's surveillance algorithms.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

RWSL exists to add a fully automated layer of runway incursion protection that operates independently of, and in parallel with, air traffic control instructions and pilot situational awareness — both of which can be compromised by workload, communication breakdowns, or a surveillance gap that a controller cannot instantly perceive. By continuously fusing surveillance data on every aircraft and vehicle near the runway, the system can detect a developing conflict and illuminate the relevant lights (Runway Entrance, Runway Intersection, and Take-Off Hold Lights) automatically and immediately, without waiting for a human to notice the hazard and issue an instruction. Its purpose is best understood as a safety backstop: it does not replace ATC clearances or a pilot's own vigilance, but catches the specific failure mode where a conflict exists and neither party has yet recognized it in time to act.

Application

Where Is It Used?

RWSL systems are installed at busy airports with high traffic volumes where runway incursion risk is elevated. They are typically used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where safety margins are critical
  • Major international airports with complex taxiway layouts
  • Intersecting runways where crossing traffic needs to be managed
  • Runways with high traffic density where incursion risk is significant
  • Airports implementing advanced surface movement control
Placement

Installation & Location

RWSL is a system installation rather than a single fixture: the field elements are in-pavement Runway Entrance Light, Runway Intersection Light, and Take-Off Hold Light fixtures (distinct from the elevated Runway Guard Lights described elsewhere in this library), so ground-side installation follows those fixtures' own base can, cabling, and frangibility requirements. What is specific to RWSL is the addition of an airport surface surveillance processing unit — typically housed in the airport's ATC facility or a dedicated equipment room — that ingests ASDE-X, ADS-B, or surface movement radar data, runs the occupancy and conflict-detection logic, and sends activation commands over a dedicated fibre or copper network to the field lighting controllers at each holding position and take-off position. Installation therefore centres on running this control network between the surveillance processing unit and each affected location, together with extensive integration testing to confirm the detection logic activates the correct lights within the required response time before the system is commissioned for live operational use.

Function

How Does It Work?

The RWSL system operates through a sophisticated integration of surveillance data, processing algorithms, and visual output devices:

  • Surveillance: Radar, ADS-B, and surface movement radar data continuously track all aircraft and vehicles on the airfield.
  • Processing: The RWSL processing system analyzes this data to determine runway occupancy status and predict potential conflicts.
  • Activation: When a runway is occupied, the system illuminates the affected Runway Entrance Lights and/or Runway Intersection Lights (steady red, in-pavement, at the intersection) and, for departing traffic, the Take-Off Hold Lights (steady red, on the runway centreline).
  • Indication: When the runway is clear, the system extinguishes the lights, allowing safe entry or crossing.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

RWSL is a fully automated, non-cooperative safety layer that sits on top of existing surface surveillance rather than depending on any controller action. Surveillance feeds — typically ASDE-X or ASSC surface radar/multilateration, supplemented by ADS-B where available — stream target position and velocity continuously into the RWSL processing system, which runs independent algorithm sets for each light function: Runway Entrance Light and Runway Intersection Light logic for the in-pavement fixtures at taxiway/runway and runway/runway intersections, and the Takeoff Hold Lights logic for the in-pavement lights at the departure end. Each algorithm evaluates occupancy and closing-speed predictions against the runway in question and issues an illuminate/extinguish command directly to the light control and monitoring subsystem, which drives the field circuits for that light group. Because the logic is deterministic and reaction time is sub-second, RWSL lights change state ahead of or in step with the conflict itself, with no controller-in-the-loop delay. The tower retains an ATC display showing overall system and light-group health, and the light control and monitoring subsystem reports back circuit status and individual light-group faults for maintenance dispatch, but tower personnel cannot directly command individual lights — the algorithms are the sole source of illumination commands.

Construction

Physical Construction

RWSL has no dedicated luminaire of its own — the field elements are in-pavement Runway Entrance Light, Runway Intersection Light, and Take-Off Hold Light fixtures, so their housings, optics, and base-can construction follow the standard in-pavement fixture designs described elsewhere in this library. What is specific to RWSL is the processing and control hardware: the surveillance processing system is typically built as a redundant server pair mounted in a 19-inch rack, housed in an environmentally controlled equipment room within the ATC facility or an airfield electrical building, with dual power feeds and UPS backup to keep detection logic running through a mains interruption. Field-side light control and monitoring units sit in weatherproof cabinets at or near each holding position and take-off position, sealed to at least IP65 to protect the switching electronics from the outdoor environment, and connected back to the processing racks over a dedicated fibre-optic or hardened copper network rather than the general airport LAN, keeping activation latency low and deterministic. Cabling between the processing system, field cabinets, and the tower ATC display follows the same duct and trench routes as other airfield electrical services, with fibre runs typically diverse-routed so a single cable cut cannot take down surveillance-to-light communication across the whole system.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Surveillance Data Latency

Delays in surveillance data processing can cause the RWSL system to react slowly, potentially failing to activate lights in time. Regular system performance monitoring and network optimization are essential.

Communication Infrastructure Failure

RWSL systems rely on network communication between surveillance sensors, processing units, and output devices. Network failures can result in system-wide loss of functionality. Redundant communication paths are critical.

False Alarms

The RWSL system can trigger false alarms due to surveillance errors, wildlife detection, or system faults. False alarms can desensitize pilots and vehicle operators, reducing the effectiveness of the system.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Lights Fail to Activate for an Occupied Runway
Likely Cause
Loss or degradation of a surveillance feed (radar, ADS-B, or multilateration) into the RWSL processing system, or a fault in the light control and monitoring subsystem at that light group.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the ATC display for surveillance/system health alerts; confirm the affected sensor feed is reporting valid tracks; test the field light control cabinet and circuit independently of the algorithm to confirm the fixture itself can illuminate.
Corrective Action
Restore or fail over the degraded surveillance source, reset or replace the faulted light control unit, and re-run integration/response-time testing before returning the light group to live service.
Lights Activate with No Traffic Present (False Alarm)
Likely Cause
Spurious or ghost targets from a surveillance sensor (radar clutter, multilateration multipath, wildlife), or a processing-system software fault in the occupancy/conflict algorithm.
Diagnostic Steps
Correlate the activation event against recorded surveillance track data and video/ASDE-X playback if available; check sensor calibration and known clutter zones; review processing-system logs for the specific algorithm that fired.
Corrective Action
Retune or recalibrate the offending sensor, apply a clutter/exclusion map update, or apply the relevant software patch; log the event for the false-alarm rate trend used in system acceptance monitoring.
Slow or Delayed Response to a Genuine Conflict
Likely Cause
Surveillance data latency, network congestion between the processing racks and field cabinets, or a processing load issue on the surveillance server pair.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure end-to-end detection-to-activation time against the sub-1-second design target; check network switch and fibre link statistics for errors or congestion; review server CPU/memory load during the event.
Corrective Action
Clear network congestion or reroute over the diverse fibre path, restart or rebalance the processing server pair, and re-verify reaction time meets spec before returning to service.
System-Wide Loss of Function
Likely Cause
Failure of both processing servers, loss of the dedicated communication network to all field cabinets, or a mains power interruption not covered by UPS backup.
Diagnostic Steps
Check server redundancy status and failover logs; verify UPS state of charge and runtime; test network links segment by segment from the processing rack outward to identify the break.
Corrective Action
Fail over to the standby server or replace the failed unit, restore or reroute the communication network, and confirm UPS capacity is restored; notify ATC to revert to procedural runway safeguards until the system is re-verified.
Single Light Group Reports Fault but Others Operate Normally
Likely Cause
Fault in the local field light control cabinet, a damaged run of the dedicated fibre/copper link to that holding position or take-off position, or a failed fixture within that group.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the light-group fault reported on the ATC display and maintenance terminal; inspect the field cabinet for power and communication status; test the underlying REL/RIL/THL fixtures and circuit per their own troubleshooting procedure.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the field cabinet electronics, restore the communication link, or rectify the fixture/circuit fault as identified; confirm the light group re-reports healthy on the ATC display before closing out.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Surveillance Sensor Feed Loss or Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Radar, ADS-B, or multilateration sensor hardware fault, antenna/feed line damage, or loss of the data link carrying track data into the processing system.
Effect
Blind spots in surveillance coverage that can delay or prevent light activation for a genuine conflict, or force reliance on a degraded sensor mix.
Detection Method
Continuous sensor health monitoring on the ATC display, automated data-quality alarms, and periodic surveillance coverage verification testing.
Processing Server / Software Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Software defect in the conflict-detection algorithm, a corrupted configuration or map database, or a hardware fault on the primary processing server.
Effect
Missed or delayed activations, false alarms, or a full failover to the standby server with a brief interruption in coverage.
Detection Method
Server health and failover-status monitoring, processing-system event logs, and scheduled response-time verification testing.
Field Light Control Cabinet Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Power supply or control-board component failure inside the cabinet, moisture ingress, or a failed interface card between the network and the fixture circuit.
Effect
The light group served by that cabinet fails to activate or reports a persistent fault while the rest of the RWSL system continues operating normally.
Detection Method
Cabinet fault reporting on the ATC and maintenance terminal, and scheduled functional testing of the cabinet independent of the surveillance algorithm.
Communication Network Link Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Fibre or copper link damage from excavation or a cable fault, a failed network switch, or sustained congestion between the processing racks and field cabinets.
Effect
Delayed or lost activation commands to one or more light groups, ranging from a single stop bar to a full loss of function if the diverse path is also affected.
Detection Method
Network switch and fibre-link error/utilisation monitoring, segment-by-segment link testing, and end-to-end activation latency measurement against the design target.
Sensor Miscalibration / Clutter False Alarms
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Radar clutter from terrain or structures, multilateration multipath near buildings, wildlife activity, or sensor calibration drift over time.
Effect
Nuisance light activations with no actual runway conflict, eroding controller and pilot confidence in the system if left uncorrected.
Detection Method
Correlation of activation events against recorded track and video data, false-alarm rate trending, and periodic sensor calibration checks.
Fixture / Lamp Failure Within a Light Group
Medium Severity
Root Cause
LED module or lamp end-of-life, moisture ingress at the fixture, or a degraded L-823 connector feeding an individual light within a REL, RIL, or THL group.
Effect
Reduced conspicuity or a fully dark fixture within an otherwise correctly triggered light group.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, photometric intensity survey, and fixture-level continuity checks during scheduled maintenance of the underlying circuit.
UPS / Backup Power Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Battery end-of-life or a failed charger circuit in the UPS supporting the processing racks or field cabinets, undetected until a mains interruption occurs.
Effect
Loss of system function coincident with a power outage, with no ride-through to bridge the gap to standby generation.
Detection Method
UPS state-of-charge and runtime monitoring, and scheduled battery load testing as part of routine preventive maintenance.
Response-Time Degradation Under Load
Low Severity
Root Cause
High track density during peak traffic periods, background software processes competing for server resources, or accumulated data-latency across the network path.
Effect
Activation time creeps closer to, or exceeds, the sub-1-second design target without an outright system failure.
Detection Method
Ongoing end-to-end latency logging and periodic peak-load response-time verification against the specified performance envelope.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

System, Not a Single Fixture
Operational

RWSL is a surveillance-driven system commanding dedicated REL, RIL, and THL fixtures; before working on any element, confirm whether you're isolating the field fixture's own series circuit, the control network link, or the central processing unit, since each has its own isolation point.

Processing Unit Redundant Supply
Electrical Safety

The surveillance processing rack runs on dual redundant mains feeds with UPS backup; isolating one feed does not de-energise the rack, so confirm both feeds and the UPS are addressed per the manufacturer's lockout procedure before servicing it.

Field Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Field lights remain series circuits fed by their own CCR per the underlying REL, RIL, or THL specification; de-energise and lock out the correct CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling connectors.

Do Not Disable the Automated Function Unannounced
Compliance

RWSL provides automated, real-time runway occupancy alerting relied on by both pilots and vehicle operators; never take the system, or an individual light it drives, out of service without prior ATC coordination and a NOTAM covering the affected function.

Integration Testing After Any Change
Compliance

After any change to field wiring, the control network, or the processing unit, run the full detection-to-activation test sequence to confirm the correct lights still activate within the required response time before returning the system to live operational use.

Confirm Fail-Safe Behaviour Before Closing Out
Compliance

Verify the system reverts to a safe, conservative state (rather than a false-clear indication) in the event of a sensor or network fault before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Engineering & Expert Notes

Surveillance Latency Is the Real Spec, Not Just Flash State
System Integration

RWSL activation depends on the surface surveillance feed detecting a conflict and commanding the lights in near real time; benchmark end-to-end latency from actual aircraft/vehicle position to light activation after any surveillance sensor or software update, not just confirm that the lights illuminate on command.

A Correct-Looking Test Doesn't Confirm the Live Detection Logic
Testing

A manual lamp test or forced activation confirms the fixture and wiring but says nothing about whether the underlying detection algorithm still correctly interprets real traffic patterns; schedule periodic live-traffic validation against actual surveillance data in addition to fixture-level testing.

Treat Software and Sensor Updates as Full-System Changes
Change Management

An update to the surface surveillance radar, ADS-B feed, or RWSL control software can change detection behaviour even when no fixture or cabling changes; run a full functional revalidation after any such update rather than treating it as a fixture-level maintenance item.

Distinguish a False Activation From a Genuine Conflict
Troubleshooting

A pattern of unexplained light activations is more often a surveillance data quality issue — ghost tracks, multipath, or a misconfigured detection zone — than a fixture fault; correlate activation logs against actual traffic before assuming the problem is at the light itself.

Log and Trend Activation Response Times
Reliability

RWSL response time can degrade gradually as sensor coverage or processing load changes, well before it fails outright; trend activation-to-illumination timing over successive test cycles rather than relying on a single pass/fail check at commissioning.

Fixture-Level Redundancy Doesn't Cover a Central Software Fault
Design

Because many RWSL fixtures share a common detection and control backbone, redundant fixture wiring protects against a local failure but not a fault in the shared surveillance or processing chain; understand where the actual single points of failure sit in the system architecture, not just at the light fixtures.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about Runway Status Lights (RWSL): what they do, light types, surveillance inputs, response time, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system?

RWSL is an automated safety system that gives pilots and vehicle operators direct, real-time visual indications of runway status. It uses surveillance data to detect aircraft and vehicles on the runway, and lights dedicated in-pavement red lights when the runway is occupied or a conflict is imminent.

Q2What lights does RWSL use?

Steady-burning red in-pavement lights: Runway Entrance Lights (REL) at taxiway/runway intersections, Runway Intersection Lights (RIL) at runway/runway crossings and Take-Off Hold Lights (THL) on the runway centreline at the departure position.

Q3Why add RWSL when air traffic control already gives instructions?

RWSL adds an automated layer of runway incursion protection that operates independently of, and in parallel with, ATC instructions and pilot awareness. Workload, communication breakdowns or a surveillance gap can compromise those, while the system continuously fuses surveillance on every aircraft and vehicle near the runway.

Q4What surveillance data does RWSL use?

Radar, ADS-B, surface movement radar and multilateration. A processing system analyses the data to determine runway occupancy and predict conflicts.

Q5How fast does RWSL react?

Under 1 second from detection to light activation. Reaction time is verified regularly, and drift usually points to surveillance data latency.

Q6Do RWSL lights flash?

No. They are steady-burning and are either illuminated or extinguished by the algorithm, with no intermediate state. The lights are at least 2,000 cd (red).

Q7Where is RWSL used?

Busy airports with high traffic volumes and elevated incursion risk, including precision approach runways (CAT II and III), major international airports with complex taxiway layouts, intersecting runways and airports implementing advanced surface movement control.

Q8What does an RWSL installation involve?

It is a system installation rather than a single fixture. Field elements are in-pavement REL, RIL and THL fixtures (distinct from elevated Runway Guard Lights), on series circuits fed by CCRs, plus an airport surface surveillance processing system with dual redundant mains and UPS backup, integrated with ALCMS and A-SMGCS.

Q9Which standards apply to RWSL?

The main references are FAA Engineering Brief No. 64D (RWSL system specification), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (L-850T and L-852S in-pavement fixtures), FAA JO 7110.65 §3-4-20 (ATC procedures) and ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, where RWSL-type systems fall under ICAO's ARIWS guidance. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q10How often should an RWSL system be tested?

Typical intervals are monthly for output device activation tests, quarterly for reaction time verification and network and communication link audits, and semi-annual for sensor fusion and track accuracy checks and UPS backup and failover tests. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11Why do the lights fail to activate for an occupied runway?

Loss or degradation of a surveillance feed (radar, ADS-B or multilateration), or a fault in the light control and monitoring subsystem at that light group. Check ATC display health alerts, confirm sensor feeds report valid tracks and test the field cabinet and circuit independently of the algorithm.

Q12Why do the lights activate with no traffic present?

Spurious or ghost targets from a surveillance sensor, such as radar clutter or multilateration multipath, are the likely cause. Investigate the sensor source and re-run response testing before returning the light group to live service.
