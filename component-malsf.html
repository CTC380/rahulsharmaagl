MALSF

A medium‑intensity approach lighting system with sequenced flashing lights, providing enhanced visual guidance for non‑precision runways. Featuring a single row of steady‑burning lights and a line of sequenced flashing lights (SFL) that create a "rolling" effect toward the threshold, MALSF improves approach direction awareness and depth perception, particularly during night and marginal weather conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

MALSF (Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System with Sequenced Flashing lights) is a non‑precision approach lighting system that combines a row of medium‑intensity steady‑burning lights with a line of sequenced flashing lights (SFL). The steady‑burning light bars extend from the runway threshold out to 427 metres (1,400 ft), with three sequenced flashers positioned at the outermost three bar stations. The SFL flashes in sequence toward the threshold, creating a "rolling" effect that indicates the approach direction and helps pilots maintain the correct descent path. This combination improves depth perception and directional awareness, especially in low visibility and nighttime conditions, making MALSF a popular choice for airports that need better visual cues than basic SALS/MALS but without the complexity and cost of full precision systems like ALSF.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

MALSF exists to add depth perception and directional confidence to a basic steady-burning approach system without the cost of a full precision installation, addressing the specific problem that a static row of lights conveys distance poorly compared to a dynamic, sequenced pattern. The sequenced flashing component's rolling effect draws the eye along the approach direction and makes the pilot's sense of closure rate more intuitive, particularly valuable in low-visibility or nighttime conditions where the steady lights alone would leave the pilot with less confidence in depth judgement. Its purpose is to occupy the middle tier between basic SALS/MALS and full ALSF-class precision systems, giving airports a meaningfully better visual reference without the infrastructure and redundancy requirements of a true precision approach lighting system.

Application

Where Is It Used?

MALSF systems are installed at airports that support non‑precision approaches with enhanced visual requirements. They are typically used on:

  • Non‑precision runways with higher traffic volumes or more demanding weather conditions
  • Regional airports serving commercial turboprop and regional jet operations
  • Military airfields where improved approach guidance is needed without full precision capability
  • Runways with moderate approach speeds where sequenced flashing provides useful depth perception
  • Airfields transitioning from basic to enhanced lighting where SFL is a cost‑effective upgrade
  • Locations where terrain, obstacles, or local weather benefit from the added visual cue of flashing lights
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out on lightweight frangible stands along the extended runway centreline at 61 m (200 ft) spacing, running from the threshold out to 427 m (1,400 ft), with the three sequenced flashing light heads mounted on their own dedicated stands at the outermost three bar positions. Steady-burning and SFL circuits are cabled separately in duct back to their own regulators, so a fault on the flasher circuit does not take down the centreline row. Stands are sized for the lower loads of a medium-intensity system, and elevated piers are used only where the extended centreline crosses a road, ditch, or other minor obstacle. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline extended and threshold survey.

Function

How Does It Work?

The MALSF system provides enhanced visual guidance through the combination of steady‑burning lights and sequenced flashing lights:

  • Steady‑Burning Lights: Seven five-light bars, medium‑intensity white, spaced at 61m (200 ft) intervals, extend from the runway threshold out to 427 metres (1,400 ft), providing a visual centreline reference and distance cues.
  • Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL): A separate row of lights (often in the same line or offset) flashes in rapid sequence from the outer end toward the threshold, creating a "rolling" or "chasing" effect that indicates the approach direction and helps pilots judge depth and glide slope.
  • Combined Guidance: The steady lights provide lateral and distance reference, while the SFL adds dynamic directional information, improving depth perception and helping pilots maintain the correct descent angle.
  • Intensity Control: Both the steady and flashing lights are typically controlled by CCRs with intensity steps (usually 5‑step), allowing the ATC to adjust brightness for visibility conditions. The SFL may have separate intensity control for optimal flashing visibility.
  • Simplicity: Compared to ALSF systems, MALSF has fewer lights and simpler control, making it more cost‑effective and easier to maintain while still providing significant visual benefits.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

MALSF is built from two independent control paths that never share hardware. The steady centreline row is a conventional series circuit loop: an Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects a brightness step, the CCR regulates the loop to the corresponding fixed current, and every steady fixture draws the same current through its own isolating transformer, so the row switches and dims together as a single unit — identical in principle to MALS. The Sequenced Flashing Lights (SFL) run on a completely separate path: a dedicated flasher controller (sequencer) fires each flash lamp's capacitor-discharge circuit in turn, working outward-to-threshold, at a rate and pattern fixed by the sequencer's timing logic rather than by the CCR's current steps, producing the "rolling" or "chasing" effect. The flasher controller has its own intensity setting (also normally selectable in steps) so ATC can adjust flash brightness independently of the steady row's brightness. Because the two systems are electrically and logically separate, a fault on one path — a burnt-out steady lamp reported as a loop voltage rise on the CCR, or a flash tube or trigger fault reported by the sequencer — never affects the other; the ALCMS receives status from the CCR and the flasher controller as two distinct feeds.

ALCMS → [CCR → Steady Series Loop → Isolating Transformer → Centreline Fixture] + [Flasher Controller → Capacitor-Discharge Trigger → SFL Lamp, sequenced outer-to-threshold]

Engineering View

Steady Row & Sequenced Flash Overview

The steady row is the same fixed-30m centreline reference as MALS. What MALSF adds on top is a separate SFL circuit: lights fire in rapid sequence from the outer end inward, so at any instant one pulse is brightest near the threshold while the trail behind it is already fading — that's the "rolling" cue pilots read for approach direction and depth, and it runs on its own intensity control independent of the steady lights.

Construction

Physical Construction

Steady-burning fixture heads follow the same construction as a standard MALS unit: a die-cast aluminium housing on a frangible aluminium or composite stand, sealed to IP67/IP68 and wired through L-823 connectors to its own isolating transformer. The sequenced flashing light (SFL) heads are a distinct assembly — a xenon strobe tube and trigger electronics housed in a weatherproof dome, backed by a capacitor bank in a sealed enclosure at the base of the stand, engineered to deliver the high-current discharge pulse that produces each flash. Because the SFL heads run on a separate circuit from the steady row, their stands carry independent cabling in duct back to a dedicated sequencer and regulator, keeping the two systems mechanically and electrically isolated so a flasher fault cannot affect the steady centreline. All stands are frangible and sized for the lighter loads of a medium-intensity system, with stainless-steel fasteners and corrosion-resistant finishes used throughout for exposure on unprepared approach terrain.

Installation Requirements

Frangible Stand Footing
Civil

Set each centreline and SFL stand footing at the 61 m (200 ft) spacing out to 427 m (1,400 ft), sized for the lighter loads of a medium-intensity system.

Elevated Piers at Minor Obstacles
Civil

Install piers only where the extended centreline crosses a road, ditch, or other minor obstacle, since most of the run needs no special foundation work.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay separate duct runs for the steady-burning and sequenced flashing lights (SFL) circuits back to their own regulators, so a fault on the flasher circuit does not take down the centreline row.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal primary connectors on both circuits per manufacturer procedure, keeping every splice fully submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm each fixture's isolating transformer wattage matches its circuit's rated current step, with steady and SFL transformers kept on their respective loops.

Stand & SFL Head Assembly
Mechanical

Fix steady-burning heads to their stands and mount SFL heads with their capacitor enclosures on dedicated stands in line with the centreline, torquing all fittings to spec.

Setting-Out to Extended Centreline
Mechanical

Tie stand positions to the extended runway centreline and threshold survey so both the steady bars and SFL sequence stay true out to 427 m (1,400 ft).

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record stand positions, cable routes, connector seals, and torque values as-built for both circuits before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the steady-burning circuit and the separately controlled sequenced flashing lights (SFL) circuit before backfill, confirming each clears the acceptable minimum.

Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity and record total loop resistance for the steady bars and the SFL circuit along the full 427m (1,400 ft) run before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at each steady light and SFL transformer matches the rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

Sequenced Flashing Timing Verification
Energisation

Confirm the SFL controller fires each flasher in the correct outer-to-threshold sequence at the specified flash rate, independent of the steady-light circuit.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Light Row Alignment & Aim Verification

Check the 30m-interval steady light spacing and the flasher positions against the design aiming diagram, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Record insulation, continuity, voltage, flash-sequence, and photometric readings alongside as-built positions in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify beam intensity for both steady lights and the SFL against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer; degraded output usually points to lens haze or an aging light source.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean environmental pollutants, bird droppings, and de-icing residue from steady and SFL fixtures alike, since the flashing units must be clearly seen for the rolling effect to work.

SFL Synchronisation Test
Semi-annual

Verify the flashing sequence and timing across the SFL array to catch controller drift before it disrupts the rolling effect pilots rely on.

Alignment Check
Semi-annual

Verify fixture aim for both steady and SFL units using a theodolite or laser alignment system, since wind, wildlife, or vehicle strikes can knock elevated fixtures out of tolerance.

Site Survey & Vegetation Clearance
Semi-annual

Walk the approach line to confirm no vegetation or structures have grown up to obstruct the light output since the last inspection.

CCR & Power Quality Check
Annual

Monitor voltage stability and CCR performance on both the steady and SFL circuits, since either can suffer inconsistent brightness or sequence errors under fluctuation.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test isolation transformers and series circuits to confirm insulation resistance stays above the minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a fault.

Frangible Coupling & Base Inspection
Annual

Inspect elevated fixture couplings and base housings for corrosion or fatigue to ensure breakaway performance and structural stability along the full 427m (1,400 ft) run.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

Both steady and flashing lights are exposed to environmental pollutants, bird droppings, and de‑icing chemicals. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is essential to maintain light output and visibility, especially for the SFL which must be clearly seen for the rolling effect.

SFL Synchronisation Drift

The sequenced flashing lights rely on precise timing. Control units can drift over time, causing the sequence to become irregular or out of sync. Regular calibration and testing of the SFL system is critical to maintain the rolling effect.

Misalignment of Elevated Fixtures

Elevated approach lights and SFL units can be knocked out of alignment by wind, wildlife, or maintenance vehicles. Regular alignment checks using a theodolite or laser alignment system are essential to maintain the correct beam direction and SFL visibility.

Power Supply and CCR Issues

MALSF systems draw significant power, and the SFL may have separate circuits. Voltage fluctuations, CCR faults, or cable damage can cause inconsistent brightness or total failure of either the steady or flashing lights. Regular power quality monitoring and cable testing are recommended.

Vegetation and Obstruction Growth

At smaller airfields, vegetation or structures can grow up around approach lights and SFL units, obstructing the light output. Regular site inspections and vegetation management are essential to maintain clear lines of sight.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Light/Bar Dark, Rest of Array Normal
Likely Cause
Failed lamp/LED source, or an open isolating transformer at that fixture (blown primary link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp/LED source if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Array Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop feeding the approach lights (severed cable, corroded splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs along the approach centreline to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
SFL Sequence Irregular or Out of Step
Likely Cause
Timing drift in the sequenced flashing light controller, or a failed flasher unit, has broken the rolling flash pattern.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify controller timing against the specified sequence rate; check each flasher head in turn for correct firing order; inspect flasher control cabling for damage.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the SFL controller, or replace the faulty flasher head; confirm the rolling sequence is restored end to end.
Steady or SFL Circuit Dead While the Other Stays Lit
Likely Cause
MALSF typically runs the steady lights and the SFL on separate circuits; an open circuit or CCR fault on one leaves the other unaffected.
Diagnostic Steps
Identify which circuit — steady or SFL — has lost output; check that circuit's CCR fault indication and loop continuity independently of the other.
Corrective Action
Isolate and repair the faulted circuit using the standard open-loop or CCR fault procedure, without disturbing the circuit still in service.
Elevated Fixture or SFL Unit Misaligned
Likely Cause
Wind loading, wildlife strike, or vehicle contact has knocked an elevated steady fixture or SFL unit off its aiming tolerance.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-check fixture aim with a theodolite or laser alignment system against the approach centreline; inspect the frangible coupling and mounting stake for cracking or play.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-aim the fixture, replace the frangible coupling if fatigued, and address any base or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reduced or Hazy Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from environmental pollutants or de-icing residue, or an aging light source.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the lens; confirm the CCR is set to the correct intensity step for prevailing conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lens, replace a degraded light source, or correct the CCR step setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Light Source (Lamp/LED) Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised fixture seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single light or bar within the approach array goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the array continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol along the approach centreline, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire array.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or wildlife activity, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop feeding the approach lights.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Elevated Fixture / Crossbar Frangible Coupling Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading, wildlife strike, or contact from a maintenance vehicle, combined with ordinary material fatigue over the service interval.
Effect
Fixture or crossbar light leans, loosens, or separates from its mounting stake, pulling it out of aim tolerance along the approach centreline.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim checks with a theodolite or laser alignment system.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on an otherwise healthy circuit.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
SFL Flasher / Condenser Discharge Unit Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing capacitor bank in the condenser discharge unit, a failed trigger transformer, or timing drift in the sequenced flashing light controller feeding the five SFL heads.
Effect
Flasher fails to fire or fires out of sequence, weakening or breaking the rolling flash cue that supplements the steady-burn bars in low-visibility conditions.
Detection Method
Verify controller timing against the specified sequence rate, check each flasher head in firing order, and inspect the condenser discharge unit for capacitor swelling or leakage.
Independent Steady-Burn / SFL Circuit Isolation Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A cable, connector, or transformer fault isolated to one of the two independent circuits feeding the steady-burn bars and the SFL heads respectively.
Effect
One circuit goes fully dark while the other remains lit, since the steady-burn and SFL circuits on MALSF are wired and regulated independently.
Detection Method
Test the steady-burn and SFL circuits separately at the CCR, and confirm which loop is faulted before dispatching a field crew.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

MALSF bar and crossbar circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR, then verify zero energy at the tower base or bar before opening any enclosure.

Sequenced Flasher Capacitor Discharge
Electrical Safety

Condenser discharge units driving the sequenced flashing lights store a high-energy charge even after the master flasher controller is switched off; discharge each unit through its bleed resistor and confirm zero voltage before servicing a flasher head.

Elevated Tower & Off-Runway Access
Personnel Safety

Light bar and flasher towers extend along the extended centreline over terrain that may be uneven or off the paved movement area; use fall protection when climbing frangible towers and assess ground conditions before positioning vehicles or ladders.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible coupling or tower base with a rigid fitting, and do not over-torque connections during installation or repair; the structure must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Work on approach lighting affects an active runway's usable minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any bar, crossbar, or flasher is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

FOD Control Along the Approach
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every tower worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work on a bar, crossbar, or flasher circuit, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the system with a bar out of aim tolerance, a dark flasher, or an out-of-sequence discharge pattern; verify photometric output and flasher timing before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Rolling Effect Depends on Relative Timing, Not Individual Flash Accuracy
Photometric

A single SFL unit can flash at the correct absolute rate and still break the rolling effect if its timing has drifted relative to its neighbours. Verify the sequence as a whole along the array, not fixture by fixture.

Keep SFL and Steady-Burn Circuits Independently Diagnosable
Electrical

Where the SFL and steady-burning centreline lights share loop infrastructure, a wiring topology that can't isolate a fault to one or the other slows troubleshooting significantly. Verify separate fault indication exists for each subsystem.

SFL Units Are More Sensitive to Aim Drift Than Steady-Burn Lights
Structural

Because the sequenced flashing effect depends on the pilot perceiving a directional roll, a flasher with even modest aim drift can weaken that cue well before it would fail a simple on/off intensity check. Prioritise SFL aim verification over the steady-burn row.

Rule Out Controller Drift Before Replacing Flash Hardware
Troubleshooting

An irregular rolling sequence is more often a controller synchronisation issue than a failed flash tube or LED module. Pull controller diagnostics first rather than dispatching a crew to swap hardware on the reported fixture.

SFL Housings See More Thermal Cycling Than Steady-Burn Fixtures
Durability

The higher-power flash discharge in SFL units puts them through more pronounced thermal cycling than adjacent steady-burning lights, which can accelerate seal and solder-joint fatigue at those specific fixtures. Weight replacement planning accordingly.

Calibrate the Full Sequence After Any Single-Unit Swap
Maintenance Planning

Replacing one SFL unit without re-verifying its timing against the rest of the array risks leaving a subtly desynchronised rolling effect that passes a quick visual check but still doesn't read correctly to an approaching pilot.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about MALSF (MALS with Sequenced Flashing lights): layout, flashing lights, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is MALSF?

MALSF is a Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System with Sequenced Flashing lights. It combines a row of steady-burning medium-intensity white lights with sequenced flashing lights (SFL) that add a dynamic cue.

Q2How do the sequenced flashing lights work?

They flash in rapid sequence from the outer end toward the threshold, creating a rolling or chasing effect that indicates the approach direction and helps depth perception and descent angle.

Q3How long is a MALSF installation?

427 metres (1,400 ft), with seven five-light bars of steady white lights spaced at 61 m (200 ft) intervals.

Q4What is the difference between MALS and MALSF?

MALS is steady-burning only, while MALSF adds sequenced flashing lights. The steady-burning part of both has the same layout.

Q5Does MALSF include crossbar lights?

Not typically, although some variations may have them.

Q6Which standards apply to MALSF?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46, IEC TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief 67 for LED components. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q7How often should MALSF be maintained?

Typically quarterly lens cleaning, semi-annual SFL synchronisation tests and annual photometric intensity checks of both steady and flashing lights. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q8What are the common problems with MALSF?

Lens contamination, SFL timing drift as control units age, misalignment of elevated fixtures, and power supply or CCR issues, since the SFL may have separate circuits.
