Earth Pit

A dedicated excavation or enclosure containing one or more earth electrodes, used to establish a reliable low‑impedance connection to the general mass of earth. Earth pits are fundamental to the earthing system of Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) infrastructure, providing a safe path for fault currents, lightning strikes, and static discharge, ensuring personnel safety and equipment protection.

Overview

What Is It?

An Earth Pit (also known as an earthing pit or grounding pit) is a physical structure that houses the earth electrode and its associated connections, providing a point of access for testing, inspection, and maintenance of the earthing system. The key components of an earth pit include:

  • Earth Electrode: The conductive element that makes contact with the soil. Common types include:
    • Copper‑bonded steel rods: Most common, with a copper coating for corrosion resistance and steel core for mechanical strength.
    • Copper plates: Used in areas with high resistivity or for large‑current installations.
    • Chemical (maintenance‑free) electrodes: Filled with conductive compounds (e.g., bentonite) to reduce resistance.
    • Pipe electrodes: Galvanised iron or copper pipes, driven vertically.
  • Pit Enclosure: A robust, weatherproof chamber (typically made of brick, concrete, or heavy‑duty plastic) that protects the electrode and connections from mechanical damage, moisture, and corrosion. It includes a removable cover for access.
  • Connection Lugs: Heavy‑duty copper or aluminium lugs for connecting the earth conductor (from the equipment or lightning protection system) to the electrode.
  • Backfill Material: The soil or conductive material (e.g., bentonite, charcoal, salt) used to surround the electrode and enhance conductivity.
  • Test Terminal: A removable link or terminal that allows the earth resistance to be measured without disturbing the permanent connections.

Earth pits are strategically located throughout the AGL infrastructure, including near CCR rooms, ALCMS shelters, lighting control panels, and at the base of tall structures (masts, towers).

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The earth pit's purpose is to make the earth electrode buried beneath it a permanently testable, maintainable part of the earthing system rather than an inaccessible connection lost underground. Because an earthing system's effectiveness depends on its resistance staying below a specified threshold over years of soil movement, moisture change, and corrosion, the pit's accessible test terminal is what allows that resistance to actually be measured periodically rather than assumed — turning an invisible safety-critical connection into one that can be verified and, if necessary, remediated before it fails during an actual fault or lightning event.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Earth pits are essential in every part of the AGL earthing network:

  • CCR Rooms and Substations: Providing a low‑resistance earth connection for the main switchgear, transformers, and CCR panels.
  • ALCMS and Communication Shelters: Earthing for sensitive electronic equipment and lightning protection systems.
  • Lighting Control Panels (LCPs): Local earthing for outdoor panels and their internal surge protection devices.
  • Airfield Lighting Circuits: Earthing of cable trays, junction boxes, and metal enclosures along the series lighting circuits.
  • Obstruction Lighting Structures: Earthing of masts, towers, and poles supporting obstruction lights.
  • Apron Floodlight Masts: Earthing for tall floodlight structures and their associated power supplies.
  • Equipment Shelters: Earthing of remote equipment huts housing RTUs, modems, and power supplies.
  • Lightning Protection Systems: The final termination point for down conductors from air terminals.
Placement

Installation & Location

Earth pits are installed directly above the buried electrode at every earthing point in the network — CCR rooms, substations, lighting control panels, junction boxes along the series circuits, and the base of masts and poles — with the chamber's cover set flush with finished grade or paving so it doesn't create a trip or mowing hazard while remaining findable for testing. The pit is sized to give a technician room to access the electrode connection, test link, and any maintenance-free earthing compound or backfill, and is positioned clear of building foundations, buried services, and heavy vehicle loading where practical. Its location is recorded on the site earthing drawing so each pit can be located, opened, and tested as part of the periodic resistance-testing programme without needing to trace cabling first.

Function

How Does It Work?

An earth pit works by providing a low‑impedance path for electrical current to flow into the earth, thereby dissipating fault currents and lightning surges safely. The key principles are:

  • Low Resistance Path: The earth electrode, buried in conductive soil (or backfill), offers a resistance typically less than 10Ω (and often below 5Ω for critical systems). This ensures that fault currents are rapidly diverted to ground, preventing dangerous touch voltages.
  • Dissipation of Lightning Current: During a lightning strike, the high‑frequency surge current is conducted through the down conductors to the earth pit, where it spreads into the surrounding earth without causing damage.
  • Equipotential Bonding: All metallic parts connected to the earth pit are brought to the same potential, eliminating dangerous voltage differences that could cause sparking or electric shock.
  • Testing and Maintenance: The test terminal allows periodic measurement of earth resistance using a 3‑point or 4‑point earth tester, ensuring the earthing system remains effective over time.
  • Corrosion Protection: The electrode material and backfill are chosen to resist corrosion, ensuring long‑term stability. Copper‑bonded steel rods, for example, provide excellent corrosion resistance while maintaining mechanical strength.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The earth pit does not exert active control, but it is the architectural access point for managing the electrode buried beneath it: the test terminal inside the pit lets the electrode be electrically isolated from the rest of the bonded system for a clean four-point resistance measurement, without disturbing any permanent connection. In this sense the "control logic" of an earth pit is procedural rather than electronic — a maintenance schedule dictates how often the link is opened and the resistance measured, and the result is compared against a threshold (commonly below 5–10Ω depending on the installation) to decide whether remedial work such as additional electrodes or chemical backfill is required. On more heavily instrumented sites, a continuous earth-monitoring relay can be wired across the test link to watch loop impedance in real time and report a fault condition to a central panel, but the earth pit's core role remains giving inspectors physical, testable access to a component that would otherwise be inaccessible once buried.

Construction

Physical Construction

The pit is a below-grade chamber built around the driven earth electrode, formed from precast concrete rings, brick masonry, or a heavy-duty PVC housing set into an excavated hole with the electrode's connection point sitting near the base or mid-height of the chamber. Conductive backfill — bentonite, charcoal, or a proprietary compound — is packed around the electrode inside the pit to keep contact resistance low and stable through seasonal changes in soil moisture. A cast-iron, steel, or heavy-duty plastic cover plate with lockable bolts sits flush with the surrounding grade, protecting the chamber from surface water ingress and unauthorised access while still allowing periodic opening for inspection. Inside, a removable test link or bolted joint isolates the electrode from the rest of the earthing system so its individual resistance can be measured without disturbing the wider bonding network, and the connection lug is sized in electrolytic copper to carry full fault current without overheating.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

High Earth Resistance

Dry soil conditions, soil resistivity changes, or electrode corrosion can increase earth resistance above acceptable limits. Regular testing and corrective measures (e.g., adding more electrodes, using chemical backfill, or installing additional pits) are necessary.

Corrosion of Electrode and Connections

Copper‑bonded rods can corrode over time, especially in aggressive soils (high salinity, acidic). Use of stainless steel or solid copper electrodes, and ensuring proper backfill, can mitigate this. Regular inspection and replacement are recommended.

Loose or Poor Connections

Connection lugs and test terminals can become loose due to vibration or thermal cycling, increasing resistance and potentially causing arcing. Torque‑tighten connections and use lock washers or anti‑vibration compounds.

Water Ingress and Mechanical Damage

Earth pits can be flooded or suffer mechanical damage (cracks in the cover or enclosure). Ensure proper drainage, use water‑tight covers, and protect pits from vehicle traffic or heavy equipment.

Inadequate Number of Pits

For large AGL installations, a single earth pit may not provide sufficiently low resistance. Multiple pits interconnected by a ring earth conductor are often required to achieve the desired resistance.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Measured Resistance Above Acceptable Limit
Likely Cause
Dry soil conditions, electrode corrosion, or an insufficient number of pits for the installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Test resistance at the pit using a fall-of-potential method; inspect the electrode and connections through the open pit; review the ring-earth interconnection to nearby pits.
Corrective Action
Add chemical backfill, install additional interconnected pits, or replace the electrode, then re-test to confirm resistance is within limits.
Loose or Corroded Test Terminal
Likely Cause
Vibration or thermal cycling has loosened the connection lug, or corrosion has built up on the terminal.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the terminal for looseness, corrosion, or signs of arcing; measure resistance across the connection.
Corrective Action
Clean and torque-tighten the connection, and apply lock washers or anti-vibration compound to prevent recurrence.
Water Ingress or Flooding in the Pit
Likely Cause
Poor drainage around the pit or a damaged/worn cover allowing water to enter.
Diagnostic Steps
Open the pit and check for standing water or silt buildup; inspect the cover seal and surrounding grading for drainage issues.
Corrective Action
Improve site drainage, fit a water-tight cover, and clear the pit of accumulated water and debris.
Cover Cracked or Pit Damaged by Traffic
Likely Cause
Vehicle traffic or heavy equipment has crossed the pit without adequate load rating or protection.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the cover and surrounding pavement for cracking or displacement; confirm the pit's location and marking against the site plan.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged cover with a load-rated unit, and mark or relocate the pit to keep it clear of routine vehicle traffic.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Assume Fault or Lightning Current May Be Present
Electrical Safety

Treat the electrode and its connections inside the pit as potentially carrying fault or lightning current at any time; do not disconnect a bonding link without first confirming the associated system is isolated or that the disconnection is planned and safe.

Confined Space & Pit Access
Operational

Check for water, gas accumulation, or unstable fill before entering a deep earth pit, and follow the aerodrome's confined-space and lone-working procedures for below-grade access.

Water Ingress in the Pit
Electrical Safety

Treat standing water in an earth pit as a potential conduction path during a fault or lightning event; avoid reaching into a flooded pit and pump it out before working on the electrode or test link.

Test Before Relying on a Connection
Electrical Safety

Measure earth resistance at the test link and verify continuity to the main earthing system after any work in the pit, and do not treat a reinstated connection as effective protection until it has been tested.

Cover & Frame Manual Handling
Personnel Safety

Use correct lifting technique or lifting tools for heavy pit covers and frames, and never leave a cover partially raised or unsecured over an open pit on the movement area.

Secure the Test Link After Testing
Compliance

Reinstate and torque the removable test link correctly after taking a resistance reading; a test link left open disconnects the electrode from the system it is meant to protect.

FOD Control at the Open Pit
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and covers accounted for at all times when the pit is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the cover, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a pit cover unseated or a test link disconnected after maintenance; verify a compliant, secure closure and a tested connection before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Seasonal Drift Is Normal, Sudden Jumps Are Not
Diagnostics

Earth pit resistance naturally rises and falls with soil moisture across wet and dry seasons, so judge a reading against the pit's own seasonal history rather than a single fixed number. A sudden jump outside that seasonal band, rather than the slow seasonal swing itself, is the signal worth chasing.

A Corroded Test Link Reads Like a Bad Electrode
Troubleshooting

Before condemning an earth electrode over a high reading, check the test link connection itself; a corroded or loose test link joint adds resistance that has nothing to do with the electrode or soil, and cleaning the joint alone can resolve an apparent failure.

Salt/Chemical Backfill Wears Out Over Years
Maintenance

Conductive backfill compounds used to lower resistance in poor soil leach out gradually over years of rainfall. A pit that met spec at commissioning can drift out of tolerance purely from backfill depletion, so factor replenishment into the long-term maintenance plan, not just electrode inspection.

Isolate the Pit From the Grid Before Testing
Technique

Testing an earth pit while it is still bonded into the wider earthing grid can mask a genuinely poor individual electrode, since parallel paths through other pits carry the test current. Disconnect the bonding link before a fall-of-potential test to get a true reading for that pit alone.

Probe Spacing Errors Distort Fall-of-Potential Results
Technique

Placing the potential probe too close to the electrode under test, rather than out near the theoretical 62% distance, is a common source of an artificially low or unstable reading. Where results look implausibly good, verify probe placement before trusting the number.

Log Readings Against the Same Pit Every Cycle
Process

Keep resistance readings tied to a fixed pit ID rather than a general area, since pits a few metres apart in different soil strata can behave very differently. A per-pit history is what actually reveals a genuine degrading trend versus normal site-to-site variation.
