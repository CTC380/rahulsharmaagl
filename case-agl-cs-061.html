AGL-CS-061 — Runway Edge Light Photometry Failure

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A runway edge lighting photometric investigation where optical degradation caused reduced intensity, beam distortion, and chromaticity shift — from electrical verification through photometric measurement, optical inspection, and alignment restoration.

1Case Study Overview

During a scheduled airfield lighting photometric inspection, a section of runway edge lights was found to be significantly below the required photometric intensity, even though the lights were operating electrically and no CCR alarms were present.

Pilots had also reported that the runway edge lights appeared dim and difficult to identify during wet-night operations, particularly from intermediate approach distances.

The Constant Current Regulator (CCR) was maintaining the selected 6.6 A current, output voltage was normal, and all affected fixtures were illuminated.

A detailed photometric investigation was initiated covering light intensity, beam distribution, chromaticity, fixture alignment, optical contamination, and LED optical performance.

The investigation identified severe optical degradation caused by lens contamination, UV-related lens aging, reflector deterioration, and minor fixture misalignment, resulting in failure to meet ICAO Annex 14 photometric requirements.

This case demonstrates that a runway edge light can be electrically operational while failing photometric compliance and providing inadequate runway visual guidance.

7Photometric Failure Principle

Electrical operation → Light emitted → Correct intensity → Correct beam distribution → Correct chromaticity → ICAO-compliant runway guidance
Photometric failure → Reduced intensity → Incorrect beam → Color deviation → Reduced visual range → Non-compliant runway guidance

A runway light can be electrically functional but operationally inadequate.

10Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The runway edge light photometric failure was caused by combined optical degradation, including:

  • Lens contamination.
  • UV-related lens aging.
  • Reflector deterioration.
  • LED optical degradation.
  • Minor fixture misalignment.

The electrical system remained healthy, but the fixture no longer produced ICAO-compliant intensity and beam distribution.

11Corrective Action

The maintenance team:

  1. Removed the affected fixture.
  2. Replaced the optical lens assembly.
  3. Replaced the degraded optical module.
  4. Restored reflector condition.
  5. Cleaned the optical compartment.
  6. Realigned the fixture.
  7. Performed photometric testing.
  8. Verified beam distribution.
  9. Confirmed chromaticity compliance.
  10. Updated photometric maintenance records.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: Runway edge lights ON. Pilots report poor wet-night visibility. CCR healthy. No electrical faults. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace the CCR.
B. Increase the current setting.
C. Measure photometric intensity, verify beam distribution, inspect the optical assembly, and check fixture alignment.
D. Replace all transformers.
Correct answer: C. Poor visibility with healthy electrical operation points to a photometric or optical problem. Photometric measurement, beam distribution verification, optical inspection, and alignment checking will identify the cause without unnecessary equipment replacement.

Quick Reference: Runway Lighting Photometry Procedure, Equipment, FAA Photometry & Guard Lights

A runway lighting photometry procedure sets the measurement distance, aligns the photometric head with the beam axis, records the output at each brightness step and compares it with the required values. Runway lighting photometry equipment is a calibrated photometric head, a data logger and alignment aids.

FAA runway lighting photometry requirements apply where the project cites FAA documents. Runway guard light photometry is tested in the same way as other fixtures, using the requirements for that light type.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about runway edge light photometric failure and ICAO Annex 14 compliance.

Q1What is AGL-CS-061?

AGL-CS-061 is an engineering training case study on a runway edge light that is electrically healthy but fails photometric requirements because of optical degradation. It covers intensity, beam distribution, chromaticity, alignment and optical inspection, through to corrective action and post-repair verification. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2Can a runway edge light be working electrically but still fail ICAO Annex 14 requirements?

Yes. A light can be illuminated, with the CCR holding the correct current and no alarms, and still fall short on intensity, beam distribution or chromaticity. Electrical health and photometric compliance are separate checks, and a lit fixture is not automatically a compliant one.

Q3How do you tell an electrical fault from a photometric failure?

Verify the electrical side first: CCR operation, current regulation, secondary voltage and fixture operation. If these are normal but the lights look dim or the beam looks uneven, the problem is likely optical. Photometric measurement then confirms it by comparing measured intensity and beam distribution against the required values.

Q4What causes runway edge light photometric failure?

Common causes include lens contamination, UV-related lens aging, optical scratching, LED lumen depreciation, reflector deterioration, internal condensation, fixture misalignment and incorrect replacement components. In this case, several of these combined: contaminated and aged lenses, degraded reflector and LED optics, and minor misalignment.

Q5Why did pilots report poor visibility in AGL-CS-061?

The fixtures produced reduced peak intensity and a distorted beam. In the training scenario the measured peak was 32,000 cd against a representative requirement of at least 50,000 cd. Combined with wet-night conditions, this made the edge lights dim and harder to identify from intermediate approach distances.

Q6Which test equipment is used for a runway edge light photometric investigation?

The case uses a portable AGL photometer, goniophotometric measurement equipment, a chromaticity meter, optical inspection tools, a true-RMS multimeter, fixture alignment instruments and a thermal imaging camera, with ICAO Annex 14 photometric reference data. All work must follow the airport's approved procedures, with appropriate PPE.

Q7Why does fixture alignment matter for photometric performance?

Beam distribution depends on the fixture being aimed correctly. Vertical aiming error or outward rotation reduces runway-edge coverage and creates inconsistent beam overlap, so even a good optical module can give poor runway guidance if it is misaligned.

Q8What was the root cause in AGL-CS-061?

Combined optical degradation: lens contamination, UV-related lens aging, reflector deterioration, LED optical degradation and minor fixture misalignment. The electrical system remained healthy, but the fixture no longer produced ICAO-compliant intensity and beam distribution.

Q9How was compliance restored after the fault was found?

The maintenance team replaced the optical lens assembly and degraded optical module, restored the reflector condition, cleaned the optical compartment and realigned the fixture. They then repeated photometric testing, verified beam distribution and chromaticity, and updated the photometric maintenance records.

Q10Why should runway edge lights undergo periodic photometric testing?

Optical performance degrades gradually through contamination, aging and misalignment, often without any electrical alarm. Periodic photometric testing detects this loss before it reduces runway conspicuity or breaches ICAO Annex 14 requirements.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

Runway edge light photometric failure is an optical-performance problem caused by lens contamination, optical aging, reflector deterioration, and beam misalignment, and a runway edge light can be electrically operational while failing ICAO Annex 14 photometric requirements and providing inadequate runway visual guidance.

Systematic photometric measurement, beam-distribution verification, chromaticity testing, optical inspection, LED assessment, and fixture alignment verification can identify photometric failures and restore compliant runway edge lighting performance.

In AGL engineering, the aircraft crew judges the runway by the light they see, not by the current flowing in the circuit, and photometric compliance is the true measure of runway edge lighting performance.
