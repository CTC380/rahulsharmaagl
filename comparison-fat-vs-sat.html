Testing & Commissioning Comparison

FAT VS SAT

Factory Acceptance Test vs Site Acceptance Test — two distinct stages in the quality-assurance and commissioning lifecycle of AGL equipment and systems. FAT proves the equipment is ready for the site. SAT proves the installed equipment and system are ready for operation at the airport.

02 · Definitions

What Is FAT & SAT?

FAT — Factory Acceptance Test

FAT is performed at the manufacturer's facility before dispatch. The test verifies that the equipment has been manufactured in accordance with the approved design and project specification, and that it performs its intended functions correctly.

Typical AGL equipment that may undergo FAT

  • Constant Current Regulators (CCR)
  • AGL control panels
  • Remote-control / monitoring panels
  • Lighting control systems
  • Transformers
  • Other packaged electrical equipment

Main objective: Prove that the manufactured equipment complies with the approved design and specification before shipment.

SAT — Site Acceptance Test

SAT is performed at the project site after installation. The test verifies that the installed equipment and system function correctly under actual site conditions, with all site interfaces, cabling, fixtures and supporting infrastructure in place.

SAT verifies

  • Correct installation
  • Cable connections and polarity
  • Insulation resistance
  • CCR output and regulation
  • Lighting operation and brightness steps
  • Control and remote operation
  • Monitoring and alarms
  • Integration with other installed equipment

Main objective: Prove that the installed equipment/system is ready for operation at the airport.

06 · Controlled vs Actual

Controlled Environment vs Actual Site Conditions

FAT — Controlled Factory Environment

  • Controlled temperature and conditions
  • Test load instead of actual fixtures
  • Simulated control signals
  • No site cable runs
  • No site interference
  • No site installation variables

SAT — Actual Airport Site

  • Actual site environment
  • Actual cables and cable runs
  • Actual fixtures and loads
  • Actual control system
  • Actual monitoring interfaces
  • Actual site installation conditions

SAT therefore provides information that cannot always be obtained during FAT — site cabling, actual fixture loads, control-system integration and the influence of the installed environment.

11 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: A successful FAT means SAT will also pass
Fact

No. A successful FAT does not automatically mean that the installed system will pass SAT. Site installation, cabling and integration issues can cause SAT failure even when the equipment is perfectly manufactured.

Myth: SAT can replace FAT
Fact

No. SAT does not replace FAT. Manufacturing defects should be identified during FAT, not discovered at site. Relying on SAT to catch equipment defects is not an acceptable quality-assurance strategy.

Myth: FAT and SAT are the same test performed in different locations
Fact

They are fundamentally different tests with different objectives, scopes and environments. FAT tests the equipment; SAT tests the installed system.

Myth: SAT is only about turning the system on
Fact

SAT involves comprehensive testing of installation, cabling, insulation, control, monitoring, alarm functions, intensity steps, system integration and operational performance — not just "power on."

Myth: If the equipment has a type-test certificate, FAT is not required
Fact

Type testing and FAT serve different purposes. Type testing validates the design; FAT verifies that the specific manufactured unit meets the specification and is free from manufacturing defects.

13 · Engineering Verdict

FAT vs SAT — The Engineering Perspective

Engineering Verdict

FAT and SAT are not interchangeable — they are complementary stages in the quality-assurance and commissioning lifecycle.

FAT provides confidence that the manufactured equipment meets the specification before it leaves the factory. SAT provides confidence that the installed system operates correctly at the airport, with all site interfaces, cabling and fixtures in place.

For an AGL project, FAT is primarily equipment-level verification before dispatch, while SAT is installation, functional and system-integration verification at the airport.

FATFactory — Equipment — Pre-dispatch verification.
SATSite — Installed system — Post-installation verification.

Critical engineering point: A successful FAT does not guarantee a successful SAT, and SAT should not be relied upon to catch manufacturing defects. Both tests are necessary and serve different purposes in the project quality-assurance framework.

14 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between FAT and SAT?

FAT is performed at the factory before shipment to verify equipment manufacture and functionality. SAT is performed at the site after installation to verify the installed system and site integration.

Does a successful FAT guarantee SAT success?

No. SAT can fail due to site installation issues — cabling errors, incorrect terminations, damaged cables, site environment, control-system integration problems — even when the equipment itself is perfectly manufactured.

Can SAT replace FAT?

No. SAT does not replace FAT. Manufacturing defects should be identified during FAT, not discovered at site. Relying on SAT to catch equipment defects is not an acceptable quality-assurance approach.

Who typically witnesses FAT?

The client or consultant may witness FAT according to the approved inspection and test plan. The manufacturer conducts the test; the client/consultant provides verification.

What happens if FAT fails?

Corrective action is taken at the factory, followed by retesting. The equipment should generally not be dispatched until FAT is passed.

What happens if SAT fails?

Site rectification is carried out — this may involve rework, repair, replacement or adjustment. After rectification, the affected portion is retested. The system is accepted only after SAT is passed.

Is FAT required for all AGL equipment?

The requirement for FAT depends on the project specification, contract requirements and the inspection and test plan. Critical equipment such as CCRs, control panels and packaged systems typically require FAT.

FAT and SAT requirements are project-specific. Always verify the approved ITP, contract requirements and applicable standards for the specific project — do not rely on a generic comparison page for project execution.
