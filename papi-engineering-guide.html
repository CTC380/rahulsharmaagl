PAPI ENGINEERING GUIDE

PAPI is one of the most critical visual approach aids at an airport. A properly engineered PAPI system depends not only on the light units themselves, but on siting, elevation, approach geometry, optical aiming, foundation stability, electrical supply, control, environmental conditions, photometric performance and commissioning.

Design → Site → Align → Install → Test → Commission → Maintain

01 · Definition

What Is PAPI?

Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) is a visual approach slope guidance system consisting of a horizontal array of light units positioned beside the runway. The units produce combinations of white and red light depending on the aircraft's vertical position relative to the designed approach path.

PAPI is therefore both a lighting system and an approach guidance system.

02 · Engineering Objective

Engineering Objective of PAPI

The engineering objective is not simply to install four light boxes. It is to establish a reliable visual reference.

Correct location + correct elevation + correct aiming + correct optical performance + reliable power + stable structure.

A PAPI installation can be electrically functional and still provide incorrect approach guidance if its geometry or optical alignment is incorrect.

4-Unit PAPI — Red/White Split Along the Approach

On the desired approach path, the pilot sees an even split — 2 red, 2 white — across the four units

Too high shows more white; too low shows more red — the balanced 2-red/2-white split is the design reference for the on-slope indication (2-unit PAPI wing-bar systems use an analogous single red/white split).
Quick Reference

PAPI Working Principle, Light Colours & Pilot Indications

The PAPI working principle is simple: each light unit projects a beam that is white in its upper part and red in its lower part, and the units are aimed at slightly different elevation angles. The pilot's height relative to the approach slope therefore decides how many units look red and how many look white.

Pilot positionPAPI 4 unit systemPAPI 2 unit system (APAPI)
Above slope4 white2 white
Slightly above slope3 white, 1 red—
On slope2 white, 2 red1 white, 1 red
Slightly below slope1 white, 3 red—
Below slope4 red2 red

How Does PAPI Work? Why Does PAPI Show Red and White?

Inside each unit a lamp or LED, a lens and a red filter split the beam vertically. The PAPI red signal means the aircraft is below that unit's aiming angle; the PAPI white signal means it is above. With the units aimed in small angular steps, the PAPI pilot indication changes step by step as the aircraft climbs or descends through the approach slope. The PAPI red white lights use only two PAPI light colors, red and white, so the signal reads at a glance.

PAPI 4-unit vs APAPI 2-unit PAPI optical principle

PAPI Optical System, Transition Sector, Sensitivity & Safe Zone

The PAPI optical system (source, lens, filter and, depending on the design, reflector or PAPI prism elements) must give a sharp PAPI transition sector — the narrow band where a unit changes from white to red. PAPI sensitivity, meaning how quickly the PAPI signal changes for a small height error, follows from the angular spacing of the unit aiming angles. A pilot who keeps an on-slope or above-slope indication stays inside the obstacle-protected PAPI safe zone, within the coverage defined by the applicable standard.

PAPI Light Colors

PAPI light colors are red and white only. Each unit's beam is white in its upper part and red in its lower part, so the colour a pilot sees from a unit depends on whether the pilot is above or below that unit's aiming angle.

PAPI Red Signal & PAPI White Signal

A PAPI red signal from a unit means the aircraft is below that unit's aiming angle. A PAPI white signal means it is above. Read the red and white units together to judge the position on the slope.

PAPI Pilot Indication

The PAPI pilot indication runs from four white lights (well above the slope), through two white and two red (on slope), to four red (well below). An APAPI shows one white and one red on slope.

PAPI Prism

Some PAPI designs use a prism or reflector element in the optical system, alongside the lens and red filter, to shape the beam and give a sharp white-to-red transition. The design differs by manufacturer, so follow the datasheet and keep the optical elements clean and undamaged.

PAPI Sensitivity

PAPI sensitivity is how quickly the indication changes for a small height error. It follows from the angular spacing of the unit aiming angles and the sharpness of each transition, which is why aiming has to be accurate.

PAPI Safe Zone

The PAPI safe zone is the part of the approach where a pilot seeing an on-slope or above-slope indication is protected from obstacles, within the coverage defined by the applicable standard. A below-slope indication means the aircraft is leaving it.

PAPI Site Survey & PAPI Layout

A PAPI site survey records runway centreline, threshold, ground profile, obstacles and cable routes. The PAPI layout drawing then fixes unit positions, spacing, offset, elevations, transformer locations and the cable route before installation begins.

PAPI glide path PAPI alignment PAPI geometry calculator

PAPI Threshold Crossing Height

PAPI threshold crossing height is the height of the aircraft's wheel path over the threshold when the pilot flies the on-slope indication. It depends on the units' distance from the threshold, the slope angle and the unit elevation, so re-confirm it by calculation and survey after any change. Use the PAPI geometry calculator for the geometry.

PAPI Accuracy & PAPI Tolerance

PAPI accuracy depends on holding each unit's aiming angle within the angular tolerance in the applicable standard. PAPI tolerance is tight, and it is met only with rigid foundations, a calibrated inclinometer and a repeatable procedure. Record measured angles against design values.

PAPI Electrical Supply, Isolating Transformers, Connectors & Wiring Diagram

In a conventional installation the PAPI electrical supply is a series circuit from a constant-current regulator. Each unit is fed through its own PAPI isolating transformer, and L-823 connectors join the primary and secondary sides. Some LED designs use a local driver instead, so always follow the manufacturer's PAPI wiring diagram.

PAPI Circuit Diagram & PAPI Wiring Diagram

The PAPI circuit diagram below shows conventional wiring: a CCR feeds a primary series loop and each light unit is fed through its own isolating transformer. The PAPI wiring diagram for a project must follow the manufacturer's drawing, because LED designs may differ.

PAPI circuit diagram — series-circuit wiring overview (conventional installation)

Why the PAPI Isolating Transformer Matters

The isolating transformer lets a lamp or LED failure on the secondary side happen without breaking the primary series circuit, and it must be rated for the unit load. A faulty PAPI connector is among the commonest causes of dark or flickering units.

What Is PAPI Photometric Testing? How Is PAPI Calibrated?

PAPI photometric testing is the PAPI photometry procedure: PAPI light measurement of PAPI light intensity and colour with a calibrated PAPI photometer, checked against the PAPI photometric requirements of the applicable standard. PAPI calibration procedure: set each unit's elevation angle against the surveyed reference, lock the adjustment, verify the transition photometrically, record before and after values and confirm by flight inspection where required.

PAPI Light Measurement & PAPI Light Intensity

PAPI light measurement uses a calibrated PAPI photometer to measure the intensity and colour of each unit and to check the red-white transition. PAPI light intensity is checked at each brilliancy step against the photometric requirements of the applicable standard.

How Is PAPI Calibrated?

PAPI calibration sets each unit's elevation angle against the surveyed reference with a calibrated inclinometer, locks the adjustment, then verifies the transition photometrically. Record values before and after, and confirm by flight inspection where required.

PAPI Acceptance Test & PAPI Commissioning Test

A PAPI acceptance test is the formal sign-off that the installed system meets the design and the standard. On a new system it forms part of the PAPI commissioning test. Both follow the six steps above and end in a signed report.

PAPI Test Report

A PAPI test report records the measured electrical, alignment and photometric results against design values, with the instruments and their calibration, date, location and a pass or fail result for each unit.

PAPI Troubleshooting Guide & PAPI Common Faults

Start with which units are affected. One dark unit points to its lamp or LED, transformer or connector. All units dark points to the series circuit or CCR. Wrong colours or a shifted slope point to alignment or the foundation.

What Causes PAPI Failure?

PAPI failure usually comes from electrical faults (lamp or LED, isolating transformer, connector, CCR), dirty optics or water ingress, misalignment from foundation settlement or impact, and lightning or surge damage. The cause table above groups them.

Keyword Reference

PAPI Lighting System: Working Principle, Angle Setting & Calibration

How a PAPI gives the pilot a glide path signal, and how it is set and checked.

PAPI lighting system

A PAPI lighting system is a row of four light units beside the runway that shows the pilot whether the aircraft is on, above or below the correct approach slope. It is sited, aimed and powered to the design for the runway it serves. See the earlier sections of this guide for siting and approach geometry.

PAPI lights working principle

Each PAPI unit projects a beam that is white above a set angle and red below it. The four units are aimed at slightly different angles, so the pilot sees a different mix of red and white lights depending on the aircraft's height on the approach. When the pilot sees an even mix, the aircraft is on the correct slope.

PAPI light angle setting

PAPI light angle setting is the vertical aiming angle of each unit, which fixes where its red-white transition falls. The angles are derived from the approach slope and the standard being followed, so none are given here. They are set with a calibrated instrument and recorded against each unit.

PAPI light calibration

PAPI light calibration checks that each unit's red-white transition is at the correct angle and that the beam is stable and level. It is done at installation, after any impact or unit change, and at intervals set by your maintenance plan. The results go into the PAPI test record. See the alignment and photometry sections of this guide.

Keyword Reference

PAPI System Installation, VASI Lighting System & LED PAPI Lights

Installing a PAPI, how it relates to the older VASI, and the move to LED.

PAPI system installation

PAPI system installation covers siting the units to the design, building the foundations, installing the units level and aligned, connecting power and control, and then aiming, calibrating and testing. Siting and alignment errors are hard to correct later, so each step should be checked before the next. See the installation checklists in this guide.

VASI lighting system

A VASI lighting system is an older visual approach slope indicator that uses bars of red and white lights to show the correct glide path. PAPI has replaced VASI at many aerodromes because it gives a clearer and more precise signal. Where VASI remains, it is maintained to the same standard of alignment and serviceability.

LED PAPI lights

LED PAPI lights use LED light sources in the PAPI units. They offer low power use and long life, but they must still meet the required photometric and colour performance and the aiming accuracy of the standard. Check compatibility with the existing circuit and CCR before replacing halogen units. See PAPI Maintenance, Heavy Weather & LED Systems above.

21 · Common Mistakes

Common PAPI Engineering Mistakes

01

Treating PAPI as four independent lights

PAPI is an integrated approach guidance system.

02

Ignoring foundation elevation

Small physical movement can affect optical geometry.

03

Setting alignment by visual judgement alone

Engineering references and measurement should be used.

04

Checking only electrical performance

A PAPI can be electrically healthy but optically incorrect.

05

Ignoring nearby lights

Visual interference can affect pilot interpretation.

06

Selecting cable without voltage-drop consideration

Ignoring connector condition

Failing to record final alignment

Replacing a unit without rechecking geometry

Treating commissioning as an ON/OFF test

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is PAPI?

Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) is a visual approach slope guidance system consisting of a horizontal array of light units positioned beside the runway. The units produce combinations of white and red light depending on the aircraft's vertical position relative to the designed approach path, so it is both a lighting system and an approach guidance system.

Q2What does a pilot see from a PAPI?

More white means the aircraft is above the desired approach path, more red means below, and a balanced transition means near the path. On a 4-unit PAPI the on-slope indication is an even split of 2 red and 2 white. The exact interpretation must follow the applicable aviation standard and approved procedure.

Q3Is a PAPI just four light boxes?

No. The objective is a reliable visual reference, combining correct location, elevation, aiming, optical performance, reliable power and a stable structure.

Q4Can a PAPI work electrically and still give wrong guidance?

Yes. A PAPI can be electrically functional and still provide incorrect approach guidance if its geometry or optical alignment is incorrect, and it can appear visually normal while incorrectly aimed.

Q5How should PAPI location be chosen?

Never simply because there is convenient space beside the runway. Location must be established against the runway centreline, threshold, intended approach path, threshold crossing height, approach slope, runway elevation, obstacle environment, runway safety areas, aircraft operating characteristics and applicable airport design standards, then checked against the approved design.

Q6Why is PAPI engineering fundamentally a geometry problem?

Because a small change in elevation, foundation settlement or optical aiming can affect the perceived approach guidance. PAPI location, elevation, aiming angle and threshold crossing height are all defined relative to the same approach geometry.

Q7How is PAPI alignment done?

Alignment should be treated as an engineering measurement rather than a visual guess: reference, measurement, adjustment, verification and recording. Controls include vertical aiming, horizontal alignment, unit-to-unit alignment, mechanical level, reference elevation, optical cleanliness and beam integrity.

Q8When should PAPI alignment be re-checked?

After initial installation, foundation work, mechanical adjustment, light-unit replacement and major maintenance, among other events.

Q9What components make up a PAPI system?

Light units, an optical system, mounting structure and foundation, power supply, a Power and Control Unit where applicable, and a CCR, isolation transformers and connectors in series-circuit installations. Control, monitoring, cabling, frangible mounting and photometry equipment complete the picture. The exact configuration depends on the design, manufacturer, airport and standards.

Q10What is the PAPI engineering chain?

It runs through runway requirement, approach geometry, PAPI location and elevation, light-unit arrangement, foundation and mounting, optical aiming, power and control, electrical installation, commissioning, photometric verification, operational monitoring and maintenance. A problem at any stage can affect the final guidance the pilot receives.

Q11How does PAPI work?

Each PAPI unit projects a beam that is white above and red below, and the units are aimed at slightly different elevation angles. The number of red and white units the pilot sees shows whether the aircraft is above, on or below the approach slope.

Q12Why does PAPI show red and white?

Red means the aircraft is below the unit's aiming angle and white means it is above. On a 4-unit PAPI an even 2 red, 2 white split indicates the aircraft is on the slope.

Q13What is the difference between PAPI and VASI?

VASI uses bars of light units and is largely superseded. PAPI uses a single wing bar of four units (or two in APAPI) with a sharper red-white transition, so the indication is easier to read and more precise.

Q14What is PAPI photometric testing?

It measures the intensity, colour and red-white transition of each PAPI unit with a calibrated photometer and compares the results with the applicable photometric requirements.

Q15How is PAPI calibrated?

Each unit's elevation angle is set against the surveyed reference with a calibrated inclinometer, locked, verified photometrically and recorded, with flight inspection where required.

Q16What causes PAPI failure?

Common causes are lamp or LED failure, transformer or connector faults, dirty or flooded optics, foundation movement that spoils alignment, and surge or lightning damage.

Q17What is the difference between a PAPI 4 unit system and a PAPI 2 unit system?

A PAPI 4 unit system gives five graded indications from all white to all red. A PAPI 2 unit system (APAPI) gives three: both white, one red and one white, or both red.

Q18What should a PAPI acceptance test include?

Visual, electrical, alignment, photometric and functional checks, then a PAPI test report recording measured against required values.

Q19How to set PAPI angle?

Set each PAPI unit's vertical angle from the design values for the approach slope and the standard being followed. Use a calibrated instrument to aim each unit so its red-white transition falls at its required angle, check that the housing is level, then lock the setting and record it against the unit. Re-check after any impact, unit change or pavement work, and confirm the result during the PAPI acceptance and calibration tests. The angle values come from your design and standard, so none are given here.
