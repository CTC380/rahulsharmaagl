FAA AC 150/5340 SERIES EXPLAINED

The FAA AC 150/5340 series is a key reference family for airport visual aids — markings, signs, the segmented circle, visual-aid installation and maintenance. For AGL engineers, the important point is that AC 150/5340 is not one single standard. It is a family of Advisory Circulars, each addressing a different part of the airport visual-aid system.

01 · Definition

What Is the FAA AC 150/5340 Series?

FAA Advisory Circulars in the 150/5340 family provide FAA guidance and standards related primarily to airport visual aids. Visual aids communicate information to pilots through lighting, markings, signs, visual guidance systems, location and installation arrangements, and maintenance practices. The series therefore connects directly with the operational visibility of an airport.

03 · Common Mistake

AC 150/5340 Is a Series, Not One Document

One of the most common mistakes is saying "According to FAA AC 150/5340..." without identifying the actual AC. That statement is incomplete — the suffix matters because each AC has a different purpose.

Incomplete

"According to FAA AC 150/5340..."

Correct

"According to FAA AC 150/5340-30J..." or "According to FAA AC 150/5340-18H..."

05 · AC 150/5340-1M

Standards for Airport Markings

This AC contains FAA standards for markings used on runways, taxiways and aprons. Markings provide visual information to pilots and surface vehicle operators — they can identify runway location, centreline, threshold, designation, taxiway routing, holding positions and other operational information. Markings therefore work together with AGL; a common engineering mistake is to treat visual guidance as only a lighting problem.

Typical Runway Marking Considerations

The visual system is a combination — Marking + Sign + Light + Geometry + Operational Procedure — where the effectiveness of one element can depend on the others. The exact marking arrangement must be established from the applicable FAA requirements and airport configuration.
06 · AC 150/5340-5D

Segmented Circle Airport Marker System

AC 150/5340-5D provides standards for a segmented circle airport marker system consisting of pilot aids and traffic-control devices. This is a more specialized document within the 5340 family, and the FAA currently lists it as active.

Segmented Circle — Typical Elements

Wind direction indicator at the centre, with landing-strip indicators, traffic-pattern indicators and a landing direction indicator (tetrahedron or T) arranged around it

07 · AC 150/5340-18H

Standards for Airport Sign Systems

AC 150/5340-18H addresses the siting, design and installation of signs on airport runways and taxiways. An airport sign is not simply a box containing LEDs — its operational purpose depends on message, location, orientation, colour, visibility and installation. A technically functioning sign can still be operationally ineffective if it is incorrectly located or oriented.

09 · AC 150/5340-30J

Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids

AC 150/5340-30J provides guidance and recommendations on installation of airport visual aids. For AGL engineers, this is one of the most important documents in the series — the difference between a good product and a good airport installation is often installation quality.

A Correctly Specified Fixture Can Still Perform Incorrectly Because Of

Incorrect LocationIncorrect OrientationIncorrect ElevationIncorrect AlignmentIncorrect MountingIncorrect Installation DetailPoor DrainagePoor Cable Routing
Requirement → Design → Drawing → Equipment → Installation → Testing

Installation should not be treated as an independent activity — it should originate from an approved design.

Inset Light Installation

Correct base, orientation, level, pavement interface, drainage, cable connection, mechanical protection, water-ingress prevention, torque, optical orientation.

Elevated Light Installation

Mounting, frangibility where applicable, height, alignment, orientation, mechanical stability, cable entry, structural condition.

PAPI Installation

Longitudinal/lateral position, elevation, azimuth, vertical aiming, unit-to-unit relationship, approach geometry — installation engineering plus optical verification.

Installation confirms the equipment is physically installed correctly. Commissioning confirms the complete system operates as intended. Acceptance testing confirms specified acceptance requirements are satisfied. These are different activities.

FAA Runway Lighting Standards

FAA runway lighting standards are split between two documents. Layout and installation follow AC 150/5340-30, and the fixtures follow AC 150/5345-46. FAA documents are not interchangeable with ICAO ones, so confirm the current edition of each and which framework the project specification names.

FAA Taxiway Lighting Standards

FAA taxiway lighting standards work the same way. The edge and centerline lighting layout comes from AC 150/5340-30, and the FAA taxiway light fixtures follow AC 150/5345-46. Check the current edition letters before specifying.

FAA PAPI Standards & FAA PAPI System

FAA PAPI standards sit in two documents. The equipment specification for the FAA PAPI system is AC 150/5345-28, and siting and installation follow AC 150/5340-30. Check the current edition letter of each before specifying. The PAPI Engineering Guide covers siting, aiming and photometry in detail.

FAA REIL

FAA REIL are runway end identifier lights: a pair of synchronised flashing lights, one on each side of the runway threshold, that identify the runway end. They are covered by the visual-aid design and equipment ACs, so confirm the current editions for the project.

FAA Runway and Taxiway Signs

FAA runway and taxiway signs follow AC 150/5345-44 for the equipment and AC 150/5340-18H for sign systems, which covers siting, design and installation. A sign has to match the actual runway and taxiway geometry.

FAA Obstruction Lighting

FAA obstruction lighting follows AC 70/7460-1 for the marking and lighting of obstructions, with equipment under AC 150/5345-43. Confirm the current editions and the requirement for the structure concerned.

10 · Critical Distinction

AC 150/5340 and the AC 150/5345 Series

The 150/5340 series primarily addresses visual aids — their design, installation, maintenance and related systems. The 150/5345 series contains specifications for airport lighting equipment and related equipment. This distinction is extremely important.

5340 = System

What the visual aid system needs to accomplish, and how it is arranged, installed and maintained.

5345 = Equipment

Equipment specifications and characteristics. For example: AC 150/5345-46F (light fixtures), AC 150/5345-10H (CCRs and regulator monitors), AC 150/5345-7F (underground electrical cable), AC 150/5345-3G (L-821 control panels).

The FAA's AIP/PFC checklist also identifies these 5345 documents alongside the 5340 visual-aid documents. This is a simplified engineering interpretation, not a replacement for reading the applicable AC.
System vs. Equipment — How the Two Series Relate

AC 150/5340 governs the visual-aid system as a whole; AC 150/5345 specifies the individual pieces of equipment used within it

12 · Compliance Philosophy

FAA Visual-Aid Compliance Is System-Based

A visual aid should not be declared compliant simply because the equipment is FAA-certified or FAA-specified. An FAA equipment specification establishes requirements for a particular equipment type — but installing a compliant product incorrectly does not automatically produce a compliant installation.

Compliance Can Involve

EquipmentInstallationLocationAlignmentElectrical SupplyControlOperational PerformanceMaintenance
Approved/Suitable Equipment ≠ Automatically Correct Installation

Electrical Testing

Insulation resistance, continuity, circuit resistance, CCR output, transformer verification, cable testing, earthing/bonding, functional switching.

Photometric Testing

Intensity, colour, beam alignment, optical coverage, uniformity where applicable.

For Photometry Engineers, the Practical Question Is

"What visual performance requirement applies to this particular light or visual aid?" Then determine the required measurement method, measurement geometry, intensity requirement, colour requirement, beam alignment, coverage, uniformity where applicable and acceptance criteria — do not apply one generic photometric test criterion to every AGL fixture.

A light can pass one discipline and fail the other. A fixture may illuminate but have incorrect beam alignment, reduced intensity, incorrect colour or optical contamination — ON ≠ Correct. A sign can illuminate correctly but still be wrong on location, orientation, message, visibility or clearance — Electrical Function ≠ Operational Compliance.

14 · Regulatory Context

FAA vs ICAO vs Indian Requirements

FAA ACs and ICAO Annex 14 are not interchangeable documents. ICAO provides international Standards and Recommended Practices; FAA ACs provide FAA-specific standards, guidance and recommendations. For Indian airport projects, FAA documents should not automatically be treated as the governing authority — the project may instead be governed by DGCA, AAI, ICAO, Indian Standards, or approved project specifications. FAA references apply where specifically required or adopted.

A Useful Engineering Hierarchy

1

Regulatory Requirement

What the authority requires.

2

Project Specification

What the project contract requires.

3

International Standard

For example, applicable ICAO provisions.

4

National Standard

For example, applicable Indian Standards.

5

FAA Standard

Applicable where FAA requirements are specified or adopted.

6

Manufacturer Requirement

Equipment-specific installation and operating requirements.

A common technical mistake is writing "This equipment is ICAO/FAA/IS compliant" — that statement is too broad. Instead identify: which requirement, which document, which revision, which equipment, which test, which installation, which evidence.

What Is the Difference Between ICAO and FAA Airport Lighting?

ICAO sets international Standards and Recommended Practices in Annex 14, which each State adopts. The FAA issues Advisory Circulars with detailed equipment specifications and installation details for US airports. The two cover similar ground but differ in structure and detail, so use the standard your project requires.

If the chain breaks, compliance evidence becomes weak.

Do not confuse equipment certification with system acceptance. A product can satisfy its equipment specification, but the installed system can still fail because of incorrect location, orientation, installation, wrong electrical configuration, poor maintenance, incorrect control or poor photometric performance. Equipment Compliance + Installation Compliance + System Verification = Stronger Compliance Evidence.

FAA Airport Lighting Certification

What is FAA airport lighting certification? It is the FAA airport lighting equipment certification program under AC 150/5345-53, in which certified equipment is listed so that owners can specify it. 19 · Non-Conformance & Troubleshooting

Non-Conformance & the FAA Troubleshooting Framework

When a visual aid fails, follow a structured non-conformance process rather than jumping straight to a fix.

Identify → Record → Determine (Electrical / Mechanical / Optical / Operational) → Investigate Root Cause → Rectify → Retest → Record Result → Close NCR

Troubleshooting Path

Symptom → Affected Visual Aid → Equipment → Electrical Circuit → Installation → Optical/Visual Condition → Control System → Root Cause → Corrective Action → Verification

Example: Multiple Taxiway Lights Fail

Check CCR → Circuit → Primary Cable → Transformers → Secondary Connections → Fixtures. If multiple lights fail together, investigate the common upstream element before replacing individual fixtures.

Example: A Single Inset Fixture Is Dark

Check fixture, secondary connector, secondary cable, isolating transformer, primary circuit, CCR, physical damage and water ingress — in that order, to avoid unnecessary replacement.

20 · System Reliability

ALCMS, Emergency Power, Lightning Protection & Water Ingress

ALCMS

Distinguish command, feedback, status, alarm and actual field condition. A control screen showing ON does not necessarily prove the field light is producing the required optical output.

Emergency Power

Primary power, secondary/emergency power, transfer, CCR behaviour, control system behaviour, restart, monitoring and failure indication.

Lightning Protection

Earthing, bonding, surge protection, cable protection, equipment protection and lightning-related failures — AGL equipment sits in an exposed environment.

Water is a major field reliability concern. Inspection should consider inset fixtures, bases, junctions, connectors, cables, transformer housings, drainage and sealing — a water-related failure can appear as an electrical fault but originate from installation or environmental conditions.

A Good Inspection Asks Three Questions

Is it installed? — physical condition. Is it working? — electrical/functional condition. Is it performing correctly? — operational/visual/photometric condition. All three matter.

Corrective Action

Detect → Isolate → Diagnose → Rectify → Test → Verify → Document

Final Engineering Principle

A Series, Not a Standard

FAA AC 150/5340 should never be treated as a single "FAA AGL standard." It is a family of documents addressing different aspects of airport visual aids.

The better engineering question is not "Is this AGL equipment FAA compliant?" — it is "Which FAA requirement applies to this visual aid, how has it been implemented, and what objective evidence proves that the installed system satisfies the applicable requirement?"

Identify → Select → Check Revision → Design → Specify → Install → Test → Measure → Verify → Document → Maintain → Reassess

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is the FAA AC 150/5340 series?

It is a family of FAA Advisory Circulars providing guidance and standards primarily for airport visual aids: lighting, markings, signs, visual guidance systems, location and installation arrangements, and maintenance practices. It is not one single standard; each AC addresses a different part of the airport visual-aid system.

Q2Is AC 150/5340 one document?

No. A common mistake is citing AC 150/5340 without identifying the actual AC. The suffix matters because each AC has a different purpose, so the correct form is to cite the specific document, such as AC 150/5340-30J or AC 150/5340-18H.

Q3Which ACs in the 150/5340 series does the FAA list as active?

The page lists five: 150/5340-1M (Airport Markings), 5D (Segmented Circle Airport Marker System), 18H (Standards for Airport Sign Systems, published Sep 30, 2024), 26C (Maintenance of Airport Visual Aid Facilities) and 30J (Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids). Always verify revision status on the FAA's Advisory Circular pages before use.

Q4What does AC 150/5340-18H cover?

It addresses the siting, design and installation of signs on airport runways and taxiways. An airport sign is not simply a box containing LEDs, and a technically functioning sign can still be operationally ineffective if it is incorrectly located or oriented. Siting must consider pilot viewing direction, taxiway geometry, runway intersections, clearance, visibility, orientation, nearby structures and mounting.

Q5What does AC 150/5340-26C cover?

It provides guidance and specifications for maintenance of airport visual-aid facilities. It matters because AGL performance changes over time: a system can pass commissioning and later deteriorate from dirt, water ingress, damaged lenses, corrosion, cable faults, connector deterioration, LED or lamp degradation, misalignment and other causes.

Q6What does AC 150/5340-30J cover?

It provides guidance and recommendations on installation of airport visual aids, and the page calls it one of the most important documents in the series for AGL engineers. A correctly specified fixture can still perform incorrectly because of incorrect location, orientation, elevation, alignment, mounting, installation detail, drainage or cable routing.

Q7What is the difference between AC 150/5340 and AC 150/5345?

The 150/5340 series primarily addresses visual aids: their design, installation, maintenance and related systems, meaning what the visual aid system needs to accomplish and how it is arranged, installed and maintained. The 150/5345 series contains specifications for airport lighting equipment and related equipment.

Q8What is the segmented circle covered by AC 150/5340-5D?

AC 150/5340-5D provides standards for a segmented circle airport marker system consisting of pilot aids and traffic-control devices. Typical elements include a wind direction indicator at the centre with landing-strip indicators, traffic-pattern indicators and a landing direction indicator such as a tetrahedron or T arranged around it.

Q9Why is AC 150/5340 important for AGL engineering?

AGL cannot be engineered only by selecting a light fixture. An installation has to work within the complete visual guidance environment of runway, taxiway, markings, signs, lighting, installation, maintenance and operational use. The 150/5340 series provides important FAA references for several of these elements, but not all of them on its own.

Q10Are installation, commissioning and acceptance testing the same thing?

No. Installation confirms the equipment is physically installed correctly, commissioning confirms the complete system operates as intended, and acceptance testing confirms that specified acceptance requirements are satisfied. Installation should originate from an approved design and not be treated as an independent activity.

Q11What is FAA AC 150/5345-46?

It is the FAA specification for runway and taxiway light fixtures, covering their construction, performance and photometric requirements.

Q12What is FAA AC 150/5345-28?

It is the FAA equipment specification for PAPI systems; check the current edition letter before specifying.

Q13What is FAA AC 150/5340-30?

It is the FAA guidance for design and installation details of airport visual aids, including runway, taxiway and approach lighting.

Q14

What is FAA airport lighting certification?

It is the FAA program under AC 150/5345-53 that lists certified airport lighting equipment, which owners and designers can specify.

Q15What is the difference between ICAO and FAA airport lighting?

ICAO sets international Standards and Recommended Practices in Annex 14, which each State adopts. The FAA issues Advisory Circulars with detailed equipment specifications and installation details for US airports. The two cover similar ground but differ in structure and detail, so use the standard your project requires.
