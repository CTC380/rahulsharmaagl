Earth Tester

A specialized instrument used to measure the resistance of grounding systems and earth electrodes in AGL installations. Also known as an earth resistance tester or ground resistance meter, this tool verifies that earthing systems provide a low-resistance path to ground — essential for lightning protection, equipment safety, and proper operation of sensitive electronic systems.

Overview

What Is It?

An Earth Tester (also known as a ground resistance tester or earth resistance meter) is a specialized instrument designed to measure the resistance of an electrical grounding system to earth. In AGL applications, it is used to verify that:

  • Earthing systems provide a low-resistance path to ground for fault currents and lightning strikes.
  • Earth electrodes (rods, plates, or grids) are properly installed and maintained.
  • Equipment grounding meets safety standards and provides adequate protection.
  • Lightning protection systems have sufficient conductivity to safely dissipate strike energy.
  • Earth resistance remains within acceptable limits over time despite corrosion or soil changes.

Earth testers typically use the three-terminal (or four-terminal) fall-of-potential method or the clamp-on (stakeless) method. They are essential for commissioning new AGL installations, performing periodic safety inspections, and troubleshooting earthing-related issues.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The earth tester exists because a grounding system that looks intact can still be electrically useless if the resistance to true earth has crept up over time from corrosion, soil drying, or a loosened connection — and that failure is invisible until it's needed, during a lightning strike or fault current event, when it's too late to fix. Its purpose is to catch that silent degradation before it matters, by giving technicians an actual resistance value to compare against acceptable limits rather than assuming a grounding system installed correctly years ago still performs the same way today.

This matters because earthing is what keeps a fault current or lightning strike from finding its way through equipment, cabling, or a person, rather than dissipating safely into the ground. Periodic testing turns that protection from an assumption into a verified fact, which is why it's a standard part of both commissioning new AGL installations and ongoing safety inspection cycles.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Earth testers are used across all AGL infrastructure where grounding is critical:

  • CCR Rooms and Equipment Shelters: Verifying that the main earthing system provides low resistance for equipment safety.
  • Lightning Protection Systems: Testing earth electrodes for lightning rods and air terminals on structures.
  • Distribution Panels: Ensuring that all metallic enclosures and equipment are properly grounded.
  • Junction Boxes and Transformers: Verifying earthing of isolating transformers and cable screens.
  • Lighting Fixtures: Checking the earthing of metallic fixture components.
  • Underground Cable Networks: Testing earthing points along primary and secondary cable routes.
  • Standalone Structures: Testing earthing at PAPI units, wind cones, beacons, and other standalone equipment.

Testing is performed during installation, as part of routine preventive maintenance, and after lightning strikes or electrical faults.

Care

Storage & Handling

Earth stakes and test leads are cleaned of soil and moisture before being packed away, since dried mud left on a stake or clamp can corrode contacts and affect readings on the next test. Leads are coiled without sharp bends and stored in the case's dedicated reels or compartments, keeping the current and potential leads separated from each other to avoid tangling on the next setup. The instrument itself is kept dry and within its rated storage temperature range, with batteries removed for extended storage to prevent leakage into the meter's electronics. Given how central this tester is to safety verification, it's calibrated on the interval specified by the manufacturer and checked against a known reference before any campaign of commissioning or post-lightning-strike testing, rather than assumed accurate from a stored calibration certificate alone.

Function

How Does It Work?

Earth testers operate using two primary methods. The most common is the three-terminal (or four-terminal) fall-of-potential method, which requires placing auxiliary test spikes (current and potential electrodes) into the ground at specific distances from the earth electrode under test. The tester injects a known current between the earth electrode and a remote current electrode, then measures the voltage drop between the earth electrode and a potential electrode placed at varying distances. The resistance is calculated using Ohm's Law (R = V/I). This method is highly accurate but requires driving test spikes into the ground.

The clamp-on (stakeless) method uses a clamp with two coils — one to inject a signal and one to measure the resulting current — and does not require auxiliary electrodes. It is faster and more convenient but requires that the earth electrode under test be part of a complete loop. Modern earth testers often offer both methods, along with data logging, Bluetooth connectivity, and auto-ranging capabilities.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The two methods use different physical set-ups but converge on the same internal architecture. In fall-of-potential mode, an internal AC signal generator (deliberately using a non-mains, often frequency-swept, test signal to reject 50/60 Hz interference) drives current out through the C1 terminal, through the earth electrode, and back via the remote current spike C2. A separate, high-impedance potential path measures the voltage between the electrode and the P1 spike, isolating this sense path from the current-carrying path so that spike and lead resistance don't corrupt the reading — the same four-terminal principle used in loop resistance testing. In clamp-on mode, the MCU instead drives an injection coil in the clamp and reads the induced current in a second sense coil around the same jaw, since there are no auxiliary spikes to connect. Either way, the front-end signal is synchronously demodulated (locked to the injected test frequency) before the ADC, which is what lets the instrument reject stray earth currents and 50 Hz mains pickup that would otherwise swamp a simple DC measurement. The MCU then computes R = V/I, and on multi-point tests, checks whether resistance has plateaued as spike distance increases to flag an invalid fall-of-potential curve before displaying a final value.

Both test methods reduce to the same current-injection / potential-sense pair feeding a synchronous demodulator — this is what lets the instrument measure a real earth resistance in the presence of stray ground currents.
Operating Principle

Two Measurement Methods

Both methods resolve to the same Ohm's Law calculation, but they trade off differently: fall-of-potential needs open ground to drive test spikes, while clamp-on trades that requirement for the need for a genuinely closed earthing loop.
Construction

Physical Construction

The earth tester is housed in a rugged, weather-resistant polymer case sized for field use, with recessed, shrouded terminals for the earth electrode, current, and potential test leads to prevent accidental contact during live testing. Internally, the AC test-signal generator, synchronous detector, and battery pack sit on a shielded PCB assembly designed to reject mains-frequency interference picked up through the long test-lead runs used in fall-of-potential testing. A backlit LCD sits behind a protective window, and a sealed keypad or rotary selector switch provides splash-proof control input. The unit typically ships with a reel or spool of insulated test lead, a set of driven earth stakes, and a carrying case or harness sized to transport the extended leads and stakes needed to reach the 20–50m electrode spacing used in standard test setups.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Stray Voltage Interference

Nearby energized cables or stray currents can affect earth resistance measurements, especially in industrial environments. Using a test frequency of 125 Hz (rather than 50/60 Hz) helps filter out interference. Some testers include a "noise" indicator to warn of high interference levels.

Improper Electrode Spacing

For the fall-of-potential method, incorrect spacing of the current and potential electrodes can lead to inaccurate readings. The potential electrode should be placed at 62% of the distance between the earth electrode and the current electrode for optimal accuracy.

Dry or Frozen Soil

Soil resistivity increases significantly in dry or frozen conditions, leading to higher earth resistance readings. Testing should be performed when soil conditions are representative of normal operating conditions, or moisture should be added to the electrode area to simulate worst-case scenarios.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Unstable or Wildly Fluctuating Reading
Likely Cause
Stray voltage or noise from nearby energised cables is being picked up by the test leads, especially in industrial or airfield environments with buried power circuits.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the tester's noise indicator (if fitted); switch the test frequency to 125 Hz if currently on 50/60 Hz to filter out mains-related interference; move test spikes further from known live cabling and repeat.
Corrective Action
Retest once interference is reduced, and if a noise indicator is available, don't accept a reading taken while it is active; document the interference source for future test planning at that site.
Reading Doesn't Match Previous Test at Same Location
Likely Cause
Incorrect electrode spacing or placement for the fall-of-potential method, or soil conditions (dry, frozen, or waterlogged) have changed significantly since the last test.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the potential electrode is positioned at 62% of the distance between the earth electrode and current electrode; confirm both auxiliary spikes are firmly seated and free of corrosion; note current soil moisture/temperature conditions.
Corrective Action
Correct electrode placement and retest; if soil conditions are the cause, add moisture to the electrode area to simulate worst-case conditions or schedule a repeat test when conditions are representative, and record both readings for trending.
Tester Shows Open Circuit or No Reading
Likely Cause
A broken or poorly seated test lead, a corroded spike connection, or the earth electrode itself is disconnected from the system under test.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect all test leads and crocodile clips for continuity and secure contact; check spike penetration into the soil; confirm the earth electrode connection point is clean and making good contact.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged leads or clean corroded connections, reseat spikes fully into the ground, and repeat the test; if the electrode itself is suspect, verify its continuity separately before concluding the earthing system has failed.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Disconnect Bonding Before Testing
Electrical Safety

Disconnect the electrode or system under test from the rest of the earthing/bonding network before measuring, so the reading reflects that electrode alone and stray current from the wider system does not affect it or the tester.

Never Test on a Live or Recently-Struck System
Electrical Safety

Do not connect the tester to a system that may be carrying fault current, and avoid earth testing immediately after a lightning event until the site has been confirmed safe.

Locate Buried Services Before Placing Spikes
Operational

Check for buried cables, pipes, or ducts before driving auxiliary test spikes into the ground, and keep spike placement clear of live cable routes.

PPE for Spike Driving
Personnel Safety

Wear appropriate PPE (gloves, eye protection) when driving or removing test spikes, particularly in hard or stony ground.

Airside Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations before testing earthing systems within the movement area or its safety zones.

Verify Instrument Calibration
Compliance

Confirm the tester's calibration is current and check it against a known reference resistance periodically, since an out-of-calibration reading can mask a genuine high-resistance earth fault.

Reinstate Bonding After Testing
Compliance

Reconnect and verify all bonding and earthing connections disturbed for the test before leaving the site, so the system is not left with a compromised fault path.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Trend Readings Across Seasons, Not Just Year-on-Year at the Same Time
Testing

Soil moisture and temperature swing earth resistance readings significantly between wet and dry seasons; a single annual test taken at the same time each year can mask a genuinely degrading earth system if that reading happens to fall during a consistently favourable season. Logging readings across the year gives a far more reliable trend than one spot-check.

Auxiliary Electrode Spacing Affects Reading Accuracy More Than People Expect
Technique

The fall-of-potential method's accuracy depends heavily on placing the auxiliary test electrodes far enough apart and outside the resistance area of the electrode under test; cramped spacing due to limited space on an airfield can produce a misleadingly low or unstable reading even with a perfectly functioning instrument.

Test Individual Electrodes Before Assuming a Grid Reading Reflects Every Rod
Diagnostics

A combined earthing grid or ring can measure well overall while one individual electrode within it has failed or corroded almost completely, its poor performance masked by the parallel paths from healthy electrodes nearby; where a grid reading looks marginal, testing individual electrode contributions helps find which one actually needs attention.

Stray Currents Near Active Equipment Can Distort Readings
Field Practice

Testing near energised equipment, buried cabling, or other earthing systems can introduce stray currents that bias an earth resistance reading; where possible, de-energise nearby circuits during testing or use an instrument and technique designed to reject interference, and treat a suspiciously unstable reading as a possible interference symptom rather than a genuine fault.

Corroded Electrode Connections Fail Silently Between Tests
Durability

A test lead connection or bonding point can corrode to high resistance without any visible external sign, skewing the reading independently of the electrode's actual condition in the ground; inspect and clean physical connection points at every test rather than relying on the meter reading alone to judge overall system health.

Calibrate the Tester Itself, Not Just the System It's Testing
Calibration

An earth tester carried in a field kit sees rough handling and can drift out of calibration without any obvious symptom on the display; verify it against a known resistance standard on the same interval as other precision test equipment rather than assuming it's accurate simply because it still produces a plausible-looking reading.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about earth testers in AGL maintenance: what they measure, methods, accuracy, standards, calibration and common faults.

Q1What is an earth tester?

An earth tester, also called a ground resistance tester or earth resistance meter, measures the resistance of an electrical grounding system to earth. It verifies that earthing and lightning protection systems give a low-resistance path for fault currents and lightning strikes.

Q2Why test earth resistance?

A grounding system that looks intact can still be electrically useless if resistance has crept up from corrosion, soil drying or a loose connection. That failure is invisible until a lightning strike or fault, so testing catches the silent degradation before it matters.

Q3Where is an earth tester used in AGL?

CCR rooms and equipment shelters, lightning protection earth electrodes, distribution panels, junction boxes and transformers, lighting fixtures and underground cable networks.

Q4How does an earth tester measure resistance?

Mainly by the fall-of-potential method. It injects a known current between the electrode under test and a remote current electrode, measures the voltage drop to a potential electrode, and calculates R = V/I. Clamp-on (stakeless) testers are also available.

Q5How far apart should the test spikes be placed?

The current electrode is typically 20–50 m from the earth electrode, and the potential electrode at about 62% of the current electrode distance. Incorrect spacing is a common cause of readings that don't match previous tests.

Q6What are the typical specifications?

Resistance range of 0.01–2,000 Ω, accuracy of ±2% of reading ± 2 counts, a 125 Hz test frequency at 50 V AC or less, battery power and a CAT III 300 V / CAT IV 150 V safety rating.

Q7Why does an earth tester use 125 Hz?

It avoids interference from mains frequency, which helps filter stray voltage from nearby energised cables. Some testers also have a noise indicator, and a reading should not be accepted while it is active.

Q8Which standards apply to earth testers?

The main references are IEC 61557-5 (earth resistance test equipment), IEEE 81 (measuring earth resistivity and ground impedance), BS 7430 (protective earthing) and FAA AC 150/5340-26 (maintenance of airport visual aid facilities). Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should an earth tester be calibrated and checked?

Typical intervals are before each use for a functional self-test, quarterly for test lead and electrode inspection and battery checks, and annually for calibration against a traceable resistance standard and noise indicator verification. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is my earth resistance reading unstable?

Stray voltage from nearby energised cables or buried power circuits is being picked up by the test leads. Check the noise indicator, use 125 Hz, move the spikes further from live cabling and retest.

Q11Why doesn't a reading match the previous test at the same location?

Incorrect electrode spacing or placement, or changed soil conditions such as dry, frozen or waterlogged ground. Re-check spacing and record soil conditions with each test.
