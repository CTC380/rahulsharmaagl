LED PCB

A specially designed printed circuit board that hosts LED chips and their associated electronic components in AGL fixtures. Provides the electrical interconnections, thermal management, and mechanical support essential for reliable LED operation in demanding airfield environments.

Overview

What Is It?

An LED PCB (Light‑Emitting Diode Printed Circuit Board) is a specialised circuit board that forms the core of LED‑based airfield ground lighting fixtures. It is designed to mechanically support and electrically interconnect LED chips, drivers, resistors, and other passive components, while also providing a path for thermal dissipation to prevent overheating. Unlike standard PCBs, LED PCBs are often constructed with metal‑core substrates (MCPCB – Metal Core Printed Circuit Board) to enhance heat transfer away from the LEDs.

Key functions of an LED PCB in AGL applications include:

  • Electrical Interconnection: Providing traces and pads to connect LEDs in series or parallel configurations, and to interface with the LED driver.
  • Thermal Management: Using copper planes, thermal vias, and a metal substrate to conduct heat away from the LED junctions to the fixture's heat sink or housing.
  • Mechanical Support: Holding the LEDs and other components securely in place, with mounting holes or pads for attachment to the fixture.
  • Optical Alignment: Ensuring that LEDs are precisely positioned to achieve the required beam pattern when combined with lenses or reflectors.
  • Reliability: Designed to withstand extreme temperatures, vibration, moisture, and thermal cycling typical of airfield environments.

LED PCBs are custom‑designed for each fixture type, with specific layouts to accommodate the number and arrangement of LEDs, and are often potted or coated to protect against moisture and corrosion.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The LED PCB's purpose is to hold the fixture's actual light-emitting elements in the precise electrical and thermal environment they need to perform reliably — carrying current to each LED without excessive voltage drop, and carrying heat away from each LED junction fast enough to keep it within its rated temperature. Because an LED's light output, colour, and lifespan are all directly sensitive to junction temperature, the PCB's metal-core construction and thermal-via design are not just a manufacturing convenience but a core part of what keeps the fixture's photometric performance stable over its service life. Its role in physically positioning each LED also feeds directly into the fixture's optical performance, since the beam pattern produced by the lens or reflector above it depends on the LEDs being placed exactly where the optical design assumes they are.

Application

Where Is It Used?

LED PCBs are used in every LED‑based AGL fixture, including:

  • Runway Edge and Centreline Lights: PCBs with multiple high‑power LEDs arranged to produce the required beam pattern and colour.
  • Taxiway Edge and Centreline Lights: Similar PCBs but with blue or green LED chips.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: High‑intensity LED arrays on PCBs for sequenced flashing lights and barrettes.
  • Obstruction Lights: PCBs with red or white high‑power LEDs for aviation warning beacons.
  • PAPI and VASI Units: PCBs with closely matched LED pairs to achieve the required colour‑transition characteristics.
  • Apron Floodlights: Large PCBs with many high‑power LEDs for area illumination.
  • Stop Bars and Guard Lights: PCBs with LEDs arranged in linear arrays for visual barriers.

They are also used in:

  • Retrofit Upgrades: Replacing halogen lamps with LED modules that include a custom PCB.
  • Prototyping and R&D: Developing new fixtures and testing LED configurations.
  • Spare Parts: Replacement PCBs for field maintenance of LED fixtures.
Placement

Installation & Location

The PCB mounts directly onto the fixture's internal heat sink, usually via a thermal interface pad or paste, and secured so the full board area makes even thermal contact rather than being fastened only at the corners. Positioning within the housing is set by the fixture's optical design (the PCB sits at the reflector's focal point or the lens's designed working distance) rather than by a general rule, since even a small misplacement shifts the beam pattern out of specification. Power and control leads from the LED driver terminate on the PCB's own connector or solder pads, kept short and strain-relieved so vibration in the fixture housing doesn't fatigue the joint over time; no separate field cabling is involved beyond the fixture's existing driver leads.

Function

How Does It Work?

The LED PCB serves as the central hub for the LED module:

  • Substrate Material: Most AGL LED PCBs are fabricated on an aluminium‑core (or copper‑core) substrate, which is a sandwich of a metal base, a dielectric layer, and a copper circuit layer. The metal core provides excellent thermal conductivity, transferring heat from the LEDs directly to the fixture's heat sink.
  • Circuit Traces: Copper traces on the top layer carry current from the connector or driver to the LED pads. The trace width is designed to handle the required current without excessive voltage drop or heating. For high‑power LEDs, thick copper (≥ 2 oz) is common.
  • Thermal Vias: In some designs, thermal vias (plated holes) connect the top copper layer to the metal core, further enhancing heat transfer.
  • LED Placement: LEDs are soldered onto the PCB pads using reflow soldering. The pads are designed to match the LED's footprint, with proper spacing to avoid short circuits and to allow for optical alignment.
  • Additional Components: The PCB may also hold resistors for current limiting, Zener diodes for ESD protection, and connectors for wiring. Some advanced PCBs integrate the LED driver circuitry directly.
  • Optical Interface: The PCB often includes alignment markers or mounting features for secondary optics (lenses, reflectors) to ensure that the light output is directed correctly.
  • Environmental Protection: After assembly, the PCB may be coated with a conformal coating or potted in resin to protect against moisture, dust, and corrosive agents.

The PCB's design must balance electrical performance, thermal dissipation, and mechanical robustness to meet the stringent requirements of airfield lighting.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The LED PCB is the lowest, purely passive layer of the fixture's control architecture — it carries no logic, makes no decisions, and holds no feedback loop of its own. Everything that determines the intensity the board's LEDs actually emit — the ALCMS's chosen step, the CCR's regulated loop current, the driver's constant-current or constant-voltage output — is decided upstream and simply arrives at the PCB's input connector as a current the copper traces are sized to carry without excessive voltage drop or resistive heating. The board's only "response" to that incoming current is physical rather than logical: proportionally more light output, and proportionally more heat generated at each LED junction, conducted down through the dielectric layer and metal core toward the fixture's heat sink (or, in higher-power designs, into a heat pipe). Because the PCB has no sensing or protection circuitry of its own, it depends entirely on the driver upstream to keep the delivered current within the LEDs' rated limits and on the metal-core substrate's thermal design to keep junction temperature within range — if either of those upstream safeguards fails, the PCB itself has no mechanism to intervene. Its place in the architecture is therefore purely as the electrical and thermal interface where the control chain's final output becomes visible light: everything before it is logic and regulation, everything at it is fixed, physical response.

Construction

Physical Construction

The board is built on either an aluminium-core (MCPCB) substrate — a thin dielectric layer sandwiched between the copper circuit layer and an aluminium base, chosen specifically for its thermal conductivity — or a standard FR4 board with thermal vias drilled through to a copper pour, used where cost matters more than peak heat extraction. Copper layer thickness runs 1–4oz depending on the current the LEDs draw, and 1 to 48 LEDs are reflow-soldered directly onto the board per fixture design, with a conformal coating applied afterward to protect the joints from moisture and the pavement or fixture environment. The finished board bolts to the fixture housing through pre-drilled mounting holes positioned to press the aluminium core (or thermal via field) flat against the housing's own heatsink surface, since the PCB's construction only works as a thermal path if that mechanical contact is solid.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Thermal Management Failures

Inadequate thermal contact between the PCB and the fixture's heat sink can lead to LED overheating, causing reduced light output, colour shift, or premature failure. Ensure proper thermal interface material (TIM) is used and that mounting screws are tightened to the correct torque. Regular inspection of thermal paste/pad condition is recommended.

Moisture and Corrosion

Despite conformal coating, moisture can penetrate through cracks or at component leads, causing corrosion and short circuits. This is especially prevalent in coastal or high‑humidity environments. Use of high‑quality coating materials and sealing of all entry points is essential. Periodic inspection for signs of corrosion is advised.

Solder Joint Fatigue

Thermal cycling (expansion and contraction) can cause solder joints to crack, leading to intermittent connections or complete failure. This is particularly critical for large LEDs with significant thermal mass. Using lead‑free solder with appropriate alloy and proper reflow profile is necessary to minimise fatigue.

Mechanical Damage

During installation or maintenance, the PCB can be damaged by impact or excessive bending. This can crack the substrate or break traces. Handle PCBs with care and ensure they are securely mounted to prevent vibration‑induced fatigue.

Incorrect LED Bin or Orientation

Replacing a PCB with mismatched LED bins (colour or flux) or incorrectly orienting the LEDs can result in incorrect light output or beam pattern. Always use manufacturer‑approved replacement PCBs and follow orientation markings.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced Light Output or Colour Shift
Likely Cause
Poor thermal contact between the PCB and the fixture's heat sink causing LED overheating.
Diagnostic Steps
Check thermal interface material condition and mounting screw torque; measure PCB temperature under normal operation against the rated maximum.
Corrective Action
Re-apply thermal interface material and correct mounting torque to restore full thermal contact with the heat sink.
Intermittent or Corroded Connections
Likely Cause
Moisture penetration through cracks in the conformal coating or at component leads, especially in coastal/high-humidity sites.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the PCB under magnification for coating cracks, discolouration, or visible corrosion at leads and connections.
Corrective Action
Replace a corroded PCB, and specify a high-quality conformal coating with fully sealed entry points for the replacement.
Intermittent LED Failure Under Vibration or Thermal Cycling
Likely Cause
Solder joint fatigue from repeated thermal expansion and contraction cycles.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect solder joints under magnification for cracking, especially around larger LEDs with higher thermal mass; flex-test gently while monitoring for intermittent operation.
Corrective Action
Replace the PCB if solder joint fatigue is confirmed; ensure replacement PCBs use lead-free solder with an appropriate reflow profile.
Cracked Substrate or Broken Traces After Installation
Likely Cause
Mechanical damage from impact or excessive bending during handling or maintenance.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the PCB substrate and trace routing for visible cracks or breaks; test continuity across suspect traces.
Corrective Action
Replace a physically damaged PCB and handle future replacements with care, ensuring secure mounting to prevent vibration-induced fatigue.
Incorrect Colour or Beam Pattern After PCB Replacement
Likely Cause
A mismatched LED bin (colour or flux) was fitted, or the PCB was installed with incorrect orientation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the replacement PCB's part number and LED bin code against the manufacturer's approved list; verify orientation markings against installation instructions.
Corrective Action
Replace with a manufacturer-approved, correctly binned PCB and confirm correct orientation before re-commissioning the fixture.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate the Series Circuit Before Opening the Fixture
Electrical Safety

Although the PCB itself runs on low-voltage DC from the internal LED driver, that driver is fed from the primary series loop via the isolating transformer; de-energise and lock out the circuit at the CCR and verify zero energy before opening the fixture to access the board.

ESD-Sensitive Components
Operational

Use anti-static precautions (wrist strap, grounded mat) when handling the board, since LED chips and driver-interface components can be damaged by electrostatic discharge in a way that isn't visible until the fixture is back in service.

Hot Surface After Operation
Personnel Safety

Allow the board and its metal-core substrate to cool before handling after active operation; the MCPCB is specifically designed to conduct heat away from the LEDs, so the board itself runs hot during normal use.

Bright Light Exposure During Bench Testing
Personnel Safety

Avoid direct, close-range viewing of the LED array when energising the board for a bench test; treat it the same as looking at an installed fixture at close range.

Handle Without Flexing the Board
Operational

Support the board across its full area when handling, and avoid flexing it, since bending an MCPCB or FR4 board can crack solder joints, delaminate the copper layer, or degrade the thermal interface to the housing without any immediately visible sign.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep fasteners, thermal-interface material backing, and any removed hardware accounted for at all times when the fixture is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify all LED channels illuminate correctly and the driver reports normal current before closing out the fixture and re-energising the circuit.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Junction Temperature Drives Lumen Depreciation More Than Drive Current
Reliability

Two boards run at identical drive current can age at very different rates if one has a compromised thermal path to the heat sink. Track board-level thermal performance as its own metric rather than assuming current spec compliance guarantees a consistent service life.

A Retrofit Board Needs Step-Curve Matching, Not Just Voltage Matching
Retrofit

An LED PCB that accepts the right input voltage for a legacy series circuit can still track the CCR's current steps differently than the original board, producing a brightness curve that looks uneven step-to-step even when each step measures within tolerance. Verify step response, not just nominal voltage compatibility, when qualifying a replacement board.

ESD Damage Can Be Latent
Handling

A board that survives an ESD event without immediately failing can still have a weakened component that fails prematurely months later, which makes ESD-related failures easy to misattribute to unrelated causes. Enforce grounding and ESD precautions consistently rather than only when a failure has already been traced to static discharge.

Mechanical Flex During Installation Is a Common Root Cause
Failure Mode

Flexing a board while seating it against a heat sink or securing mounting screws can crack a solder joint or fracture a trace without any visible external sign, and the fault may only surface intermittently under thermal cycling. Support the board fully during installation rather than levering it into place at one edge.

Colour Shift Is an Earlier Signal Than Output Drop
Troubleshooting

LED phosphor and die degradation frequently shows up as a measurable chromaticity shift before total lumen output falls outside tolerance. Where photometric equipment allows it, trend chromaticity alongside intensity rather than relying on intensity alone to catch an aging board.

Reused Thermal Interface Material Undermines a Good Board Swap
Installation

Installing a new LED PCB onto old, compressed, or damaged thermal interface material carries over the exact problem the board swap was meant to fix. Replace the thermal pad or paste as a matter of course whenever the board is disturbed, not only when it visibly looks degraded.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the LED PCB: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an LED PCB in an AGL fixture?

An LED PCB is the specialised circuit board at the core of an LED-based AGL fixture. It mechanically supports and electrically interconnects the LED chips, drivers and passive components while providing a path for heat dissipation.

Q2Why are AGL LED PCBs usually metal-core?

Most are aluminium-core (or copper-core) boards, a sandwich of a metal base, a dielectric layer and a copper circuit layer. The metal core transfers heat from the LED junctions to the fixture heat sink, and thermal vias can do the same on FR4 designs.

Q3What does an LED PCB do?

It provides electrical interconnection through traces and pads (LEDs in series or parallel and the driver interface), thermal management through copper planes, vias and the metal substrate, and mechanical mounting for the LED array.

Q4Where are LED PCBs used?

In every LED-based AGL fixture: runway edge and centreline lights, taxiway edge and centreline lights with blue or green LEDs, approach lighting arrays, and obstruction lights with red or white LEDs.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

A 1.0 mm to 3.0 mm aluminium core, 1 oz to 4 oz copper (35 µm to 140 µm), dielectric thermal conductivity of 1.0 W/m·K to 3.0 W/m·K, 1 to 6 LED channels and 1 to 48 LEDs per board. Operating range is -40°C to +85°C ambient with LED junction temperature of 125°C or less.

Q6How is the PCB powered?

By low-voltage DC from the fixture's internal LED driver, not directly from the primary series loop, using a constant-current feed matched to the LED channel count and forward voltage. The PCB itself is a passive current-carrying element, and protection relies on driver-level current limiting.

Q7How is the PCB protected?

Dielectric isolation of 2 kV to 4 kV between circuit traces and the metal core is verified during manufacture, and a conformal coating (acrylic, silicone or urethane) protects against moisture. On-board ESD and surge protection is fitted per channel where designed.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are IPC-2221 (PCB design), IPC-6012 (rigid PCB qualification), IPC-2152 (current-carrying capacity) and FAA AC 150/5345-46 (light fixtures, covering LED-based fixture assemblies). Always verify the current edition and your requirements.

Q9How often should an LED PCB be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover the thermal pathway, corrosion and coating, solder joints and mechanical damage. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with LED PCBs?

Common problems are thermal management failures, moisture and corrosion, solder joint fatigue, mechanical damage, and incorrect LED bin or orientation. Reduced light output, colour shift, intermittent LED failure under vibration or thermal cycling, or incorrect colour or beam pattern after a PCB replacement are typical symptoms.
