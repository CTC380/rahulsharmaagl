Runway Edge Light

The primary visual aid defining runway lateral limits for night and low-visibility operations. Installed as elevated or inset fixtures along the runway edges, providing white light (or yellow on the last 600m / 2,000ft for CAT II/III) with selectable intensity steps.

Overview

What Is It?

A Runway Edge Light is a visual navigation aid that defines the lateral boundaries of the runway. Installed in pairs along the full length of the runway, these lights provide pilots with a clear outline of the runway's edges during takeoff, landing, and roll-out. The lights are typically white, but on precision approach runways (CAT II/III), the last 600 metres (or 2,000 feet) of runway edge lights are amber/yellow to indicate the final section. Intensity is variable to match prevailing visibility conditions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The primary purpose of the runway edge light is to give pilots continuous, unambiguous reference to the lateral extremities of the runway during night operations and periods of reduced visibility, supporting safe alignment, directional control, and speed judgement throughout takeoff roll, final approach, landing, and rollout. Beyond simple edge delineation, the fixture also communicates positional context along the runway: the colour transition from white to yellow in the final 600 m / 2,000 ft on CAT II/III precision runways warns the pilot that the remaining runway length is limited, while the selectable intensity steps allow the light output to be matched to prevailing visibility so the edge remains visible without causing glare in clear conditions. In this way, the fixture serves both a delineation function (marking the usable runway width) and a situational-awareness function (signalling remaining distance and adapting to conditions).

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on both sides of the runway, spaced at uniform intervals (typically 60 m / 200 ft for precision runways, up to 100 m / 300 ft for non-precision). Used on all civil and military airfields that support night or low-visibility operations. Elevated fixtures are common on soft shoulders, while inset (flush) fixtures are used on hard surfaces or where aircraft overrun protection is required.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out along both runway edges at the design spacing, positioned no more than 3 m outward from the pavement edge and clear of the graded runway strip's load-bearing requirements. Elevated units mount on a frangible coupling atop an L-867 base can set flush with grade, with the fitting height kept within the obstacle-limitation surface for the runway category. Inset units seat into an L-868 base can recessed into the pavement or shoulder, sealed against surface water ingress. Each base can is fed by armoured cable run in a trench (or duct) back to the isolating transformer pit, with the transformer housed in its own secondary base can adjacent to or below the fixture to keep the series-circuit joint out of standing water. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline survey so the edge line remains parallel and true along the full runway length, and elevated fixtures are typically omitted or replaced with inset units within the runway end safety area and other frangibility-sensitive zones.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each runway edge light is powered from the constant current series circuit via its own isolating transformer. The light's intensity is controlled by the CCR stepping current (typically 5 steps: 2.8A, 3.4A, 4.1A, 5.2A, 6.6A). The fixture uses either a halogen lamp or an LED array with a precision optical system to project the required beam pattern: a symmetric or asymmetric beam to ensure the light is visible to pilots on approach or during rollout. Elevated lights have a frangible coupling to break away on impact, minimizing aircraft damage.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Runway edge lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or (at smaller aerodromes) pilot-controlled lighting input. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A). Every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, draws from the same current and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no way to dim or brighten a single fixture independently of the rest of the circuit. Fault detection is likewise loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Fixture (with loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is typically a die-cast aluminium alloy housing, machined and anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance against runway de-icing fluids, fuel spillage, and UV exposure. The optical assembly sits under a borosilicate glass or high-strength polycarbonate prism, gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring or silicone seal to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating against water and dust ingress. Elevated units bolt to a frangible coupling, an aluminium or composite fitting engineered to shear cleanly under aircraft impact loads, which in turn seats on an L-867 base can set flush with the surrounding grade; inset (flush) units instead seat directly into an L-868 base can recessed into the pavement, with the top surface load-rated for aircraft wheel loading. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors, sealed splice connectors rated for direct burial and continuous immersion, which mate to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer housed in its own base can nearby. Stainless-steel fasteners are used throughout to resist corrosion, and the base can assembly includes a drainage provision to prevent water pooling around the transformer and cable entry points.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Prism / Lens Degradation

UV exposure and erosion can cause the optical prism to become hazy, reducing light output and altering beam shape. Regular photometric testing and cleaning (or replacement) is required.

Moisture Ingress

Failed O-rings or gaskets allow water to enter the fixture, causing lamp failure, corrosion of contacts, and reduced insulation resistance. Annual seal inspection and torque check of the top cap is critical.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated fixtures rely on a frangible coupling to break on impact. Over time, coupling may become brittle due to weather or shear fatigue; routine replacement intervals are recommended for high-impact zones.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module / lamp, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity is unbroken through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop (severed cable, corroded L-823 splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Reduced or Uneven Light Output
Likely Cause
Hazy or eroded optical prism, an aging LED array, or the CCR running on a lower brightness step than the operational requirement.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the prism; confirm the CCR is set to the correct intensity step for prevailing conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the prism, replace a degraded LED module, or correct the CCR step setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Flickering or Intermittent
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded primary/secondary crimp connections, or a marginal transformer winding under thermal load.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque all cable connections at the fixture base can; check for corrosion on crimps; monitor secondary voltage for drop under load once the CCR has warmed up.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded connections with new crimps and sealant, or replace the transformer if voltage sag is confirmed under load.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling has begun to fatigue or partially shear, or the base can/mounting stake has settled due to pavement or subgrade movement.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually and physically check the frangible coupling for cracking or play; check fixture aim against tolerance; inspect surrounding pavement/turf for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling on schedule if fatigued, re-level and re-aim the fixture, and address any base can or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Module / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised LED board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single fixture goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the series circuit continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at the fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or rodent activity, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic loading from mower or snowplough contact, material fatigue over the service interval, or corrosion at the coupling threads.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or separates entirely from its base, taking the light out of tolerance or out of service.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim/level checks against tolerance.
Optical Prism Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure, abrasive particulate erosion, and rubber-deposit or grime build-up on the outer lens surface.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output and an altered beam pattern that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of the prism during quarterly cleaning.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on healthy circuits.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Base Can / Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure allowing water to pool and erode the surrounding fill.
Effect
Fixture tilts out of aim tolerance, cable connections come under mechanical stress, and water can pool around the base can.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of the base can and surrounding pavement or turf, and a check of drainage paths during annual maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Runway edge lights run on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling cable connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near an active runway requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance, with vehicle and personnel movement kept clear of the runway strip per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

Hot Lamp / Housing Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the fixture housing and lamp/LED module to cool before handling after an active period; incandescent units in particular retain significant heat and can cause burns during lens or lamp replacement.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the base can is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the fixture, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the fixture and base can are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture out of aim tolerance, at reduced intensity, or otherwise non-compliant after maintenance; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
LED Retrofit Step Matching
Retrofit

When retrofitting LED fixtures onto a legacy series circuit, check that the fixture's brightness-step curve actually tracks the CCR's current steps the way the original incandescent lamp did; a mismatched curve leaves the runway looking uneven between "steps" even though every fixture measures within spec at each step individually.

Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED fixture is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual LED load rather than assuming the existing transformer is "close enough."

Trend Loop Readings, Don't Just Spot-Check
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at an L-823 connector usually shows up first as a slow drift in loop insulation resistance, not a hard fault. Logging megger readings over successive maintenance cycles catches a failing splice months before it takes the loop down.

Frangible Couplings Age, Even Without Impact
Mechanical

UV and thermal cycling can shift the shear characteristics of a composite or aluminium frangible coupling over years of service. A coupling that looks visually fine can no longer shear at its rated load, so periodic torque/shear verification belongs in the overhaul cycle, not just the post-impact inspection.

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven edge-light brightness runway-to-runway even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

De-Icing Fluid Ages Seals Faster Than UV Alone
Durability

Repeated exposure to glycol-based de-icing fluid degrades gaskets and lens seals faster than sun and weather alone, especially on units near taxiway/runway intersections that see heavy de-icing traffic. Specify seal materials rated for glycol contact and inspect gasket condition at overhaul, not only at initial install.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about runway edge lights: colour, spacing, control, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a runway edge light?

A runway edge light is a visual navigation aid that defines the lateral boundaries of the runway. Installed in pairs along the full length of the runway, it gives pilots a clear outline of the runway edges during takeoff, landing and roll-out, especially at night and in reduced visibility.

Q2What colour are runway edge lights?

Runway edge lights are typically white. On CAT II/III precision approach runways, the last 600 m (2,000 ft) of runway edge lights are yellow (amber) to warn the pilot that the remaining runway length is limited.

Q3How far apart are runway edge lights spaced?

Fixtures are installed on both sides of the runway at uniform intervals, typically 60 m (200 ft) for precision runways and up to 100 m (300 ft) for non-precision runways. They are positioned no more than 3 m outward from the pavement edge.

Q4How is the brightness of runway edge lights controlled?

Brightness is set at the loop level by the Constant Current Regulator (CCR), which steps the series circuit current through five settings: 2.8 A, 3.4 A, 4.1 A, 5.2 A and 6.6 A. The step is selected through the Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) based on visibility (RVR), ATC instruction or pilot-controlled lighting. Every fixture on the loop changes intensity together; a single fixture cannot be dimmed on its own.

Q5What is the difference between elevated and inset runway edge lights?

Elevated fixtures mount on a frangible coupling above an L-867 base can and are common on soft shoulders; they break away on aircraft impact to minimise damage. Inset (flush) fixtures seat in an L-868 base can recessed into the pavement and are used on hard surfaces or where aircraft overrun protection is required.

Q6Why does a runway edge light need an isolating transformer?

Each fixture is powered from the constant current series circuit through its own isolating transformer (typically 45 W or 65 W). The transformer taps the fixed loop current for the fixture and keeps the series circuit continuous, so a fixture fault does not have to break the whole loop.

Q7Which standards apply to runway edge lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (colour, spacing and intensity), FAA AC 150/5345-46F (specification for runway, taxiway, heliport and vertiport light fixtures), IEC 61827 (electrical and photometric characteristics of inset and elevated luminaires) and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8What are the photometric and insulation requirements for runway edge lights?

Typical main-beam intensity at 100% is at least 10,000 cd for white and 5,000 cd for yellow, with a field tolerance of ±25% at each CCR step. Insulation resistance of the cable and transformer should be at least 50 MΩ, measured cable-to-ground with a megger.

Q9How often should runway edge lights be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens and prism cleaning, semi-annual for alignment and levelling, and annual for photometric intensity checks, seal and gasket inspection, frangible coupling checks, insulation resistance tests, cable and connector inspection, and base can inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is a single runway edge light dark while the rest of the circuit works?

The usual causes are a failed LED module or lamp, or an open isolating transformer. Confirm loop continuity through the transformer primary, megger the transformer, and check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised. Replace the LED module if the transformer is healthy; otherwise replace the transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors.

Q11What causes runway edge lights to trip the CCR on a ground fault?

The most common cause is moisture ingress at a fixture or connector, which lowers insulation resistance and creates a partial ground fault on the series loop. Megger the full circuit to find the low-resistance segment, inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets or damaged cable jackets, and confirm insulation resistance has recovered before re-energising.
