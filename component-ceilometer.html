Ceilometer

A ground-based remote‑sensing instrument that measures cloud base height and vertical visibility using laser or infrared technology. Essential for aerodrome weather reporting, it provides pilots and ATC with critical cloud ceiling data for approach and departure procedures.

Overview

What Is It?

A Ceilometer is a ground-based active remote‑sensing instrument that measures the height of the cloud base (ceiling) and, in some configurations, vertical visibility. It operates by transmitting a pulsed laser or infrared beam vertically into the atmosphere and measuring the time‑of‑flight of the backscattered signal from cloud droplets or aerosol layers. The instrument calculates the cloud base height from the time delay between the transmitted pulse and the received return signal. Ceilometers are a critical part of aerodrome meteorological observation systems, providing data that determines approach minima, runway selection, and overall flight safety.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

A ceilometer exists to answer a question a pilot on approach cannot answer for themselves until it may be too late: how low is the cloud base, and will the runway environment actually become visible in time to land safely. Because cloud ceiling directly determines whether a given approach category can be flown at all, an inaccurate or absent ceiling measurement risks either an unnecessary diversion or, worse, an approach continued below a safe decision height with the runway still obscured.

By measuring ceiling height continuously and automatically rather than relying on a human visual observation, the ceilometer removes both the delay and the subjectivity that come with judging cloud base by eye — giving air traffic control and pilots a consistent, defensible basis for approach minima and runway selection decisions, particularly in rapidly changing weather.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at airfields as part of the meteorological observation system, typically near the runway touchdown zone or at a central location. The ceilometer provides continuous cloud base height measurements, which are used for weather reporting (METAR, TAF), ATC information, and pilot briefings. It is also used at heliports and military bases. The instrument is mounted on a stable base or mast, with the optical window oriented vertically to ensure a clear view of the sky, free from obstructions such as buildings or trees.

Placement

Installation & Location

Sited on level, stable ground close to the runway touchdown zone, or in a central meteorological compound where a single instrument serves the airfield, on a concrete plinth or shelter roof that keeps the unit rigid and free of vibration. The optical window faces vertically upward with a clear, unobstructed cone of sky above it, clear of overhanging structures, aircraft movement areas, and anything that could cast a shadow or deposit exhaust residue on the lens, and the housing is orientated so the window can be reached safely for routine cleaning. Power and data cabling run underground in duct to the meteorological equipment shelter or AWOS cabinet, and mounting height keeps the optical window clear of standing water, snow accumulation, and vehicle strike zones while remaining accessible from a straightforward maintenance platform or ladder.

Function

How Does It Work?

The ceilometer emits short pulses of near‑infrared (e.g., 905 nm) or visible laser light vertically upwards. As the pulse travels, it encounters particles (cloud droplets, aerosols, precipitation) that scatter a fraction of the light back toward the instrument. A sensitive receiver (a photodiode or avalanche photodiode) detects the backscattered signal. The time delay between the transmission and reception of the signal is measured, and since the speed of light is known, the range (height) to the scattering layer is calculated. The instrument records the backscatter profile over a range of heights (typically from 30 m to 7,500 m or more) and identifies the first significant increase in backscatter, which is reported as the cloud base height. Many modern ceilometers also calculate vertical visibility (up to 1,500 m) and can detect multiple cloud layers.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Like the anemometer, the ceilometer has no relationship to the airfield's constant current lighting circuit — it is a data source within the automated weather observation system, not a load on a lighting loop. The instrument's internal processing unit computes the backscatter profile and reported cloud base height itself, then passes that result over a serial (RS-232/RS-485) or Ethernet link to a data logger or data collection platform, which timestamps it and forwards it to the central AWOS/ASOS processor. There it is merged with other sensor inputs to generate the ceiling entries in METAR/TAF products, the controller's live weather display, and ATIS broadcasts. Because the ceilometer reports over an individually addressed data link rather than sharing a current loop with other equipment, a fault is reported against that specific instrument, with the built-in self-test continuously checking laser output power, receiver sensitivity, and window contamination (a dirty or iced optical window scatters the outgoing pulse and is flagged separately from a genuine low-cloud reading, since an uncorrected contamination fault can otherwise be mistaken for a very low ceiling). Where an airfield runs more than one ceilometer — for example alongside a transmissometer for full CAT II/III weather minima — each unit's reading is reported and validated independently before the AWOS/ASOS processor combines them into the published ceiling and visibility values.

Construction

Physical Construction

A ceilometer is built as a self-contained, weatherproof optical unit housing the laser transmitter, receiver optics, and processing electronics within a single sealed enclosure, typically of powder-coated aluminium or stainless steel construction rated to IP66/IP67. The transmit and receive apertures are fitted with optical windows kept clear of condensation and contamination by internal heating elements and, in many designs, a window-blower or wiper mechanism, since any obscuration of the optics directly degrades the backscatter signal the instrument depends on. The laser source and detector are mounted on a rigid internal optical bench, isolated from vibration and thermal drift to keep the beam alignment stable over years of unattended operation. The unit is mounted on a fixed pedestal or plinth, precisely levelled so the beam is transmitted vertically, with the enclosure's internal electronics bay accessible via a sealed access panel for servicing without disturbing the optical alignment. Cable entries for power and data are sealed against moisture ingress, and the housing includes ventilation or climate control to keep internal components within their rated operating temperature range across extreme outdoor conditions.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Window Contamination

Dust, dirt, bird droppings, and insect debris on the optical window can attenuate the laser pulse and reduce detection range. Regular cleaning with lens-safe solutions and soft cloths is essential, especially in industrial or agricultural environments.

Signal Attenuation in Heavy Precipitation

Heavy rain, snow, or fog can attenuate the laser beam, reducing the effective measurement range. While modern ceilometers have high sensitivity, the measured cloud base may be underestimated during intense precipitation; operators should be aware of this limitation.

Calibration Drift

The optical and electronic components can drift over time, affecting the accuracy of range measurements. Annual calibration using a certified test target or reference instrument is recommended to maintain performance.

Obstructions in the Field of View

Buildings, trees, or other objects near the ceilometer can obstruct the vertical beam or cause false returns. Careful siting according to ICAO and WMO guidelines is essential to avoid these issues.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

No Data Output / Communication Loss
Likely Cause
Failed communication link (RS-232/485, SDI-12, Ethernet, or 4-20mA) between the ceilometer and the AWOS/ALCMS system, or a power fault at the unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify power at the ceilometer; check cable and connector integrity on the output interface; confirm the host system is polling the correct address/port.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged interface cable/connector, restore power, and confirm the output signal is reporting cleanly to the host system.
Reduced Detection Range
Likely Cause
Dust, dirt, bird droppings, or insect debris on the optical window is attenuating the laser pulse.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the optical window for contamination; check the unit's internal window-transmission diagnostic if available; compare reported range against known conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean the window with lens-safe solutions and a soft cloth, and re-verify detection range returns to the expected specification.
False or Erratic Cloud Base Reports
Likely Cause
Nearby buildings, trees, or other objects are obstructing the vertical beam or generating false returns, or heavy precipitation is attenuating the beam and underestimating cloud base.
Diagnostic Steps
Cross-check reports against current weather conditions and any nearby obstruction survey; review siting against ICAO/WMO clearance guidelines; correlate erratic reports with precipitation intensity.
Corrective Action
Relocate or re-site the unit if an obstruction is confirmed, and document the precipitation-attenuation limitation for operator awareness where relocation isn't practical.
Reading Drift Versus Reference Standard
Likely Cause
Gradual drift in the optical or electronic components affecting range measurement accuracy.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a calibration check using a certified test target or reference instrument; review calibration history for a trend indicating progressive drift.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the ceilometer to bring range measurements back within specification, or return the unit to the manufacturer if drift cannot be corrected in the field.
Window Heater or Blower Failure
Likely Cause
A failed window heater or blower is allowing condensation, frost, or precipitation to build up on the optical window.
Diagnostic Steps
Check heater/blower power draw and operation; inspect the window for condensation or frost during cold or humid conditions.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed heater or blower assembly, and confirm the window stays clear through a full condensation/frost cycle.
Complete Sensor Failure
Likely Cause
Failure of the laser transmitter or receiver module, or an internal fault flagged by the unit's built-in diagnostics.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the unit's self-test/fault code output; power-cycle the unit and confirm whether the fault clears; inspect internal fuses and power supply if accessible.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed transmitter/receiver module or return the unit for factory repair, and confirm normal operation with a full self-test after reinstallation.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise the ceilometer's mains or DC supply and the separate window heater/blower circuit before opening the housing or optical head, and verify zero energy before disconnecting cable connectors.

Laser Optical Safety
Personnel Safety

Do not stare into the transmit aperture during operation or bench testing; although the source is normally low-power and eye-safe under intended use, avoid direct or reflected beam viewing and follow the manufacturer's laser safety class guidance.

Hot Surfaces from Window Heater
Personnel Safety

Allow the integral window heater and blower assembly to cool before touching the optics housing after active operation, since the heater runs continuously in cold or humid conditions to prevent condensation and frost.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Ceilometer sites are typically within or near the movement area for AWOS/ASOS integration; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM where cloud-base reporting will be degraded or unavailable during the outage.

Optics Cleanliness and Protection
Operational

Cap or shield the transmit/receive optics from direct sunlight and dust ingress whenever the housing is open, since a contaminated or scratched window directly degrades cloud-height accuracy and cannot be corrected by recalibration alone.

PPE and Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools when working on mains-powered connections, and confirm the unit's housing and mast (if mast-mounted) are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting supply.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify cloud-base height readings against a known reference or visual observation before closing out the work, and cancel the associated NOTAM once reporting is confirmed reliable.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Window Contamination Biases Ceiling Low Before It Fails
Maintenance

Dust or film on the optical window scatters and attenuates the laser return, which tends to make the reported cloud base look lower than it actually is well before the instrument flags a hard fault; keep window cleaning on a defined interval rather than a reactive one, especially in dusty or polluted environments.

Track Laser Output Trend, Not Just Pass/Fail
Diagnostics

Laser diode output typically degrades gradually rather than failing suddenly; logging the internal power diagnostic over successive visits catches an ageing diode months before it affects data quality enough to trigger an alarm.

Transmit/Receive Overlap Alignment Affects Near-Range Readings First
Alignment

A slight misalignment between the transmit and receive optics reduces the overlap function at low altitude while higher cloud bases can still appear to read correctly; a ceilometer that seems fine for higher layers but inconsistent at low ceilings warrants an alignment check, not just a software recalibration.

Distinguish Heavy Precipitation From a True Low Ceiling
Calibration

Heavy rain, snow, or fog can saturate the return signal in ways that resemble a very low or obscured ceiling; confirm the firmware's precipitation-discrimination settings are tuned for local weather patterns rather than assuming a generic default handles the site's conditions.

Verify Zero-Height Reference After Any Mount Disturbance
Verification

Foundation settling or a remount can shift the sensor's baseline zero-height reference without any change in the instrument's internal calibration; re-survey the mounting height and re-zero the reference after any structural work, not just after an electronics service.

Use a Certified Reference Target on a Defined Cycle
Compliance

Relying solely on the ceilometer's internal self-check between manufacturer service visits can let a slow calibration drift go undetected for a long time; schedule a verification against a certified reference target on a defined interval rather than only at major service events.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the ceilometer: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a ceilometer?

A ceilometer is a ground-based active remote-sensing instrument that measures the height of the cloud base (ceiling) and, in some configurations, vertical visibility. It is a critical part of aerodrome meteorological observation systems.

Q2How does a ceilometer measure cloud height?

It transmits short pulses of near-infrared laser light (for example 905 nm) vertically upward. A sensitive receiver detects light backscattered from cloud droplets or aerosol layers, and the cloud base height is calculated from the time delay between the transmitted pulse and the return.

Q3Why is cloud base height important at an airport?

Ceiling data determines approach minima and runway selection and underpins overall flight safety. It feeds weather reporting (METAR and TAF), ATC information and pilot briefings.

Q4How accurate is a ceilometer?

Typical measurement range is 30 m to 7,500 m, up to 12,000 m on some models, with accuracy of ±10 m or ±2%, whichever is greater. Output resolution is typically 5 m or 10 m, and vertical visibility is reported from 0 m to 1,500 m.

Q5Where is a ceilometer installed?

Typically near the runway touchdown zone or at a central location, on a stable base or mast with the optical window oriented vertically. It is also used at heliports and military bases.

Q6Is a ceilometer laser eye-safe?

Ceilometers typically use an eye-safe Class 1M laser at 905 nm, with an internal supply interlock holding output within those limits. IEC 60825 covers laser safety.

Q7What power and outputs does a ceilometer need?

Power is typically 100-240 V AC or 24 V DC, often UPS-backed with AWOS/ASOS, at roughly 40 W to 100 W including the window heater and blower that keep the optics clear of condensation, frost and precipitation. Outputs include RS-232, RS-485, SDI-12, Ethernet or 4-20 mA, with surge protection and earthing given the exposed siting.

Q8Which standards apply to ceilometers?

The main references are ICAO Annex 3, WMO Guide to Instruments No. 8, FAA AC 150/5220-16 (AWOS) and IEC 60825 (laser safety). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should a ceilometer be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for optical window cleaning, semi-annual for a field of view survey, and annual for calibration, laser output verification, housing and seal inspection and signal output tests, plus a window heater test before winter. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with ceilometers?

Common problems are optical window contamination, signal attenuation in heavy precipitation, calibration drift and obstructions in the field of view. Symptoms include reduced detection range, false or erratic cloud base reports, or no data output.
