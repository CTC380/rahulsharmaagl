Lead-off Light

A specialized taxiway centreline light that provides colour-coded guidance to pilots transitioning from the runway onto the taxiway after landing. Lead-off lights form a single row of in-pavement fixtures along the curved exit path, alternately green and yellow while still within the runway strip, turning solid green once the aircraft is established on the taxiway.

Overview

What Is It?

A Lead-off Light is the length of taxiway centreline lighting, coloured alternately green and yellow, that runs along the curved exit path from the runway centreline to the point where an aircraft is fully established on the taxiway. It is the same L-852D-type in-pavement fixture used for ordinary green taxiway centreline lighting, laid as a single row rather than paired left/right — the yellow segments simply mark the portion of the path that is still, functionally, on the runway. Lead-off lights are essential for ensuring efficient runway occupancy and safe aircraft movement during the post-landing phase.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The lead-off light exists to manage one of the highest-workload transitions in a landing sequence: the moment an aircraft leaves the runway and must correctly track the taxiway centreline while still moving at a meaningful groundspeed, often at night or in reduced visibility. The colour itself carries the guidance: yellow tells the pilot they are still tracking a centreline that is technically part of the runway environment, and the switch to green is the unambiguous cue that the aircraft has cleared onto the taxiway proper. That single, unmistakable colour change is easier to read under time pressure than judging position against markings alone. Its purpose is ultimately about runway throughput as well as safety: guiding aircraft off the runway quickly and correctly minimizes the time the runway stays occupied by a landing aircraft before the next arrival or departure can use it.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Lead-off lights are installed at runway exits where taxiways connect to the runway. They are used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where efficient runway exit is critical
  • Busy airports with high traffic volumes requiring rapid runway clearance
  • Rapid exit taxiways where high-speed exits are required
  • Complex taxiway layouts where accurate guidance is essential
  • Low visibility operations where visual guidance is critical
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed as a single row on the taxiway centreline, beginning at the point where the exit curve leaves the runway centreline and continuing along the curved portion until the aircraft is established on the taxiway centreline, typically spaced closer together than straight-taxiway centreline lighting to better define the curve. Each light mounts on an L-852 base can recessed flush with the pavement, fed by cable run in pavement ducting back to the taxiway lighting circuit, typically on its own isolating transformer tap so intensity can be stepped independently of the general taxiway lighting. Fixtures within the runway strip/runway pavement portion of the curve show yellow; fixtures beyond the point where the aircraft is clear of the runway show green, with that colour-change point set by the runway/taxiway geometry rather than a fixed distance. Placement is coordinated with the runway exit design (angle, radius, and speed category) so the row of lights follows the intended exit path rather than a simplified constant-radius curve.

Function

How Does It Work?

Lead-off lights operate as part of the taxiway and runway guidance system:

  • Installation: Lead-off lights form a single row on the taxiway centreline at the runway exit point, typically at 15-30 metre intervals along the curved path from the runway to the taxiway.
  • Lighting: Each fixture is yellow within the runway strip and green beyond it, providing colour-coded guidance to the pilot. The lights may be steady burning or flashing, depending on the application.
  • Guidance: The alternating green/yellow colour, and the point where it switches to solid green, guides the pilot to safely exit the runway and track onto the taxiway.
  • Integration: Lead-off lights are integrated with taxiway centreline lights, edge lights, and other taxiway guidance systems.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Lead-off lights are not individually addressable — like the rest of the taxiway lighting circuit, they are controlled at the loop level. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or a routine linked to runway occupancy, selects the intensity step for the taxiway guidance circuit that the lead-off row belongs to; on smaller aerodromes this may instead be driven by pilot-controlled lighting (PCL). The command is passed to a CCR, which regulates the loop to a fixed current, and every fixture on that loop — every yellow and green light along the row, via their own isolating transformers — burns at the same intensity simultaneously. The green/yellow colour split is fixed by which lamp or filter each fixture carries, not by any independent switching logic, so intensity stepping is uniform along the whole row even though the colour is not. Where lead-off lights flash rather than burn steady, a separate sequential flasher control unit superimposes the flash timing on top of the loop's steady current; this flasher logic sits alongside, not instead of, the CCR-driven intensity control. Fault detection remains loop-based: the CCR monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise (typically an open-circuit lamp or failed transformer) is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to a specific fixture without further sectionalised testing.

Engineering View

Circuit & Colour-Transition Overview

Each lead-off light draws power from the CCR through its own isolating transformer on the series circuit, so one fixture can fail without breaking the loop. The fixtures form a single row along the taxiway centreline; it's the colour of each fixture — yellow inside the runway strip, green beyond it — not the geometry of a paired layout, that guides the pilot off the runway.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is typically a die-cast aluminium alloy housing, machined and anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance against de-icing fluids, fuel spillage, and UV exposure. The green (and where fitted, yellow) lens is a borosilicate glass or high-strength polycarbonate prism, gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring or silicone seal to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating against water and dust ingress, and shaped to project a uni- or bi-directional beam back towards the exiting aircraft. Elevated units bolt to a frangible coupling engineered to shear cleanly under aircraft impact loads, seated on an L-867 base can set flush with the surrounding grade; flush-mounted (inset) units instead seat directly into an L-868 base can recessed into the pavement, with the top surface load-rated for aircraft wheel loading of up to 100,000 kg. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors, sealed splice connectors rated for direct burial and continuous immersion, which mate to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer housed in its own base can nearby. Stainless-steel fasteners are used throughout to resist corrosion, and the base can assembly includes a drainage provision to prevent water pooling around the transformer and cable entry points.

Civil

Drainage Provision
Civil

Provide a weep hole or drainage path at the base can to prevent water pooling around the transformer and cable entry, especially on flush-mounted installations.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay the series circuit duct/cable route to design depth and separation from other services, avoiding sharp bends that could stress the L-823 connector at final termination.

L-823 Connector Termination

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer wattage and ratio match the fixture and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Frangible Coupling / Flush-Fit Fixing
Mechanical

For elevated units, torque the frangible coupling to the specified value so it shears cleanly under lateral load; for inset units, torque the fixing bolts and seal the rim so the fixture sits flush and load-bearing.

Beam Orientation & Aim Setting
Mechanical

Orient the lens so the beam projects back towards the exiting aircraft along the centreline, checking elevation and aim against the design diagram before final fixing.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

Lead-off lights are exposed to rubber deposits from aircraft tyres and de-icing fluids. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and visibility.

Misalignment

Lead-off lights can become misaligned due to pavement settlement or impact damage, shifting the beam off the exit curve and reducing guidance effectiveness. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are recommended.

LED Driver Failure

LED drivers in lead-off lights can fail due to thermal stress or power surges. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers is essential to maintain system reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED driver or LED array, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity is unbroken through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED driver/array if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop (severed cable, corroded L-823 splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Reduced or Uneven Light Output
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits, tyre debris, or de-icing fluid residue built up on the lens is blocking or scattering light output.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for contamination; run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds to quantify the loss.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush; re-verify intensity with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Aim Misaligned
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement or impact damage has shifted the fixture's aim off the designed exit path.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture aim against the design angle using a digital level or alignment jig; inspect the flush-mounted frame and surrounding pavement for settlement or cracking.
Corrective Action
Re-align the fixture to the specified beam angle and address any frame or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Fixture Flickering or Intermittent
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded primary/secondary crimp connections, or an LED driver cycling under thermal stress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque all cable connections at the fixture base can; check for corrosion on crimps; monitor driver output and temperature once the CCR has warmed up.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded connections with new crimps and sealant, or replace the LED driver if thermal cycling is confirmed under load.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Driver Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal stress from repeated cycling, a power surge on the secondary circuit, or a manufacturing defect in the driver's output stage.
Effect
Fixture goes fully dark, flickers, or shows reduced output while the rest of the series circuit continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Monitor driver output and temperature once the CCR has warmed up, and substitute a known-good driver to isolate the fault.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at the fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or rodent activity, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Fixture Aim Misalignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Pavement settlement around the flush-mounted frame, impact damage from ground vehicles or aircraft, or subgrade movement beneath the fixture.
Effect
Beam shifts off the designed exit path, reducing guidance effectiveness for exiting aircraft.
Detection Method
Aim verification against the design angle using a digital level or alignment jig, and visual inspection of the frame and surrounding pavement for settlement or cracking.
Lens Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits from aircraft tyres, de-icing fluid residue, and general grime accumulation on the lens surface.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of the lens during routine cleaning.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Dedicated Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lead-off lights are typically fed from their own isolating transformer tap so intensity can be stepped independently; confirm which tap and CCR feeds the fixture before isolating, and verify zero energy before opening a base can or handling connectors.

Runway Exit Zone Coordination
Operational

These fixtures guide aircraft off an active runway immediately after landing; work here requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM where lights will be dark, with personnel and vehicles kept clear of the exit path.

Flush Fit & Wheel-Load Tolerance
Mechanical

The inset fixture must sit precisely flush with the pavement through the exit curve; a proud edge or depression becomes a wheel-impact point during a high-speed rollout.

Yellow Lens Verification
Compliance

Confirm the yellow lens is correctly fitted and distinct from the green taxiway centreline lenses either side of the transition point, since a mismatched colour here blurs the clean colour change the lead-off system relies on.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the exit path.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture dark, out of position, or with the wrong colour after maintenance; verify flushness, colour, and photometric conformance before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Colour Transition Is the Real Design Target
Design

The lead-off light's job is the yellow-to-green colour change perceived by the pilot during rollout, not just meeting yellow chromaticity in isolation; verify the transition against the adjacent taxiway centreline fixtures in situ, since two individually compliant lenses can still read as an ambiguous handoff under low visibility.

Isolate the Correct Transformer Tap
Electrical

Lead-off lights are frequently stepped independently of the main runway edge series, so confirm the specific isolating transformer tap and CCR channel before fault-finding; chasing an intensity complaint on the wrong tap wastes a maintenance window and can mask a genuine loop fault elsewhere.

Flush-Fit Drift Is Progressive, Not Sudden
Mechanical

Repeated high-speed wheel loading through the exit curve gradually settles the base can and packing beneath an inset fixture; a unit that was flush at install can work loose over seasons of freeze-thaw and rollout traffic, so recheck flushness on a set interval rather than only after a reported strike.

Curve Geometry Drives Placement, Not a Fixed Offset
Layout

Exit taxiway geometry varies enough between rapid-exit and standard turnoffs that a standard offset-from-centreline table can misplace the lead-off sequence on tighter curves; check the fixture positions against the actual as-built exit geometry rather than a generic layout drawing.

Log Insulation Trends on Exit-Zone Loops
Troubleshooting

Connectors in the exit-zone base cans see more mechanical flexing from pavement deflection under fast rollouts than centreline connectors do; trending megger readings over successive cycles catches a developing loop fault before it darkens the exit path on short notice.

Glycol Exposure Concentrates at Exit Points
Durability

Aircraft slow and sometimes hold briefly near the exit, so de-icing fluid runoff tends to pool around lead-off fixtures more than along open runway edge; specify glycol-rated gaskets and lenses here and inspect seal condition at overhaul rather than waiting for a reported ingress fault.
