Installation & Civil Works Comparison

CONCRETE BASEVSPRECAST BASE

Cast-in-place concrete base vs precast base — both are concrete foundations that support or surround the light base or mounting assembly of an AGL fixture, sign or associated equipment. A cast-in-place base is formed and poured at the installation site, while a precast base is manufactured in advance at a factory or controlled production facility and transported to the airfield for installation.

02 · Scope

Where the Concrete Sits in the System

An AGL fixture is normally mounted on a certified light base or on a frangible support, and that in turn sits in or on a concrete foundation. In the FAA system, for example, AC 150/5345-42 specifies the light bases (typically metal cans) that also serve as transformer housings and junction boxes: Type L-867 for elevated lights and Type L-868 for in-pavement, load-bearing installations. The cast-in-place or precast “base” compared on this page is the concrete foundation or encasement that supports or surrounds such a base or mounting — it is not the light base itself.

The comparison is most relevant to foundations for elevated lights, signs, approach-light supports, transformer housings and junction pits. For in-pavement fixtures the base can is installed as part of the pavement construction, so the pavement design and approved drawings govern. The exact configuration depends on the fixture, light base, pavement design and applicable installation standard.

03 · Basic Difference

Make It There vs Make It First

Cast-in-Place

A cast-in-place base is created directly at its final location: the site is excavated, formwork is erected, concrete is poured into the formwork, and the base cures in place before the fixture or mounting assembly is installed.

Easy memory

  • Cast-in-place = make it there

Precast

A precast base is manufactured before it ever reaches the airfield: it is cast in a mould and cured under factory conditions, then transported and positioned at the installation site.

Easy memory

  • Precast = make it first, install it later
04 · Installation Time

Curing Time vs Positioning Time

Cast-in-Place

  • Excavate
  • Set out and form (with reinforcement, ducts and inserts as designed)
  • Pour and compact
  • Cure to the specified strength
  • Strip forms and backfill
  • Install light base / fixture

Precast

  • Excavate and prepare bedding
  • Lift and position the unit
  • Level, align and set orientation
  • Connect ducts and earthing
  • Grout, backfill and compact
  • Install light base / fixture

The curing period can influence the overall construction schedule for a cast-in-place base. Precast construction can reduce on-site construction time, but transport, lifting, bedding, alignment and backfill still require planning — it is not installation-free.

05 · Quality Control

Site-Dependent vs Factory-Controlled

Cast-in-Place

Quality is influenced by site conditions, mixing, pouring, compaction and curing together. It can be strongly influenced by site workmanship.

Precast

Controlled production, a standard mould and controlled curing produce a more consistent product, with more repeatable dimensions, surface finish, concrete quality and reinforcement positioning.

Acceptance follows the project specification in both cases: site concrete sampling and testing for cast-in-place, and factory quality records plus inspection on delivery (dimensions, damage, lifting inserts) for precast.

06 · Dimensional Accuracy

Formwork Tolerance vs Factory Repeatability

For a cast-in-place base, final dimensions depend on accurate formwork and installation, and are subject to site tolerance. A precast base is produced in a factory mould that yields repeatable dimensions and a standardized base — particularly useful where fixture alignment and mounting interfaces require repeatability.

A precise unit does not guarantee a precise installation: the final position, level and orientation of a precast base still depend on site setting-out and bedding.

07 · Customization

Flexible Geometry vs Repeatable Installation

Cast-in-place construction is often advantageous when geometry is unusual, existing infrastructure creates constraints, or a special foundation is required — custom formwork can be built for the custom base. Precast bases are particularly suitable for repeated installations using standardized dimensions; special shapes need custom moulds, which is only economical at volume.

08 · Frangibility & Clearance

A Requirement That Applies to Both

ICAO Annex 14 requires equipment and supports on or near runways and taxiways to be frangible and mounted as low as possible; ICAO Doc 9157 Part 6 gives guidance on frangible design. A concrete foundation does not change this. For cast-in-place and precast alike:

  • The frangible coupling or break point must sit above the foundation, so the foundation should not project above the design break point (follow the fixture manufacturer and standard details)
  • A massive rigid foundation standing proud of grade in a runway or taxiway strip can itself become a hazard, so size it to the structural design rather than by default
  • Top level and tolerance need to be controlled: formwork and finishing on site for cast-in-place, unit height and bedding for precast
09 · Ducts, Earthing & Drainage

Interfaces Built Into or Onto the Base

Both options must accommodate the same electrical and drainage interfaces. Where they differ is when and how those details are fixed.

Cast-in-Place

Ducts, inserts and anchors are cast in at their set-out positions, so an error is locked in once the concrete is poured. Adjustments during construction are easier before the pour.

Precast

Duct openings, inserts and lifting points are fixed at the factory and must match the site duct layout. Modifying a cured unit on site may be limited by its reinforcement and the manufacturer's design.

  • Cable and duct entries suitably located, sealed and protected
  • Earthing and bonding continuity to the light base and fixture as specified
  • Drainage of the base cavity so water does not accumulate around cables and connections
  • Correct position and orientation of the light base, since a directional fixture is only as well aimed as the base it sits on
10 · Site Disruption

Duration of On-Site Activity

Cast-in-place construction involves excavation, formwork, concrete pouring and curing together on site, which can result in a longer on-site construction activity period. With a precast base, site activity is largely excavation, bedding, positioning and backfill/integration, since much of the manufacturing work occurs off-site — this can be valuable where airfield operational closures must be minimized.

11 · Transportation & Handling

The Major Practical Difference

Cast-in-Place

Raw materials are transported to site and the base is constructed there. The finished base does not need to be transported as a complete heavy component.

Precast

The finished base must be transported, lifted, handled and installed. Precast bases require consideration of transport weight, access routes, lifting equipment, handling points and installation tolerances.

12 · Maintenance & Replacement

Break-Out-and-Rebuild vs Unit Swap

Replacing a damaged cast-in-place base may require significant site work — removal/break-out followed by reconstruction. A damaged precast base can potentially be removed as a unit and a new unit installed. Easier replacement is an advantage of modular precast construction, although actual replacement still depends on how the base is integrated into the pavement and surrounding structure, and on the cables and ducts connected to it.

13 · Cost Considerations

What Drives the Cost

Cast-in-place cost is influenced by site labour, formwork, concrete work and curing time. Precast cost is influenced by factory production, transport, lifting and installation. For a small number of customized installations, cast-in-place construction may be practical; for many repeated standardized installations, precast production may offer economies of repetition.

The lowest-cost option depends on project quantity, logistics, labour availability and installation constraints — not on the base type alone.

15 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: Precast is always stronger
Fact

Strength depends on the complete engineering design — concrete design, reinforcement, quality and installation — not simply whether the concrete was cast on-site or precast.

Myth: Cast-in-place always has lower quality
Fact

Properly controlled site construction can produce high-quality concrete. The real difference is that precast manufacturing generally provides a more controlled and repeatable production environment.

Myth: Precast eliminates installation work
Fact

Incorrect. The manufacturing work is completed off-site, but transport, excavation, bedding, positioning, alignment and integration on site remain critical.

Myth: The concrete base is the AGL light base
Fact

No. The certified light base (for example FAA Type L-867 or L-868) is a separate component that also serves as a transformer housing or junction box. The concrete foundation supports or surrounds it.

Myth: A bigger, heavier concrete base is always safer
Fact

Not near runways and taxiways. A massive rigid foundation that projects above the frangible break point defeats the purpose of frangible mounting. Size the foundation to the structural design and standard details.

16 · Engineering Selection

Choosing Between Cast-in-Place and Precast

When selecting between a cast-in-place and a precast base, consider:

  • Whether the foundation is custom or unique, or repeated and standardized
  • Pavement construction and required structural loading
  • AGL fixture and light base compatibility, and installation tolerances
  • Frangibility: break-point height and foundation top level
  • Duct, earthing and drainage interfaces
  • Drainage and underground services
  • Available airfield closure time
  • Available lifting equipment, unit weight and site access routes
  • Project quantity, logistics and labour availability
17 · Engineering Verdict

Which Base Applies Where?

Engineering Verdict

A cast-in-place concrete base offers maximum site-specific flexibility, but requires more on-site work and curing time. A precast base is factory-manufactured and pre-cured, offering faster installation and greater dimensional repeatability, but requires transport and handling. Either must respect the frangibility and duct/earthing interfaces of the fixture it supports.

For an AGL project, the best choice depends on whether the installation prioritizes customization and site-specific construction, or rapid, repeatable installation with factory-controlled production — decided against pavement design, structural loading and the applicable installation standard, not on a generic comparison alone.

Cast-in-PlaceMaximum flexibility for custom or large foundations, at the cost of on-site time.
PrecastFaster, repeatable installation for standardized foundations, with transport and lifting to manage.

19 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual airport project, verify the current applicable editions of the relevant standards and the approved civil/structural design before using this comparison for project design. This page is an educational reference and does not replace the structural drawings or the project specification.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I and Doc 9157

Annex 14 requires equipment and supports on or near runways and taxiways to be frangible and mounted as low as possible. The Aerodrome Design Manual (Doc 9157) Part 4 (Visual Aids) and Part 6 (Frangibility) give guidance. They do not specify the structural design of the foundation.

FAA AC 150/5345-42 and AC 150/5340-30

AC 150/5345-42 specifies airport light bases, transformer housings and junction boxes (Types L-867 and L-868). AC 150/5340-30 (Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids) covers installation details and options.

DGCA / AAI Requirements and IS Codes

For Indian airport projects, verify DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I (Aerodrome Design and Operations), AAI requirements, airport-specific structural and civil specifications, project drawings and contract specifications. Concrete design and materials typically follow IS 456; IS 15916 (prefabricated concrete, written for buildings) is a useful reference for precast handling, transport and erection.

Final Engineering Note

Base type, dimensions, reinforcement and structural loading requirements can vary with the governing standard, pavement design and approved drawings. For final design, use the project structural drawings and applicable current standard rather than a generic comparison page.

Cast-in-place and precast bases are both structural foundations for AGL fixtures. Their key distinction is where and how the concrete is manufactured — on site versus in a factory — which drives the trade-off between customization and repeatable, faster installation.
