Primary Connector

The plug (male) half of the L‑823 connector system, terminating the primary series cable and providing a secure, waterproof interface to isolating transformers, junction boxes, and other primary circuit equipment. Designed for field assembly, it ensures a reliable, low‑resistance connection that withstands the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

The Primary Connector (also known as the L‑823 plug or male connector) is the cable‑mounted half of the two‑pin waterproof connector system used for primary series circuit connections in airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems. It is field‑terminated to the primary cable and mates with the receptacle (female) connector mounted on isolating transformers, junction boxes, or other inline equipment.

The connector features a robust, knurled coupling ring that threads onto the mating receptacle, compressing internal O‑rings to achieve an IP68 submersible seal. The two contacts—typically gold‑ or tin‑plated brass pins—carry the series current with minimal resistance. Designed for repeated mating and disconnection, the primary connector is a critical link in the AGL power distribution chain.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The primary connector's purpose is to be the part of the circuit that a technician actually handles, repeatedly, whenever a fixture or transformer needs to be disconnected for maintenance or replacement. Field-terminating it directly to the primary cable means the reliability of that connection depends on correct assembly at the time of termination, rather than on a factory process, which is why its knurled coupling ring and O-ring seals are designed to make the correct mated state both easy to achieve and easy to verify by feel or sight. Because this connector sees more physical handling over the life of an AGL circuit than almost any other component, its ability to reseal cleanly after each disconnection is central to keeping the series loop watertight between maintenance visits.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The primary connector is used throughout the airfield wherever primary circuit terminations are required, including:

  • Isolating Transformer Inputs: The primary side of every isolating transformer is fitted with a receptacle that accepts the primary connector from the series cable.
  • CCR Output Cables: The output cables from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) are terminated with primary connectors for connection to the field cable or distribution panel.
  • Junction Boxes and Splices: Inline splices and junction boxes use primary connectors to terminate incoming and outgoing primary cables.
  • Test Points and Disconnects: Primary connectors provide convenient points for isolating sections of the circuit during maintenance or testing.
  • Transition Points: At the boundary between underground and above‑ground cable runs, primary connectors facilitate cable transitions.

The connector is available in straight and right‑angle configurations, with cable entry sizes to accommodate various primary cable gauges (typically 6mm² to 16mm²). It is rated for continuous operation at 6.6A or 20A primary current.

Placement

Installation & Location

The connector is field-terminated to the primary cable at the location where a connection to equipment is required — an isolating transformer, junction box, or CCR output — typically within a below-grade pit or handhole that gives future access without excavation. Termination follows the same sequence each time: cable prepared, contacts crimped or soldered to the conductor, and the assembly threaded into its mating receptacle until the coupling ring is fully home and the internal O-rings are compressed. Because this connector is the one most frequently disconnected and remade over the life of the circuit, it's positioned with enough slack cable to allow it to be unmated and pulled clear of the pit for servicing without placing strain on the termination. Connectors used purely as test or isolation points are sited to remain reachable at finished grade rather than under permanent backfill.

Function

How Does It Work?

The primary connector provides a secure, low‑resistance electrical joint while maintaining a waterproof seal. Its design incorporates several key functional elements:

  • Two‑Pin Contact System: Two machined brass pins (male) with gold or tin plating provide low‑contact‑resistance mating with the receptacle's socket contacts. The pins are designed for gas‑tight connections that resist corrosion.
  • Threaded Coupling Ring: A large, knurled ring on the connector body threads onto the receptacle, compressing the internal seals and locking the connection against vibration and accidental disconnection. The ring provides mechanical strain relief.
  • Elastomeric Seals: Multiple O‑rings (typically silicone or EPDM) provide IP68 sealing when the connector is fully mated and torqued. The seals are compressed by the coupling ring to form a watertight barrier.
  • Cable Termination: The connector is field‑terminated using a compression‑type gland or a solder/crimp‑type termination inside the connector shell. The cable entry is sealed by a compression gland that grips the cable jacket and prevents moisture ingress.
  • Durable Housing: The connector shell is typically made of corrosion‑resistant materials such as brass, bronze, or high‑grade polyamide with UV stabilizers, ensuring longevity in outdoor environments.

When properly installed with the recommended torque (3.5–5.5 N·m), the primary connector provides a connection that can remain submerged in water for extended periods without degradation. Its low contact resistance ensures minimal voltage drop and heating, critical for maintaining the performance of the constant‑current series circuit.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The primary connector sits inline in the series circuit loop, most often at the primary-cable-to-transformer or primary-cable-to-fixture interface, and carries the loop's full commanded current across its two-pin joint. It is not individually addressable — the ALCMS and CCR regulate the entire loop as one unit and cannot see any single connector in isolation. When mated and torqued correctly, the connection is electrically transparent to the control system. As the connection ages, pin wear, an under-torqued coupling ring, or a compressed seal raises contact resistance at that joint, and because the CCR holds current constant rather than voltage, that added resistance surfaces as increased loop voltage at the commanded current step. A connector that fails to seal or mate fully is reported by the CCR as an open-circuit or ground-fault loop condition, indistinguishable at the control level from any other break in the loop, and confirming the connector as the cause still requires physical inspection at that point in the field.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Primary Connector (mated pair) → Loop continues (with loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The primary connector's body is a moulded thermoplastic or cast‑metal shell housing two gold‑ or tin‑plated brass pin contacts at its mating face, field‑terminated to the cable conductors by crimping or soldering inside the shell. A knurled outer coupling ring threads onto the mating receptacle's shell, and as it is tightened it draws the two halves together and compresses the internal elastomeric O‑rings at the pin face and the shell interface, forming the seal that gives the mated pair its IP68 submersible rating. The cable entry at the rear of the shell is itself sealed, typically with a gland or moulded strain relief, so the termination point is protected from moisture ingress along the cable as well as at the connector face. Because the coupling ring is designed for hand‑tightening and repeated disconnection, the connector can be unmated for testing or fault‑finding and remated without special tools.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Contact Pins

Despite plating, pins can corrode in saltwater or chemically aggressive environments, increasing contact resistance. Regular inspection and cleaning with contact cleaner, along with application of dielectric grease, are recommended.

O‑Ring Hardening and Cracking

O‑rings can degrade due to UV exposure, temperature cycles, or chemical attack. Replace O‑rings periodically and use silicone grease to prolong their life.

Incomplete Mating & Loose Coupling

If the coupling ring is not fully tightened, the connection may be loose, leading to intermittent contact, arcing, and overheating. Always tighten to the recommended torque and verify the connection is secure.

Damaged Cable Termination

Poorly stripped or crimped cable terminations can cause high resistance and overheating. Use the proper stripping and crimping tools, and ensure the cable is fully inserted into the termination point.

Cross‑Threading of Coupling Ring

Cross‑threading can damage the threads, making proper mating impossible. Start the coupling ring by hand and ensure it threads smoothly before using a wrench.

Incorrect Cable Gland Selection

Using a gland that does not match the cable diameter will fail to seal properly, allowing water ingress. Always select the correct gland size for the cable being used.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

High Resistance or Intermittent Contact at the Pins
Likely Cause
Corroded contact pins from saltwater or chemical exposure, despite plating, increasing contact resistance across the mated connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure contact resistance across the mated connector; visually inspect pins for corrosion, pitting, or discolouration once separated.
Corrective Action
Clean corroded pins with contact cleaner and apply dielectric grease, or replace the connector if corrosion has pitted the plating.
Water Ingress at the Connector
Likely Cause
O-ring hardening or cracking from UV exposure, temperature cycling, or chemical attack has broken the connector's seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the O-ring for hardening, cracking, or flattening; check for moisture or corrosion inside the connector body once opened.
Corrective Action
Replace the O-ring with a new one lubricated with silicone grease, and re-test the seal before re-energising.
Overheating, Arcing, or Intermittent Power
Likely Cause
The coupling ring was not fully tightened to the recommended torque, leaving a loose connection that arcs and heats under load.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the coupling ring is torqued to specification; look for heat discolouration, pitting, or arcing marks at the mating face.
Corrective Action
Fully re-tighten the coupling ring to the correct torque, or replace the connector if arcing has damaged the contact faces.
High Resistance or Overheating at the Cable Termination
Likely Cause
A poorly stripped or crimped cable termination inside the connector body is creating a high-resistance joint.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure resistance across the termination; inspect the crimp or termination point for incomplete insertion or a visibly poor crimp.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate the cable using the correct stripping and crimping tools, ensuring full insertion, then re-verify resistance.
Connector Will Not Mate or Seat Fully
Likely Cause
Cross-threading of the coupling ring during a previous mating attempt has damaged the threads.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coupling ring and mating threads for visible cross-threading or deformation before forcing another attempt.
Corrective Action
Replace the connector half with damaged threads; always start the coupling ring by hand before using a wrench.
Water Ingress at the Cable Entry
Likely Cause
An incorrectly sized cable gland — mismatched to the cable's actual diameter — is failing to seal properly.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the gland size against the cable diameter it is sealing; inspect for gaps or a loose seal around the cable entry.
Corrective Action
Replace the gland with the correct size for the cable, and re-verify the seal before returning to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Mating or Unmating
Electrical Safety

The primary connector carries the CCR's constant-current output at up to several kV; de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy at the pins before separating a mated pair, regardless of how the circuit appears at the fixture end.

Correct Coupling Ring Torque
Workmanship

Tighten the coupling ring to the manufacturer's specified torque; under-torquing leaves the mated connector loose under vibration and heating, while cross-threading can prevent a full seat on a subsequent mating.

O-Ring & Gland Inspection
Workmanship

Inspect the O-ring for hardness or cracking and confirm the cable gland matches the actual cable diameter before mating; a compromised seal on a primary-voltage connector degrades insulation resistance quickly once water enters.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger and check contact condition, coupling torque, and seal integrity before restoring power; a marginal primary-side connection found after re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Confined Space
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on primary-side connections inside a base can, and follow confined-space precautions where the can is deep or poorly ventilated.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the connector is exposed, and secure or remove loose material before closing the base can.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a primary connector unmated, under-torqued, or with a suspect seal; verify the connection and reseal the base can before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Primary-Side Insulation Resistance Degrades Faster Than It Looks
Electrical

A primary connector operating at up to several kV stresses its insulation more than a low-voltage secondary connection, so a seal that would be a minor concern on the secondary side can allow rapid insulation resistance degradation on the primary side once moisture enters; treat any doubt about the seal as a reason to megger-test immediately, not at the next scheduled visit.

Track Insulation Resistance Trend, Not Just Pass/Fail
Troubleshooting

A primary connector's insulation resistance reading trending downward across visits, even while still passing the minimum threshold, is an early warning of a developing fault; log readings over time rather than treating each test as a standalone pass or fail.

Coupling Ring Torque Affects Contact Pressure, Not Just Mechanical Security
Workmanship

Under-torquing the coupling ring doesn't just risk the pair working loose under vibration, it can leave contact pressure at the pins below spec even while the connector appears fully mated, which matters more at primary voltage due to arcing risk at marginal contacts.

O-Ring Compression Set Happens Even Without Visible Damage
Durability

An O-ring can lose its sealing force through compression set after repeated mate/unmate cycles or years in place, without showing cracking or visible wear; replace it on schedule at overhaul rather than only when it looks obviously degraded.

Confirm Gland Torque Independently of Coupling Ring Torque
Workmanship

Technicians sometimes assume a correctly torqued coupling ring also means the cable gland is sealed; the gland must be checked and torqued to its own specification, since the two seals are independent.

Log Which Base Cans Have Had Recent Primary-Side Work
Compliance

Because primary connector faults commonly reappear at the same location shortly after a repair, keeping a simple log of recent connector work by base can location helps a follow-up crew recognize a recurring problem rather than treating each callout as unrelated.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about primary connectors (L-823 plug): ratings, termination, standards, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is a primary connector?

The primary connector, also known as the L-823 plug or male connector, is the cable-mounted half of the two-pin waterproof connector system used for primary series circuit connections in AGL. It is field-terminated to the primary cable.

Q2Why is the primary connector important?

It is the part technicians handle repeatedly whenever a fixture or transformer must be disconnected for maintenance or replacement. Field termination directly onto the primary cable makes those operations practical.

Q3Where are primary connectors used?

Wherever primary circuit terminations are needed, including isolating transformer inputs (whose receptacle accepts the connector), junction boxes and splice points.

Q4What are the typical ratings?

6.6 A or 20 A continuous depending on variant, up to 5 kV transient and 600 V continuous, cable compatibility of 6-16 mm² (AWG 10-6), IP68 when mated, and at least 500 mating cycles.

Q5What contact resistance and insulation resistance apply?

Initial contact resistance is typically 0.5 milliohm or less per contact pair, and insulation resistance should be at least 50 megohms when megger tested from the mated connector to ground.

Q6Does the connector isolate the fixture from the series circuit?

The series loop remains isolated from the fixture body through the downstream isolating transformer. The connector itself only carries the primary current across its two-pin contact system.

Q7Which standards apply?

FAA AC 150/5345-26 (L-823 plug and receptacle cable connectors, where L-823 is the equipment type designation rather than the AC number), IEC 63067, IEC 61820-1 and ICAO Annex 14.

Q8How often should primary connectors be maintained?

Annually: clean pins and reapply dielectric grease, inspect and replace O-rings, verify coupling torque (starting the ring by hand to avoid cross-threading), check terminations and gland seal, and megger test insulation resistance.

Q9Why is there high resistance or an intermittent contact at the pins?

Corroded pins from saltwater or chemical exposure raise contact resistance across the mated connection. Measure contact resistance and inspect the pins for discolouration or pitting.
