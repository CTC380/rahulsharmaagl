Method & Reference

How the coverage is calculated

Every step is shown so the result can be checked by hand.

1. Coverage width

The beam widens with range. At range d the full width across the axis follows from the full spread angle θ, then the usable percentage is applied.

W = 2 × d × tan(θ ÷ 2) × usable %

2. Overlap or gap

Adjacent fixtures overlap when the width is more than the spacing, and leave a gap when it is less.

Overlap = W − spacing    Overlap % = Overlap ÷ W × 100

3. Where beams meet

Beams from neighbours first touch where the width equals the spacing.

d = spacing ÷ (2 × tan(θ ÷ 2) × usable %)

4. Meeting a target overlap

For a target overlap fraction t, the width must be the spacing divided by (1 − t). That gives the spread needed, or the largest spacing for a given spread.

θ = 2 × atan( spacing ÷ (1 − t) ÷ (2 × d × usable %) )    spacing = W × (1 − t)

What this tool does not do

It does not model intensity across the beam, aiming tilt, or the visibility needed by a pilot. Beam spread depends on the intensity level it is defined at, so two fixtures with the same quoted spread can differ in usable width. Use the photometric data for intensity. For fixtures certified to ICAO Annex 14 or FAA specifications, read the angle from the isocandela diagram at the intensity level you need, because a lower intensity level gives a wider beam.

FAQ

Beam Coverage Calculator: Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers on the coverage width of a fixture beam and how neighbouring beams overlap.

What does the Beam Coverage Calculator do?

It finds the coverage width of a fixture beam at a given range, and how much neighbouring beams overlap. It also gives the range at which beams first meet, and the spread or spacing needed to reach a target overlap.

What do I need to enter?

Enter the horizontal beam spread as a full angle from the fixture data sheet, the range to the point of interest, and the spacing between adjacent fixtures. The vertical spread is optional and gives the height of the beam at range. Note the intensity level at which the data sheet defines the spread, such as 50 percent of peak. Lengths can be in metres or feet.

How is the coverage width calculated?

The full width across the axis at range d is 2 times d times the tangent of half the spread angle, times the usable width percentage. The usable width trims the coverage to the part of the beam you consider effective, which is your own rule.

How are the overlap and gap worked out?

Adjacent fixtures overlap when the width is more than the spacing, and leave a gap when it is less. The overlap is the width minus the spacing, and the overlap percentage is that divided by the width, times 100. Fixtures are taken to point the same way, side by side.

At what range do neighbouring beams meet?

Beams from neighbours first touch where the width equals the spacing. That range is the spacing divided by 2 times the tangent of half the spread angle, times the usable percentage. The tool also shows how many beams cover the point, which is the width divided by the spacing.

How do I reach a target overlap?

For a target overlap fraction t, the width must be the spacing divided by 1 minus t. That gives the spread needed at your range and spacing, and the largest spacing for your spread and range, which is the width times 1 minus t. The target overlap is your own rule, not a standard value.

Can you give a worked example?

A 10 degree spread at 300 m gives a width of 52.5 m. With a spacing of 60 m there is a gap of 7.5 m, and the beams first meet at 342.9 m. A 20 percent overlap needs a width of 75 m, which means a 14.25 degree spread, or a largest spacing of 42.0 m at 10 degrees.

What does the tool not do?

It uses flat-plane geometry with the beam axis square to the coverage plane. It does not model intensity across the beam, aiming tilt, or the visibility needed by a pilot. The edge of a real beam is not sharp, so treat the width as the stated spread and not a cut-off. Two fixtures with the same quoted spread can differ in usable width, so take the beam spread and its intensity level from the manufacturer's photometric data.
