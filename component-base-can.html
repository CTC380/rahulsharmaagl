Base Can

A rugged, cylindrical steel enclosure embedded in the pavement that serves as the primary foundation for elevated AGL fixtures. It houses the isolating transformer, cable connections, and provides a stable, level mounting platform for the fixture's base plate, ensuring long‑term reliability under heavy aircraft loads and harsh environmental conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Base Can (also known as a transformer can, fixture base, or ground can) is a heavy‑duty cylindrical steel housing that is embedded in the pavement (concrete or asphalt) to provide a robust foundation for elevated AGL light fixtures. It serves as the primary structural and protective enclosure for the isolating transformer, cable terminations, and connector interfaces that power the fixture. Base cans are supplied in two distinct load classes that are not interchangeable: FAA Type L-867 is rated for occasional light vehicular traffic only and must never be installed where it could see aircraft wheel loads, while Type L-868 is load-bearing and rated for direct aircraft traffic. Selecting the wrong class for a given location — for example, an L-867 can where an aircraft excursion or routine taxi could load it — is a genuine safety defect, not just a spec mismatch.

Key characteristics of base cans include:

  • Robust Construction: Made from high‑strength steel (typically hot‑dip galvanised or stainless) to withstand heavy aircraft loads, impact, and corrosion.
  • Watertight Design: Sealed to prevent moisture ingress, with gasketed lids and cable entry points.
  • Integrated Mounting: Includes a top flange or threaded holes for attaching the fixture's base plate, and internal brackets for securing the isolating transformer.
  • Cable Management: Provides internal space for primary and secondary cable connections, with cable glands or conduits for entry and exit.

Base cans are designated L-867 (light-duty, for locations subject only to occasional light vehicular loads — not aircraft or heavy vehicle traffic) or L-868 (heavy-duty, rated for aircraft and heavy vehicular loads). Both types support elevated fixtures; the choice depends on the load the installation location will see, not on whether the fixture is elevated or inset — flush-mounted inset fixtures use their own in-pavement fixture housings (e.g. the L-850 series) rather than an L-867/L-868 base can. Base cans are typically installed during pavement construction or retrofitted into existing pavement, and are available in various diameters (typically 150mm to 300mm) and depths (200mm to 500mm) to accommodate different transformer sizes and cable configurations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The parts of an elevated fixture most likely to actually fail — the isolating transformer and the cable terminations — are also the parts least able to tolerate water, dirt, or physical disturbance. The base can's purpose is to give those components a dedicated, protected space below the pavement surface, separated from both the aircraft loading experienced above and the surrounding soil and groundwater. It also fixes the fixture's exact position and height before the fixture is even installed, so the frangible pole or base plate that sits on top of it starts from a known, level, and correctly located reference point rather than relying on field adjustment. In effect, the base can turns "install a light fixture in a runway" into two separable jobs — a below-grade electrical and structural foundation, and an above-grade optical fixture — that can be built, sealed, and maintained independently of each other.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Base cans are used wherever elevated AGL fixtures are installed on paved surfaces, including:

  • Runway Edge Lighting: For elevated runway edge light fixtures, especially on curves, intersections, or where flush mounting is not feasible.
  • Taxiway Edge Lighting: For elevated taxiway edge lights, particularly on curves and at intersections.
  • Approach Lighting: For elevated approach light fixtures, barrettes, and flashing light units.
  • Obstruction Lighting: For elevated obstruction lights on poles or structures.
  • Airfield Signage: For elevated mandatory instruction signs and direction signs that require a stable foundation.
  • Sensor Mounting: For anemometers, wind cones, and other instrumentation that requires a raised mounting.

The base can is designed to be installed flush with or slightly above the pavement surface, with a removable top cover that allows access to the transformer and connections for maintenance. The can's internal volume is sized to accommodate the isolating transformer, cable splices, and any necessary connectors, while maintaining adequate clearance for heat dissipation.

Placement

Installation & Location

The base can is set into a formed or cored foundation pit at the fixture's surveyed position, with its top rim set flush with or at the specified height above finished grade depending on whether the installation is elevated or inset. Backfill around the can is compacted in layers to prevent later settlement, which would otherwise leave the can proud of or sunk below grade; cable entry to the can is made through a sealed gland or knockout kept below any water table in the surrounding backfill where possible. Can position and level are checked against the pavement or shoulder survey before backfilling, since a can set at the wrong height is far more disruptive to correct once the surrounding pavement or turf is finished.

Function

How Does It Work?

The base can functions as a combined structural foundation, protective enclosure, and cable management hub for the elevated fixture. Its design ensures that the fixture is securely mounted, electrically connected, and protected from the environment:

  • Structural Support: The can is embedded in the pavement with a concrete or asphalt surround, providing a rigid, stable base that can withstand the dynamic loads from aircraft, snowploughs, and maintenance vehicles.
  • Transformer Housing: The isolating transformer is mounted inside the can on a bracket or base plate, keeping it dry and protected from mechanical damage. The secondary cable runs from the transformer to the fixture, while the primary cable enters the can through a cable gland or conduit.
  • Weatherproofing: A gasketed lid or cover plate seals the top of the can, preventing water, de‑icing fluids, and debris from entering. Cable entry points are also sealed with glands or compound to maintain a watertight seal.
  • Accessibility: The top cover can be removed for maintenance, allowing technicians to access the transformer, cables, and connections without disturbing the pavement.
  • Frangible Feature (if applicable): In some designs, the base can includes a frangible coupling or break‑away flange that allows the fixture to shear off on impact, minimising aircraft damage.

The installation process typically involves:

  • Excavation: A hole is dug in the pavement to the required depth and diameter.
  • Placement: The base can is positioned in the hole, levelled, and secured with a concrete or asphalt surround.
  • Wiring: The primary cable is routed into the can, and the isolating transformer is connected. The secondary cable is routed from the transformer to the fixture location.
  • Fixture Mounting: The fixture's base plate is bolted to the top flange of the can, and the fixture is installed.
  • Sealing: The lid is installed and sealed to prevent water ingress.

The base can is designed to provide a service life of 20+ years, with regular inspection of the seal, cables, and transformer condition.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The base can is the one point in the installation where the electrical and mechanical sides of the architecture physically meet. The series circuit cable arrives from the trench and terminates inside it, generally at the isolating transformer housed in the same can; at the same time, the can itself bolts down onto the anchor bolts and carries the structural load of whatever sits above it — a frangible coupling and elevated fixture, or an inset fixture seated directly in it. Because both the power connection and the mechanical foundation converge at this one component, a base can problem can present as either an electrical fault (a wet or failed transformer) or a mechanical one (a loose or corroded mounting), and diagnosing which requires opening the can rather than reading a single status from either the ALCMS or a structural inspection alone.

Electrical and mechanical architectures converge in one component — a fault here can be either kind, or both.

Construction

Physical Construction

A base can is fabricated from structural steel plate, rolled and welded into a cylindrical body, then hot-dip galvanised or specified in stainless steel for installations demanding higher corrosion resistance. The top of the can terminates in a welded flange or ring, machined or drilled with a bolt pattern matched to the fixture's base plate, so the fixture bolts directly onto the can once it is set into the pavement. A removable steel lid, gasketed with a neoprene or rubber seal and secured with bolts or quick-release clamps, closes the top opening around the fixture base, keeping the transformer and connector compartment inside protected from surface water while still allowing access for maintenance. Cable entries, typically two to four gland or conduit fittings, are welded or threaded into the can's side wall below grade, sized to accept the incoming and outgoing series circuit cables with a sealed, strain-relieved connection. The can's wall thickness and overall structural design are engineered to support the vertical load of the fixture above and the surrounding pavement load without deforming, while its buried depth and diameter are matched to the isolating transformer and connector hardware it houses.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Lid Gasket Inspection
Annual

Check the neoprene lid gasket for compression set or cracking, and confirm bolts or quick-release clamps still seat the lid tightly enough to hold the IP67/IP68 rating.

Internal Moisture / Debris Check
Annual

Open the can and check for standing water, silt, or debris that may have entered through the lid seal or cable glands.

Cable Gland / Entry Inspection
Annual

Inspect each cable gland or conduit entry for a secure seal, since this is the most common route for moisture ingress into the can.

Top Flange & Bolt Condition
Annual

Check the top flange threaded holes and fixture mounting bolts for corrosion or thread damage that could compromise the fixture connection.

Corrosion Check
Annual

Inspect galvanised or stainless can walls, inside and out, for coating breakdown or pitting, particularly near the base where moisture pools.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test cable terminations inside the can to confirm insulation resistance remains above minimum, catching moisture ingress before it causes a series-circuit fault.

Drainage Check
Annual

Confirm any drainage provision at the base of the can is clear, and that surrounding soil or pavement grading directs water away from the unit.

Load Capacity / Structural Check
Annual

Inspect for wall deformation or cracking that could reduce the can's rated ≥50 kN vertical load capacity, particularly on cans under trafficked pavement.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Water Ingress Through Lid Seal

The lid gasket can deteriorate over time, allowing water and de‑icing fluids to enter the can, causing corrosion of the transformer and cables. Regularly inspect and replace the gasket. Use a silicone‑based sealant on the lid bolts.

Corrosion of Can Interior

Condensation or water ingress can cause internal corrosion, compromising the structural integrity. Use stainless steel or galvanised cans, and consider installing a desiccant or moisture‑absorbing pack inside the can.

Damaged Cable Glands

Cable glands can be damaged during installation or by movement of the pavement, allowing water ingress. Use heavy‑duty glands and check them periodically for tightness and seal integrity.

Insufficient Depth for Transformer

If the base can is too shallow, the isolating transformer may not fit, or may be too close to the lid, causing overheating. Verify the transformer dimensions and select a can with adequate depth and ventilation.

Loose Fixture Bolts

Vibration from aircraft can loosen the fixture‑to‑can bolts, affecting alignment and safety. Use lock washers and thread‑locking compound, and schedule regular bolt torque checks.

Pavement Settlement Around Can

Settlement of the surrounding pavement can cause the can to tilt or sink, misaligning the fixture. Ensure proper compaction of the backfill and consider using a concrete collar to prevent settlement.

Rodent Infestation

Rodents can enter the can through cable entry points and damage insulation or chew cables. Use metal conduits, seal gaps with expanding foam, and consider installing rodent‑repellent materials.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fixture Dark, Water Found Inside Can on Excavation
Likely Cause
A perished or improperly torqued lid gasket has allowed surface water and de‑icing fluid to enter, shorting or corroding the transformer and cable connections.
Diagnostic Steps
Excavate to the can, remove the lid, and inspect the gasket seat for compression set or cracking; megger the transformer and secondary cable; check for standing water or silt at the base of the can.
Corrective Action
Bail out and dry the can, replace the gasket and any corroded hardware, re‑torque lid bolts to spec with a silicone sealant, and replace the transformer if insulation resistance is below minimum.
Can Interior Shows Corrosion or Rust‑Through
Likely Cause
Recurring condensation or slow water ingress over time has attacked an untreated or poorly galvanised can wall, weakening the structure.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the can wall and base for pitting, flaking, or thin spots; check whether a desiccant/moisture‑absorbing pack is present and still active; confirm the can material spec against the original installation record.
Corrective Action
Replace the can with a stainless steel or properly galvanised unit if structural integrity is compromised, install a fresh desiccant pack, and confirm the lid seal is restored before backfill.
Cable Gland Leaking or Cracked
Likely Cause
Installation damage, UV/thermal ageing of the gland material, or pavement movement transmitting stress into the cable entry point.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect each gland for cracking, deformation, or a loose compression nut; check for water staining or ingress paths immediately below the gland; confirm cable is not under lateral strain from settlement.
Corrective Action
Replace with a heavy‑duty gland rated for the cable size, re‑seat and re‑tighten to spec, and add strain relief if pavement movement is contributing to the fault.
Transformer Overheating or Fitment Too Tight
Likely Cause
An undersized or shallow base can leaves the isolating transformer crowded against the lid, restricting airflow and trapping heat.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify transformer dimensions against the can's usable depth; check for discoloration or a heat‑damaged smell on the transformer casing; confirm clearance to the lid meets the manufacturer's minimum.
Corrective Action
Upsize to a can with adequate depth and ventilation, reposition the transformer for clearance, and replace the transformer if heat damage is confirmed.
Fixture Loose or Misaligned Atop the Can
Likely Cause
Aircraft-induced vibration has backed off the fixture‑to‑can bolts, or the bolts were installed without lock washers or thread‑locking compound.
Diagnostic Steps
Check bolt torque against spec at each fixing point; inspect for missing or sheared lock washers; visually confirm fixture aim has drifted from tolerance.
Corrective Action
Re‑torque all fixture bolts with lock washers and thread‑locking compound, re‑aim the fixture, and add the can to the routine bolt‑torque inspection schedule.
Can Tilted, Sunk, or Surrounding Pavement Cracked
Likely Cause
Settlement or heave of the backfill or subgrade around the can, often from inadequate compaction at original installation or frost/thaw cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the can's level and fixture aim against tolerance; inspect surrounding pavement for cracking, dishing, or a gap opening at the can collar; probe backfill compaction where accessible.
Corrective Action
Re‑set and re‑level the can, recompact backfill in lifts, and install a concrete collar around the can to prevent recurrence before reinstating the pavement surface.
Chewed Insulation or Signs of Rodent Entry
Likely Cause
Rodents have entered through an unsealed cable entry point or gap around the gland and damaged cable insulation inside the can.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect cable jackets inside the can for gnaw marks or exposed conductors; check for nesting material or droppings; identify the entry gap around glands or conduit penetrations.
Corrective Action
Replace any chewed cable sections, seal entry gaps with expanding foam or rodent‑resistant compound, and fit metal conduit sleeves at vulnerable penetrations.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Water Ingress Through Lid Seal
High Severity
Root Cause
A degraded or missing lid gasket, or a lid not correctly seated after the last maintenance access.
Effect
The fixture goes dark and water is found inside the can on excavation, having accelerated corrosion and insulation breakdown before the fault was traced.
Detection Method
Lid gasket inspection and an internal moisture/debris check performed at every maintenance access.
Corrosion of Can Interior
High Severity
Root Cause
Standing water, a damaged corrosion coating, or a can material not suited to prolonged buried, damp conditions.
Effect
The can interior shows corrosion or rust-through, which can eventually compromise the can's watertightness and structural support for the fixture above.
Detection Method
Corrosion check and a drainage check to confirm the can isn't retaining water against the affected surface.
Damaged Cable Glands
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Gland wear from repeated cable movement, incorrect gland sizing for the cable jacket, or physical damage during a maintenance access.
Effect
The cable gland is leaking or cracked, giving water a direct path into the can even where the lid seal itself is intact.
Detection Method
Cable gland/entry inspection performed at every maintenance access, checking for cracking or a compromised seal.
Pavement Settlement Around the Can
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure eroding the fill surrounding the can.
Effect
The can tilts, sinks, or the surrounding pavement cracks, stressing the fixture's alignment and the cable connections inside.
Detection Method
Load capacity/structural check, and a visual inspection of the can's level against the surrounding pavement.
Rodent Infestation
Low Severity
Root Cause
An unsealed cable entry or drainage opening providing rodents access to the can's interior space.
Effect
Chewed insulation or other signs of rodent entry, which can progress to an insulation fault if not addressed.
Detection Method
Internal moisture/debris check that also looks for chewed insulation or nesting material during routine access.
Insufficient Depth for Transformer
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A can specified or substituted during maintenance that doesn't match the internal depth the installed transformer model requires.
Effect
Transformer overheating or a fitment too tight for proper cable dressing inside the can.
Detection Method
Load capacity verification and a dimensional check confirming the can's internal depth matches the transformer it houses.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the circuit and verify zero energy at the isolating transformer and connectors before opening the base can lid.

Standing Water Shock Hazard
Electrical Safety

Base cans can accumulate groundwater; treat any water in the can as potentially energised until the circuit is confirmed isolated, and pump or drain before reaching in.

Lid & Housing Handling
Personnel Safety

Use proper lifting technique or lifting tools for the heavy steel lid and housing; a dropped lid or housing at an awkward angle is a crush and pinch-point hazard.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside and raise a NOTAM where the fixture served by the base can will be dark, and never leave an open base can unattended on the movement area.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for while the can is open, and secure or remove loose material before refitting the lid.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the circuit after any transformer or connector work inside the can before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same loop.

Restore Seal & Load Rating
Compliance

Confirm the lid is correctly seated, gasketed, and rated for the traffic loading of its location before reopening the area, and verify the can hasn't shifted or settled out of level.

Quick Reference

Airport Light Base Can

An airport light base can is the cylindrical housing set into the pavement or ground that supports an elevated or inset light and holds the transformer and connections beneath it. The right load class (L-867 or L-868) must be used for the location. After installation, check that the base can is level, correctly set to height, sealed, and that its cable entries are watertight, because a poorly set base can causes tilted lights and water ingress.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Watertight Seal Only Works if the Gasket Is Actually Inspected
Durability

Base can watertightness depends entirely on gasket condition at the lid and cable entries; a gasket that's compressed, cracked, or displaced defeats the can's protective function even though the can itself looks structurally fine. Include gasket condition in every scheduled inspection, not just when moisture ingress is already suspected.

Isolating Transformer Access Shouldn't Compromise the Seal
Electrical

Frequent transformer access for testing or replacement stresses the lid seal and cable gland seals over time; re-seat and verify seals properly after every access rather than assuming a quick re-close is sufficient.

Galvanised Cans Corrode From the Inside Out When Drainage Fails
Corrosion

If the can's drainage path is blocked or was never properly graded, water accumulates inside where galvanising is often thinnest at weld seams, corroding the can from the inside before any external sign appears. Verify internal drainage is clear during every service visit.

Verify Load Rating Matches Actual Pavement Traffic, Not Just Design Category
Mechanical

A base can rated for the pavement's design traffic category can still be under-specified if actual traffic (de-icing vehicles, snow removal equipment) exceeds what was assumed at design time. Confirm the installed can's load rating against actual observed traffic, especially after any change in ground vehicle operations near the location.

Can Tilt Transfers Directly to Fixture Aim
Alignment

Because the can is the foundation the entire fixture assembly sits on, even minor can tilt from installation error or ground movement propagates directly into fixture misalignment above it. Verify can level as part of any alignment troubleshooting, not just the visible fixture.

Confirm Zero Energy Before Opening, Even on a 'De-Energised' Circuit
Safety

Isolating transformers can retain induced or residual voltage briefly after de-energisation; verify zero energy with a meter at the can before handling internal connections, regardless of confirmation that the loop breaker is open.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the base can: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a base can?

A base can (also called a transformer can, fixture base or ground can) is a heavy-duty cylindrical steel housing embedded in the pavement. It is the structural and protective enclosure for the isolating transformer, cable terminations and connector interfaces that power an elevated AGL fixture.

Q2What is the difference between L-867 and L-868 base cans?

They are two load classes that are not interchangeable. FAA Type L-867 is rated for occasional light vehicular traffic only and must never be installed where it could see aircraft wheel loads. Type L-868 is load-bearing and rated for direct aircraft traffic. Using the wrong class is a genuine safety defect.

Q3Where are base cans used?

They are used wherever elevated AGL fixtures are installed on paved surfaces: runway edge lighting (especially on curves and intersections), taxiway edge lighting, approach lighting and obstruction lighting.

Q4What sizes and materials are typical?

Internal diameter is typically 150 mm to 300 mm, depth 200 mm to 500 mm depending on transformer size, and wall thickness 4 mm to 8 mm. Material is structural steel (S235JR or S355JR) hot-dip galvanised to at least 85 µm, or 316L stainless steel.

Q5How is a base can sealed?

It has a removable steel lid with a neoprene gasket secured by bolts or quick-release clamps, and 2 to 4 cable glands or conduit entries. With the lid fitted, the typical rating is IP67 or IP68 (submersible).

Q6What load can a base can carry?

An L-868 can is load-bearing, typically at least 50 kN depending on size, and rated for direct aircraft traffic. An L-867 can is for light vehicular traffic only and is not rated for aircraft wheel loads.

Q7Which standards apply to base cans?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-42 (L-867 and L-868 base cans), ICAO Annex 14, EN 10025 for structural steel and ASTM A123 for hot-dip galvanised coatings. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a base can be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover the lid gasket, internal moisture and debris, cable glands and entries, top flange and bolts, corrosion, insulation resistance, drainage and load and structural condition. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9Why is there water inside a base can?

The most common route is water ingress through a failed lid seal. Damaged cable glands and inadequate drainage also contribute, and water inside the can is a typical cause of a dark fixture. Inspect the gasket, glands and drainage path, and test insulation resistance.

Q10What are the common problems with base cans?

Common problems are water ingress through the lid seal, corrosion of the can interior, damaged cable glands, insufficient depth for the transformer, loose fixture bolts, pavement settlement around the can and rodent infestation.
