Lifecycle Case Studies & Decision Models

The practical decision-learning and engineering-judgement division of the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide. Divisions 01–23 establish the lifecycle framework, controls, data, risk, maintenance, cost, performance and governance. Division 24 shows how those principles are actually applied to real AGL assets — walking each case through Asset Condition → Performance → Reliability → Risk → Lifecycle Cost → Options → Decision → Implementation → Verification, and packaging the recurring judgement calls into 8 reusable decision models.

Initial Issue

The question this division answers — Given everything we know about an asset's condition, performance, reliability, risk and lifecycle cost, what is the defensible engineering decision — continue, repair, refurbish, upgrade or replace — and how do we prove it was the right one?

Where Division 24 Sits in the Lifecycle

Division 24 does not operate as a standalone case-study chapter. It sits at the point where the asset data, criticality, design, maintenance, cost, performance and governance work of the preceding divisions converges into an actual engineering decision.

CSD-19 Continue Operating Versus Replace
Purpose

Recognizes that age alone should not automatically trigger replacement.

Continue Operating When
  • Condition is acceptable
  • Performance remains compliant
  • Reliability is acceptable
  • Critical spares are available
  • Obsolescence risk is manageable
  • Lifecycle cost remains reasonable
  • Future risk is acceptable
Replace When

Multiple factors demonstrate unacceptable future risk or lifecycle value.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Lifecycle Case Studies & Decision Models in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 24 — Lifecycle Case Studies & Decision Models in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 24 is the practical decision-learning and engineering-judgement division. It covers 23 topics, made up of 15 lifecycle case studies and 8 decision models, spanning repair, refurbish, upgrade, replace and lifecycle-cost decisions for runway lights, taxiway lights, CCR, transformers, cables, signs, PAPI, approach lighting, ALCMS and base cans.

Q2What are lifecycle case studies and decision models?

Division 24 is the practical decision-learning and engineering-judgement division of the guide. It contains 15 lifecycle case studies and 8 decision models (23 topics in total) that apply Divisions 01 to 23 to real asset decisions.

Q3Which AGL assets have lifecycle case studies?

Case studies cover runway lights, taxiway lights, CCRs, isolation transformers, cables, signs, PAPI, approach lighting, ALCMS and base cans. They are followed by case studies on refurbishment, replacement, obsolescence, failure-driven replacement and lifecycle-cost optimization.

Q4Which decision models does Division 24 include?

Repair versus replace, refurbish versus replace, upgrade versus replace, continue operating versus replace, life extension versus replacement, technology migration, critical-spare decision and capital investment decision.

Q5What is the standard case study template?

Every case study uses the same 13-part structure so any two can be compared directly: case background, asset profile, current condition, performance, lifecycle history, risk assessment, lifecycle cost, options, option evaluation, decision, implementation, post-decision verification and lessons learned.

Q6Should an asset be replaced simply because of its age?

No. The continue-operating-versus-replace model recognizes that age alone should not automatically trigger replacement. Continuing can be justified where condition is acceptable, performance remains compliant, reliability is acceptable and critical spares are available.

Q7Why is the lowest purchase price not always the best option?

The lifecycle-cost optimization case study demonstrates that the lowest purchase price is not necessarily the lowest lifecycle-cost option, since acquisition, installation, energy, maintenance, failure and spares costs all count.

Q8What can obsolescence and repeated failures trigger?

The obsolescence case study shows that an asset can become a lifecycle risk before physical failure. The failure-driven replacement case study shows replacement triggered by a repeated-failure pattern rather than by age or a scheduled review.
