PAPI Installation & Aiming Handbook

Working principle, 4-unit and 2-unit systems, aiming angles, threshold crossing height, siting, electrical supply, photometry, testing and troubleshooting. PAPI is a geometric system: each unit is aimed at its own elevation angle, so a small error in elevation, aiming, spacing or siting changes what the pilot sees.

Start Learning PAPI Engineering Guide
Overview

Why PAPI Installation and Aiming Matter

PAPI looks simple from the runway: four light boxes producing red and white indications. Engineering-wise it is a controlled combination of siting, elevation, geometry, optics, aiming, electrical supply, photometry and obstacle clearance.

A small error in unit elevation, aiming, spacing or siting can change the visual indication presented to the pilot, even when every unit is lit and the electrical system is healthy.

Working Principle

What Is PAPI and How Does It Work?

Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) is a visual approach slope guidance system. A conventional four-unit PAPI is a horizontal row of light units beside the runway. Each unit projects a sharply divided beam: aviation white in the upper sector, aviation red in the lower sector.

Because each unit is aimed at a different elevation angle, the pilot sees a different red/white combination depending on where the aircraft is relative to the approach path. Aircraft position changes → viewing angle changes → red/white indication changes. The indication is optical geometry, not a colour change commanded from a controller.

Aircraft position4-unit PAPI2-unit APAPI
Too high4 White2 White
Slightly high3 White + 1 Red—
On approach path2 White + 2 Red1 White + 1 Red
Slightly low1 White + 3 Red—
Too low4 Red2 Red
Memory aid: more white = high, more red = low, an even split = on path. PAPI is normally sited on the left of the runway as seen from the approach, unless siting constraints require otherwise.

Optics

Light Unit, Optical System and Red/White Transition

Light unit

Generates output, forms the beam, separates red and white sectors and allows accurate horizontal and vertical aiming. LED sources must meet the applicable equipment standard.

Red/white transition

A sharp, narrow transition gives the pilot a clear indication. FAA AC 150/5345-28H limits the transition band at 1,000 ft (300 m) in minutes of arc, tighter at beam centre.

Colour and beam

Aviation white and aviation red. Intensity, distribution, coverage and colour stability all matter, so a unit that merely lights up is not proof of compliance. A PAPI can look acceptable in intensity and still have an optical or chromaticity problem.

Poor transition can come from filter damage, contamination, incorrect optical assembly, misalignment or an equipment defect.
Siting

PAPI Distance from Runway, TCH and Obstacle Clearance

Distance from threshold

There is no single universal distance. PAPI location is a calculated result of the approach angle, threshold crossing height, aircraft height group, runway profile and elevations, PAPI elevation, obstacles and the obstacle clearance surface, ILS glide path, the instrument approach procedure and local regulations.

Threshold Crossing Height (TCH)

TCH is the height of the lowest on-course signal above the intersection of runway centreline and threshold. Required TCH depends on the most demanding aircraft height group using the runway. Moving the PAPI changes the approach-path geometry at the threshold: PAPI elevation, PAPI-to-threshold distance and the selected approach angle together set it, but the final calculation must also account for runway profile and obstacle clearance.

With an ILS

Do not design PAPI in isolation. Visual and electronic glide paths must be reconciled during siting and commissioning, and FAA guidance has specific aiming provisions for certain height-group-4 aircraft on ILS runways. The aim is to avoid a conflicting visual and electronic indication.

Obstacle clearance

An obstacle clearance surface starts ahead of the PAPI and extends into the approach area, related to the lowest on-course signal. Survey terrain, buildings, trees, masts, poles, relevant vehicle positions and planned development, not just the area around the units.

PAPI location is a calculated siting result, not a fixed distance from the threshold.

Angles & Aiming

PAPI Angle and Aiming Angle Explained

Side view (angles exaggerated): each unit switches from red to white at its own elevation angle

Two different things are often confused. The approach path angle is the selected visual glide path, normally around 3°. The unit aiming angle is the elevation each individual unit is set to around that path. The four units are not all aimed at 3°.

Unit (outer → inner)Offset (FAA L-880 standard)Example for 3° path
Outer unit30′ below path2°30′
Second unit10′ below path2°50′
Third unit10′ above path3°10′
Inner unit (nearest runway)30′ above path3°30′
The example uses the standard offsets and an ICAO-style 3° arrangement. Some aircraft/runway configurations use different offsets, so final angles must come from the approved design and applicable standard, never a generic field setting.

PAPI Installation Procedure

01

Site survey

Runway geometry, threshold, centreline, elevations, profile, aircraft group, obstacles, ILS relationship, access.

02

Siting calculation

Position, TCH, approach angle, unit aiming angles, obstacle clearance, lateral location.

03

Foundation and mounting

Elevation, stability, drainage, frangible requirements, hardware, maintenance access.

04

Unit positioning

Sequence, spacing, runway-edge distance, perpendicularity, physical elevation.

05

Levelling

Common horizontal reference plane. Tilt error changes the effective aiming angle.

06

Horizontal alignment

Azimuth, parallelism, unit-to-unit consistency against a reference.

07

Vertical aiming

Reference plane → inclinometer/aiming device → angle setting → locking → verification.

08

Electrical connection

AC supply and power control unit, or CCR series circuit with isolating transformer arrangement; control and monitoring.

09

Functional testing

All units operate, correct red/white presentation, intensity steps, monitoring and control.

10

Photometric testing

Intensity, colour, beam pattern, transition, angular performance.

11

Flight check

Operational verification where the authority requires it. Field alignment alone does not prove acceptability.

12

Documentation

Coordinates, elevations, spacing, aiming angles, TCH, survey, obstacle assessment, test and photometric results, as-builts.

Electrical

PAPI Electrical Supply and Series Circuit

Voltage-powered

AC supply → Power control unit → PAPI light units

Series circuit

CCR → primary cable → primary connector → isolating transformer / approved PAPI interface → secondary connection → light unit

An apparently weak PAPI unit may have an electrical fault upstream of the optics. Check supply, CCR, series circuit, transformer, connectors, cable, unit input, internal electronics, light source and monitoring.
Photometry vs Alignment

PAPI Photometry and Why It Differs from Alignment

Alignment testing asks

Is the unit pointing at the correct angle and direction?

Photometric testing asks

Does the unit produce the required optical performance?

Photometry verifies intensity, beam pattern, colour, red/white transition, horizontal alignment and vertical aiming. A PAPI can have correct intensity with wrong aiming, correct aiming with inadequate intensity, or both faults together, so commissioning must control both.

Key Concepts

8 Key PAPI Engineering Concepts

PAPI is a geometric system

The visible indication is produced through controlled angular relationships.

Four units are not aimed at the same angle

Each unit contributes a different portion of the visual guidance.

PAPI location affects TCH

Moving the PAPI changes the geometry of the approach path at the threshold.

Siting affects obstacle clearance

Location and aiming must protect the applicable obstacle clearance surface.

Alignment affects operational meaning

A mechanically misaligned unit can give the pilot an incorrect visual indication.

Photometry is more than intensity

Colour, beam pattern and angular transition are also important.

Electrical faults can look like optical faults

An abnormal electrical input may produce reduced or inconsistent light output.

Commissioning should end with evidence

Final aiming, electrical, photometric and siting results should be documented.

Final Engineering Perspective

PAPI looks like four light boxes. It is siting, elevation, geometry, optics, aiming, electrics, photometry and obstacle clearance working as one system.

Design correctly. Position accurately. Aim precisely. Measure objectively. Document completely.

FAQ

PAPI Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What does PAPI stand for?

Precision Approach Path Indicator.

Q2What is the purpose of PAPI?

It gives visual approach slope guidance so the pilot can judge the aircraft position relative to the desired approach path.

Q3How many lights are in a PAPI?

A conventional PAPI has four light units (FAA L-880). The abbreviated two-unit system, APAPI, is L-881.

Q4What does 4 white lights mean on PAPI?

The aircraft is significantly above the approach path.

Q5What does 3 white and 1 red mean?

The aircraft is slightly above the approach path.

Q6What does 2 white and 2 red mean?

The normal on-path indication for a four-unit PAPI.

Q7What does 1 white and 3 red mean?

The aircraft is slightly below the approach path.

Q8What does 4 red lights mean?

The aircraft is significantly below the approach path.

Q9What angle is PAPI set at?

The visual glide path is commonly around 3°, but the selected value comes from the approved design and siting requirements.

Q10Are all PAPI units aimed at 3 degrees?

No. Units are aimed at different elevations around the selected glide path, for example 2°30′, 2°50′, 3°10′ and 3°30′ for a 3° path. Exact settings follow the applicable standard and design.

Q11How far should PAPI be from the runway threshold?

There is no universal distance. Location is determined from TCH, approach angle, aircraft requirements, runway geometry, obstacle clearance and, where applicable, ILS geometry.

Q12What is PAPI TCH?

Threshold Crossing Height: the height of the lowest on-course PAPI signal above the runway threshold centreline.

Q13What colours does PAPI use?

Aviation white (upper sector) and aviation red (lower sector).

Q14Why is PAPI photometry required?

It verifies intensity, colour and beam pattern, and supports confirmation of optical performance.

Q15Can PAPI be installed on the right side of the runway?

It is normally on the left as seen from the approach, but alternative siting is allowed where the standard location is impracticable or specific operational requirements apply.

Q16What is the difference between PAPI and APAPI?

PAPI normally uses four units; APAPI is an abbreviated two-unit arrangement, so indications and aiming differ.

Q17What can cause a PAPI to fail photometry?

Optical contamination, damaged optics, light-source degradation, incorrect aiming or beam pattern, colour deviation, electrical problems, or measurement and setup errors.

Q18Is PAPI alignment the same as PAPI photometry?

No. Alignment verifies physical direction and aiming; photometry evaluates optical performance.
