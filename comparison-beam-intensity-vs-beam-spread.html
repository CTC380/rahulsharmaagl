BEAM INTENSITY VS BEAM SPREAD

Strength vs Width — beam intensity describes how strong an AGL light is in a particular direction, measured in candela, while beam spread describes how wide that beam extends over angular directions, measured in degrees. They are closely related but not the same parameter, and an AGL fixture needs both specified correctly.

02 · Definitions

What Is Beam Intensity & Beam Spread?

Beam Intensity

Beam intensity describes the luminous intensity in a particular direction. Picture an AGL fixture pointing at an approaching aircraft: the intensity value tells you how strong the emitted light is along that specific line of sight.

Example

A fixture might read 10,000 cd at 0°, 8,000 cd at +2°, and 2,000 cd at +5° — the intensity changes with direction.

Beam Spread

Beam spread describes how far the beam extends angularly. Picture the same fixture's cone of light widening or narrowing: the spread value tells you the angular width between the beam's boundaries.

Example

A beam specified with a 20° spread extends its useful light output across a 20-degree angular sector, regardless of how strong that light is at the peak.

04 · Worked Example

Same Beam, Two Different Numbers

Suppose an AGL light has a peak intensity of 10,000 cd, and its specified beam extends over 20°. These describe two different things about the same light:

Peak Intensity
10,000 cd
Strength at beam centre
Beam Spread
20°
Angular extent of the beam

The 10,000 cd figure describes intensity; the 20° figure describes angular spread. They are different parameters, and neither one can be inferred from the other.

05 · Same Intensity, Different Spread

Narrow vs Wide Beam

Consider two lights with the same peak intensity but different optical distributions:

Light A · Narrow Beam
10,000 cd peak · narrow angular coverage
Light B · Wide Beam
10,000 cd peak · wide angular coverage

Same peak intensity does not mean same beam spread. Light B distributes its 10,000 cd peak over a wider angular region than Light A.

06 · A Common Misreading

A Wide Beam Doesn't Automatically Mean More Light

A wider beam does not automatically mean higher intensity. The optical system may simply be distributing the available light over a larger angular region rather than concentrating it. The actual intensity distribution across angle must be determined photometrically — it can't be assumed from the beam's apparent width alone.

07 · AGL Context

Why AGL Fixtures Need Both Parameters

AGL fixtures are designed to produce specific directional distributions toward an approaching or taxiing aircraft. The designer needs both parameters satisfied at once, not just one:

Beam Intensity

Enough luminous intensity in the required viewing direction, so the light is visible at the necessary range and conditions.

Beam Spread

Enough angular coverage to serve the required viewing sector, so the light remains visible across the intended approach or taxi geometry.

08 · Two Planes of Spread

Horizontal and Vertical Beam Spread

Beam spread is not a single number — an AGL fixture can have a different angular width in the horizontal (azimuth) plane than in the vertical (elevation) plane, which connects directly to horizontal vs vertical photometry.

Plane Describes
Horizontal spreadAngular width in the azimuth direction
Vertical spreadAngular width in the elevation direction

An AGL fixture's horizontal and vertical beam spreads are typically specified separately, since a fixture may need a wide horizontal sector but a narrow vertical sector, or vice versa.

09 · Intensity Falls With Angle

Beam Spread and Photometric Intensity

A photometric distribution typically shows intensity decreasing gradually away from the beam centre:

Angle Intensity
−10°500 cd
−5°3,000 cd
0°10,000 cd
+5°3,000 cd
+10°500 cd

A specified intensity threshold can then be used to define a particular beam width or spread, depending on the applicable photometric definition being applied.

How the threshold is usually set: ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5340-30 typically define beam spread from the isocandela diagram, as the angle between the points where intensity has fallen to a specified fraction of the peak — commonly the 50% (half-intensity) points — not simply where the beam "looks like" it ends. In the table above, the true beam edges sit wherever the curve crosses that threshold, which will not necessarily line up with the ±5° or ±10° sample points shown. Many AGL specifications also work from main beam average intensity — the average intensity across the isocandela diagram — rather than the single peak figure used for simplicity in the examples on this page. Always confirm which definition the applicable standard and manufacturer's photometric report are using.

10 · Not the Same as Coverage

Beam Spread vs Light Coverage

Beam spread is an angular characteristic. Coverage, by contrast, considers the actual physical area or region illuminated at a particular distance and geometry. The same angular spread covers a wider physical width as distance increases, because the angle stays fixed while the geometry scales.

11 · Beam Spread vs Distance

15 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: "A wider beam always means a brighter light"
Fact

No. A wider beam spreads the available light over a larger angular region — it does not automatically increase peak intensity, and can even reduce it if the same light output is distributed more broadly.

Myth: "Two lights with the same candela rating have the same coverage"
Fact

No. Peak intensity alone says nothing about angular width. Two fixtures can share the same peak candela figure while having very different horizontal and vertical beam spreads.

Myth: "Beam spread and light coverage on the ground are the same thing"
Fact

Not quite. Beam spread is an angular characteristic of the fixture. Physical coverage on a surface depends on that angle combined with distance and geometry.

16 · Engineering Verdict

Beam Intensity vs Beam Spread

Engineering Verdict

Beam intensity and beam spread are not interchangeable quantities. The fundamental difference is what each one describes — how strong the light is versus how wide it reaches.

Beam intensity describes the strength of light an AGL fixture emits in a given direction, expressed in candela. Beam spread describes how far that beam extends angularly, expressed in degrees. A complete beam specification always needs both, since neither one can be inferred from the other.

IntensityHow strong the light is in a direction + measured in cd + varies by angle.
SpreadHow wide the beam extends + measured in ° + varies horizontally and vertically.

For AGL: specify both — sufficient intensity in the required viewing directions, and the correct horizontal/vertical angular coverage to serve the intended sector.

17 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the simplest way to remember the difference?

Intensity → STRENGTH (how strong the light is in a direction). Spread → WIDTH (how wide the beam extends angularly).

Can two lights have the same intensity but different spread?

Yes. Two fixtures can share an identical peak intensity while distributing that light over very different angular widths — one narrow, one wide.

Does a wider beam mean a lower peak intensity?

Not necessarily by definition, but in practice, distributing a given light output over a wider angular region often reduces the peak intensity compared to concentrating it narrowly. The actual figures must be confirmed photometrically.

Why does AGL specify horizontal and vertical spread separately?

Because the required angular coverage in the azimuth plane and the elevation plane usually serve different operational purposes, so an AGL fixture's beam can be wide in one plane and narrow in the other.

Is beam spread the same as light coverage on the runway?

No. Beam spread is the fixture's angular characteristic. Coverage on a surface also depends on distance and geometry, since the same angular spread covers a larger physical width further from the fixture.

How does beam intensity relate to candela vs lux?

Beam intensity is expressed in candela, the same source-side quantity used in the candela vs lux comparison. Once that beam reaches a surface at some distance, the resulting light level there is illuminance, in lux.

Final Engineering Note

Do not treat a candela figure and a beam-spread figure as substitutes for each other in a project specification. Confirm both are stated, and for the correct viewing directions and planes.

Beam intensity is not beam spread — one describes how strong the light is, the other describes how far it reaches angularly.
