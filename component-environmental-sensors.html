Airfield Environmental Sensors

The network of visibility, wind, and weather sensors that feed real-time data to air traffic control, pilots, and ALCMS — enabling safe operations and informed intensity decisions for approach lighting and runway visual range reporting.

Overview

What Are They?

Airfield environmental sensors are the suite of instruments that measure meteorological conditions critical to aviation safety — visibility (RVR – Runway Visual Range), wind speed and direction, temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, and precipitation. These sensors are typically installed alongside runways, at thresholds, and at mid‑points, and they feed data to the control tower, MET office, and increasingly to the ALCMS for automated lighting intensity adjustments.

RVR (Runway Visual Range) is a derived value that combines visibility, background luminance, and runway light intensity to estimate the distance a pilot can see runway markings or lights. It is measured using forward-scatter or transmissometer sensors installed at the touchdown, mid, and stop‑end points of each runway.

Wind sensors (anemometers and wind vanes) provide real-time wind speed, gust, and direction, essential for takeoff and landing decisions. Wind data is also used to calculate crosswind and tailwind components.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The safety margins built into aviation operations depend on knowing the actual visibility and wind conditions at the runway right now, not an estimate from a tower window or a forecast from an hour ago. Environmental sensors exist to replace that kind of subjective or stale judgement with a continuous, instrumented measurement pilots, ATC, and airfield systems can rely on. RVR sensors give a specific, defensible distance figure for how far a pilot can actually expect to see runway lights or markings — the number that determines whether an approach or takeoff is permitted to proceed under a given category of operation. Wind sensors give the crosswind and tailwind components that decide which runway is safe to use and whether conditions remain within an aircraft's operating limits. Feeding this same data into the ALCMS closes a further gap: rather than running the runway lighting at a fixed default intensity, the system can match light output to the visibility that has actually been measured, so pilots see the intensity level appropriate to conditions rather than one set for the worst case or guessed by an operator.

Application

Where Are They Used?

Environmental sensors are strategically placed across the airfield to capture representative conditions:

  • RVR Sensors (Transmissometers / Forward Scatter): Installed at three positions along each runway — touchdown zone (typically 300m from threshold), mid-point, and stop end — to provide RVR values for the full runway.
  • Wind Sensors: Typically mounted on masts near the runway thresholds (both ends) and at mid-field, to capture surface wind conditions.
  • Automatic Weather Observing Systems (AWOS): Integrated sensor suites that include temperature, humidity, dew point, barometric pressure, and precipitation detection.
  • Ceilometers: Measure cloud base height, important for approach minima.
  • Present Weather Sensors: Detect precipitation type (rain, snow, drizzle) and intensity.

Data from these sensors is distributed to ATC, the aerodrome meteorological office, and, in modern systems, directly to the ALCMS for automated intensity step selection based on RVR thresholds.

Placement

Installation & Location

RVR transmissometers and forward-scatter sensors are mounted on short masts a set offset from the runway centreline at each of the touchdown, mid-point, and stop-end positions, sited so the sensor's optical path is clear of any obstruction and positioned to keep the equipment within the runway strip's obstacle-limitation surface. Wind masts are erected near each runway threshold and at mid-field, at a height and distance from the runway that avoids local turbulence from nearby structures or terrain skewing the reading, with the sensor itself mounted at the standard reference height above ground level used for the airfield's wind reporting. All sensor types are fed by cable — power and signal — run in duct or direct-buried conduit back to a local remote terminal unit or straight to the AWOS processing equipment, with the cable route planned to avoid the runway strip's graded and load-bearing areas. Because these sensors report values pilots and ATC act on directly, foundation and mast installation is carried out under a survey that fixes each sensor's exact position and height, since even a modest siting error can bias the readings the whole safety margin depends on.

Function

How Do They Work?

Each sensor type uses a distinct measurement principle:

  • Transmissometer: Measures the extinction of a light beam over a known baseline (typically 75m). The receiver measures the transmitted light, and the instrument calculates the Meteorological Optical Range (MOR). RVR is then derived using background luminance and runway light intensity.
  • Forward Scatter Sensor: Measures the amount of light scattered by particles in a small sample volume. It is more compact than a transmissometer and is widely used as an alternative.
  • Anemometer: Uses a three‑cup or ultrasonic design to measure wind speed, and a vane or sonic array for direction. Ultrasonic anemometers are increasingly preferred for their lack of moving parts.
  • Temperature / Humidity Sensors: Typically platinum resistance thermometers (PRT) and capacitive humidity sensors housed in a radiation shield.
  • Ceilometer: Uses a laser or infrared beam to detect cloud base height by measuring the time of flight of backscattered light.

All sensors are connected to a data acquisition system (often an AWOS) that processes, formats, and transmits data to displays and networked systems. Data is typically updated once per minute or faster.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Individual environmental sensors — transmissometers, forward-scatter sensors, anemometers, temperature/humidity probes, ceilometers — each measure a single physical quantity and have no interconnection with one another; the control architecture lives in the data acquisition and processing layer above them. Each sensor's analogue or digital output is polled by a local data acquisition unit, which timestamps and validates the reading (flagging out-of-range or stuck values) before forwarding it to the central AWOS processor. The AWOS processor combines readings from multiple sensor sites, applies the required algorithms — deriving RVR from MOR and background luminance, for instance — and pushes the resulting product to ATC and pilot-facing displays, typically refreshing at least once a minute; if a sensor drops out or its data fails validation, the processor flags that specific parameter as unavailable rather than silently substituting a stale or estimated value.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each sensor type within an environmental sensor suite follows construction appropriate to its measurement principle, but shares common design features: a weatherproof sensor head or optical assembly, typically aluminium or corrosion-resistant composite, rated to IP66/IP67, mounted on a rigid pole or mast at a height standardised for that measurement type. Optical sensors such as RVR and visibility instruments house their transmitter and receiver components in sealed enclosures with heated windows to prevent condensation or icing from obscuring the measurement path, while wind and temperature sensors use sealed bearing assemblies or radiation shields appropriate to their mechanism. All sensor heads connect via shielded, weather-rated cable to a common signal processing or data concentrator unit, often housed in a nearby equipment cabinet, which aggregates readings from the full sensor suite before forwarding them to the ALCMS or MET office over the site communication network. Mounting structures are corrosion-resistant steel or aluminium poles set in concrete foundations, positioned to keep each sensor clear of obstructions or turbulence sources that could bias its readings, and all outdoor enclosures and cabling are built to withstand continuous exposure to airfield weather extremes.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Sensor Contamination (Dirt, Ice, Birds)

Optical sensors (transmissometers, forward scatter, ceilometers) are sensitive to lens soiling from dust, bird droppings, or ice accretion. Regular cleaning and anti‑icing systems are essential to maintain accuracy.

Calibration Drift

All sensors require periodic calibration against reference standards. Drift can cause RVR values to be over‑ or under‑estimated, affecting operational minima decisions.

Communication / Network Failures

Sensor data is transmitted over field networks. Cable cuts, radio interference, or power supply issues can cause data loss, requiring redundancy (dual sensors, alternate paths).

Lightning / EMI Damage

Sensors mounted on masts are vulnerable to lightning strikes and electromagnetic interference. Proper grounding and surge protection are critical to prevent hardware damage.

Incorrect Siting

Siting errors (e.g., wind sensors too close to buildings, or RVR sensors not aligned with the runway centreline) can produce unrepresentative data. ICAO and WMO siting guidelines must be followed.

Power Supply Interruptions

Remote sensors often rely on local power (mains or solar). Battery failure or UPS issues can take sensors offline, reducing the availability of critical data.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

RVR Reading Erratic or Suspiciously Low
Likely Cause
Transmissometer or forward-scatter lens contaminated with dust, insects, or condensation, or ice accretion on the optical path.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the affected sensor's output against the other RVR positions on the runway; inspect and clean the transmit/receive lenses; check heater and anti-icing element operation.
Corrective Action
Clean the optics, confirm heaters are functioning, and re-verify against a reference standard; recalibrate if the offset persists after cleaning.
Wind Speed / Direction Reading Frozen or Implausible
Likely Cause
Ice or debris jamming a mechanical anemometer/vane, or a fouled transducer path on an ultrasonic sensor.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for a stuck or constant reading regardless of actual conditions; visually inspect the sensor head for ice, debris, or insect nests; compare against an adjacent wind mast if available.
Corrective Action
Clear ice or debris, confirm sensor heater operation where fitted, and replace the sensor head if bearings or transducers are damaged.
Sensor Data Not Reaching ATC / ALCMS
Likely Cause
Broken or degraded communication link (cable fault, radio interference, or network switch/converter failure) between the sensor and the receiving system.
Diagnostic Steps
Check local sensor status/output directly at the unit to confirm it is measuring correctly; trace the RS-232/RS-485/Ethernet link for loose connectors, damaged cable, or a failed converter; check network switch and router status.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted cable, connector, or converter; fail over to the redundant sensor or path where fitted while repairs are completed.
Reading Drifts Out of Tolerance Over Time
Likely Cause
Normal calibration drift in the sensing element, or a slow change in siting conditions (new obstruction, vegetation growth) skewing the reading.
Diagnostic Steps
Cross-check the sensor against a calibrated reference instrument; review site photos or recent survey notes for new obstructions, vegetation, or ground cover changes near the sensor.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the sensor to bring it back within tolerance, or clear/relocate per ICAO and WMO siting guidance if the drift is siting-related.
Sensor Offline After Storm Activity
Likely Cause
Lightning strike or induced surge damage to the sensor electronics, or a tripped/failed surge protection device.
Diagnostic Steps
Check surge protection devices and grounding continuity at the mast; inspect the sensor and its power supply for visible damage; attempt a local power-cycle and re-test.
Corrective Action
Replace tripped or damaged surge protection devices, verify grounding resistance meets spec, and replace the sensor electronics if damage is confirmed.
Remote / Solar-Powered Sensor Intermittently Drops Out
Likely Cause
Degraded battery capacity, a fouled solar panel, or a marginal charge controller unable to sustain the sensor overnight or in low-light conditions.
Diagnostic Steps
Check battery voltage and charge/discharge behaviour over a full day-night cycle; inspect the solar panel for dirt, shading, or physical damage; verify charge controller operation.
Corrective Action
Clean the solar panel, replace a degraded battery, and confirm the charge controller is regulating correctly before returning the sensor to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Optical Path Contamination
High Severity
Root Cause
Dust, insects, or condensation building up on the transmissometer or forward-scatter sensor's optical windows.
Effect
RVR reading erratic or suspiciously low, potentially triggering low-visibility procedures that aren't actually warranted.
Detection Method
Visual inspection and cleaning of optical windows during scheduled maintenance, and cross-check against a second RVR sensor where available.
Anemometer Mechanical Seizure
High Severity
Root Cause
Ice accumulation, insect nesting, or bearing wear seizing the wind speed/direction sensor's moving parts.
Effect
Wind speed or direction reading frozen or implausible, removing a key data input from wind-dependent operational decisions.
Detection Method
Functional check of anemometer rotation during scheduled maintenance, and comparison against a nearby reference sensor.
Communication Loss to ATC / ALCMS
High Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
Sensor data not reaching ATC or the ALCMS, leaving a gap in the operational picture until communication is restored.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the receiving system, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Calibration Drift Over Time
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing of the sensing element, or accumulated environmental exposure shifting the sensor's reference away from factory calibration.
Effect
Reading drifts out of tolerance over time, producing data that looks plausible but is quietly inaccurate.
Detection Method
Scheduled calibration verification against a certified reference instrument, at the interval specified by the manufacturer.
Storm-Related Sensor Outage
High Severity
Root Cause
Lightning strike damage, wind-driven debris impact, or power/communication infrastructure damage during severe weather.
Effect
Sensor offline after storm activity, at precisely the time its data may be most operationally relevant.
Detection Method
Post-storm functional check of all field sensors, and priority dispatch to restore any sensor found offline.
Remote Power Supply Instability
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Battery degradation or a fouled solar panel reducing available power at a remote, off-grid sensor location.
Effect
Remote or solar-powered sensor intermittently drops out, especially during extended low-light periods.
Detection Method
Battery/solar charge trend monitoring, and inspection of the solar panel and battery during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Power Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the sensor's local power supply before opening its enclosure or disconnecting field wiring, particularly for mains-powered or remotely-fed units.

Working at Height
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate fall protection and follow site procedures when servicing mast- or pole-mounted sensors such as anemometers or visibility sensors.

Airside Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations before taking a sensor out of service or working near the movement area, since ATC may rely on live wind, visibility, or ceiling data for operational decisions.

Weather Awareness
Personnel Safety

Avoid servicing elevated or exposed sensors during high wind, lightning, or icing conditions; check the forecast and stand down work if conditions deteriorate.

Lock Out Moving Parts
Personnel Safety

Secure or lock rotating components such as anemometer cups before working near them, and be aware that a seized bearing can release suddenly once cleared.

Verify Calibration After Service
Compliance

Check the sensor reading against a reference standard or a known local condition after cleaning, replacement, or calibration before returning it to service.

Confirm Data Reaches ATC/ALCMS
Compliance

After maintenance, confirm the sensor's data is being received correctly and within expected tolerance at ATC and/or the ALCMS before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Insect Ingress Fouls Optical Sensors Before They Fail Outright
Maintenance

A visibility or RVR sensor reading that drifts gradually rather than failing cleanly is often insects or debris partially obstructing the optical path rather than an electronic fault. Include a physical lens and aperture inspection in routine maintenance, not just an electrical function check.

Direct Sun Angle Can Saturate a Sensor Briefly Each Day
Diagnostics

A sensor that reports an anomalous reading at roughly the same time every clear day is frequently reacting to a specific sun angle relative to its optical axis rather than an intermittent hardware fault. Check whether the anomaly correlates with time of day and season before troubleshooting the unit itself.

Heater Elements Need Verification, Not Just Presence
Electrical

A sensor's anti-icing or condensation heater can fail while the sensor otherwise continues to report plausible-looking data, until icing or condensation eventually degrades accuracy. Verify heater function directly during cold-weather checks rather than inferring it from normal-looking sensor output.

Cross-Check Against a Nearby Reference Sensor When Available
Verification

Where a second environmental sensor covers a similar or overlapping area, a reading that diverges from that reference over time is a stronger signal of calibration drift than a single sensor's trend on its own. Use available redundancy for cross-checking, not just for failover.

Siting Relative to Obstructions Affects Wind Readings Long-Term
Siting

New construction, vegetation growth, or even a parked large aircraft near an anemometer can introduce turbulence that skews wind readings in a way that looks like sensor drift. Periodically reassess the site's exposure against current siting criteria, not only at initial installation.

Calibrate Against Traceable References on a Fixed Schedule
Compliance

Sensor drift is often too gradual to notice from day-to-day readings alone, which is why a fixed calibration schedule against a traceable reference matters more than reacting only when a reading looks obviously wrong.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about airfield environmental sensors: RVR, wind, temperature, humidity and ceilometers, accuracy, siting, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What are airfield environmental sensors?

They are instruments that continuously measure runway and airfield conditions such as visibility (RVR), wind, temperature, humidity and cloud base. They replace subjective or stale judgement with instrumented measurements that pilots, ATC and airfield systems can rely on.

Q2What does an RVR sensor measure?

Runway Visual Range, giving a specific, defensible distance figure for how far a pilot can see along the runway. Measurement range is 50–2,000 m (or more for CAT III), with accuracy of ±10% or ±20 m, whichever is greater, over 100–1,500 m.

Q3What other measurements are typical?

Wind speed of 0–99 m/s (±0.2 m/s) with direction accuracy of ±2° (ultrasonic) or ±3° (mechanical), temperature from −40 °C to +60 °C (±0.2 °C), humidity (±2% RH up to 90%) and ceilometer cloud base height up to 7,500 m.

Q4Where are the sensors installed?

RVR transmissometers and forward-scatter sensors sit on short masts at a set offset from the runway centreline at the touchdown, mid-point and stop-end positions, with a clear optical path and within the obstacle-limitation surface. Wind masts go near each threshold and at mid-field.

Q5How often do sensors update?

Typically every minute for RVR and every 10 seconds for wind gust, over RS-232, RS-485 or Ethernet (Modbus, SDI-12 or proprietary interfaces).

Q6How are environmental sensors powered?

From 230 V AC or 12/24 V DC, with solar options for remote sensors and battery or solar-charged backup recommended at remote or unmanned sites. Signal cable is shielded instrumentation cable, and surge suppression on power and signal lines is required.

Q7Which standards apply to environmental sensors?

The main references are ICAO Annex 3, FAA AC 150/5220-17 (RVR systems), FAA AC 150/5220-16 (AWOS), the WMO CIMO Guide and DGCA CAR Section 4 for Indian aerodromes. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q8How often should environmental sensors be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for optics and lens cleaning, semi-annual for communication link checks, and annual for RVR calibration, wind sensor calibration, temperature and humidity checks and ceilometer function tests. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9Why is my RVR reading erratic or suspiciously low?

Transmissometer or forward-scatter lenses contaminated with dust, insects or condensation, or ice on the optical path. Compare with other RVR positions, clean the lenses, check heater and anti-icing operation and recalibrate if the offset persists.

Q10Why is a wind reading frozen or implausible?

Ice or debris is jamming a mechanical anemometer or vane, or fouling the transducer path on an ultrasonic sensor. Look for a constant reading regardless of conditions, inspect the sensor head for ice, debris or insect nests and compare with an adjacent wind mast.
