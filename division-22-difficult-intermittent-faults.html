Difficult / Intermittent Faults Troubleshooting

Diagnostic methodology for the AGL faults that will not sit still — conditions correlated with night, rain, dry weather, warm-up, heat, cold, CCR step, and switching, faults that clear on restart or cannot be reproduced at all, intermittent faults isolated to a connector, cable, transformer, fixture, CCR, or communication link, and the compound cases of multiple simultaneous intermittent faults, hidden underground faults, and chronic recurring faults that have outlasted several prior repairs. Each topic follows the full engineering diagnostic record: symptom → observation → measurement → decision → fault location → root cause → verification → corrective action → preventive action.

DIF-526 Fault Appears Only at Night

Symptom — A reported fault — a dark fixture, flicker, wrong intensity, or an earth-fault trip — is present only during night-time energised operation and cannot be reproduced during daytime testing or with the circuit run at reduced load in daylight.

Equipment / System

Any AGL circuit or component under its actual night-time energised operating condition, as distinct from a daytime de-energised or low-load bench check.

Possible Causes
HighThermal cycling from a full day of cooling followed by night-time full-current operation opens up a marginal connector or cable joint that reads fine cold and unloaded
HighThe fault is genuinely load- or voltage-dependent and only manifests at the circuit's actual night-time series current, which a daytime low-load or open-circuit check does not reproduce
MedA photocell- or ALCMS-scheduled step or circuit only energises at night, so a fault on that specific step or circuit is invisible to any daytime test that never exercises it
MedOvernight condensation forms inside a base can or connector as ambient temperature drops after sunset, creating a leakage path that dries out and disappears by the following daytime inspection
LowThe defect is cosmetic to a daytime observer — a dim fixture, subtle colour shift, or slight flicker — that is only perceptible against a dark background at night
LowWildlife or vehicle activity that only occurs after dark is mechanically disturbing a connector or cable at a specific location
Required Tools
CCR output/temperature datalogger for an overnight spanNight inspection vehicle and portable floodlightThermal imaging cameraTrue-RMS multimeter with min/max holdWeather/temperature log for the reporting period
Safety Precautions
  • Coordinate all night airside access through ATC/apron control before approaching any fixture on an active movement area.
  • Treat the circuit as energised at full night-time current during any inspection intended to catch the fault live; LOTO before touching any suspect connection once located.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationCross-check every reported occurrence against the night flight-check log and any available CCR trend history to confirm the fault is genuinely absent during daytime energised checks.
  2. MeasurementFit a temporary datalogger on the affected circuit's CCR output and, if available, ambient temperature, spanning at least one full day-to-night cycle.
  3. MeasurementSchedule a night inspection timed to the specific reported condition (time of night, intensity step, weather) rather than a generic walk-through.
  4. DecisionFault correlates with the overnight temperature drop → treat as thermal/condensation-related and proceed under DIF-531 or DIF-538. Fault correlates only with actual night-time load current → treat as load-dependent and proceed under the relevant component division.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific fixture, connector, or circuit segment active at the time the fault was captured.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by reproducing the fault under controlled night conditions matching the original report before closing out the ticket.
Test Points
CCR output current/voltage trend across a full day-night cycle
Ambient temperature and humidity log for the reporting period
Connector contact resistance measured both cold (day) and after night-time thermal cycling
Night visual inspection log at the exact reported time and intensity step
Root Cause Verification

Confirm the fault does not recur over at least two further night-time operating cycles under conditions similar to the original report, and log the correlating condition (temperature swing, specific step, or load current) in the maintenance record.

Corrective Action
  • Re-terminate or re-torque a connector shown to open up under night-time thermal cycling.
  • Reseal a base can or connector found to admit condensation overnight.
  • Adjust or correct the ALCMS/photocell schedule if a specific night-only step was never being exercised or tested.
Preventive Action
  • Include a night-time energised check alongside the standard daytime PM cycle rather than relying on daytime testing alone.
  • Log ambient temperature alongside CCR trend data as standard practice to make day/night correlation possible.
  • Brief flight-check and night-inspection staff to record time, intensity step, and weather whenever an intermittent fault is observed.

DIF-527 Fault Appears Only During Rain

Symptom — A fault — a dark or flickering fixture, an earth-fault trip, or reduced insulation resistance — is reported only during or shortly after rainfall and cannot be reproduced during dry-weather testing.

Equipment / System

Any below-grade or in-pavement AGL component and its cable/joint network exposed to surface or groundwater ingress — base cans, L-823 connectors, cable joints, and isolation transformers.

Possible Causes
HighMoisture ingress through a degraded gasket, cracked lens, or improperly seated L-823 connector creating a leakage path that only closes when water is present
HighA cracked or porous cable jacket admitting water at a joint or splice, dropping insulation resistance below the CCR's earth-fault threshold only while wet
MedRising groundwater or surface water pooling in an under-rated or poorly drained base can during heavy rain
MedA marginal insulation fault that is present but above the trip threshold when dry, and drops below it once moisture reduces the resistance further
LowSurface water bridging a damaged duct or handhole allowing water to track along the cable route to a distant weak point
LowA grounding/earthing conductor connection that only completes a stray path when the surrounding soil is saturated
Required Tools
1000V/2500V insulation resistance tester (megger)CCR earth-fault trend logMoisture detection meterCable route and joint location drawingWeatherproof torch and mirror for base-can inspection
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before opening any base can, particularly during or after rain when water may be present around energised connections.
  • Treat a rain-correlated earth-fault trip as a genuine hazard indicator, not a nuisance trip, until insulation resistance is confirmed acceptable.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationConfirm from the CCR trip log and maintenance history that the fault correlates specifically with rainfall events rather than temperature or load.
  2. MeasurementMegger the affected circuit or segment as soon as practical after a rain event, before the base cans have had time to dry out, and compare against the dry-weather baseline.
  3. MeasurementPhysically inspect base cans, connectors, and accessible joints along the affected run for standing water, a failed gasket, or a cracked lens.
  4. DecisionInsulation resistance recovers once dry and drops again after rain → confirmed moisture-related fault; isolate to the wettest base can or joint. Resistance stays low regardless of weather → treat as a standing insulation fault, not rain-dependent, and route to Division 25.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific base can, connector, or cable joint showing the greatest insulation resistance drop when wet.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the water entry point by physical inspection rather than assuming the location of the lowest reading is necessarily the entry point, since water can track along ducting.
Test Points
Insulation resistance wet vs. dry, segment by segment
Base can and connector condition (gasket, lens, seating)
Duct/handhole condition along the suspected route
CCR earth-fault trip log correlated against local rainfall records
Root Cause Verification

Re-megger the repaired segment during or immediately after the next rain event and confirm the reading holds above the acceptable threshold; do not close out on a dry-weather reading alone.

Corrective Action
  • Replace a failed gasket, cracked lens, or degraded L-823 connector seal.
  • Repair or replace the cable segment or joint found to be admitting water.
  • Improve drainage at a base can shown to be collecting standing water.
Preventive Action
  • Schedule insulation resistance testing during or immediately after the wet season rather than relying solely on dry-weather PM readings.
  • Inspect and replace ageing connector gaskets and lens seals proactively ahead of the monsoon/wet season.
  • Verify base-can drainage as a standard PM item, particularly on low-lying sections of the airfield.
Related References

SOP: SOP-DIF-02 (Weather-Correlated Insulation Fault Procedure), SOP-CCR-04 (CCR LOTO Procedure)

Standards: IEC 60364 (insulation testing), ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, IS 12290

DIF-528 Fault Appears Only During Dry Weather

Symptom — A fault is reported only during dry periods and clears or does not recur once rain returns — the reverse pattern of a typical moisture fault — most often surfacing as an earthing/grounding-related trip, a dust-related dimming, or a heat-related step fault.

Equipment / System

Airfield earthing/grounding system, exposed connectors and lenses subject to dust and contamination, and any component whose behaviour is temperature-linked to dry-season heat.

Possible Causes
HighRising earth/ground resistance in dry, low-moisture soil pushing the airfield earthing system above its acceptable threshold, only recovering once the soil is wet again
HighDust and airborne contamination bridging a marginal connector or terminal that a damp environment would otherwise keep from arcing
MedDry-season ambient temperatures pushing equipment into a heat-related fault mode covered separately under DIF-530
MedStatic discharge from very low humidity affecting sensitive CCR control cards or an ALCMS interface
LowDust accumulation on lenses reducing apparent luminance to the point of a reported dim/failure complaint that clears once rain washes the lenses
LowSeasonal ground movement/shrinkage in dry, expansive soils placing mechanical stress on a duct or cable route
Required Tools
Earth resistance tester (fall-of-potential or clamp-on)Insulation resistance tester (megger)Luminance/photometric meterThermal imaging cameraSoil moisture reference log
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before accessing any earthing conductor or connection for testing.
  • Treat an elevated earth resistance reading as a genuine safety and lightning-protection concern, not merely a seasonal nuisance, until corrected.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationConfirm the fault correlates specifically with dry periods and not with a coincidental rise in ambient temperature or CCR loading.
  2. MeasurementTest earth/ground resistance at the affected location and compare against both the design threshold and the same point's wet-season reading.
  3. MeasurementInspect the affected fixture, connector, or terminal for dust, contamination, or a visibly degraded lens.
  4. DecisionEarth resistance rises well above threshold in dry soil → confirmed seasonal earthing issue; consider supplementary grounding. Reading stays within tolerance → investigate contamination or heat-related causes instead.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific earth electrode, terminal, or lens/connector responsible for the seasonal variation.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrected reading holds through at least one further dry spell before closing out.
Test Points
Earth/ground resistance, dry-season vs. wet-season baseline
Lens/connector contamination level
CCR control card condition where static discharge is suspected
Soil moisture and ambient temperature log for the reporting period
Root Cause Verification

Re-test earth resistance and/or luminance at the same location during the next dry spell and confirm the corrected value holds within the acceptable range.

Corrective Action
  • Install supplementary earth electrodes or a chemical earthing treatment where dry-season resistance consistently exceeds the design threshold.
  • Clean lenses and connectors as part of a dry-season-specific PM pass.
  • Add anti-static/surge protection at a control card shown to be affected by low-humidity static discharge.
Preventive Action
  • Test earth resistance at both the height of the wet season and the height of the dry season to capture the full seasonal range, not a single annual reading.
  • Increase lens-cleaning frequency during known dusty/dry periods.
  • Track dry-season earth resistance trends year over year to catch a worsening pattern before it becomes a lightning-protection risk.
Related References

SOP: SOP-DIF-03 (Seasonal Earthing Verification Procedure)

Standards: IS 3043 (earthing), ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, IEEE 142

DIF-529 Fault Appears After Warm-Up

Symptom — Equipment operates correctly for a period after being switched on cold, then develops a fault — drift in output, a dropped fixture, a nuisance trip — once it has reached normal operating temperature.

Equipment / System

CCR control electronics, thyristor/SCR banks, and any AGL component whose parameters are known to drift with self-heating during sustained operation.

Possible Causes
HighThermal drift of a CCR control card, feedback sensor, or thyristor firing circuit once it reaches its normal operating temperature
HighA marginal connection whose contact resistance rises as the surrounding enclosure heats up, eventually opening under thermal expansion
MedA cooling fan or ventilation path in the CCR cabinet that is inadequate under sustained full-current operation, allowing internal temperature to climb past a component's rated limit
MedAn electronic component (capacitor, semiconductor) nearing end of life that only fails once self-heating pushes it past its degraded tolerance
LowA calibration or feedback loop that has a temperature-dependent offset not accounted for in the control algorithm
LowA cable or transformer insulation weak point that only breaks down once conductor temperature rises under load
Required Tools
Thermal imaging cameraTrue-RMS multimeter with min/max holdCCR cabinet temperature dataloggerOscilloscope for control-signal drift analysisManufacturer's thermal derating specification
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before any internal cabinet inspection; allow the cabinet to cool if opening it live is not required for the specific test.
  • Be alert to genuinely hot internal components during a live thermal inspection and avoid contact.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationEstablish the typical elapsed time or duration of operation before the fault appears, and whether it is consistent across occurrences.
  2. MeasurementLog CCR cabinet and key component temperatures alongside output parameters from cold start through to the point the fault appears.
  3. MeasurementUse thermal imaging at the point the fault manifests to identify any component running hotter than its neighbours or above its rated temperature.
  4. DecisionA specific component is identified as running abnormally hot → isolate to that component for replacement. Temperatures are within normal range but drift still occurs → treat as a calibration/feedback issue rather than a thermal failure.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific control card, connection, or component whose behaviour changes with temperature.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by artificially warming the isolated component (within safe limits) on the bench and reproducing the drift or fault.
Test Points
CCR cabinet internal temperature from cold start to fault onset
Thermal image of control cards and power components at fault onset
Cooling fan/ventilation operation under sustained load
Control signal drift vs. temperature on the oscilloscope
Root Cause Verification

Run the corrected equipment through a full sustained-operation cycle matching or exceeding the original time-to-fault and confirm no drift or fault recurs.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the identified drifting or degraded component (control card, capacitor, connector).
  • Improve CCR cabinet ventilation or cooling if internal temperatures are found to exceed manufacturer limits.
  • Recalibrate a feedback loop found to have a temperature-dependent offset.
Preventive Action
  • Include a sustained-operation thermal check in the periodic PM cycle rather than testing only at cold start.
  • Trend CCR cabinet temperatures over time to catch a developing cooling deficiency early.
  • Replace ageing electronic components proactively once they approach their rated service life, rather than waiting for a thermal-onset failure.

DIF-530 Fault Appears When Equipment Is Hot

Symptom — A fault appears specifically once equipment reaches an elevated operating or ambient temperature — during peak daytime heat, after extended full-load running, or in a poorly ventilated enclosure — and clears once the equipment cools.

Equipment / System

CCR cabinets, isolation transformers, and any AGL component subject to a temperature-dependent thermal cutout, derating, or component failure mode.

Possible Causes
HighA thermal protection cutout (CCR or transformer) tripping once internal temperature exceeds its set point under sustained high load or high ambient conditions
HighContact resistance at a connector or terminal increasing with heat until it crosses into an open-circuit or high-resistance fault state
MedA transformer or cable operating above its thermal rating for the ambient conditions, with insulation resistance dropping as temperature rises
MedInadequate cabinet ventilation, a blocked filter, or a failed cooling fan allowing internal temperature to climb beyond design limits
LowA semiconductor device (thyristor, diode) exhibiting a known temperature-dependent failure mode as it approaches its rated junction temperature
LowExternal heat sources — direct sun on a cabinet, proximity to other hot equipment — pushing ambient temperature at the enclosure beyond the design envelope
Required Tools
Thermal imaging cameraCabinet/component temperature dataloggerTrue-RMS multimeterManufacturer's thermal cutout and derating specificationInsulation resistance tester (megger)
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before touching any component suspected of being at an elevated temperature; allow time to cool if practical.
  • Do not defeat or bypass a thermal protection device to keep equipment running — investigate and correct the underlying cause instead.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationConfirm the fault correlates with elevated ambient temperature, extended run time under full load, or both, using available trend data.
  2. MeasurementLog cabinet/component temperature against time and load, capturing the point at which the fault occurs.
  3. MeasurementCheck ventilation, filters, and cooling fan operation for the affected enclosure.
  4. DecisionA thermal cutout is confirmed tripping at its set point → investigate the cooling deficiency or load condition causing the over-temperature. No cutout involved, but a connection or component still fails when hot → isolate to that specific component.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific cutout, connection, or component whose behaviour changes at elevated temperature.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by reproducing the fault under a controlled heat-soak test matching the original conditions.
Test Points
Cabinet/component temperature vs. time under sustained full load
Cooling fan and filter condition
Thermal cutout set point vs. actual trip temperature
Insulation resistance at elevated operating temperature vs. cold baseline
Root Cause Verification

Run the corrected equipment through a full heat-soak cycle under conditions equal to or more severe than the original fault conditions and confirm no recurrence.

Corrective Action
  • Clear a blocked filter or replace a failed cooling fan.
  • Replace a connector or component confirmed to fail specifically at elevated temperature.
  • Relocate or shield equipment exposed to an avoidable external heat source.
Preventive Action
  • Include a summer/peak-heat thermal check in the seasonal PM cycle.
  • Trend cabinet temperatures against ambient conditions to catch a developing cooling deficiency before it causes a trip.
  • Verify thermal cutout set points against manufacturer specification during periodic calibration checks.
Related References

SOP: SOP-DIF-05 (Elevated-Temperature Fault Diagnostic Procedure)

Standards: FAA AC 150/5345-10, IS 12290

DIF-531 Fault Appears When Equipment Is Cold

Symptom — A fault — a dark fixture, a nuisance trip, or a flicker — appears specifically during cold starts, early-morning low-temperature operation, or in winter conditions, and clears once the equipment warms up or ambient temperature rises.

Equipment / System

Any AGL component subject to cold-related contraction, condensation, or low-temperature material and electronic behaviour — connectors, cable jackets, isolation transformers, and CCR electronics.

Possible Causes
HighThermal contraction of a connector or terminal at low temperature loosening a contact that was adequately seated when warm
HighCondensation forming inside a base can or cabinet as temperature drops overnight, then freezing or creating a leakage path at cold start
MedA cable jacket or connector seal becoming brittle and cracking at low temperature, admitting moisture that would not otherwise enter
MedElectronic components (capacitors, control cards) exhibiting known low-temperature parameter drift or start-up instability
LowIce or frost accumulation on a lens or photocell affecting apparent output or triggering activation logic
LowA lubricant or grease in a mechanical connector stiffening at low temperature and preventing full seating
Required Tools
True-RMS multimeter with min/max holdThermal imaging cameraInsulation resistance tester (megger)Ambient temperature log for the reporting periodManufacturer's low-temperature operating specification
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before opening any base can or cabinet, particularly where ice or frost is present, since moisture and cold both increase shock risk.
  • Handle cold, brittle cable jackets and seals carefully during inspection to avoid causing additional damage.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationConfirm the fault correlates specifically with low ambient temperature or cold-start conditions rather than a coincidental factor such as time of day.
  2. MeasurementInspect suspect connectors and terminals for looseness at low temperature, comparing torque/seating against the same point once warmed.
  3. MeasurementMegger the affected circuit at cold-start conditions and again once the circuit and ambient have warmed, to isolate a condensation- or contraction-related insulation drop.
  4. DecisionFault clears once equipment warms and connections re-seat → confirmed cold-contraction issue; re-terminate affected connectors. Fault persists regardless of temperature once triggered → treat as a separate standing fault rather than cold-related.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific connector, seal, or electronic component showing cold-dependent behaviour.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by reproducing the fault under controlled cold-start conditions matching the original report.
Test Points
Connector torque/seating, cold vs. warmed
Insulation resistance, cold-start vs. warmed
Condensation/frost presence inside base cans and cabinets
CCR control card behaviour through a cold-start cycle
Root Cause Verification

Confirm the fault does not recur through at least two further cold-start cycles under conditions similar to or colder than the original report.

Corrective Action
  • Re-terminate and properly torque connectors shown to loosen under cold contraction.
  • Replace a cracked or brittle cable jacket or connector seal.
  • Add or improve base-can/cabinet insulation or heating where condensation and freezing are a recurring issue.
Preventive Action
  • Include a cold-start energised check in the winter-season PM cycle.
  • Use low-temperature-rated seals and connectors in locations with a known cold-season history.
  • Trend cold-start faults by location year over year to identify sites needing a permanent fix rather than repeated seasonal repair.
Related References

Standards: FAA AC 150/5345-10, IS 12290

DIF-532 Fault Appears at Higher CCR Step

Symptom — A fault — a fixture dropping out, an earth-fault trip, or abnormal harmonics — appears only when the CCR is commanded to a higher intensity step and is absent at lower steps.

Equipment / System

CCR output stage and the full series circuit under it, evaluated across its full range of commanded intensity steps.

Possible Causes
HighA marginal insulation weak point on the cable or a transformer winding that only breaks down once voltage/current rises to the level associated with a higher step
HighA connector or joint with elevated contact resistance overheating and failing only once current reaches the higher-step level
MedA CCR output stage (thyristor bank, tap) with a fault specific to its higher-output configuration
MedHarmonic or transient stress at higher steps stressing a marginal component that tolerates lower-step operation without issue
LowA series circuit loop with a marginal joint that only draws enough current at a higher step to generate a detectable heating or arcing signature
LowAn earth-fault protection threshold calibrated close to the normal leakage current at higher steps, tripping on a marginal but not yet failed insulation reading
Required Tools
Clamp-on series circuit ammeterCCR output trend log across all intensity steps1000V/2500V insulation resistance tester (megger)Thermal imaging cameraPower quality/harmonics analyser
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before any hands-on component testing; do not attempt to trace a live higher-step fault by touch.
  • Coordinate step-by-step live testing with ATC/apron control given the circuit will be run at full commanded output during diagnosis.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationConfirm from the CCR trend log or trip history the specific step(s) at which the fault reliably appears and the steps at which it does not.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, megger the full loop and individual accessible segments, noting any reading that is marginal rather than a hard failure.
  3. MeasurementRe-energise under controlled conditions and step the CCR incrementally, using thermal imaging to spot a connector or joint that heats disproportionately as output rises.
  4. DecisionA specific segment or connector is confirmed heating/failing only at the higher step → isolate and repair that point. Fault is CCR-internal rather than circuit-side → route to Division 01 for output-stage investigation.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the specific cable segment, joint, transformer, or CCR output stage responsible for the step-dependent behaviour.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by re-testing across the full step range after repair, holding at the previously faulting step for an extended period.
Test Points
Insulation resistance across accessible segments, marginal vs. hard-failed
Thermal image of connectors/joints stepped from low to high output
CCR output current and harmonics at each intensity step
Earth-fault trip threshold vs. actual leakage current at the higher step
Root Cause Verification

Hold the circuit at the previously faulting step for an extended period after repair and confirm no recurrence, then re-verify across the full step range.

Corrective Action
  • Repair or replace the cable segment, joint, or transformer confirmed to fail at higher current/voltage.
  • Re-terminate a connector confirmed to overheat only at higher-step current.
  • Adjust CCR earth-fault threshold only if confirmed to be miscalibrated relative to actual leakage current, not as a substitute for fixing the insulation fault.
Preventive Action
  • Include a full step-range test, not just a single intensity check, in the periodic PM cycle.
  • Trend insulation resistance readings to catch a segment trending toward a step-dependent failure before it becomes a live fault.
  • Verify CCR earth-fault thresholds are correctly calibrated to expected leakage current at full output.
Related References

SOP: SOP-DIF-07 (Step-Dependent Fault Diagnostic Procedure), SOP-CCR-04 (CCR LOTO Procedure)

Standards: ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5345-10, IEC 60364

DIF-533 Fault Appears at Lower CCR Step

Symptom — A fault — flicker, dimming below spec, or an unstable output — appears only when the CCR is commanded to a lower intensity step and is absent at higher steps.

Equipment / System

CCR output/regulation stage and the series circuit under it, particularly its behaviour at minimum or reduced-output configurations.

Possible Causes
HighThyristor/SCR firing-angle instability at the shallow conduction angles required for low-output steps, common as a control card ages
HighA minimum-load condition on the series circuit close to the CCR's rated minimum, causing regulation instability at the lowest steps
MedA dimming/regulation feedback loop with reduced accuracy or hunting behaviour at the low end of its control range
MedAn LED driver or lamp/LED module with poor low-current performance, flickering or dropping out at reduced drive current
LowElectrical noise or interference proportionally more significant relative to a low control signal than to a high one
LowA standby/emergency step configuration that is rarely exercised and has developed an undetected fault
Required Tools
Oscilloscope for firing-angle/waveform analysisCCR output trend log across all intensity stepsClamp-on series circuit ammeterTrue-RMS multimeter with min/max holdManufacturer's CCR regulation specification
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before any internal CCR inspection.
  • Coordinate live step-by-step testing with ATC/apron control since low-step operation is often used during specific traffic conditions.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationConfirm from the CCR trend log the specific low step(s) at which the fault appears and its absence at higher steps.
  2. MeasurementCapture the CCR output waveform on an oscilloscope at the faulting low step to check for firing-angle instability or hunting.
  3. MeasurementConfirm the series circuit's total connected load is within the CCR's rated minimum for the affected step.
  4. DecisionWaveform shows firing-angle instability or hunting → CCR control card/regulation issue, route to Division 01. Waveform is stable but individual fixtures flicker → investigate lamp/LED module low-current performance instead.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the CCR control card, the specific fixture/module with poor low-current behaviour, or a marginal circuit load condition.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by holding the circuit at the faulting low step for an extended period after correction and verifying stable output.
Test Points
CCR output waveform at each low intensity step
Total connected circuit load vs. CCR rated minimum
Individual fixture behaviour at the lowest commanded step
Control card firing-angle stability under sustained low-step operation
Root Cause Verification

Hold the circuit at the previously faulting low step for an extended period and confirm stable, flicker-free output before closing out.

Corrective Action
  • Replace or recalibrate a CCR control card exhibiting firing-angle instability at low output.
  • Replace an LED module or lamp confirmed to have poor low-current performance.
  • Adjust circuit configuration if total load is found to be below the CCR's rated minimum for stable regulation.
Preventive Action
  • Include a full low-step stability check, not just a nominal-output check, in the periodic PM cycle.
  • Trend low-step waveform stability over time to catch a control card degrading before it produces a visible flicker.
  • Verify circuit loading remains within the CCR's rated operating range whenever fixtures are added or removed.
Related References

SOP: SOP-DIF-08 (Low-Step Regulation Diagnostic Procedure)

Standards: ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5345-10

DIF-534 Fault Appears During Switching

Symptom — A fault — a nuisance trip, a fixture dropout, or a visible flash/flicker — occurs specifically during a CCR intensity-step change, circuit changeover, or on/off switching event, rather than during steady-state operation at any step.

Equipment / System

CCR switching/contactor stage, transfer/changeover equipment, and any downstream component sensitive to switching transients.

Possible Causes
HighInrush or transient current during step change or energisation stressing a marginal connector, joint, or component that tolerates steady-state current without issue
HighContactor or relay contact bounce/wear generating a transient during switching that a protection device interprets as a fault
MedThyristor commutation issues during a step transition producing a brief but detectable disturbance
MedA changeover/transfer switch (e.g., standby power or circuit selector) with worn or misaligned contacts generating a transient during operation
LowElectromagnetic interference generated by the switching event itself affecting a nearby sensitive control or communication circuit
LowA protection relay/earth-fault device with a trip threshold too close to the normal transient signature of a switching event
Required Tools
Oscilloscope with transient capture capabilityCCR/relay event log with timestamped switching recordsClamp-on series circuit ammeter with inrush captureContact resistance meter for contactor/relay inspectionManufacturer's switching transient specification
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before any hands-on inspection of contactors, relays, or transfer switches.

Single-line diagram showing shared supply, earthing, and routing relationships between affected circuitsPower quality logger at the shared upstream supply pointEarth resistance tester for the shared earthing systemWeather/environmental log for the reporting periodCable/component batch and installation records
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO each affected circuit individually before investigation, even where a common cause is suspected — do not assume isolating one circuit protects work on another.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationMap every affected circuit/system against the single-line diagram to identify any shared upstream supply, earthing point, cable route, or installation batch.
  2. MeasurementLog power quality at the shared upstream supply point and earth resistance at the shared earthing system, correlating timestamps against each individual fault occurrence.
  3. MeasurementCross-reference installation and maintenance records for the affected circuits to check for a shared cable, connector, or transformer batch.
  4. DecisionA shared supply, earthing, or batch link is confirmed → treat as a single common-cause investigation and address the shared root cause. No shared link found → treat each fault as independent and route each to its own relevant division/procedure.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to the shared upstream point, earthing system, or batch if a common cause is confirmed; otherwise isolate each fault individually.
  6. Root CauseConfirm by observing whether correcting the shared root cause resolves all affected circuits simultaneously, which is strong evidence the link was real.
Test Points
Power quality at the shared upstream supply point
Earth resistance across the shared earthing system
Timestamp correlation across all affected circuits' fault logs
Installation batch records for cable, connectors, and transformers across affected circuits
Root Cause Verification

After addressing the shared root cause, monitor all previously affected circuits simultaneously over an extended period and confirm all resolve together rather than one recurring independently.

Corrective Action
  • Correct the shared upstream supply issue (voltage regulation, harmonics filtering) if confirmed as the common cause.
  • Repair the shared earthing system if a common ground-reference fault is confirmed.
  • Proactively inspect or replace remaining components from a confirmed defective installation batch, even where they have not yet faulted.
Preventive Action
  • Maintain an up-to-date single-line diagram showing shared supply, earthing, and routing relationships to speed future common-cause investigation.
  • Log installation batch information (cable reel, connector lot, transformer serial) at the time of installation to enable future batch tracing.
  • Monitor upstream power quality and shared earthing resistance continuously rather than only when multiple faults prompt an investigation.

Case Study: Division 24 — Complete Runway Lighting Failure

Rev 0 — Initial issue
DIF-544 Hidden Underground Fault

Symptom — A confirmed insulation, continuity, or earth-fault condition exists somewhere within a buried or ducted cable run, but the fault location cannot be determined from accessible base cans, pull boxes, or surface inspection alone.

Equipment / System

Buried or ducted primary series cable, joints, and duct/handhole infrastructure between accessible above-grade access points.

Possible Causes
HighA cable fault located mid-span between accessible pull boxes or base cans, with no surface indication of its position
HighA duct or handhole collapse or blockage preventing physical access to inspect a suspected joint or splice location
MedA fault location technique giving an ambiguous or low-confidence reading due to cable characteristics (splices, varying cable types, or branch points) that complicate reflectometry
MedMultiple candidate fault locations along a long run, requiring systematic narrowing rather than a single test pinpointing the exact spot
LowA fault whose signature (very high resistance, intermittent) is inherently difficult for standard TDR or bridge methods to localise precisely
LowIncomplete or inaccurate as-built records making it unclear exactly where the cable route runs relative to surface features
Required Tools
TDR (time-domain reflectometer)Cable fault bridge / Murray loop or equivalent method for high-resistance faultsGround-penetrating locator / cable route tracerAcoustic/thumper fault pinpointing method, where appropriate and permittedAs-built drawings and any available GPR/utility survey records
Safety Precautions
  • Obtain full utility/services clearance and airside works permits before any excavation.
  • LOTO the circuit before any fault-locating method that involves applying test voltage to the cable.
  • Coordinate excavation timing and location with airfield operations given the proximity to active pavement.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationCompile all available records — as-built drawings, previous repair history, and any prior fault-location data — for the affected run before starting a new investigation.
  2. MeasurementRun TDR and, for high-resistance faults where TDR is inconclusive, a bridge-method test from both accessible ends of the suspected segment to triangulate the fault distance.
  3. MeasurementUse a ground-penetrating locator or route tracer to confirm the actual physical cable route on the surface, since the calculated distance is only useful once matched to the real route.
  4. DecisionTDR/bridge results converge on a specific, physically accessible location → proceed to targeted excavation. Results remain ambiguous or point to an inaccessible/obstructed area → consider acoustic pinpointing or a wider excavation window, and re-verify the as-built route accuracy first.
  5. Fault LocationMark the calculated fault location on the surface using the confirmed physical route before excavating.
  6. Root CauseConfirm visually once the cable is exposed, and verify the fault type and location match the electrical test predictions before repairing.
Test Points
TDR distance-to-fault from both accessible ends
Bridge-method resistance-based distance calculation
Physical route trace vs. as-built drawing
Post-excavation visual confirmation of the fault
Root Cause Verification

Re-megger and load-test the repaired and backfilled segment, and update the as-built drawing with the confirmed route and repair location before closing out.

Corrective Action
  • Excavate, expose, and repair the identified cable fault using a jointing method rated for the buried application.
  • Repair or replace a collapsed or blocked duct section obstructing access.
Preventive Action
  • Update as-built drawings immediately after any excavation or repair, including the exact GPS/measured location of the fault and repair.
  • Consider periodic TDR baseline surveys on ageing buried circuits to have a healthy reference trace on file before a fault occurs.
  • Maintain duct and handhole infrastructure proactively to preserve future access for fault location.

Case Study: Division 08 — Taxiway Centreline / Guidance Lighting Troubleshooting

Rev 0 — Initial issue
DIF-545 Recurring Unidentified Fault

Symptom — The same fault symptom recurs repeatedly at the same location or circuit over an extended period despite multiple maintenance visits, component replacements, or repairs, with no single corrective action yet producing a lasting fix.

Equipment / System

Any AGL circuit or component with an open, chronic fault history spanning multiple maintenance cycles and, typically, more than one prior corrective attempt.

Possible Causes
HighPrevious repairs addressed a symptom or the most recent failed component rather than the true underlying root cause, allowing the same failure mode to recur
HighAn underlying design, installation, or routing issue (inadequate cable slack, poor drainage, a marginal earthing design) that will keep producing failures until corrected at the systemic level, not the component level
MedMultiple independent fault mechanisms are present at the same location, so fixing one leaves another to surface and appear as a "recurrence"
MedA component or material genuinely unsuited to the specific environmental exposure at that location (e.g., standard-rated equipment in an unusually wet or high-traffic-vibration area)
LowInconsistent repair quality or specification across multiple maintenance visits, with different technicians addressing the symptom differently each time
LowIncomplete diagnostic investigation at each prior visit, closing out on a plausible cause without full verification
Required Tools
Complete maintenance and repair history for the location, compiled chronologicallyAs-built and revision-history drawings for the affected circuitFull diagnostic toolkit relevant to the fault type (megger, TDR, thermal camera, oscilloscope, as applicable)Environmental and load history log for the locationRoot-cause analysis / fishbone or 5-why documentation template
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO before any investigation, as with any live AGL work.
  • Treat a chronic recurring fault as a priority investigation rather than a routine repeat repair, given the cumulative safety exposure of an unresolved recurring issue.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationCompile the complete history of every prior occurrence, repair, and component replacement at this location, looking for a pattern in timing, conditions, or the specific component replaced each time.
  2. MeasurementConduct a full, unbiased diagnostic investigation from first principles — not assuming the previous diagnosis was correct — covering every plausible cause category (connector, cable, transformer, fixture, CCR, environmental, design).
  3. MeasurementAssess whether the location has any distinguishing environmental or design factor (drainage, traffic loading, cable slack, earthing) relative to comparable locations that have not had recurring failures.
  4. DecisionA systemic design/installation issue is identified → escalate for a permanent design-level correction, not another like-for-like component swap. Multiple independent mechanisms are found → address all identified causes together rather than one at a time across further visits.
  5. Fault LocationIsolate to every contributing factor identified, not just the most recent or most obvious one.
  6. Root CauseDo not close out until the specific factor that differentiates this location from non-recurring comparable locations has been identified and addressed.
Test Points
Full chronological repair and replacement history for the location
Comparative environmental/design factors vs. non-recurring comparable locations
Complete first-principles diagnostic sweep across all plausible cause categories
Post-repair extended monitoring log
Root Cause Verification

Monitor the location through at least one full seasonal cycle and multiple representative operating conditions after the systemic correction, and confirm no recurrence before considering the chronic ticket closed.

Corrective Action
  • Implement the systemic design or installation correction identified (improved drainage, cable slack, upgraded component rating, earthing improvement) rather than repeating a prior like-for-like repair.
  • Address every contributing cause identified during the full diagnostic sweep, not only the most recent failure mode.
Preventive Action
  • Maintain a chronic-fault register flagging any location with more than a defined number of repeat visits for a mandatory root-cause-level investigation rather than continued routine repair.
  • Review and update design/installation standards where a recurring fault is traced to a systemic rather than component-level cause.
  • Ensure diagnostic findings and the specific corrective action taken are fully documented at every visit, not just "fault cleared", to make future pattern analysis possible.
