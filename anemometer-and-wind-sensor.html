What is an anemometer and wind sensor?

The instruments that measure surface wind speed and direction at 10 m — cup and vane, propeller vane and ultrasonic — and the cable, surge-protection and display chain that carries the reading to the tower and the met office.

The Fact ICAO Annex 3 · WMO-No. 8

An anemometer measures wind speed. A wind sensor (a wind vane, or a combined unit) also gives direction, quoted as the direction the wind blows from. At an aerodrome the pair provides the surface wind that controllers, pilots and the met office use to choose the runway in use and to check crosswind and tailwind. The reference height is 10 m above ground, the WMO standard exposure.

Three sensor types are common. The cup and vane pairs a three-cup rotor, whose turn rate follows wind speed, with a separate vane for direction. The propeller vane puts a propeller and tail fins on one body. The ultrasonic sensor has no moving parts: transducer pairs send sound pulses both ways, and the difference in travel time gives the wind.

The sensor is not a light, but it shares the airfield with AGL: its mast stands near the runway, it is fed and read over cable, and it needs the same earthing and surge-protection discipline. Masts are normally sited clear of the runway strip. Where one has to stand on or near it, ICAO Annex 14 requires it to be frangible and mounted as low as possible, and the obstacle-limitation surfaces still apply. The windsock is separate: a visual indicator the pilot reads directly (see Wind Cone Assembly, entry 9).

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is the difference between an anemometer and a wind sensor?

An anemometer measures wind speed. A wind sensor usually means a sensor that reports speed and direction, either as one combined unit (propeller vane, ultrasonic) or as an anemometer plus a separate wind vane. At aerodromes the terms are used loosely, so check whether a datasheet quotes direction as well as speed.

Q2How does a cup and vane sensor work?

Wind pushes the cups round, and the rotor's turn rate is converted to speed, usually by counting pulses from a magnetic or optical pickup. The vane turns to point into the wind and an encoder or potentiometer reports its angle as direction. Both have bearings, so friction raises the wind speed at which they start to turn as they wear, and cup rotors tend to speed up faster in a gust than they slow down in a lull.

Q3How does an ultrasonic wind sensor work?

Pairs of transducers send sound pulses to each other in both directions. Wind speeds the pulse up in one direction and slows it in the other, so the difference in travel time gives the wind component along that path. Two or more paths give speed and direction. There are no moving parts to wear or freeze, and heaters keep the transducers clear in cold weather, but ice, deposits or heavy rain on the transducer faces can still affect the reading.

Q4Where is it mounted, and why 10 m?

The standard measuring height is 10 m above ground, so readings from different sites can be compared. The sensor is placed where the wind is representative of the runway and clear of obstacles; WMO guidance is roughly ten times an obstacle's height as the minimum clear distance. On long or precision runways there is often more than one sensor set, and the meteorological authority decides the number and the positions.

Q5What is the reading used for?

Controllers and pilots use it to select the runway in use and to check crosswind and tailwind components. It feeds the local report on the tower display, ATIS and the METAR. Some aerodromes combine several anemometers into a low-level wind shear alerting system. The averaging period and direction reference differ between local reports and METAR, so the two figures are not always identical.

Q6How does it connect to the AGL system?

It is a separate meteorological system and not part of the series circuit, but it shares the site. The mast needs earthing and lightning protection, the signal and supply cables need surge protection, and the cable routes must be recorded and protected like any other airfield cable. At some aerodromes the wind data is carried to the same control room or SCADA as the lighting status.

Q7What goes wrong, and is it safe to work on?

Common faults are worn or iced bearings, failed heaters, water in connectors, a loose or mis-set vane that shows a constant direction offset, and damaged cable. Work on a mast near a runway needs the permit-to-work and the aircraft-movement controls. Lower a tilt-down mast rather than climbing it, isolate the supply first, and stay off the mast when lightning is near. Tell the meteorological authority before work starts, because the wind data is in use.
