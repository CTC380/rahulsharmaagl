Product Overview

Sequenced Flashing Light System

What It Is

Instead of a continuous steady-burning pattern, the system flashes individual units in sequence. This helps pilots identify the approach-lighting system at greater range and supports approach-path identification, runway alignment, the transition from instrument to visual flight, early recognition of the approach axis and improved conspicuity against high background lighting.

FAA describes the effect as a ball of light travelling toward the runway.

Selected According To

  • Approach lighting system
  • Runway classification and approach category
  • System length
  • Number of flasher stations and spacing
  • Flash sequence, rate and synchronization
  • Light intensity, optical direction and beam distribution

  • Mounting arrangement and environmental conditions
  • Project technical specification

The final system should always be selected according to the approved approach-lighting design and applicable aviation standard.

Function

An Early, Conspicuous Dynamic Reference

Why the Motion Matters

The principal purpose is an early and conspicuous dynamic reference toward the runway. A steady-burning light creates a static pattern; the sequence adds a moving cue that helps the pilot identify the correct system and runway alignment.

FAA states that MALSR sequenced flashers appear as a ball of light travelling toward the runway.

Notes

Flashes occur sequentially from the outer approach area toward the runway. The exact number of flashers and their spacing depend on the selected approach-lighting system.

Sequence Direction

Outermost → Innermost → Threshold

One of the Most Important Characteristics

For the principal runway approach systems the sequence progresses outermost → innermost → threshold, creating apparent motion toward the runway.

Standards Basis

ICAO precision approach provisions specify sequential flashing beginning with the outermost light and progressing toward the threshold. FAA approach systems similarly operate the flashers from the outer approach area toward the threshold.

Flash Rate

Timing Defines the Visual Effect

The product should not be specified using an assumed generic flash rate.

Controlled Accurately

FAA guidance for systems such as MALSF identifies sequenced flashers operating at approximately twice per second, meaning the whole sequence repeats about 120 times per minute, with each flasher firing once per cycle. FAA AC 150/5345-51B sets different rates for unidirectional and omnidirectional flashers (for example, runway end identifier lights), so take the rate for your flasher type from the current edition of that document and the approved system drawing.

Standards & References

Governing Standards

Select, install, control and test as part of the complete approved approach-lighting system.

ICAO

Annex 14 approach-lighting provisions address sequenced flashing elements within configured systems. For applicable precision approach systems, flashers operate in sequence from the outermost light toward the threshold, with the electrical design allowing independent operation. The approach category and configuration determine whether flashers are required. Verify the current publication, amendments and corrigenda before procurement.

FAA

FAA AC 150/5340-30J — Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids is listed as active and covers approach lighting, including MALSF layouts and sequenced flashers.

  • MALSF: three sequenced flashers at the outer three light-bar stations
  • MALSR: typically five sequenced flashers (up to eight) along the extended centreline
  • ALSF-2: typically 15 sequenced flashers on a 2,400 ft system, up to 21 on a 3,000 ft system, operating from the outer approach area toward the threshold
  • FAA Order JO 6850.2 — Visual Guidance Lighting Systems, which defines the MALSR, ALSF-1/ALSF-2 and SSALR configurations and flasher stationing

FAA AC 150/5345-51B covers discharge-type flashing light equipment (the flasher equipment used in sequenced flashing light and RAIL arrangements); verify the current edition. Where LED flashers are offered, confirm with the airport authority how their performance is qualified against the project specification. MALSF and MALSR are not the same system. Establish the project system designation before selecting flasher equipment.

Sequenced Flasher vs RAIL

Closely Related, Not Universally Identical

Do Not Treat as Identical

RAIL and sequenced flashing lights are closely related in some FAA approach systems but should not be treated as universally identical products.

The FAA System Designation Determines

  • Number of units
  • Location and sequence
  • Operational effect
  • Approach-category implications

FAA inspection guidance distinguishes SFL and RAIL and describes different consequences for inoperative units by approach category.

FAQ

Sequenced Flashing Light System — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a sequenced flashing light system?

It is a dynamic visual indication along the extended runway centreline. Individual flashing units operate in a controlled sequence, creating the impression of light moving toward the runway threshold, which the FAA describes as a ball of light travelling toward the runway. It helps pilots identify the approach-lighting system at greater range and supports approach-path identification, runway alignment and early recognition of the approach axis.

Which approach lighting systems use sequenced flashers?

FAA identifies ALSF-1, ALSF-2, SSALF, MALSF and other configurations that incorporate sequenced flashing lights. They are used in precision approach systems including Category I, II and III, in MALS, MALSF and MALSR medium-intensity systems, in SALSF and SSALF short systems, in RAIL configurations, in high-background-light environments, and in upgrade and new installations.

In which direction does the sequence run?

For the principal runway approach systems the sequence progresses from the outermost flasher to the innermost and then the threshold, creating apparent motion toward the runway. ICAO precision approach provisions specify sequential flashing beginning with the outermost light and progressing toward the threshold, and FAA approach systems similarly operate the flashers from the outer approach area toward the threshold.

How many flashers does a system have?

The number is not universal and depends on the approach-lighting system, its length and the approved layout. The FAA describes MALSF as having three sequenced flashers at the outer three light-bar stations, MALSR as typically five sequenced flashing lamps (up to eight) along the extended centreline, and ALSF-2 as typically 15 sequenced flashers on a 2,400 ft system, up to 21 on a 3,000 ft system. The final spacing must be taken from the approved system drawing.

What is the flash rate?

The product should not be specified using an assumed generic flash rate. FAA guidance for systems such as MALSF identifies sequenced flashers operating at approximately twice per second, so the whole sequence repeats about 120 times per minute, with each flasher firing once per cycle. FAA AC 150/5345-51B sets different rates for unidirectional and omnidirectional flashers, so take the rate for your flasher type from the current edition of that document and the approved system drawing.

What colour are sequenced flashers?

They are normally white, associated with the white approach-light field, and the FAA identifies the flashers in systems such as MALSR and ALSF-2 as white flashing lights. Colour verification covers chromaticity, white output, consistency between flashers, intensity and optical distribution.

What does the sequencer or controller do?

The sequencer or timing controller is the critical part of the system. It must maintain the correct order and timing, synchronization, intensity, reliable triggering and fault monitoring. A timing fault can make the system appear static, irregular or out of sequence. It should also give a correct restart after a power interruption, and synchronization should be verified during commissioning where several independent flasher modules are used.

Do the flashers need their own circuit?

They can use a dedicated electrical and control architecture, which may include a dedicated flasher circuit, a CCR or dedicated power supply, the sequencer, individual flasher channels, a control cabinet, a monitoring circuit and an ALCMS interface. For certain precision approach systems, ICAO requires the circuit to be arranged so the flashing lights can be operated independently of the other approach lights. The technical submission should state the circuit arrangement, control relationship and CCR allocation, fault isolation and maintenance isolation.

How is flasher intensity controlled?

Intensity depends on the approach-lighting system and category, ambient background lighting and visibility, the optical design and flasher type, and the control configuration. FAA material notes the effective intensity can need adjustment in high-background-light environments, and FAA inspection guidance states the three MALSF flashers have no intensity control of their own and follow the steady-burning lights’ settings. Coordinate the settings with the steady-burning elements.

How are sequenced flashers mounted?

Installation forms include elevated fixtures, light-bar assemblies, low-level approach fixtures, dedicated flasher heads, frangible-mounted equipment and modular LED units. Supports should be coordinated with the approach geometry, frangibility, height, beam axis, wind loading, ground conditions, cable routing and maintenance access. For FAA projects, select support structures according to the applicable FAA low-impact and frangibility requirements for approach lighting.

How are sequenced flashers tested and what are the common faults?

Testing is functional and timing verification, not merely lamp illumination. It covers visual inspection, electrical tests, sequence testing (correct first and last flasher, direction, repeat cycle, no skipped station, no unintended simultaneous flash), flash-rate testing and photometric testing. Typical faults include all flashers off (power supply, CCR, controller or circuit), one flasher off (light source, channel, cable or connector), wrong sequence (configuration, wiring or sequencer), incorrect flash rate (timing controller or reference), simultaneous flashing (sequencer or synchronization failure) and weak flashes (contamination, LED degradation or intensity setting).

Which standards apply to sequenced flashing light systems?

References include ICAO Annex 14 approach-lighting provisions, FAA AC 150/5340-30J, FAA Order JO 6850.2 on visual guidance lighting systems and FAA AC 150/5345-51B on discharge-type flashing light equipment. Where LED flashers are offered, confirm with the airport authority how their performance is qualified against the project specification. MALSF and MALSR are not the same system, so establish the project system designation before selecting flasher equipment. For India, also verify DGCA requirements, the airport-specific AGL specification and the approved approach-lighting layout, and confirm the applicable edition of each document.

Engineering Note

A sequenced flashing light system should not be specified merely as “flashing approach lights.” The important parameters are system type, flasher quantity, station spacing, sequence direction, flash rate, synchronization, effective intensity, beam alignment, controller architecture and interaction with steady-burning approach lights.

FAA configurations differ materially: MALSF uses three sequenced flashers, MALSR typically uses five, and a typical ALSF-2 uses 15 (up to 21 on a 3,000 ft system). The approach-lighting system designation must be confirmed before the flasher product is selected, and the equipment checked against the current standard, approved airport drawing and manufacturer documentation.
