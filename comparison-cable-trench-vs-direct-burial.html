Installation & Construction Comparison

Cable Trench vs Direct Burial

Cable Trench
VS
Direct Burial

Two underground cable installation methods used across AGL series-circuit infrastructure. Cable trench routes the cable through a deliberately engineered, more accessible corridor — often with structured bedding, protection and access provisions. Direct burial places the cable straight into a prepared route in the ground, backfilled without a dedicated accessible structure. Both can be used on an airfield; the right choice depends on maintainability, pavement conditions, cable protection, drainage and the applicable standard — not on installation cost alone.

02 · Definitions

What Is Cable Trench & Direct Burial?

Cable Trench

A cable trench is a deliberately excavated, engineered route in which the cable is installed as part of a planned system — often with defined bedding, protection, separation and access arrangements. The route can be backfilled, ducted or covered, but its defining feature is that it was engineered as a corridor rather than a simple excavation.

Typically includes

  • Sand or approved bedding
  • Ducts or conduits
  • Concrete protection or covers
  • Warning tape and markers
  • Draw pits / junction structures and spare ducts, where provided

Direct Burial

Direct burial places the cable straight underground in a prepared route, without a dedicated accessible trench structure once installation is complete. The cable itself must be suitable for direct burial, and it is protected by burial depth, bedding and surrounding backfill rather than by a maintained corridor.

Typically includes

  • Selected bedding / soil
  • Selected backfill
  • Warning layer / tape
  • Adequate burial depth

Terminology note: Direct-buried cable is itself laid in an excavated trench — in FAA practice (AC 150/5370-10, Item L-108) that trench also carries the counterpoise, marking tape and specified fill, and is then backfilled. Cable in conduit or duct banks is specified separately (Item L-110), with electrical manholes and junction structures under Item L-115. On this page, "cable trench" means a route engineered to stay accessible or replaceable after installation (ducts, duct banks, covers, pits); "direct burial" means cable backfilled in the ground with no such provision. Terminology varies between specifications, so confirm how your contract defines each term.

03 · Key Difference

Engineered Corridor vs Backfilled Route

The simplest way to remember the difference between cable trench and direct burial:

Cable Trench

Structured + More Accessible Cable Route

Direct Burial

Simpler Underground Installation + Less Accessibility

Engineering point: A trench can still be backfilled and covered, and a direct-buried cable can still be well protected. The real distinction is whether the route was engineered to remain maintainable, not whether the cable is visible.

05 · Cable Protection

What Protects the Cable in Each Method

Cable trench may incorporate

  • Sand or approved bedding
  • Ducts or conduits
  • Concrete protection
  • Protective covers
  • Warning tape
  • Cable markers

Direct burial must protect against

  • Stones
  • Mechanical damage
  • Accidental excavation
  • Ground movement
  • Moisture conditions
  • Rodent / termite attack (site-dependent)

In both methods the cable itself must be rated for the intended installation — AGL series cable (for example FAA L-824 type) is designed for underground use, but confirm the cable datasheet and project specification for direct-burial versus duct use — and the surrounding protective arrangement must suit the actual soil, loading and route conditions.

Lightning protection applies to both: Where the specification requires a counterpoise, it is a bare copper conductor laid along the route above the cable or duct. FAA Item L-108 calls for bare No. 6 AWG copper, at least 8 in below grade and at least 4 in above the cable or duct bank in a shared trench. In the FAA isolation method the counterpoise is not bonded to the light base; a separate grounding electrode is provided at each base. Whichever method is chosen, the counterpoise and base grounding must be part of the design. Verify the current edition for your project.

Repair

Splice / replace and reinstate

Main advantage: Where the trench or associated duct system provides genuine access, cable trench systems generally offer better maintainability than direct burial.

This matters more on a series circuit: all fixtures share one loop, so a single cable fault can affect the whole circuit. Fault location (insulation-resistance testing and cable-fault locating) is done before any excavation in both methods, and every repair splice must use an approved method and be re-tested before the circuit is re-energised.

Direct burial can reduce infrastructure complexity, but it requires careful installation because access will be limited once the route is closed. In both methods, insulation-resistance testing before closing the route, and again before energising, is standard commissioning practice, and an accurate as-built record of route, depth and splice positions is essential for future fault location.

08 · Mechanical Protection

Behaviour Under External Load

Cable trench

The engineered installation — ducts, covers or slabs — can provide additional, controllable protection against external loads above the route.

Direct burial

Protection depends heavily on burial depth, soil condition, cable construction, protective layers and route marking rather than an engineered structure.

10 · Pavement Crossings

Crossing Beneath Critical Pavement

Cable trench

Crossings beneath runways, taxiways and aprons are normally built as ducts, conduits or concrete-encased duct banks (FAA Item L-110), often with spare ducts and junction structures, so cable can be replaced without cutting the pavement.

Direct burial

Beneath critical pavement, direct burial is generally avoided because a cable failure would force excavation through the pavement structure. It is more typical in grassed and unpaved areas; where it is considered under pavement, depth, structural loading and future excavation difficulty must be assessed against the specification.

13 · Practical Selection

Cable Trench vs Direct Burial: When to Choose Each

Selection question: Is future access and maintenance important? If yes, a trench or other accessible system may be preferred. If no, direct burial may be suitable.

Also weigh cable type, soil conditions, pavement loads, drainage, future expansion, maintenance access, and the applicable standard together — not in isolation.

Consider cable trench when

  • Future access and maintenance are important
  • The route crosses runways, taxiways or aprons, or is complex or heavily used
  • Mechanical protection needs are high
  • Route modification is likely later

Consider direct burial when

  • A simpler, permanent underground route is suitable
  • Soil and depth conditions support adequate protection
  • Lower initial installation cost is a priority
  • The applicable standard permits it for the route
14 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: A cable trench always means the cable remains exposed
Fact

Not necessarily. A trench can be backfilled, covered or ducted. The important distinction is the engineered route and installation arrangement, not visibility.

Myth: Direct burial means no protection
Fact

Incorrect. A direct-buried installation may still include selected bedding, protective layers, warning tape, markers and suitable burial depth.

Myth: Cable trenches always make maintenance easy
Fact

Not always. A completely backfilled trench without ducts or accessible structures may still require excavation. The real maintenance advantage depends on whether the design provides genuine future access.

Myth: Direct burial is always the cheaper long-term choice
Fact

Lower initial cost can be offset by higher repair costs later if excavation is needed. Long-term cost depends on fault frequency and route location.

15 · Engineering Verdict

Which Method Applies Where?

Engineering Verdict

Cable trench is a planned, engineered underground route that can provide greater cable protection, organisation and future maintainability. Direct burial is a cable installed directly into the ground with suitable bedding, depth and protection, offering a simpler installation but more difficult physical access after backfilling.

For AGL systems, the choice should be based on maintainability, pavement conditions, cable protection, drainage, future expansion and the applicable airfield standard — not simply on initial installation cost.

Cable TrenchStructured + Accessible — better maintainability.
Direct BurialSimple + Permanent — lower initial cost and complexity.

16 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a cable trench always visible or open?

No. A trench can be backfilled, covered or ducted. The defining feature is the engineered, planned route — not whether it remains open.

Does direct burial mean the cable has no protection?

No. Direct-buried cable is still protected through selected bedding, backfill, burial depth, warning tape and route marking.

Which method is cheaper for AGL installations?

Direct burial generally has a lower initial cost. However, a lower initial cost can lead to higher repair costs later if excavation becomes necessary — so cost alone should not drive the decision.

Can both methods be used on the same airfield project?

Yes. Both methods can be used depending on the airfield design, with the choice made route by route based on pavement crossings, drainage, maintenance access and the applicable standard.

Does a cable trench always make future maintenance easier?

Only if the trench or duct system provides genuine access. A fully backfilled trench without ducts or accessible structures may still need excavation for repairs.

What should drive the final selection between the two methods?

Maintainability, pavement conditions, cable protection, drainage, future expansion and the applicable airfield standard — not simply the initial installation cost.

Can AGL cable be direct-buried under a runway or taxiway?

Under pavement, cable is normally installed in ducts, conduits or duct banks so it can be replaced without cutting the pavement. Direct burial is more typical in unpaved areas. Always follow the project specification and the applicable standard.
