Isolating Transformers & Series Circuits

The fundamental building blocks of airfield ground lighting — each fixture powered through its own isolating transformer on a shared series loop, delivering fault tolerance, constant current, and uniform intensity across the entire airfield.

Overview

What Are They?

Isolating transformers are the individual power-supply units that feed each AGL fixture (or small group of fixtures) from the primary series circuit. They are typically 1:1 or step-down transformers with a primary winding connected in series with the main loop and one or more secondary windings that supply the lamp or LED module at a lower, safe voltage — the exact secondary voltage depends on the connected fixture's wattage rating, since these transformers are specified by secondary current (typically 6.6A or 20A) and wattage rather than a fixed secondary voltage.

Series circuits are the backbone of AGL power distribution. A constant current regulator (CCR) drives a fixed current (usually 6.6A) through a loop of cable that passes through every isolating transformer in that circuit. Because the current is the same through all series-connected elements, every fixture on that circuit receives the same current, and thus the same intensity, regardless of how many fixtures are installed or how long the cable run is.

This combination — isolating transformers on a series circuit — is what makes AGL systems reliable, scalable, and easy to control. A single CCR can power dozens of fixtures, and a single fixture failure does not take down the whole circuit because the isolating transformer's primary winding remains a low-impedance path for the series current.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

This combination exists to solve two problems at once that would otherwise be in tension with each other: an airfield needs every fixture on a circuit to receive exactly the same, consistent brightness regardless of its position in the loop, but it also needs a single failed fixture or transformer to never take down every other light sharing that circuit. A series circuit alone would guarantee the first but fail badly at the second — a genuine open circuit anywhere in a pure series loop stops current everywhere. Isolating transformers are what let the loop keep the uniform-current benefit of a series circuit while still tolerating individual fixture faults, because each transformer's primary winding provides a low-impedance path that keeps the loop current flowing even if that fixture's secondary side or lamp has failed.

The isolation itself is equally purposeful from a safety standpoint: each fixture sits electrically separated from every other fixture on the loop, so a technician working on one light, or a fault developing at one location, doesn't expose the rest of the circuit — or the people maintaining it — to that problem. This is what allows a single CCR to reliably drive dozens of fixtures spread across kilometres of airfield, with the confidence that the system as a whole will keep functioning even as individual components inevitably age, fail, and get serviced over the system's operational life.

Application

Where Are They Used?

Isolating transformers are installed at every lighting fixture position across the airfield, buried in below-grade pits or directly embedded with the fixture:

  • Runway Edge and Centreline: Each edge light and centreline light has its own isolating transformer, typically mounted in a shallow pit adjacent to the fixture.
  • Taxiway Edge and Centreline: Similarly, every taxiway light is fed through its own transformer.
  • Approach Lighting: Even elevated approach light structures use isolating transformers, often housed in a base pit.
  • Stop Bars and Guard Lights: These critical fixtures also use isolating transformers, often on separately switched series circuits.
  • Airfield Signage: Illuminated signs are powered via isolating transformers in the same way as lighting fixtures.
  • Primary Series Loop: The transformer primary windings are connected in series with the main cable, forming the continuous loop that starts and ends at the CCR.

The series circuit itself runs between the CCR and the furthest fixture, with the cable passing through each transformer's primary in a daisy-chain fashion (or sometimes in a loop with two feeds for redundancy).

Placement

Installation & Location

Each isolating transformer is installed in its own shallow pit or base can immediately adjacent to the fixture it feeds, so the low-voltage secondary run to the lamp or LED module stays short while the primary winding sits in-line with the series cable passing through the pit from the previous fixture to the next. The transformer is positioned above any drainage sump in the pit and set on its mounting bracket clear of standing water, with the primary and secondary connectors made up using L-823 or equivalent watertight couplers before the pit lid is closed. Because the primary winding forms part of the continuous series loop, the transformer's physical position in the daisy-chain has to match the circuit design and as-built drawing exactly, so a technician tracing the loop later can rely on the documented order of fixtures matching what is actually buried in the ground. Where a circuit uses a loop configuration with two feeds for redundancy rather than a single daisy-chain, the two cable runs into and out of each pit are kept clearly identified during installation so the loop's redundancy is not compromised by a mislabelled connection.

Function

How Do They Work?

The isolating transformer is the interface between the high-voltage, low-current series circuit and the low-voltage, high-current fixture. The primary winding is designed to carry the full series current (e.g. 6.6A) with minimal voltage drop, and its inductance presents a relatively high impedance to the series circuit, allowing the transformer to extract power while still passing the current along to the next transformer.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Constant-Current Feeding: The transformer steps down the voltage (e.g., from several hundred volts on the primary to a level set by the connected fixture's wattage on the secondary) while stepping up the current, matching the fixture's requirement. The secondary current is proportional to the primary current divided by the turns ratio.
  • Fault Isolation: If a fixture or its secondary wiring fails open, the transformer's primary remains intact, so the series current continues to flow. If a secondary short occurs, the transformer's primary impedance limits the current, preventing a total circuit failure.
  • Intensity Control: Because all transformers on a given series circuit share the same primary current, adjusting the CCR's output current changes the brightness of every fixture on that circuit simultaneously — this is how intensity steps work.
  • Electrical Isolation: The transformer provides galvanic isolation between the primary series circuit and the secondary fixture, improving safety and reducing the risk of shock or earth-leakage faults propagating through the system.
  • Multiple Secondary Windings: Some transformers have multiple secondary taps to supply different fixture types (e.g., one for an edge light and one for a sign) from the same primary.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The isolating transformer has no electronics or command input of its own; its place in the system architecture is as the passive interface node between the CCR's series-current control loop and each individual fixture. Because every transformer's primary is wired in series with the same loop, the CCR's constant-current regulation is what actually governs brightness across the whole circuit — the transformer just steps that shared current down to a safe secondary voltage for its fixture, with no local intensity decision to make. This series-primary arrangement doubles as the circuit's built-in fault-isolation logic: an open secondary (a failed lamp) doesn't interrupt the primary loop, so the rest of the circuit keeps operating, while a shorted secondary is limited by the transformer's own impedance rather than collapsing the whole circuit — with any resulting anomaly still surfacing at the CCR as a voltage change against commanded current, exactly as it would for a cable or connector fault.

Construction

Physical Construction

An isolating transformer is built around a toroidal or laminated core wound with primary and secondary copper windings, encapsulated in resin or, in older designs, housed in an oil-filled metal casing to provide electrical insulation and protect the windings from moisture during buried service. The encapsulated or oil-filled unit is sized to fit within a standard base can, with primary and secondary leads terminating at sealed L-823 connectors that mate to the incoming series circuit cable and the outgoing fixture cable respectively, keeping every connection point watertight to an IP68 rating. The insulation system between primary and secondary windings is rated to a high dielectric strength, since the transformer's core function is to isolate each fixture electrically from the rest of the series circuit so a fault at one fixture does not propagate along the loop. Class H or higher insulation materials are used throughout to tolerate the sustained thermal loading of continuous series-circuit operation, and the transformer's compact cylindrical or rectangular form factor is designed to sit alongside the fixture's connector and cabling within the confined space of a base can.

Series Loop Continuity
Electrical

Keep the primary winding in the series loop path intact end to end, since a broken primary takes the whole circuit down rather than just the one fixture.

Insulation Integrity Check
Mechanical

Inspect the resin encapsulation or oil-filled casing for damage before installation, since the primary-to-secondary insulation is what isolates each fixture from the rest of the loop on a fault.

Drainage Provision
Civil

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Transformer Primary Open Circuit

If a primary winding fails open, the entire series circuit is broken — all fixtures downstream of that transformer will go out. This is why transformer primary continuity is a critical check during fault-finding.

Secondary Short Circuit

A short on the secondary side (e.g., a faulty lamp holder) can overload the transformer, causing overheating and eventual failure, though the primary current remains limited.

Moisture Ingress in Transformer Pit

Despite IP68 ratings, prolonged submersion or damaged cable glands can allow water into the transformer housing, leading to insulation breakdown and earth leakage.

Incorrect Transformer Rating

Using a transformer with the wrong turns ratio or current rating for the fixture type can cause under- or over-driving, reducing lamp life or causing premature failure.

Connector and Cable Joint Degradation

The connections between the series cable and transformer primary, and between the transformer secondary and fixture, are common points of failure — they are addressed in detail in the AGL Connectors page.

Earth Loop and Leakage Currents

Poor earthing or insulation breakdown can cause stray currents that trip CCR earth-leakage protection, requiring systematic insulation-resistance testing of the entire series circuit.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
A transformer primary winding has failed open, or the series cable/L-823 splice feeding it has been severed or corroded, breaking the continuous loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR's open-circuit fault indication first; perform a loop-resistance test from the CCR output and sectionalise the circuit, meggering cable runs between pits to isolate the open point.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed transformer primary or repair the faulted cable/connector, then re-test loop continuity and insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
A secondary-side open — a failed lamp/LED module or a broken secondary lead — with the transformer primary still intact and passing loop current on to the next fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the primary winding shows continuity and normal impedance; check secondary voltage at the fixture connector with the CCR energised; inspect the secondary lead and L-823 connector for damage.
Corrective Action
Replace the fixture lamp/LED module if the transformer secondary reads correctly; otherwise replace the transformer and re-seal the connectors before backfill.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground/Earth Fault
Likely Cause
Insulation breakdown between a transformer's primary and secondary windings, or moisture ingress in a pit, creating a leakage path or partial short on the loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full series circuit primary-to-earth to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect transformer pits for standing water, cracked encapsulant, or damaged housings.
Corrective Action
Replace the affected transformer, clear or improve pit drainage, renew watertight connectors, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Fixture Under- or Over-Driven (Dim, Overheating, or Short Lamp Life)
Likely Cause
Incorrect transformer turns ratio or rated wattage for the connected fixture, or a shifted turns ratio from age/damage, resulting in the wrong secondary voltage.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure secondary voltage against the value expected for the transformer's wattage rating and turns ratio at the primary current step in use; verify the transformer's rated wattage matches the installed fixture; check as-built records for a rating mismatch.
Corrective Action
Replace with a correctly rated transformer matching the fixture's turns ratio and wattage, and re-verify secondary voltage across all intensity steps.
Fixture Flickering or Intermittent
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded primary/secondary terminal connections at the transformer, or a marginal winding developing a partial short under thermal load.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque primary and secondary terminal connections in the pit; check crimps for corrosion; monitor secondary voltage for drop or instability once the CCR has warmed the loop to steady state.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded connections with new crimps and sealant, or replace the transformer if voltage instability under load is confirmed.
Transformer Overheating or Housing Discoloration
Likely Cause
A developing winding short, a mismatched fixture load drawing excess secondary current, or restricted heat dissipation from a waterlogged or poorly ventilated pit.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the encapsulant or housing for deformation or discoloration; verify fixture load compatibility against the transformer's rated wattage; check the pit for drainage issues restricting cooling.
Corrective Action
Replace an overheating transformer showing physical degradation, correct any load mismatch, and improve pit drainage to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Single fixture dark while the rest of the circuit remains normal, or the entire series circuit dark if the fault extends to the loop itself.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation or ground traffic, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run.
Effect
CCR trips on overcurrent or ground/earth fault, and the entire series circuit goes dark until the fault is located and repaired.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Transformer Turns-Ratio Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
An incorrect transformer substituted during replacement, or a winding fault shifting the effective turns ratio from its rated value.
Effect
Fixture under- or over-driven, showing up as dimming, overheating, or a shortened lamp/LED life on that circuit position.
Detection Method
Turns-ratio testing of the transformer against its rated specification during scheduled maintenance.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Fixture flickering or intermittent operation, tracing back to a degraded connection rather than the fixture itself.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors during scheduled maintenance.
Transformer Overheating
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Sustained operation near or above rated load, restricted ventilation in the housing, or insulation ageing reducing thermal margin.
Effect
Transformer overheating or housing discolouration, an early warning sign that can precede a winding failure if not addressed.
Detection Method
Thermal imaging of transformers under load, and visual inspection for discolouration during scheduled maintenance.
Loop Resistance Creep
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual accumulation of minor connection resistance increases across multiple splices and terminations along a long series loop.
Effect
Loop resistance trending upward over successive maintenance cycles, narrowing the CCR's regulation margin even before any single fault appears.
Detection Method
Trend review of loop resistance measurements across successive maintenance visits to catch a slow drift pattern.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

AGL series circuits can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy before opening a transformer housing or handling cable connectors.

PPE & Insulated Tools
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm bonding/grounding is intact before reconnecting the loop.

Allow Cool-Down Before Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow the transformer housing to cool before handling after an active period, particularly on high-output-step circuits where the potting compound and casing can retain heat.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected series loop after any transformer, connector, or cable work before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy equipment on the circuit.

Confirm Turns-Ratio Before Installation
Operational

Verify the transformer's turns ratio matches the fixture's rated lamp/LED current before installation; a mismatched ratio can under- or over-drive the connected fixture.

FOD Control at the Housing
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and potting or gasket material accounted for at all times when the transformer housing is open, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify loop resistance and insulation resistance are within tolerance before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED fixture is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual load rather than assuming the existing transformer is "close enough."

Trend Loop Readings, Don't Just Spot-Check
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at a connector usually shows up first as a slow drift in loop insulation resistance, not a hard fault. Logging readings over successive maintenance cycles catches a failing splice months before it takes the loop down.

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or transformer counts between loops can produce uneven current distribution and brightness even though each fixture tests within tolerance individually. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

Let a Field-Pulled Transformer Reach Ambient Before Testing
Technique

An insulation resistance reading taken on a transformer still warm from recent operation tends to read lower than its true cold value. Where a reading is borderline, let the unit cool and retest before condemning it based on a single warm reading.

Moisture Skews Readings More Than Winding Damage
Diagnostics

Low insulation resistance on a field-pulled transformer is more often surface moisture inside the housing than actual winding insulation breakdown. Where practical, dry the unit and retest before writing it off as failed.

Keep a Per-Serial Test History, Not Just a Loop Log
Process

Transformers get swapped between circuits during maintenance, so tying test results to the transformer's serial number rather than only its loop position preserves the trend data that actually predicts failure, even after the unit is relocated.
