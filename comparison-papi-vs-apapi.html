Visual Approach Slope Guidance Comparison

PAPI VS APAPI

Same optical principle, different scale. PAPI's four-unit row gives the pilot five graduated indications of vertical position; APAPI abbreviates that to two units and three indications. Neither is "more precise" in the ILS sense — both are visual approach-slope indicators, sized differently for the installation.

02 · Definitions

What Is PAPI & What Is APAPI?

PAPI

A PAPI normally consists of four light units arranged in a horizontal row. Each unit is optically set to a slightly different elevation angle, so the pilot sees different combinations of white and red as the aircraft moves above or below the desired approach path.

APAPI

APAPI is an abbreviated version of the PAPI concept, using two light units instead of four. The two units are optically set to provide the required visual approach-path indication, retaining the same fundamental principle with fewer fixtures.

03 · Visual Indication Logic

How Each System Signals Position

Both read as a white/red count across a single row — PAPI just has twice as many units, and twice as many graduated states.

PAPI

4 units — 5 principal indications
4 White — too high
2 White + 2 Red — on path
4 Red — too low

APAPI

2 units — 3 principal indications
2 White — too high
1 White + 1 Red — on path
2 Red — too low

The missing intermediate states — 3W/1R and 1W/3R on a PAPI — are the main reason APAPI provides less detailed information than a full four-unit installation.

05 · Why "Abbreviated"?

Fewer Units, Same Principle

APAPI uses fewer light units while retaining the fundamental PAPI principle — the reduction from four units to two means less equipment, less wiring, lower installation complexity, and a lower maintenance burden. It also means less detailed visual information: three states instead of five.

"Abbreviated" refers to the configuration — the number of light units — not to the quality or reliability of the fixtures themselves.

06 · Electrical System

Shared Architecture, Different Scale

Both systems follow the same basic electrical concept: power supply → CCR / series circuit → isolation transformers → light units. APAPI simply terminates at two units instead of four, so it generally requires fewer fixtures and associated components.

  • Intensity control
  • Monitoring and fault indication
  • Remote control
  • ALCMS interface, depending on the installation
07 · Maintenance & Alignment

Fewer Fixtures, Same Alignment Discipline

PAPI

Four units require inspection of light source, lens, optical system, alignment, elevation setting, azimuth, housing, electrical connections and transformer/cable condition.

APAPI

The same types of checks apply, but with only two fixtures — so APAPI carries a lower fixture-maintenance burden overall.

Reducing four units to two does not make alignment unimportant. Correct elevation angle, azimuth, unit height and installation geometry remain fundamental — incorrect alignment on either system can produce a misleading indication.

08 · Approach Category

PAPI/APAPI Choice Does Not Set the Approach Category

The runway's operational requirements, approach procedure, obstacle-clearance considerations and applicable regulatory criteria determine the appropriate installation — not the other way around.

Avoid this oversimplification: "PAPI = precision approach, APAPI = non-precision approach." Both PAPI and APAPI are visual approach slope indicators; neither designation by itself determines the approach category.

09 · Where APAPI Fits

When an Abbreviated System Is Attractive

An APAPI may be appropriate where a full four-unit PAPI installation is not necessary, or where an abbreviated installation is specified by the applicable design criteria.

  • Smaller installation footprint
  • Fewer light units and less equipment
  • Lower wiring requirement
  • Reduced maintenance burden

The actual selection must follow the applicable airport design criteria rather than simply choosing the cheaper option.

10 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: APAPI is just a PAPI with two lights removed
Fact

No. APAPI is a defined abbreviated system with its own optical and installation requirements.

Myth: APAPI gives exactly the same information as PAPI
Fact

No. PAPI has additional intermediate indications that APAPI's two-unit arrangement does not provide.

Myth: PAPI provides lateral guidance
Fact

No. Its primary function is vertical approach-path guidance only.

Myth: Four red PAPI lights means "on path"
Fact

No — 4 Red means too low, not on path.

Myth: APAPI means "low precision"
Fact

No. "Abbreviated" refers to the configuration — the number of light units — not the quality of the lights.

12 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI vs APAPI

Engineering Verdict

APAPI is not a lower-precision PAPI — it is the same optical principle scaled down. Four units give more graduated visual information; two units give a simpler, abbreviated read using the identical white/red logic.

Neither PAPI nor APAPI replaces an ILS, and the choice between them should follow the applicable airport design criteria and operational requirements — not be treated as a shortcut for approach category.

PAPI4 units + 5 indications + more equipment and wiring.
APAPI2 units + 3 indications + smaller, simpler installation.

Both systems answer the same operational question — is the aircraft on the correct visual approach slope — PAPI simply answers it with finer graduation.

13 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between PAPI and APAPI?

PAPI uses four light units for five graduated indications; APAPI abbreviates this to two units and three indications, using the same white/red optical principle.

Does APAPI mean the runway only supports a non-precision approach?

No. PAPI vs APAPI selection does not by itself determine the approach category — that depends on the runway's operational requirements, procedures and applicable regulatory criteria.

What does the on-path indication look like on each system?

PAPI's on-path indication is 2 White + 2 Red. APAPI's on-path indication is 1 White + 1 Red.

Is alignment less critical on APAPI because it has fewer units?

No. Correct elevation angle, azimuth and installation geometry remain fundamental on both systems — incorrect alignment can produce a misleading indication regardless of unit count.

When would an APAPI be selected over a full PAPI?

Where a full four-unit installation is not necessary, or where the applicable airport design criteria specify an abbreviated system — the selection should follow those criteria rather than cost alone.

Can PAPI or APAPI replace an ILS?

No. Both are visual vertical approach-path aids. An ILS provides radio-based lateral guidance (localizer) and vertical guidance (glideslope) — a different function entirely.

14 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — visual approach slope indicator systems, including PAPI and APAPI configuration, siting and photometric requirements.

FAA AC 150/5345-28, Current Edition

Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) Systems (equipment designations L-880/L-881) — FAA equipment standards covering PAPI unit geometry, siting, electrical systems and photometric criteria; the same specification family covers APAPI as an abbreviated configuration.

IS 7785 / IS 12290 / IS 12291

IS 7785 is the multi-part Bureau of Indian Standards series for elevated-type aerodrome lighting fittings generally, rather than a dedicated PAPI/APAPI unit specification; IS 12290 covers isolating transformers and IS 12291 covers constant current regulators for airport lighting systems. Indian AGL tenders commonly call up the FAA/ICAO PAPI equipment specification for the light units themselves.

Final Engineering Note

Do not select PAPI or APAPI from a generic web diagram. The final siting, unit count and alignment must always be developed from the current governing standard, approved airport design and project-specific requirements.

APAPI is a scaled-down PAPI, not a lower-quality one. Either system, correctly aligned and maintained, gives the pilot a reliable visual reference for the designed approach slope.
