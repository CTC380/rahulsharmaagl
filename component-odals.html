ODALS

An omnidirectional approach lighting system that provides visual guidance to pilots during final approach using a series of low‑intensity, omnidirectional lights. Designed for smaller airfields and heliports, ODALS offers a cost‑effective solution for night and marginal weather operations, with lights that are visible from any direction, making it ideal for non‑precision approaches and facilities with limited infrastructure.

Overview

What Is It?

ODALS (Omnidirectional Approach Lighting System) is a simple, low‑cost approach lighting system that uses a series of omnidirectional lights to provide visual guidance to pilots during final approach. Unlike directional approach lights that project a narrow beam toward the approaching aircraft, ODALS fixtures emit light in all directions (360° horizontally), making them visible from any approach angle. This is particularly useful for smaller airfields, heliports, and facilities where the approach path may not be precisely aligned with a single direction. The system typically consists of a row of low‑intensity lights extending from the runway or helipad threshold, providing basic visual reference for night and marginal weather operations. ODALS is an economical alternative to more complex systems like SALS or MALS, offering simplicity and versatility for general aviation and remote airfields.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

ODALS exists to bring basic night and marginal-weather approach guidance to airfields that cannot justify, or physically accommodate, the cost and infrastructure of a directional precision lighting system, particularly smaller runways and heliports where traffic volume doesn't warrant that investment. Because its fixtures emit in all directions rather than along a single beam axis, the system remains useful even where the approach path isn't consistently aligned to one exact heading, which matters at facilities without a single dominant approach direction. Its purpose is therefore defined by what it deliberately trades away — the precise directional intensity and structured geometry of ALSF- or Calvert-style systems — in exchange for low cost and flexibility, making it a baseline safety improvement rather than a precision approach aid.

Application

Where Is It Used?

ODALS systems are installed at a variety of facilities where a basic, omnidirectional approach lighting solution is needed. They are typically used on:

  • Small general aviation airports with limited budgets and infrastructure
  • Heliports where approach paths are variable and omnidirectional visibility is beneficial
  • Remote and rural airfields with low traffic volumes
  • Military forward operating bases where rapid deployment and simplicity are priorities
  • Emergency and temporary landing sites where portable lighting is required
  • Non‑precision runways where basic visual guidance is sufficient
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are mounted on lightweight frangible poles set out along the extended runway or helipad centreline. The FAA standard configuration uses five centreline lights at 91 m (300 ft) intervals, the first sited 91 m (300 ft) from the threshold and the last at 457 m (1,500 ft), plus two flashers flanking the threshold itself. Because the omnidirectional beam removes the need for precise aiming, stands can be set out quickly with minimal survey work, and the low-wattage fixtures place a light load on cabling, allowing simple duct or direct-buried runs back to a compact regulator or control point. This lower installation demand is what makes ODALS suited to unprepared ground, remote sites, and temporary or emergency lighting, where a full-length precision approach system would not be practical to install.

Function

How Does It Work?

The ODALS system provides basic visual guidance through a simple row of omnidirectional lights:

  • Omnidirectional Lights: Each light fixture emits light uniformly in all horizontal directions, making them visible from any approach angle. This is achieved through a simple lens or globe that disperses light 360°.
  • Single Row: Five lights extend from 91m (300 ft) to 457m (1,500 ft) from the runway or helipad threshold at 91m (300 ft) intervals, providing a visual centreline and distance reference.
  • Low Intensity: ODALS lights are typically low‑intensity (e.g., 10‑50 cd) to avoid glare and to keep power consumption low, making them suitable for battery or solar‑powered installations.
  • Colour: Typically white or amber, though some variants use green or blue for specific applications.
  • Simplicity: No sequenced flashing, no crossbars, and no complex control systems. The lights are steady‑burning and often controlled by a simple on/off switch or photocell.
  • Versatility: Because the lights are omnidirectional, pilots can approach from any direction and still see the lights, making ODALS ideal for heliports and airfields with multiple approach paths.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

ODALS is architecturally the odd one out in the approach lighting family: it does not run on a constant-current series circuit loop with CCR-stepped brightness at all. Because the fixtures are individually low-wattage and often self-contained (mains, battery, or solar), they are typically wired on a simple constant-voltage or individually-fused circuit rather than a series loop, and switched as a group by a straightforward control point — a pilot-controlled lighting (PCL) relay triggered by radio keying, a photocell responding to ambient light, or a manual switch at the tower or site cabinet — rather than an ALCMS issuing a graduated intensity command. There is generally no multi-step brightness selection: the lights are either on at their fixed low intensity or off, which keeps the control chain deliberately minimal for a system meant to be deployed quickly on unprepared ground or as temporary and emergency lighting. This also changes how faults are found. Without a CCR monitoring loop voltage against a commanded current, there is no equivalent of loop-level fault reporting; a failed fixture is typically found by visual inspection, periodic patrol, or (on more capable installations) an individual fixture status/health indicator reporting back to the site controller, rather than by a single instrument flagging an anomaly across the whole array.

PCL Relay / Photocell / Manual Switch → Individually-Fused or Constant-Voltage Circuit → Omnidirectional Fixture (steady, on/off — no CCR loop)

Construction

Physical Construction

Each fixture is a low-wattage LED or halogen lamp housed in a compact weatherproof enclosure mounted atop a lightweight frangible pole or tripod, rated to at least IP65 and designed for quick assembly with minimal foundation work — a shallow ground stake, screw anchor, or portable base plate is typically sufficient given the low mass and omnidirectional optics involved. The omnidirectional lens or diffuser is a moulded polycarbonate dome that requires no aiming, simplifying both manufacture and field setup compared to the precision optics used in directional approach fixtures. Where mains power is unavailable, the fixture and its stand are built to accept a battery and solar panel with charge controller mounted directly on the pole, making the entire assembly self-contained and relocatable. Cabling, where used, is lightweight duct or direct-buried cable run back to a compact regulator or control point, and stainless-steel or corrosion-resistant fasteners are used throughout given the system's frequent use at remote or temporary sites with limited maintenance access.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Pole or Tripod Foundation
Civil

Set each frangible pole on a shallow ground stake, screw anchor, or portable base plate as the site calls for, since the low fixture mass needs minimal foundation work.

Minimal Survey Setting-Out
Civil

Set out fixture positions at the 91 m (300 ft) spacing along the extended centreline with a quick field survey, since the omnidirectional beam removes the need for precise aiming.

Duct or Direct-Buried Cable Route
Electrical

Run lightweight duct or direct-buried cable from each fixture back to the compact regulator or control point, sized for the low-wattage load of the array.

Cable Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal each fixture's supply connection per manufacturer procedure, keeping the joint weatherproof before the pole is finally fixed in place.

Battery / Solar Assembly Where Unpowered
Electrical

Where mains power is unavailable, mount the battery, solar panel, and charge controller on the pole and verify the self-contained assembly before deployment.

Fixture & Pole Assembly
Mechanical

Fix the weatherproof fixture housing to the frangible pole or tripod, confirming the mount is stable and the omnidirectional lens is unobstructed.

Layout Confirmation
Mechanical

Confirm fixture spacing and total run length against the site's chosen configuration (typically 200–600 m), adjusting for unprepared or uneven ground as needed.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record fixture positions, cable or battery configuration, and connector seals as-built before the array is finally commissioned.

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test each fixture and its supply cabling before backfill or final fixing, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of first energisation.

Power Source Continuity & Load Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity and correct loading on the mains, battery, or solar-with-charge-controller supply feeding each fixture, confirming the source can sustain rated output overnight where solar/battery-powered.

On/Off & Photocell Response Verification
Energisation

Confirm each fixture switches correctly via its photocell or manual on/off control, and that any 2-step dimming units respond correctly to a step command.

Omnidirectional Beam Verification
Energisation

Confirm each fixture produces a genuinely omnidirectional (360° horizontal) beam with no shadowing or obstruction from the mounting pole, tripod, or nearby structures.

Fixture Spacing & Layout Verification
Final Acceptance

Check the 30–60m fixture spacing and overall configuration length against the site-specific design layout, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Frangible Mount Verification
Final Acceptance

Where fixtures are elevated on frangible poles or tripods, confirm mounting height and frangibility meet as-built tolerances for the approach or heliport environment.

Photometric Commissioning Test
Final Acceptance

Measure luminous intensity (typically 10–50 cd) and colour with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances for the selected colour and application.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, power-source, switching, and photometric readings alongside as-built fixture positions in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Lens/Globe Cleaning
Quarterly

Wash each globe or lens with mild soap and water to remove dust, rain deposits, and bird droppings, since any contamination significantly reduces the omnidirectional beam's already-low output.

Battery & Solar System Check
Semi-annual

Test battery condition, charge controller function, and solar panel output on off-grid installations, since ageing batteries and undersized panels are a common cause of dimming or failure at night.

Photocell / Intensity Verification
Annual

Confirm the photocell or dimming step responds correctly and that output stays within the specified low-intensity range appropriate to the operation.

Frangible Coupling Inspection
Annual

Check elevated poles and tripod couplings for cracking or breakage, particularly after high winds, since a broken coupling can cause the fixture to lean or fall.

Site Survey & Vegetation Clearance
Semi-annual

Walk the site to confirm grass, bushes, or structures haven't grown up to block the 360° beam, particularly important at smaller, less-maintained airfields.

Mounting Stability Check
Semi-annual

Verify portable or tripod-mounted units remain level and securely anchored, since ODALS is often deployed at unpaved or temporary sites more prone to settling.

Wiring & Weatherproofing Check
Annual

Inspect cable connections and enclosure seals for water ingress, especially on mains-powered units, to keep the IP65+ rating intact.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination and Dirt

Omnidirectional lights are exposed to dust, rain, and bird droppings. Since they rely on a clear globe or lens to disperse light 360°, any contamination can significantly reduce light output. Regular cleaning with mild soap and water is essential.

Battery or Solar Power Failure

Many ODALS installations are off‑grid, using battery or solar power. Inadequate solar panel sizing, battery ageing, or charge controller failures can cause lights to dim or fail during critical nighttime operations. Regular testing and battery replacement are recommended.

Frangible Coupling Breakage

Elevated ODALS lights are typically mounted on frangible poles. In high winds or accidental impact, the couplings may break, causing the light to fall or lean. Regular inspection and replacement of broken couplings are necessary.

Vegetation and Obstruction Growth

At smaller airfields, grass, bushes, or structures can grow up around the lights, blocking the omnidirectional beam. Regular site maintenance and vegetation control are essential.

Low Intensity Limitations

ODALS lights are low‑intensity and may not be visible in moderate fog or heavy rain. For operations in adverse weather, a higher‑intensity system (e.g., MALS) may be required.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Others Normal
Likely Cause
Blown individual fuse, failed lamp/LED, or (on a self-contained unit) a battery that has fully discharged.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the fixture's local fuse or breaker; test lamp/LED continuity; on battery/solar units, check panel output and battery state of charge.
Corrective Action
Replace the fuse, lamp/LED module, or battery as required; confirm the solar panel is unobstructed and charging correctly.
Entire Row Fails to Illuminate at Dusk or on Activation
Likely Cause
Failed photocell, PCL relay, or manual switch at the control point — since ODALS has no CCR loop to flag the fault centrally, the control point itself is the first suspect.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the photocell is switching correctly, or test PCL radio-key activation; check relay contacts and incoming supply at the control point.
Corrective Action
Replace the faulty photocell or relay, or restore the PCL link; confirm the whole row illuminates on manual override.
Fixture Dim or Flickering
Likely Cause
Aging LED array/lamp, or, on solar units, a battery nearing end of life and unable to hold a full charge overnight.
Diagnostic Steps
Check output against rated intensity; test battery capacity and charge/discharge cycling on solar units; inspect wiring for loose connections.
Corrective Action
Replace the lamp/LED module or the battery, and re-terminate any loose connections found.
Fixture Leaning or Fallen
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling has broken from wind loading, vehicle strike, or wildlife contact.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the coupling and mounting base; check for wiring damage where the fixture has moved.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling, re-level and re-secure the fixture, and repair or replace any damaged cable before re-energising.
Reduced Beam Visibility / Hazy Output
Likely Cause
Dust, dirt, or bird droppings on the globe or lens is reducing the 360° dispersion the omnidirectional design relies on.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the globe/lens for contamination and compare output to a known-clean fixture nearby.
Corrective Action
Clean the globe/lens with mild soap and water per the maintenance schedule; replace it if the lens has yellowed or cracked.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised enclosure seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation in the individual fixture.
Effect
The affected fixture goes dark or dims, leaving a gap in the omnidirectional row that pilots rely on regardless of approach angle.
Detection Method
Visual patrol at dusk or on activation, and a local continuity/output check at the fixture.
Battery / Solar Charge Controller Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Undersized solar panel for the site's latitude and shading, battery ageing and reduced charge capacity, or a failed charge controller on an off-grid installation.
Effect
Fixture dims or fails outright overnight once stored charge is depleted, most likely on the longest nights or after a run of overcast days.
Detection Method
Test battery state of charge and capacity, verify solar panel output against expected charging current, and confirm the charge controller is regulating correctly.
Individual Fuse / Wiring Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A blown local fuse or breaker from a transient surge, or a loose/corroded wiring termination inside the compact weatherproof enclosure.
Effect
Single fixture fails to energise even though its power source (mains, battery, or solar) is otherwise healthy.
Detection Method
Check the fixture's local fuse or breaker, and inspect wiring terminations inside the enclosure for looseness or corrosion.
Frangible Pole or Mount Fatigue / Breakage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wind loading on the lightweight frangible pole or tripod, wildlife or vehicle contact, or fatigue at the base stake/screw anchor given the shallow foundation typical of ODALS installs.
Effect
Fixture leans, tips over, or separates from its base, taking the light out of service or out of its designed omnidirectional exposure.
Detection Method
Visual and physical inspection of the pole, tripod, or base plate for cracking, leaning, or looseness during routine patrol.
Omnidirectional Lens / Diffuser Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure, abrasive particulate erosion, or grime build-up on the moulded polycarbonate dome.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output that weakens the 360° visibility the omnidirectional lens is designed to provide from any approach angle.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the dome for haze or discolouration during routine maintenance, and an output check against rated intensity.
PCL Relay / Photocell Control Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A failed pilot-controlled lighting relay, a stuck or drifted photocell threshold, or a fault at the manual switch/site control point that activates the row as a group.
Effect
The entire row fails to illuminate on radio keying, at dusk, or on manual activation, even though individual fixtures are otherwise healthy.
Detection Method
Test the PCL relay response to radio keying, check photocell trip threshold against ambient light, and verify the manual switch or site controller activates the group circuit.
Corrosion at Connections
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Use of standard rather than corrosion-resistant fasteners and terminations at a remote or temporary site with limited maintenance access, combined with prolonged weather exposure.
Effect
Progressive increase in contact resistance leads to dimming, flickering, or eventual failure at the affected connection point.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of fasteners and wiring terminations for corrosion during scheduled site visits, particularly at temporary or infrequently serviced installations.
Vegetation Encroachment Blocking Omnidirectional Visibility
Low Severity
Root Cause
Unmanaged grass, brush, or crop growth around a low-profile fixture at a remote or unprepared site.
Effect
Vegetation partially or fully obscures the fixture from one or more approach angles, undermining the omnidirectional visibility the system is meant to provide.
Detection Method
Site survey and vegetation clearance check during routine patrol, particularly at sites without a formal grounds-maintenance schedule.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Omnidirectional Flasher Capacitor Discharge
Electrical Safety

Each omnidirectional flashing unit has its own condenser discharge unit that stores a high-energy charge even after the master controller is switched off; discharge it through its bleed resistor and confirm zero voltage before opening a flasher head.

Series or Branch Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

ODALS units are fed from a series or branch circuit that can carry substantial voltage. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR or panel, then verify zero energy at the base before opening any enclosure.

Off-Runway Ground Access
Personnel Safety

ODALS units are typically ground-mounted in the approach area beyond the runway end, often on uneven or unpaved terrain; assess ground conditions before positioning vehicles or personnel near a unit.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible mount with a rigid fitting, and do not over-torque connections during installation or repair; the mount must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Work on approach lighting affects an active runway's usable minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any unit is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

FOD Control at the Unit
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every unit worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or flasher unit work, megger test the affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the regulator or damage healthy units on the same circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a unit dark, mistimed, or out of sequence with the flashing pattern; verify flash rate and sequence conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Size Solar and Battery Capacity to Worst-Case, Not Average, Conditions
Power

An off-grid ODALS installation sized against average sun-hours can leave the system marginal through a run of overcast days right when reliable nighttime operation matters most. Size for a realistic worst-case autonomy period, not a seasonal average.

Battery Ageing Is Gradual and Easy to Miss Until It Isn't
Durability

Lead-acid or lithium battery capacity fades gradually over years of charge cycles, so a system that's been 'working fine' can be much closer to failure than it appears. Track actual capacity test results over time rather than relying on the lights simply still turning on.

Frangible Coupling Condition Matters More Than Usual Here
Mechanical

Because ODALS is often deployed at smaller or remote airfields with less frequent maintenance visits, a cracked or partially sheared frangible coupling can go unnoticed longer than at a major airport. Build coupling inspection into every site visit, not a separate dedicated trip.

Omnidirectional Output Degrades Differently Than Directional Beam Output
Photometric

Because ODALS relies on an even 360° light dispersion rather than a focused beam, partial lens contamination or a cracked globe can create an uneven, direction-dependent dimming that's easy to miss unless checked from multiple approach angles, not just from directly in front of the fixture.

Remote Sites Justify Remote Monitoring Where Available
Troubleshooting

Given the travel time to many ODALS locations, a telemetry or remote-monitoring option that flags a failing battery or dimming light before a scheduled visit can prevent a light being dark for weeks between routine checks.

Match Inspection Frequency to Site Accessibility, Not a Generic Schedule
Maintenance Planning

Remote and seasonal-access sites need an inspection interval that accounts for actual reachability — including winter access constraints — rather than the same fixed schedule used for lights that see maintenance staff daily.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about ODALS (Omnidirectional Approach Lighting System): layout, use, power, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is ODALS?

ODALS (Omnidirectional Approach Lighting System) is a simple, low-cost approach lighting system using omnidirectional lights that emit light uniformly in all horizontal directions, so they are visible from any approach angle.

Q2How is an ODALS laid out?

The page describes five lights on the extended centreline from 91 m (300 ft) to 457 m (1,500 ft) from the threshold, at 91 m (300 ft) intervals.

Q3Where is ODALS used?

At small general aviation airports with limited budgets, heliports with variable approach paths, remote and rural airfields with low traffic, and military forward operating bases where fast deployment and simplicity matter.

Q4Can ODALS run on solar or battery power?

Yes. The lights are low power, which makes battery or solar-powered installations suitable, and many are off-grid.

Q5What does omnidirectional mean for an approach light?

The fixture disperses light through 360° of azimuth with a simple lens or globe, so pilots see it from any direction rather than only along the approach axis.

Q6Which standards apply to ODALS?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46 where applicable, IEC TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief 67 for LED components. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q7How often should ODALS be maintained?

Typically quarterly globe or lens cleaning, semi-annual battery and solar system checks on off-grid installations, and annual photocell and intensity verification. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q8What are the common problems with ODALS?

Lens contamination, battery or solar power failure, frangible coupling breakage on elevated fixtures, and vegetation or obstruction growth around the lights at smaller airfields.
