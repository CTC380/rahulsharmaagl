Overview

What Is an Airport Isolating Transformer?

The interface between the series primary circuit and the individual fixture.

An airport isolating transformer is a key component of a series-circuit Airfield Ground Lighting system. It electrically separates the primary series circuit from the individual light fixture while transferring the required power to it. It is used for runway, taxiway, approach and other airfield lighting.

In FAA terminology, series-to-series isolation transformers are classified L-830 for 60 Hz and L-831 for 50 Hz, with a numerical suffix identifying wattage and current arrangement. FAA AC 150/5345-47C (22 July 2011) is the specification, and the FAA lists it as Active.

Principle

Why Isolation, and How It Works

Fixtures cannot simply be connected across a long primary series circuit.

AC in primary winding
Alternating flux in core
Voltage induced in secondary
Power to the fixture

The primary winding is connected into the series circuit driven by the CCR; the secondary winding supplies the individual fixture. The windings stay electrically separate while energy transfers through the core. The primary side is part of the continuous series circuit, and every lighting point has its own transformer.

Primary side

  • CCR output and primary cable
  • Primary connectors
  • Series-circuit current
  • High-voltage insulation requirements

Secondary side

  • Secondary cable and connector
  • Light fixture
  • Fixture operating voltage / current
  • Fixture power

Under AC 150/5345-47C the primary winding is rated 5,000 V and the secondary winding 600 V.

Identify the transformer type

Choose the three values from the nameplate or design data. Wattage alone never identifies a transformer.

Why wattage alone is not enough. Both L-830-4 and L-830-5 are 100 W, but one is 6.6 A primary and the other 20 A primary. Both L-830-8 and L-830-10 are 300 W, but their secondaries are 20 A and 6.6 A. Correct identification needs wattage + primary current + secondary current + frequency + type.

Current Ratings

6.6 A and 20 A Transformers

Match the circuit, the fixture and the transformer, not just the CCR label.

6.6 / 6.6 A types

L-830-1, 3, 4, 6, 10, 14, 16, 17, 18. 6.6 A is the most common series-circuit current. A "6.6 A CCR" still does not by itself determine the correct transformer — fixture wattage and type must match.

20 / 6.6 A types

L-830-2, 5, 7, 11, 15, 19. A 20 A primary supplies a 6.6 A fixture-side load, in 30/45, 100, 200, 300, 500 and 150 W classes.

20 A secondary types

L-830-8 and 12 (6.6 A primary) and L-830-9 and 13 (20 A primary) have a 20 A secondary.

Selection

Transformer Wattage Selection

Provide adequate capacity for the fixture and its operating characteristics.

Factors include fixture power, lamp aging, secondary lead losses, contact losses, transformer efficiency, power factor, operating temperature, fixture technology and manufacturer requirements. AC 150/5345-47C specifically requires enough capacity to allow for lamp aging, secondary lead losses and contact losses.

Insulation

5 kV Primary Insulation

Why AGL transformers need purpose-designed construction.

5,000 V primary

Primary winding voltage rating; primary leads use cable insulated for not less than 5,000 V.

600 V secondary

Secondary winding voltage rating.

Buried and submerged

Must operate buried and submerged with up to 5,000 V between primary winding and ground.

Do not apply an arbitrary megger voltage because a transformer is described as "5 kV". The voltage rating and a field insulation-resistance test voltage are different parameters; the approved test procedure sets the field test voltage.

For context, the AC's qualification and production tests use DC for 1 minute between each winding and ground, with the transformer heated and water-soaked: 15 kV DC on the primary and 5 kV DC on the secondary, with minimum resistance of 7,500 MΩ / 2,500 MΩ up to 150 W and 3,000 MΩ / 1,000 MΩ above 150 W (or the specified leakage-current limits). These are factory/qualification values, not field acceptance values.

Construction

Construction, Encapsulation & Watertight Design

Designed for demanding outdoor and underground service.

Windings and core

Electrically separate primary and secondary coils on a core, insulated from each other and the core. Capable of indefinite operation with the secondary short- or open-circuited at rated primary current.

Watertight case

Permanently sealed, no cracks, blisters or holes, case at least 0.25 in (6.35 mm) thick, moisture entering a lead cannot track along it into the windings.

Encapsulation

Core and coil assembly encapsulated with no internal air pockets; case material at least black cross-linked polychloroprene (TPE/TPV acceptable), durometer 75 ± 10.

Environment

Continuous outdoor service from −55 to +65 °C; withstands sunlight, oil, gasoline, water, runway deicing fluids and acid/alkaline soils over a 10-year service life.

Orientation

Operates in any orientation; fits a cylinder 8 in (20.3 cm) in diameter by 10 in (25.4 cm) high.

Mechanical robustness

Withstands shipping and installation; lead junction must not crack when carried by one lead; shock and lead-rigidity tests apply.

Water ingress is among the most important concerns in buried AGL systems. Causes include a damaged connector or case, improper sealing, lead damage, impact and long-term degradation. Effects: insulation deterioration, leakage current, corrosion, intermittent operation and winding failure. A transformer showing signs of water ingress should be investigated before it is returned to service.

Electrical Characteristics

Impedance, Efficiency & Power Factor

Two transformers of the same wattage are not necessarily interchangeable.

Leakage reactance

Magnetic flux that does not link both windings. Transformer impedance = resistance + leakage reactance; it influences voltage drop, current under load, short-circuit current, power loss and circuit behaviour.

Efficiency

AC 150/5345-47C sets minimum efficiency by type: 70% (10/15 W and 20/25 W), 80% (30/45 W and 65 W), 85% (100 W and 150 W) and 90% (200 W and above).

Power factor

Minimum 0.95 for all listed types under the specified test conditions. Evaluate together with efficiency, loading, operating current and fixture type.

Winding design, impedance, efficiency, power factor, secondary voltage characteristics and short-circuit behaviour may differ and can influence the whole series circuit. Replace a transformer on the basis of the approved type and electrical characteristics, not physical size or wattage. The AC also gives secondary full-load current, short-circuit current, load ohms and maximum open-circuit voltage for each type.

LED

LED AGL and Isolation Transformers

Replacing an incandescent fixture with LED does not automatically keep the old transformer valid.

LED and incandescent fixtures can differ substantially in power consumption, current characteristics, power factor, startup behaviour and electronic load. Review the complete system: CCR → series cable → isolation transformer → LED fixture, and confirm compatibility with the fixture's characteristics and the approved AGL design.

Substitution

Can Any Transformer Be Used With an Airport Light?

No.

An ordinary electrical transformer must not be substituted for an AGL isolation transformer. It must suit series-circuit operation, required current, fixture wattage, environment, insulation requirements, connector system, installation arrangement, applicable certification and project requirements. For FAA projects, AC 150/5345-47C is the transformer specification and AC 150/5345-53 provides the airport lighting equipment certification programme.

Standards

Standards and Applicability

FAA AC 150/5345-47C — Specification for Series to Series Isolation Transformers for Airport Lighting Systems (22 July 2011; FAA status Active). It covers L-830 (60 Hz) and L-831 (50 Hz). Related documents are AC 150/5345-26 (L-823 plug and receptacle connectors) and AC 150/5345-53 (Airport Lighting Equipment Certification Program).

Use of the AC is generally recommended; it is mandatory for projects funded through the AIP and PFC programs. L-830 and L-831 are FAA classifications — the applicable transformer standard at a given airport depends on the governing aviation authority, project specification and equipment-approval requirements. For non-FAA projects review ICAO requirements, the national authority's requirements, the airport specification and project documentation.

Pitfalls

8 Common Mistakes

1. Selecting only by wattage

Wattage alone does not identify the transformer.

2. Ignoring primary current

6.6 A and 20 A units can share a wattage but suit different circuits.

3. Ignoring frequency

A 50 Hz unit is not automatically equivalent to a 60 Hz unit.

4. Using an ordinary transformer

AGL transformers are purpose-designed for airport series circuits.

5. Ignoring connector compatibility

A similar-looking connector does not make it electrically or mechanically suitable.

6. Ignoring environment

Buried units need water, chemical and mechanical resistance.

7. Replacing without checking the fixture

Select as part of the complete fixture circuit.

8. Testing only the transformer

Consider CCR, cable, transformer, connector and fixture together.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is an airport isolating transformer?

A purpose-designed transformer used in an AGL series circuit to electrically isolate the primary series circuit from an individual light fixture while transferring electrical power to the fixture.

Q2What is an AGL isolation transformer?

An isolation transformer specifically designed for Airfield Ground Lighting series circuits.

Q3What is a runway lighting transformer?

An AGL isolation transformer used to supply a runway lighting fixture from the airport series circuit.

Q4What is an airport lighting transformer?

A transformer designed for the electrical requirements of airport visual-aid lighting systems, particularly series-circuit AGL installations.

Q5What is an L-830 transformer?

L-830 is the FAA designation for a 60 Hz series-to-series isolation transformer used in airport lighting systems.

Q6What is an L-831 transformer?

L-831 is the FAA designation for a 50 Hz series-to-series isolation transformer used in airport lighting systems.

Q7What is a 6.6/6.6 A isolation transformer?

A transformer with a 6.6 A primary current arrangement and a 6.6 A secondary current arrangement under the applicable transformer specification.

Q8What is a 20/6.6 A airport transformer?

A transformer configured for a 20 A primary and 6.6 A secondary current arrangement.

Q9Why are 6.6 A transformers used in airport lighting?

6.6 A is a common series-circuit current in AGL systems. Specific transformer ratings must still match the fixture and circuit requirements.

Q10Can a 20 A transformer be used on a 6.6 A circuit?

Not simply because the physical transformer looks similar. It must be specifically suitable for the intended circuit and application.

Q11What wattage airport isolation transformer do I need?

It depends on the light fixture, lamp or LED characteristics, secondary requirements, losses and the applicable specification. Select it from approved equipment data rather than by wattage alone.

Q12What does 5 kV mean on an airport isolation transformer?

In FAA AC 150/5345-47C the primary winding voltage rating is 5,000 V and the secondary winding rating is 600 V.

Q13Does a 5 kV transformer require a 5 kV insulation-resistance test?

Not necessarily. The voltage rating and the field insulation-resistance test voltage are different parameters; the approved test procedure must specify the test voltage.

Q14Can airport isolation transformers be buried?

Yes. AC 150/5345-47C requires the transformer to operate when buried and submerged, with runway deicing agents present.

Q15Why are airport isolation transformers watertight?

They may be installed underground and exposed to water, soil, deicing fluids and other environmental conditions.

Q16What is leakage reactance in an AGL transformer?

Part of transformer impedance, contributing to voltage drop and current behaviour and so to the transformer's electrical performance in the AGL circuit.

Q17What causes an airport isolation transformer to fail?

Insulation degradation, water ingress, connector failure, excessive loading, physical damage, incorrect selection, overheating and internal winding faults.

Q18Can a transformer cause a runway light to become dim?

Yes, but the CCR, cable, connectors, fixture and optical system should also be checked.

Q19How do you test an airport isolation transformer?

Depending on the procedure: visual inspection, continuity, insulation testing and electrical characteristic testing, followed by verification of fixture operation.

Q20Should an airport transformer be tested after replacement?

Yes. Apply the applicable commissioning and electrical tests, and verify lighting performance and photometry where required.

Q21What transformer is used with LED airport lights?

It depends on the specific LED fixture, series-circuit current and manufacturer requirements. Verify compatibility rather than assuming the incandescent-era transformer is suitable.
