AGL Cables, Connectors & Junction Infrastructure

The physical interconnectivity layer of the AGL system — primary and secondary cables, moulded connectors, splice kits, junction boxes, handholes, and the below-grade infrastructure that carries power from the CCR to every fixture, reliably and safely for decades.

Overview

What Are They?

AGL cables are the specialised power cables that carry the series current from the CCR to every isolating transformer and fixture. In FAA nomenclature they are designated L-824 underground electrical cable (AC 150/5345-7); the L-823 designation covers the connectors, not the cable itself. They are designed for underground, often submerged, installation with robust insulation (XLPE or EPR) and a metallic screen or armour for mechanical protection and earth continuity. Primary cables (typically 6 mm² or 10 mm² stranded copper) run between transformer pits; secondary cables (smaller gauge) connect the transformer to the fixture.

Connectors are the plug-and-socket or moulded joints that join cable sections to transformer leads and fixture leads — the L-823 family being the most widely used. They provide a watertight, low-resistance connection that must withstand thermal cycling and mechanical stress.

Junction infrastructure includes the handholes, pits, splice boxes, and cable glands that house and protect connections, facilitate maintenance access, and maintain the integrity of the buried cable network. Together, these elements form the complete power-distribution backbone of the airfield.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Because AGL circuits run as a series loop, this infrastructure's purpose is not just to deliver power to each fixture but to guarantee an unbroken, correctly insulated path through every cable, connector, and splice in the loop — a single failed joint or damaged cable can drop or unbalance the whole circuit rather than just the one fixture attached to it. That has to be achieved almost entirely out of sight and out of reach: buried, sometimes submerged, and rarely inspected directly, so the cables, connectors, and junction infrastructure are built to hold their insulation resistance, watertightness, and mechanical integrity for years of thermal cycling and ground movement without maintenance. Junction points (handholes, pits, splice boxes) exist specifically to give technicians a controlled way back into that otherwise-buried network when a fault does occur, so a single bad connection can be isolated and repaired without having to excavate the entire cable run.

Application

Where Are They Used?

This infrastructure is deployed across the entire airfield, from the CCR room to the furthest fixture:

  • Primary Series Cable: Runs from the CCR to each isolating transformer in a daisy-chain or loop configuration. Typically buried at a depth of 0.5–1.0 m. As a single-conductor circuit it does not require phase colour-coding; cable is usually supplied black with a printed legend identifying type, size, and rating, and individual circuits/loops are distinguished on site by tags, marker tape, and as-built records rather than insulation colour.
  • Secondary Cable: Connects the transformer secondary to the light fixture or sign, usually shorter runs within a fixture pit.
  • Connectors (L-823): Used at every transformer primary and secondary connection, and for cable-to-cable splices. Supplied as mated plug-and-receptacle pairs per FAA AC 150/5345-26 in six styles — Style 3/10 (Type I, single-conductor, 5000 V) for primary leads, and Style 4/11 (Type II, two-conductor, 600 V) or Style 5/12 (Type II, single-conductor, 600 V) for secondary leads — rather than as tee or elbow fittings.
  • Splice Kits: Used to join cable sections where a continuous run is not practical, or for repair after a cable cut.
  • Handholes / Pits: Below-grade chambers that house transformers, connectors, and splices. They provide access for installation, testing, and maintenance.
  • Cable Glands and Sealing Systems: Ensure watertight entry of cables into pits, connectors, and fixture housings.

The choice of cable type, connector style, and pit design is driven by the specific circuit (runway, taxiway, approach) and the environmental conditions (soil type, water table, frost depth).

Placement

Installation & Location

Primary cable is direct-buried or ducted at a depth set by the applicable standard and local frost line, typically 0.5–1.0 m below finished grade, and routed to keep clear of the runway or taxiway's graded strip wherever the surface layout allows, with a marker tape laid above the cable to warn future excavation of its presence. Connectors are made up inside a handhole or transformer pit rather than in the open trench, so the completed joint sits in a chamber that can be reopened for testing or repair without disturbing the surrounding buried run, and each connector is potted, heat-shrunk, or otherwise sealed to the manufacturer's method before the pit is backfilled or closed. Handholes and pits are set flush with or just below finished grade, positioned clear of aircraft wheel paths and mower traffic, with a lid rated for the loads expected at that location, and their internal drainage or sealing is checked at installation so standing water cannot collect around the connections inside. Cable routing between pits is recorded on the as-built circuit drawing at the time of installation, since a buried series loop with unlabelled routing becomes very difficult to fault-find or safely excavate around later.

Function

How Do They Work?

The cable network forms a continuous series loop that starts and ends at the CCR. Primary cables connect the CCR output to the first transformer, then daisy-chain from transformer to transformer, with the last transformer returning to the CCR to complete the loop. Each transformer's primary winding is connected in series with the cable, allowing the same current to pass through every unit.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Current Carrying Capacity: Primary cables are sized for 6.6A or 20A continuous current with adequate margin for voltage drop over long distances (typically up to several kilometres).
  • Insulation and Dielectric Strength: Cables are rated for the high voltages generated by the CCR (up to 5 kV) and must maintain integrity even when submerged.
  • Mechanical Protection: Armouring or a metallic screen protects against accidental digging, rock abrasion, and rodent damage.
  • Earth Continuity: A continuous earth conductor (often the cable screen) is bonded through connectors and pits to ensure fault current can return safely to the CCR.
  • Connector Sealing: Moulded rubber boots and compression seals exclude moisture and maintain insulation resistance, which is critical for long-term reliability.
  • Pit Drainage: Handholes are designed with drainage or sump pumps to prevent prolonged submersion, which accelerates connector and cable degradation.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Cables, connectors and junction pits carry no active electronics or control logic of their own — they are the passive medium the CCR's constant-current loop runs through, not a monitored subsystem in their own right. Their contribution to system architecture is structural continuity: primary cable carries the same series current from the CCR, through every transformer in the daisy chain, and back to the CCR to close the loop, while junction pits provide access points for splicing, testing and fault sectionalising without breaking that continuity. Because the cabling itself has no self-diagnostic capability, any degradation — a chafed conductor, a failed seal letting in moisture, a corroded connector — only becomes visible through its electrical effect on the loop: the CCR's voltage-versus-current monitoring detects the resulting impedance change and reports a loop fault, which is then localised to a specific pit or cable run through sectionalised testing rather than through any signal the cable generates.

Construction

Physical Construction

AGL cables are constructed with a stranded or solid copper conductor at the core, sized to the circuit's current rating, surrounded by cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) or ethylene propylene rubber (EPR) insulation chosen for its resistance to moisture, fuel, and de-icing fluid over decades of buried service, with a metallic screen or armour layer wrapped around the insulation for mechanical protection and to provide a continuous earth path along the cable run. Connectors joining cable to cable or cable to transformer follow the L-823 family standard, using tin- or silver-plated copper alloy contacts housed within a moulded rubber boot that compresses around the cable jacket to form a watertight, IP68-rated seal capable of withstanding continuous submersion in a flooded duct or pit. Junction boxes and cable pits housing these connections are built from reinforced concrete, polymer concrete, or heavy-duty polymer, with a load-rated lid bolted or clamped in place to withstand incidental vehicle or aircraft wheel loading while remaining removable for maintenance access. Cable routes between pits run through buried ducting or direct-burial trenches backfilled with selected material free of sharp aggregate that could damage the cable jacket over time.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Trench & Duct Route
Civil

Lay trenches or ducts to design depth and route, backfilled with selected material free of sharp aggregate that could damage the cable jacket over decades of buried service.

Junction Box / Pit Installation
Civil

Set each pit level and to design depth with the load-rated lid correctly bolted or clamped, confirmed to withstand incidental vehicle or aircraft wheel loading while staying removable for maintenance.

Cable Type & Rating Match
Electrical

Confirm primary and secondary cable conductor size, insulation, and voltage rating match the circuit design before pulling, since primary and secondary cables are not interchangeable.

L-823 Connector Seating
Electrical

Seat each connector's moulded rubber boot fully over the cable jacket to achieve the IP68-rated seal, since this is what allows the joint to withstand continuous submersion in a flooded duct or pit.

Splice Kit Preparation
Mechanical

Follow the manufacturer's strip and shield-transition dimensions exactly when preparing heat-shrink or cold-shrink splice kits, since a misprepared joint is the most common cause of a field insulation failure.

Bend Radius & Strain Relief
Mechanical

Keep every bend within the cable's minimum bend radius and provide strain relief at pit and connector entries, avoiding tension on completed joints.

Pre-Backfill Sign-off
Documentation

Record cable route, pit locations, and splice positions as-built before final backfill, since these are not inspectable once the trench is closed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test primary cable insulation to its 5kV (or higher) rating before backfill, confirming resistance clears the acceptable minimum on every run.

Continuity & Loop Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity of primary and secondary conductors and record total loop resistance, ruling out damaged cores or poor terminations before closing trenches.

Connector Seal & Compression Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm each L-823 connector's moulded rubber boot achieves correct compression and its IP68 rating before the joint is buried or submerged.

Splice Kit Installation Check
Pre-Energisation

Inspect heat-shrink or cold-shrink splice kits for correct application and sealant coverage, confirming they meet direct-burial rating before backfill.

High-Voltage Withstand (Hi-Pot) Test
Energisation

Apply a test voltage above the cable's nominal rating to confirm the insulation withstands the CCR's series-circuit voltage without breakdown.

Junction Pit & Cover Load Verification
Final Acceptance

Confirm the pit material and cover meet the required Class D/F airside load rating and are correctly seated flush with the surrounding surface.

Documentation & As-Built Cable Route Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, hi-pot, and connector test results alongside the as-built cable route and pit locations in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test primary cable insulation against its 5 kV nominal rating to catch degradation before it causes a series-circuit-wide fault.

L-823 Connector Inspection
Annual

Check L-823 connector boots for a compression seal that still holds IP68, and contacts for corrosion or pitting on tin- or silver-plated surfaces.

Splice Kit Condition Check
Annual

Inspect heat-shrink or cold-shrink splice kits for sealant migration or shrinkage that could compromise the direct-burial rating.

Junction Box / Pit Inspection
Annual

Check pit structure for cracking and confirm the cover still meets its Class D or F airside load rating, replacing any damaged covers promptly.

Standing Water Check
Annual

Confirm no standing water accumulates in junction pits, which accelerates connector seal degradation even on IP68-rated components.

Cable Route / Ground Movement Check
Annual

Look for surface indications of settlement or disturbance along the cable route that could indicate a damaged or exposed buried cable.

Continuity & Loop Resistance Test
Annual

Measure loop resistance across primary series circuits to detect developing high-resistance joints before they cause an open-circuit fault.

Secondary Cable / Termination Check
Annual

Inspect secondary cable terminations at the fixture end for corrosion or loose connections, a common source of intermittent lighting faults.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Cable Cut or Damage During Excavation

Accidental digging (third-party or airport maintenance) is a frequent cause of cable failure. Despite cable markers, strikes occur — requiring a splice repair and pit installation.

Connector Corrosion / High Resistance

Moisture ingress into a connector body leads to corrosion of the contacts, increasing resistance and generating heat. Over time, this can cause open-circuit failure or earth leakage.

Insulation Breakdown (Water Treeing)

In older XLPE cables, prolonged exposure to moisture and electrical stress can cause water treeing, leading to a reduction in dielectric strength and eventual short circuits.

Pit Flooding / Drainage Failure

If handholes are not properly drained or sealed, they can fill with water, submerging connectors and accelerating corrosion. Blocked drainage or sump pump failure is a common maintenance issue.

Poor Splice Workmanship

Incorrectly installed splice kits (e.g., insufficient heat-shrink, incorrect gel application) can create a weak point that fails prematurely. Proper training and quality assurance are essential.

Earth Continuity Loss

Corrosion or loose bonding connections can break the earth continuity path, preventing the CCR from detecting earth faults and compromising safety.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture or Segment Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
A failed L-823 connector joint at that fixture's tail — either an open primary pin or a fully corroded contact breaking the series loop locally.
Diagnostic Steps
Excavate to expose the connector pair; check for continuity across the mated joint; unmate and inspect pins/sockets for corrosion, pitting, or scorch marks; megger each half separately.
Corrective Action
Cut back damaged cable ends, re-terminate with new L-823 primary connectors, re-seal with silicone/heat-shrink boot, and re-test insulation resistance before backfilling.
Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Shows Open Loop
Likely Cause
A complete break in the cable run — typically third-party excavation damage, a severed conductor at a handhole, or a splice kit that has fully failed.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR's open-circuit fault indication; perform loop-resistance or time-domain reflectometry (TDR) testing from the CCR output to locate the break; sectionalise at handholes and megger each segment to narrow the fault.
Corrective Action
Expose the faulted section, cut out the damaged cable, install a new in-line splice kit or handhole splice, and confirm insulation resistance meets the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
CCR Trips on Ground Fault / Earth Leakage
Likely Cause
Water ingress at a connector or handhole has degraded insulation resistance to earth, most often from a failed gasket, a flooded pit, or a poorly heat-shrunk splice.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit conductor-to-earth to identify the low-resistance segment; inspect handholes for standing water or submerged connectors; check splice kits for incomplete heat-shrink or missing gel seal.
Corrective Action
Pump out and drain the affected pit, dry or replace the compromised splice/connector, restore proper sealing, and re-verify insulation resistance is above minimum before re-energising.
Progressive Voltage Drop / Circuit Runs Hot at a Joint
Likely Cause
High-resistance connection at a corroded connector or loose crimp, generating localised heating under load rather than a clean open or short.
Diagnostic Steps
Thermal-scan accessible handholes and connector pits under load; measure voltage drop across suspect joints with the circuit energised; inspect crimps for looseness, discoloration, or greenish corrosion.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate the joint with a new crimp and connector body, apply approved anti-oxidant/sealant, and re-check for temperature rise on the next load cycle.
Intermittent Flicker Localised to One Section
Likely Cause
A partially mated or vibration-loosened connector, or early-stage water treeing in an aging cable causing an unstable contact under thermal cycling.
Diagnostic Steps
Flex-test suspect connectors while monitoring circuit current; re-seat and re-torque connector pairs; if flicker persists, megger the cable segment under a hi-pot or step-voltage test to check for treeing.
Corrective Action
Fully re-mate and secure the connector, or replace the cable segment if treeing/degraded dielectric strength is confirmed, and log the segment for future condition monitoring.
Handhole Flooded or Connectors Submerged
Likely Cause
Blocked or failed drainage (clogged weep hole, collapsed sump, or a cracked pit lid seal) allowing surface or groundwater to accumulate around the connectors.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the pit drainage path and sump pump (where fitted); check the lid gasket and frame for cracks or gaps; assess connector boots for signs of prior submersion.
Corrective Action
Pump out and clear the drainage path, restore or replace the sump pump, re-seal the lid, and dry out or replace any connectors showing corrosion before returning the section to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Cable Insulation Breakdown (Water Treeing)
High Severity
Root Cause
Long-term moisture ingress into the cable insulation, progressing as microscopic tree-like channels that gradually reduce dielectric strength.
Effect
Progressive voltage drop or a circuit that runs hot at a joint, eventually leading to an earth fault or full loop failure as the insulation finally breaks down.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing at scheduled intervals, tracking the trend rather than a single pass/fail reading.
Connector Corrosion / High Resistance
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress through a degraded L-823 connector seal, or corrosion building up on the connector pins over time.
Effect
A single fixture or segment goes dark while the rest of the circuit stays normal, or an intermittent flicker localised to one section as the connection makes and breaks.
Detection Method
L-823 connector inspection and a continuity/loop resistance test to localise the high-resistance point.
Poor Splice Workmanship
High Severity
Root Cause
An incorrectly installed splice kit, inadequate compression, or a splice made without the specified sealing compound.
Effect
Insulation breakdown or a ground fault develops at the splice location well before the rest of the cable run shows any degradation.
Detection Method
Splice kit condition check and an insulation resistance test focused on sections containing a field splice.
Junction Pit Flooding / Drainage Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A blocked or undersized drain, or ground settlement changing the pit's relationship to the surrounding water table.
Effect
The handhole is found flooded or its connectors submerged, accelerating every other failure mode on this list for the connectors inside it.
Detection Method
Standing water check performed at every junction pit or handhole access during scheduled maintenance.
Earth Continuity Loss
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A corroded, loosened, or physically damaged earth bonding connection at a junction, splice, or cable termination point.
Effect
The CCR trips on ground fault or earth leakage, or a genuine fault elsewhere in the circuit goes undetected because the earth reference itself is compromised.
Detection Method
Earth continuity check at junctions and terminations, verified as part of the circuit's insulation resistance testing.
Cable Cut or Damage During Excavation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Third-party excavation work near the cable route without adequate as-built records or locate-before-you-dig practice.
Effect
The entire series circuit goes dark or the CCR shows an open loop, with the fault location unknown until the cable route is traced.
Detection Method
Cable route/ground movement check, and continuity/loop resistance testing to localise the break, cross-referenced against the as-built cable route documentation.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

AGL series circuits can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy before opening a junction pit or handling cable connectors.

Call Before You Dig
Operational

Confirm cable and duct locations against as-built drawings and use a locator sweep before any excavation near cables or junction pits, to avoid striking a live series circuit.

PPE & Insulated Tools
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm bonding/grounding is intact before reconnecting the loop.

FOD Control at Junction Pits
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and packing material accounted for at all times when a pit or enclosure is open, and secure or remove loose material before backfilling or closing the cover.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected loop after any splicing, connector, or cable work before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the circuit.

Drainage & Sealing Before Closing
Compliance

Confirm junction pits and enclosures drain properly and that gaskets/seals are correctly reinstated before closing, to prevent water ingress that leads to insulation breakdown or corrosion.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify loop resistance and insulation resistance are within tolerance and update as-built records before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Loop Resistance Trends Reveal Problems Before They're Faults
Troubleshooting

A junction connection that's beginning to corrode or loosen typically shows up first as a gradual rise in loop resistance rather than an abrupt failure. Log resistance readings by loop over successive maintenance visits so a developing problem is caught while it's still a minor repair.

As-Built Accuracy Is the Difference Between a Safe Dig and a Strike
Operational

Junction pits and connector locations that drift from their as-built records over successive repairs create real risk for the next person excavating nearby, even when the call-before-you-dig process is followed correctly. Update as-built records every time a junction location changes, not just at initial installation.

Standing Water in a Pit Is a Symptom, Not Just an Inconvenience
Design

A junction pit that regularly holds water after rain points to a drainage design or grading issue that will keep stressing seals and connections until it's addressed at the source. Fix the underlying drainage rather than repeatedly resealing connections that keep failing in the same wet pit.

FOD Risk at a Pit Extends Beyond the Work Window
Operational

Small hardware and offcuts left in or around an open junction pit can migrate onto the movement area through wind, wildlife, or vehicle disturbance well after the crew has left, not just during active work. Do a final sweep of the surrounding area, not only the pit itself, before demobilising.

Connector Compatibility Across Repair Generations Isn't Guaranteed
Maintenance

A junction repaired multiple times over its service life can end up with a mix of connector types and generations that aren't all mutually compatible, creating a latent mismatch that only surfaces during the next repair. Document the connector type used at each repair so future work isn't guessing at compatibility.

Test the Whole Loop, Not Just the Repaired Segment
Compliance

Verifying insulation and continuity only at the specific junction just repaired can miss a separate marginal fault elsewhere on the same loop that happens to coexist with the one just fixed. Run a full loop test after junction work, not a localized check limited to the repair point.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL cables, connectors and junction infrastructure: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What are AGL cables, connectors and junction infrastructure?

They are the buried power path that carries series current from the CCR to every isolating transformer and fixture: L-824 underground cable, L-823 plug-and-receptacle connectors, splices, and the handholes, pits and junction boxes that give technicians access to the buried network.

Q2Why is this infrastructure critical in a series circuit?

Because AGL circuits are series loops, one failed joint or damaged cable can drop or unbalance the whole circuit, not just the fixture attached to it. The infrastructure must hold insulation resistance, watertightness and mechanical integrity for years of thermal cycling and ground movement, largely out of sight.

Q3What is the difference between L-824 and L-823?

L-824 is the FAA designation for the underground electrical cable (AC 150/5345-7). L-823 covers the connectors (AC 150/5345-26), not the cable.

Q4What are the typical cable specifications?

Primary cable is stranded copper, 6 mm² or 10 mm², with XLPE or EPR insulation, rated 5 kV nominal and tested to 10 kV or higher. Secondary cable is smaller copper, 1.5 mm² to 2.5 mm², connecting the transformer to the fixture.

Q5How deep is primary cable buried?

Typically 0.5 m to 1.0 m below finished grade, direct-buried or ducted, depending on the applicable standard and local frost line, with marker tape laid above it to warn future excavation.

Q6How are connectors and junction points built?

L-823 connectors are moulded plug-and-receptacle pairs with a rubber boot and compression seal, IP68 rated once mated, with tin- or silver-plated contacts. They are made up inside a handhole or transformer pit rather than the open trench, so the joint can be reopened for testing without disturbing the buried cable. Pits are reinforced concrete, polymer concrete or HDPE with Class D or F airside-rated lids.

Q7What insulation resistance is required?

At least 50 MΩ, measured cable-to-ground with a megger. Ground faults and open circuits on the primary loop are detected at the CCR.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-7 (L-824 cable), FAA AC 150/5345-26 (L-823 connectors), ICAO Annex 14, IEC 60502, and for Indian installations IS 1554, IS 7098 and IS 7785. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should cables, connectors and pits be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover insulation resistance, L-823 connectors, splice kit condition, junction box and pit inspection, standing water, cable route and ground movement, continuity and loop resistance, and secondary cable terminations. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with AGL cables and connectors?

Common problems are cable cuts during excavation, connector corrosion or high resistance, insulation breakdown from water treeing, pit flooding or drainage failure, poor splice workmanship and loss of earth continuity. Symptoms include a single fixture or segment dark, an entire circuit dark with the CCR showing an open loop, ground fault trips or a joint running hot.
