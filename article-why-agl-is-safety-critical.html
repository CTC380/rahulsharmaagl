Why Is Airfield Ground Lighting Safety-Critical?

Airfield Ground Lighting is often treated as supporting infrastructure. In reality, it is a primary safety system — one that operates in direct interaction with aircraft movement, on every take-off, landing, and taxi.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

February 25, 2026 Updated October 27, 2026

AGL provides pilots with visual guidance for runway alignment, taxiway routing, hold positions, obstacle awareness, and low-visibility operations. Unlike many airport systems, AGL operates in direct interaction with aircraft movement — every night or low-visibility take-off, landing, and taxi movement depends heavily on correct visual cues from the ground.

In accordance with standards such as ICAO Annex 14 and FAA specifications, AGL systems are engineered to deliver precise intensity, colour, spacing, and beam alignment. These are not aesthetic parameters — they are safety requirements.

Key Takeaway

AGL is not background infrastructure. It is an active visual guidance system that directly influences aircraft movement and pilot decision-making — and in aviation, perception equals safety.

01

What AGL Provides Pilots

AGL provides pilots with visual guidance for:

  • Runway alignment
  • Taxiway routing
  • Hold positions and runway-incursion protection (stop bars, runway guard lights)
  • Obstacle awareness (through obstruction lighting, which is usually on separate circuits)
  • Low-visibility operations

A wrong, missing or inconsistent cue matters because pilots act on what they see, often within seconds and with little margin.

02

Direct Influence on Pilot Decision-Making

During night operations or low-visibility conditions, pilots rely heavily on runway edge lights, centreline lights, approach lighting systems, and taxiway guidance. If any of the following occur, pilot perception can be affected during critical phases of flight:

  • Incorrect light intensity
  • Colour deviation
  • Improper spacing
  • Partial circuit failure

In aviation, perception equals safety.

03

Operates in the Most Demanding Environment

AGL is unique compared with many other airport systems:

  • It works continuously in harsh weather
  • It is exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits, and heavy wheel loads
  • It must perform reliably in rain, fog, dust, and extreme temperatures

Unlike terminal systems, AGL failures directly interact with aircraft movement areas.

A runway light that is "ON" is not necessarily "SAFE." It must be photometrically compliant, electrically stable, and correctly installed.

04

Small Deviations, Large Consequences

AGL systems are designed with strict tolerances. Small deviations in the following parameters can create misleading visual cues:

Beam AngleWhere the light actually points
ChromaticityColour accuracy against the standard
Elevation SettingVertical aiming accuracy
CCR Step IntensityOutput at the selected brightness step

The difference between compliant and non-compliant lighting may not be obvious from a ground inspection vehicle, yet it can be noticeable to a pilot on approach.

05

Reliability Is a Safety Parameter

Unlike many systems that allow minor downtime, AGL reliability is part of operational continuity. Serviceability is regulated. ICAO Annex 14 sets minimum serviceability levels (for example, a minimum percentage of working lights and no adjacent lights out on key lighting of precision-approach runways), so failures beyond those limits, or a failure in a critical segment, may:

Trigger a NOTAM
Formally notifies pilots and operators that a lighting deficiency exists.
Reduce runway category
Limits the approach and landing minima the runway can support.
Affect movement rates
Slows the rate at which aircraft can safely take off, land, or taxi.
Delay operations
Cascades into wider airport-level schedule and capacity impact.

Power continuity matters just as much: Annex 14 requires a secondary power supply with defined switch-over times, which are shortest for the most critical lighting on low-visibility runways. AGL does not attract attention when it works well. It gets attention only when something goes wrong.

06

Design, Testing, and Maintenance Define Safety

AGL safety depends on four things working together:

01DesignProper design to regulatory standards
02PhotometryVerified photometric performance
03InstallationCorrect installation practices
04MaintenancePreventive maintenance discipline

Compliance, photometry, and maintenance together determine whether the system is actually serving its purpose.

Installation alone does not guarantee safety.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is airfield ground lighting considered safety-critical?

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) is an active visual guidance system, not background infrastructure. Pilots rely on it for runway alignment, taxiway routing, hold positions and low-visibility operations on every night or low-visibility take-off, landing and taxi movement. A wrong, missing or inconsistent cue can affect decisions made within seconds and with little margin.

What is the difference between a runway light being "ON" and being "SAFE"?

A light that is on is not automatically safe. To be safe it must be photometrically compliant (correct intensity, colour and beam pattern), electrically stable and correctly installed and aimed. A fixture can be lit and still give pilots a misleading cue if its output, colour or alignment has drifted outside tolerance.

How does AGL influence pilot decision-making?

During night and low-visibility operations, pilots depend heavily on runway edge lights, centreline lights, approach lighting and taxiway guidance. Incorrect intensity, colour deviation, improper spacing or a partial circuit failure can alter what the pilot perceives during critical phases of flight. In aviation, perception equals safety.

What conditions must airfield ground lighting withstand?

AGL works continuously in harsh weather and must perform reliably in rain, fog, dust and extreme temperatures. It is also exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits and heavy wheel loads. Unlike terminal systems, AGL sits directly in the aircraft movement area, so its failures interact directly with aircraft operations.

Why do small deviations in beam angle, colour or intensity matter?

AGL is designed with strict tolerances. Small deviations in beam angle, chromaticity, elevation setting or constant current regulator (CCR) step intensity can create misleading visual cues. The difference between compliant and non-compliant lighting may not be obvious from a ground inspection vehicle, yet it can be noticeable to a pilot on approach.

What happens when AGL serviceability falls below permitted limits?

ICAO Annex 14 sets minimum serviceability levels for aerodrome lighting. Failures beyond those limits, or a failure in a critical segment, may trigger a NOTAM, reduce the runway category, affect movement rates and delay operations. Annex 14 also requires a secondary power supply with defined switch-over times, which are shortest for the most critical lighting on low-visibility runways.

Which standards govern AGL safety?

Internationally, ICAO Annex 14 (Aerodromes) sets the requirements for intensity, colour, spacing, serviceability and secondary power. In FAA-governed environments, FAA specifications and advisory circulars such as AC 150/5340-30 (design and installation) and AC 150/5340-26 (maintenance) apply, and national regulators such as DGCA in India issue their own requirements. Always consult the current official editions and the aerodrome's approved design.

What does AGL safety depend on in practice?

Four things working together: proper design to regulatory standards, verified photometric performance, correct installation practices and a disciplined preventive maintenance programme. Installation alone does not guarantee safety; compliance, photometry and maintenance together determine whether the system is actually serving its purpose.
