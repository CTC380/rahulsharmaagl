Transformer Housing / Protection Box

A rugged, waterproof enclosure designed to house and protect AGL isolating transformers in buried and handhole installations. Providing mechanical protection, environmental sealing, and thermal management, it ensures the reliable long‑term operation of transformers in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

The Transformer Housing / Protection Box is a purpose‑built enclosure designed to protect AGL isolating transformers in buried, handhole, or pit installations. It provides mechanical protection against impact, soil pressure, and traffic loads, while also offering environmental sealing against moisture, chemicals, and temperature extremes.

Key characteristics of transformer housings include:

  • Robust Construction: Manufactured from high‑strength materials such as glass‑reinforced polyester (GRP), polycarbonate, or cast iron to withstand heavy loads and mechanical stress.
  • Waterproof Sealing: IP68‑rated enclosures that protect the transformer from moisture ingress, even when submerged in water.
  • Thermal Management: Designed with adequate internal volume and, where necessary, ventilation features to dissipate heat generated by the transformer during operation.
  • Cable Entry Provisions: Equipped with cable glands, knockouts, or compression seals to accommodate primary and secondary cables entering and exiting the housing.
  • Accessibility: Features a removable or hinged lid that provides access for installation, inspection, and maintenance of the transformer.

The housing is typically installed below ground level in a concrete or brick handhole, with the lid at or near grade level for access. It is designed to accommodate various transformer sizes and power ratings, from 15W to 100W and beyond.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The transformer housing's purpose is to let an isolating transformer, a component that has to sit buried at every single fixture along a series circuit, survive years underground without the soil pressure, vehicle loading, or groundwater around it ever compromising its electrical isolation. Because a failed transformer takes its fixture off the circuit and can introduce a fault into the series loop, the housing's mechanical strength and IP68 sealing are not incidental protections but the reason a below-ground transformer can be relied on at all in an environment that would otherwise corrode contacts and short windings within a few seasons. Positioning the lid at grade level for access is what balances that long-term protection against the practical need to reach the transformer again for testing or replacement without excavating the whole installation.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Transformer housings are used throughout the airfield wherever buried isolating transformers are installed, including:

  • Runway Lighting Circuits: Housing transformers for runway edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, and touchdown zone lights.
  • Taxiway Lighting Circuits: Housing transformers for taxiway edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, and clearance bars.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Housing transformers for approach lighting fixtures, especially in underground duct systems.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Protecting transformers for obstruction lights where burial is required.
  • Sign and Beacon Systems: Housing transformers for illuminated signs and airport beacons in buried installations.
  • Retrofit and Replacement: Providing new housings for transformer replacements in existing installations.

The housing is available in various sizes and configurations to accommodate different transformer models and installation requirements, including shallow‑depth designs for areas with limited cover.

Placement

Installation & Location

The housing is buried directly in the ground at the transformer's designated position beside the fixture it serves, set at a depth that clears frost and mechanical loading while keeping the transformer close enough to the fixture for a short secondary run. Cable entries are fitted with sealing glands before backfilling, and the housing's lid or access point is set flush with or just below finished grade so it stays findable and accessible without disturbing the surrounding surface. Shallow-depth variants are chosen where rock, services, or paving limit how deep a standard housing can be set, and housing size and configuration are matched to the specific transformer model it will contain before installation, since retrofitting a mismatched housing later means re-excavating the position.

Function

How Does It Work?

A transformer housing provides a protected environment that ensures the reliable long‑term operation of the isolating transformer. Its design incorporates several functional elements:

  • Mechanical Protection: The rigid enclosure protects the transformer from physical damage during installation, backfilling, and subsequent ground settlement. It also provides protection against vehicle traffic loads (where installed under paved surfaces) and impact from maintenance equipment.
  • Environmental Sealing: The IP68‑rated enclosure prevents moisture, dirt, and contaminants from entering the housing and coming into contact with the transformer. This is critical for preventing corrosion, insulation degradation, and electrical faults.
  • Thermal Dissipation: The housing is designed with sufficient internal volume and airflow pathways to allow heat generated by the transformer to dissipate. Some housings include ventilation slots or are constructed from thermally conductive materials to assist with cooling.
  • Cable Management: Cable glands or compression seals at the cable entry points prevent moisture from tracking along the cable jacket into the housing. Internal cable ties or brackets secure the cables and prevent strain on the transformer connections.
  • Access for Maintenance: The lid is designed to be easily opened with standard tools, providing access for transformer inspection, testing, and replacement without disturbing the surrounding ground or pavement.

The housing is typically installed with a bedding layer of sand or gravel to provide drainage and protect the housing from sharp objects in the backfill material.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The transformer housing has no electrical control logic and no place in the AGL command chain — it's a purely mechanical and environmental layer sitting between the buried transformer and the ground around it, not between the transformer and the CCR that regulates and monitors it electrically. Its only function is to keep soil pressure, traffic loading, moisture, and temperature extremes away from the transformer for as long as the installation is in service.

Because of that, a failing housing never registers as its own distinct fault anywhere in the control system: what the CCR actually sees is the downstream effect — a transformer whose insulation has degraded from years of moisture ingress eventually fails and is reported the same way any other transformer fault is, as a loop-level voltage deviation. The housing's condition is therefore invisible to the electrical control architecture right up until it isn't, which is why periodic physical inspection, rather than any electrical monitoring, is what catches a housing problem before it becomes a transformer failure.

Housing (mechanical/environmental) → Transformer → CCR / Series Circuit (indirect, delayed feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The box is moulded or cast from a load-rated polymer concrete, fibreglass-reinforced polymer, or corrosion-treated metal, sized to enclose the isolating transformer with clearance for its primary and secondary leads and to withstand the soil pressure and any incidental traffic loading above a buried or handhole installation. The lid is a separate load-rated cover — often a tread-plate or checker-pattern surface for foot traffic areas — secured with tamper-resistant or corrosion-resistant fasteners and gasketed along its full perimeter to keep surface water out of the pit. Cable entries are formed as knockouts or pre-fitted glands sized for the primary and secondary conduit or direct-burial cable, positioned low on the box wall so any water that does enter drains away from the termination points rather than pooling around them. Internal mounting brackets or a raised plinth keep the transformer itself lifted clear of the box floor, further reducing exposure to any residual moisture.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Water Ingress Through Cable Entries

If cable glands are not properly tightened or sealed, moisture can enter the housing and cause corrosion or electrical faults. Regularly inspect and tighten glands, and ensure they are correctly sized for the cable diameter.

Corrosion of Housing Components

In aggressive environments (coastal, industrial), metal components can corrode. Use corrosion‑resistant materials (GRP, stainless steel, or coated cast iron) and inspect regularly for signs of corrosion.

Damaged or Missing Gaskets

If the lid gasket is damaged or missing, the housing will not maintain its IP rating. Inspect gaskets regularly and replace if cracked, compressed, or degraded.

Inadequate Transformer Mounting

If the transformer is not securely mounted inside the housing, vibration and movement can damage connections or the transformer itself. Use the provided mounting plate or brackets and secure the transformer with appropriate fasteners.

Heat Build‑Up in Enclosed Spaces

In high‑ambient‑temperature environments or with high‑power transformers, inadequate ventilation can cause heat build‑up, reducing transformer life. Ensure the housing has adequate thermal capacity and consider ventilated designs where necessary.

Lid Access Obstruction

If the housing is installed too deep or the lid is covered by asphalt or soil, access for maintenance can be difficult. Ensure the lid is installed at grade level and is clearly marked to facilitate location and access.

Incorrect Transformer Size Fit

Selecting a housing that is too small for the transformer can make installation difficult and may restrict airflow. Always choose a housing that provides adequate clearance around the transformer.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Water or Moisture Found Inside the Housing
Likely Cause
A cable gland was not properly tightened or sealed, letting moisture track into the housing around the transformer.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect all cable entries for gland tightness and correct sizing; check for condensation or corrosion around the transformer.
Corrective Action
Re-tighten or replace the gland, dry out the housing, and megger the transformer before re-energising.
Housing Fails to Hold Its IP Rating
Likely Cause
The lid gasket has cracked, compressed, or degraded, compromising the housing's environmental seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the gasket for damage or compression set; check the lid seats evenly all the way around.
Corrective Action
Replace the gasket and re-torque lid fasteners evenly to restore the seal.
Transformer Damage or Connection Failure from Movement
Likely Cause
The transformer was not securely mounted inside the housing, allowing vibration and movement to stress connections.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the transformer's mounting plate/brackets and fasteners for looseness or movement.
Corrective Action
Re-secure the transformer with the correct mounting hardware and re-check terminations for damage.
Elevated Transformer Temperature or Premature Failure
Likely Cause
Inadequate ventilation in an enclosed housing is causing heat build-up, especially with a high-power transformer in a high-ambient environment.
Diagnostic Steps
Check housing thermal capacity against the transformer's rating and local ambient conditions; measure temperature if a thermal camera or sensor is available.
Corrective Action
Fit a ventilated housing design where the thermal load requires it, or relocate to a lower-ambient location.
Housing Difficult to Locate or Open for Maintenance
Likely Cause
The housing was installed too deep, or the lid has become covered by asphalt or soil since installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check installed depth and lid marking against as-built records; confirm accessibility at the recorded location.
Corrective Action
Expose and re-mark the housing location, and raise it to grade level if buried too deep for reasonable access.
Difficulty Fitting or Servicing the Transformer
Likely Cause
The housing selected is undersized for the transformer, restricting airflow and making installation difficult.
Diagnostic Steps
Check clearance around the transformer against the housing manufacturer's minimum recommended spacing.
Corrective Action
Replace with a correctly sized housing that provides adequate clearance for the installed transformer.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Gasket Seal Degradation / Water Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set over years of thermal cycling, or a lid that no longer closes flush after repeated access for transformer servicing.
Effect
Water or moisture found inside the housing, accelerating corrosion of the transformer casing and connections.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at each opening, and internal moisture check as part of scheduled maintenance.
IP Rating Failure at Cable Entries
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or improperly torqued cable glands, or a conduit fitting that was never properly sealed at installation.
Effect
Housing fails to hold its rated IP ingress protection, allowing water and debris to reach the enclosed transformer.
Detection Method
Ingress test or visual check of glands/fittings during maintenance, and inspection for standing water inside the housing after rain.
Transformer Movement / Connection Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
Inadequate internal bracing or padding allowing the transformer to shift under vibration from mowing, ground traffic, or seasonal ground movement.
Effect
Transformer damage or a connection failure at the primary/secondary plugs from repeated mechanical stress.
Detection Method
Physical inspection of transformer seating and connection security during scheduled maintenance.
Elevated Internal Temperature
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Restricted ventilation inside the housing, or an oversized/mismatched transformer generating more heat than the housing was designed to dissipate.
Effect
Elevated transformer temperature accelerating insulation ageing, and increasing the risk of premature transformer failure.
Detection Method
Internal temperature check during maintenance, and thermal imaging of the housing and transformer under load.
Buried or Obstructed Housing
Low Severity
Root Cause
Turf growth, pavement resurfacing, or drifted fill gradually covering the housing location without an accurate as-built record.
Effect
Housing becomes difficult to locate or open for maintenance, extending outage time when a fault needs to be traced.
Detection Method
GPS/as-built survey verification against the field location, and periodic clearing of vegetation or fill around the marked position.
Tight Clearance for Transformer Servicing
Low Severity
Root Cause
A housing sized close to the transformer's physical envelope, leaving little working room for connection or removal during maintenance.
Effect
Difficulty fitting or servicing the transformer, extending the time a circuit is out of service during routine work.
Detection Method
Clearance verification against the transformer model in use, noted during the first service visit after installation or replacement.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the transformer and verify zero energy at the connections before opening the housing or protection box.

Standing Water Shock Hazard
Electrical Safety

Buried and handhole installations can accumulate groundwater; treat any water in the box as potentially energised until isolation is confirmed, and drain before reaching in.

Traffic-Rated Lid Handling
Personnel Safety

Use proper lifting technique or tools for the heavy, traffic-rated lid, and never leave a housing open or a lid improperly seated where vehicles or aircraft could pass over it.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside and raise a NOTAM where the associated circuit or fixture will be affected during the work.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for while the housing is open, and secure or remove loose material before refitting the lid.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the transformer and circuit after any work inside the housing before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage other fixtures on the loop.

Restore Seal & Load Rating
Compliance

Confirm the housing is correctly sealed against moisture and chemical ingress, and rated for the soil pressure and traffic loading of its location, before backfilling or reopening the area.

Quick Reference

Airport Transformer Housing

An airport transformer housing is the enclosure that holds and protects an isolating transformer and its connections where the transformer is buried or sits in a pit or handhole. It keeps out water, soil and debris, and carries or spreads loads so that the transformer and connectors are not crushed or damaged.

When choosing a housing, check the material (such as GRP, polycarbonate or cast iron), the sealing rating, the load class for the location, the room needed for the transformer and cable bend radius, and whether it must fit a particular base can or pit. Housing and base can are related but not the same part: see the base can page for the structural housing that holds an elevated fixture.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Wet Housing Warrants a Transformer Health Check
Diagnostics

The housing's environmental seal can degrade well before the transformer itself shows any electrical symptom; if a housing is found wet or with signs of ingress during inspection, check the transformer's health even if it currently tests fine.

Reconfirm Load Rating After Pavement Upgrades
Compliance

A housing rated for its original installation load can be under-rated after a later pavement upgrade increases the traffic loads passing over it, such as heavier aircraft or different ground vehicle routes; reconfirm the load rating against current pavement usage, not just the original design load.

Check the Access Lid Seal Separately From the Housing Body
Inspection

As with any buried enclosure, the access lid or seal is usually the first point of water ingress, well ahead of any failure in the main housing body; inspect it as its own item rather than judging the housing from its exterior condition.

Open and Inspect Internally, Not Just Externally
Maintenance

A housing that looks sound from outside can have significant internal corrosion from trapped moisture or chemicals; open and inspect the interior on a defined schedule rather than judging condition from an external walk-by.

Verify Bonding Continuity as Its Own Check
Electrical

A housing that seals well and protects the transformer mechanically can still fail to maintain the bonding path needed for fault protection between the housing, transformer case, and cable shield; verify continuity directly rather than assuming it follows from a good mechanical seal.

Track Cumulative Burial Depth Through Repeated Resurfacing
Maintenance

Each pavement resurfacing layer effectively buries the housing deeper unless it's raised to match, eventually putting the access point below its design depth; track cumulative pavement buildup against the housing's original design burial depth over successive resurfacing projects.
