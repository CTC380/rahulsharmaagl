AGL Primary Cable

The heavy‑duty, single‑core or multi‑core cable that forms the backbone of the AGL series circuit, carrying constant current from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to every isolating transformer and light fixture in the loop. Engineered for buried, elevated, and duct installations, it provides reliable power transmission in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

The AGL Primary Cable (also known as the series cable or loop cable) is the main power distribution cable used in airfield ground lighting systems. It carries the constant current (typically 6.6A or 20A) from the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to all isolating transformers and light fixtures in a series circuit. The cable forms a continuous loop that connects all fixtures on a given runway, taxiway, or approach lighting system, with each fixture connected to the loop via an isolating transformer.

AGL primary cable is typically:

  • Single‑core or multi‑core: Often single‑core (one conductor) with a heavy insulation and sheath, but multi‑core versions (2‑core, 3‑core) are used for twin‑circuit or earth‑included systems.
  • Heavy‑duty construction: Designed to withstand mechanical stress, moisture, chemicals, and temperature extremes. Common insulation materials include XLPE (cross‑linked polyethylene), EPR (ethylene propylene rubber), or PVC.
  • Rated for continuous operation: At the full primary current (6.6A or 20A) with voltage drops within acceptable limits over long cable runs (up to several kilometres).
  • Colour‑coded: Typically black or red for primary circuits, but colour coding varies by standard and region.

The cable is installed in underground conduits, direct‑buried in trenches, or laid in cable ducts within concrete. It is terminated at each isolating transformer using L‑823 connectors (or similar) and at the CCR output. Proper cable selection, installation, and maintenance are critical for the reliable operation of the AGL system.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The AGL primary cable's purpose extends beyond simply carrying current: because every fixture on a series circuit shares the same loop, the cable's integrity along its entire length determines whether the whole circuit stays lit, not just the section nearest the fault. A single break, chafe, or moisture ingress point anywhere in the loop can take down every downstream fixture until the fault is located and repaired, which is why the cable's insulation and sheath are built to withstand mechanical stress, chemical exposure, and temperature extremes well beyond what an individual fixture connection would need. This shared-fate characteristic of the series loop is what makes primary cable condition, rather than any single fixture, the dominant factor in circuit-wide reliability.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The AGL primary cable is used throughout the airfield for all primary series circuits, including:

  • Runway Lighting Circuits: Powering runway edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, end lights, touchdown zone lights, and other runway fixtures.
  • Taxiway Lighting Circuits: Powering taxiway edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, clearance bars, and guidance signs.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Powering ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSR, and other approach lighting configurations.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Powering low‑, medium‑, and high‑intensity obstruction lights on structures.
  • Airport Beacons: Powering rotating beacons and heliport beacons.
  • CCR to Field Interface: Connecting the CCR output to the field distribution panel or directly to the series loop.

The cable is available in various conductor sizes (typically 6mm², 10mm², 16mm², or 25mm²) to suit the circuit length and load requirements. It is designed for use in temperatures ranging from -40°C to +85°C and is resistant to oils, fuels, and de‑icing chemicals commonly found on airfields.

Placement

Installation & Location

The cable is laid in a trench or drawn through duct at a depth typically specified in local design standards (commonly 450–600 mm below the pavement, or deeper under paved surfaces), bedded in a sand or fine-fill layer that protects the sheath from sharp stones and allows even heat dissipation along the run. Where the loop crosses beneath a runway, taxiway, or apron, the cable is drawn through a rigid duct sized with spare capacity for future pulls, with marker tape laid above the trench to warn against later excavation damage. Route is planned to follow the series circuit's fixture spacing as directly as practicable, minimising unnecessary length (and the voltage drop that comes with it) while keeping clear of other buried services such as drainage, fuel lines, and communications ducts. At each isolating transformer, junction box, or CCR interface, the cable is terminated with an L-823 (or equivalent) connector, with sufficient slack looped into the pit or handhole to allow the termination to be remade without disturbing the buried run.

Function

How Does It Work?

The AGL primary cable functions as the series conductor that carries the constant current through the entire lighting loop. Its design ensures efficient and reliable power transmission:

  • Conductor: Typically made of high‑conductivity annealed copper (or aluminium in some cases) to minimise I²R losses. The conductor is stranded to provide flexibility and reduce the effects of skin effect at power frequencies.
  • Insulation: A thick layer of cross‑linked polyethylene (XLPE) or ethylene propylene rubber (EPR) provides high dielectric strength, resistance to moisture, and excellent thermal properties. XLPE is preferred for its higher temperature rating (up to 90°C continuous).
  • Sheath: An outer PVC or thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) sheath provides mechanical protection against abrasion, impacts, and chemical attack. For direct‑burial applications, the sheath may be reinforced with armour (steel wire or tape) to protect against rodent damage and mechanical stress.
  • Voltage Drop: The cable is sized to ensure that the voltage drop along the loop does not exceed acceptable limits (typically 5‑10%) at the maximum current, ensuring that each isolating transformer receives sufficient voltage to drive its connected fixture.
  • Fault Current Capacity: The cable is rated for the prospective fault current of the system, ensuring it can withstand short‑circuit conditions without damage.

In operation, the CCR feeds a constant current (e.g., 6.6A) into the primary cable loop. The loop passes through each isolating transformer's primary winding, and the current returns to the CCR. The isolating transformers step down the voltage to the appropriate level for each fixture. The primary cable is designed to have a low resistance per metre, minimising voltage drop and ensuring uniform current distribution across all fixtures.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The primary cable carries no intelligence of its own and is not individually addressable — it is the physical medium through which the series circuit's commanded current flows. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step, and the CCR converts that command into a fixed current on the loop (typically one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A). That current travels the full length of the primary cable — out to the last isolating transformer and back to the CCR — with every fixture on the loop drawing from the identical current simultaneously. The cable's resistance per metre is built directly into the loop's control logic: the CCR continuously compares the commanded current against loop voltage, and because the cable's own resistance forms part of that voltage budget, an oversized run, a poor joint, or a corroded termination reduces the headroom available to fixtures further down the loop. A complete break in the cable presents at the CCR as a simple open-circuit loop fault — the regulator sees only that voltage has risen without limit at the commanded current, with no indication of where along the run the break has occurred until the loop is sectionalised and tested in the field.

Construction

Physical Construction

Build‑up is concentric, from the conductor outward. The core is a stranded, high‑conductivity annealed copper (or aluminium) conductor, stranding rather than a single solid wire being used to keep the cable flexible enough to draw through duct and route around obstacles in the trench. Over this sits an extruded layer of cross‑linked polyethylene (XLPE) or ethylene propylene rubber (EPR), chosen for its high dielectric strength and continuous operating temperature rating; lower‑duty runs may instead use a PVC insulation. An outer sheath of PVC, thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), or HDPE provides mechanical protection against abrasion, chemical attack from fuel and de‑icing fluid, and moisture ingress, and for direct‑burial sections this sheath is often reinforced with steel wire or steel tape armour to resist rodent damage and backfill impact. Cable ends are terminated in an L‑823 (or equivalent) connector, with the conductor exposed, ferruled, and seated in the connector's pin or socket contact before the assembly is sealed against the elements.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Insulation Damage During Installation

Sharp edges in conduits, ducts, or trenches can nick or cut the cable insulation, leading to premature failure. Use cable rollers, protect corners with radius bends, and inspect cables before pulling. Always perform an insulation resistance test after installation.

Moisture Ingress at Cable Ends

If cable ends are not properly sealed during storage or installation, moisture can enter the conductor strands and travel along the cable, causing corrosion and insulation breakdown. Seal cable ends with heat‑shrink caps or self‑amalgamating tape.

Excessive Voltage Drop on Long Runs

Long primary cable runs can result in excessive voltage drop, reducing the voltage available at the farthest isolating transformers. This can cause dim lights or failure. Use larger conductor sizes or reduce the circuit length by splitting into multiple loops.

Overheating Due to Overloading

If the primary cable is undersized for the current (e.g., using 6mm² cable on a 20A circuit), it will overheat, potentially melting insulation and causing a short circuit. Always select the cable size based on the circuit current rating and the installation method.

Chemical Attack on Sheath

Fuel, oil, de‑icing fluids, and other chemicals can degrade PVC or thermoplastic sheaths over time. Use cables with chemically resistant sheaths (e.g., TPU or HDPE) in areas where chemical exposure is likely.

Rodent Damage

In buried installations, rodents can chew through the cable sheath and insulation, causing faults. Use armoured cables (SWA or STA) or install the cable in rodent‑proof conduits in areas with high rodent activity.

Incorrect Termination

Using the wrong connector or poor termination technique can result in high resistance, overheating, and eventual failure. Always use approved L‑823 connectors or similar, and follow manufacturer's termination instructions.

Troubleshooting Guide

Circuit Open or Dark After Cable Fault
Likely Cause
A nick or crush point in the insulation from installation, rodent damage, or chemical attack on the sheath has developed into a full breakdown, opening the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the circuit in sections from the CCR outward to isolate the faulted segment; check the fault-locator/TDR trace against known cable length.
Corrective Action
Excavate at the identified fault location, cut out and replace the damaged section with a proper joint kit, and re-megger before reinstating.
Low Insulation Resistance Reading
Likely Cause
Moisture has tracked into the conductor strands through an improperly sealed cable end or a degraded outer sheath.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform an insulation resistance test at both ends of the run; inspect exposed cable ends and any joints for signs of water ingress.
Corrective Action
Reseal or re-terminate cable ends with heat-shrink caps or self-amalgamating tape, and replace any section where moisture has migrated into the strands.
Dim or Underperforming Fixtures at the End of a Long Run
Likely Cause
Excessive voltage drop across a long primary run is starving the far-end isolating transformers of adequate voltage.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure voltage at the CCR output and at the far end of the loop under load; compare the drop against the calculated value for the cable size and run length.
Corrective Action
Upsize the conductor for future extensions, or split the circuit into shorter loops to bring voltage drop back within tolerance.
Localised Overheating or Insulation Melting
Likely Cause
The cable is undersized for the circuit current given its installation method, or is overloaded beyond its rated ampacity.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify actual circuit current against the cable's ampacity rating for its installation method; check for warm spots along accessible sections with a thermal camera.
Corrective Action
Replace the undersized section with correctly rated cable, or reduce circuit loading, and re-verify temperature under normal operating current.
Repeated Faults at a Termination Point
Likely Cause
Incorrect connector selection or poor termination technique has created a high-resistance joint that runs hot and eventually fails.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the termination for signs of heat discoloration, pitting, or loose connector engagement; megger and check contact resistance if test equipment allows.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate using an approved L-823 connector following the manufacturer's procedure, and confirm the joint is properly seated and sealed.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Insulation Breakdown from Mechanical Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
A nick, crush point, or abrasion in the insulation from installation, nearby excavation, or rodent activity progressively tracks through to full dielectric breakdown.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or opens the series loop, darkening some or all fixtures downstream.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger testing to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or TDR to pinpoint the fault location.
Moisture Ingress via Damaged Sheath
High Severity
Root Cause
A compromised outer sheath, or an improperly sealed cable end, lets moisture track into the conductor strands over time.
Effect
Gradually declining insulation resistance that eventually manifests as a ground fault or nuisance CCR trip.
Detection Method
Periodic insulation resistance testing at both ends of the run, and visual inspection of exposed ends and joints for water ingress.
Excessive Voltage Drop on Long Runs
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Conductor undersized for the run length and load, or a series circuit extended beyond its original design without upsizing the cable.
Effect
Fixtures at the far end of the loop run dim or underperform even though the CCR output is correct.
Detection Method
Voltage measurement at the CCR output and at the far end of the loop under load, compared against the calculated drop for the cable size and length.
Thermal Overload from Undersized or Overloaded Cable
High Severity
Root Cause
The installed cable is undersized for the circuit current given its installation method, or the circuit has been loaded beyond the cable's rated ampacity.
Effect
Localised overheating and insulation softening or melting, which can progress to a fault if left unaddressed.
Detection Method
Current verification against the cable's ampacity rating, and a thermal camera scan of accessible sections for warm spots.
High-Resistance Termination at a Connector
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Incorrect connector selection or poor termination technique has produced a joint that runs hot under normal current.
Effect
Repeated faults at the same termination point, and eventually a burnt-back conductor or connector failure.
Detection Method
Inspection for heat discolouration, pitting, or loose connector engagement, and a contact-resistance check where equipment allows.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit Isolation Before Access
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR before opening a base can, splicing a cable, or handling connectors on the primary series circuit; the loop can carry several kV across its length.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected cable section before restoring power after any splice, repair, or connector work, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same loop.

Cable Locating Before Excavation
Operational

Obtain and follow an up-to-date cable locate before any excavation near a primary cable route, since striking a live series circuit cable is both a safety hazard and a system-wide outage risk.

PPE / Insulated Tools
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm isolation with a proving unit before treating any conductor as de-energised.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near a primary cable route within the movement area requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the affected circuit will be dark or out of tolerance.

Quick Reference

Airport Lighting Cable: Runway & Taxiway Cable, Sizing, Insulation, Testing & Faults

Runway Lighting Cable, Runway Light Cable & Taxiway Lighting Cable

The same primary cable family serves every system. Runway lighting cable (also called runway light cable) and taxiway lighting cable differ mainly in loop length, load and route, not in construction. The cable type and rating still need to match the CCR and the project specification.

Airport lighting cable sizing. Conductor size is set by the loop current, loop length, CCR capacity, voltage drop and installation method. A longer loop or higher load needs a larger conductor or a different circuit split. Confirm the sizing against the circuit design, not from a rule of thumb.

Airport lighting cable insulation. The insulation and sheath keep the high-voltage conductor separate from earth. Moisture, damage during installation and ageing at joints are the usual causes of breakdown, so insulation condition is the main thing tested over the life of the cable.

Airport Cable Testing

Airport cable testing normally combines two checks, made with the circuit isolated and earthed: insulation resistance, and continuity, which is covered next.

  • Insulation resistance: measure between the conductor and earth with an insulation tester, and compare with the commissioning value and the project limit.

Airport Cable Continuity Test

Check the conductor with a low-resistance meter to confirm there is no open circuit along the loop.

Airport lighting cable fault types are earth faults, open circuits, shorts and insulation breakdown. They are often found at joints, terminations, or places where the cable was damaged by digging. Splitting the loop at junction points narrows the search. An airport lighting cable connector that has let in water is a common cause as well, so check the L-823 connector before suspecting the cable itself. A cable tester can help locate the fault.

Trend Insulation Readings, Don't Just Pass/Fail Them
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at a splice or connector usually shows up first as a slow decline in megger readings over successive maintenance cycles, long before it becomes a hard fault. Logging trends catches a failing joint months before it takes the loop down, where a single pass/fail reading would miss it entirely.

As-Built Records Drift From Reality Faster Than Expected
Documentation

Cable routes recorded at commissioning can diverge from what's actually in the ground after years of repairs, reroutes, and emergency splices. Verify the as-built drawing against an actual locate before planning excavation near an older primary cable route, rather than trusting the original design record.

Cable Ampacity Assumes a Burial Environment That May Have Changed
Design

A cable's rated current-carrying capacity depends on the thermal resistivity of the surrounding soil and burial depth assumed at design time; adjacent excavation, added pavement, or nearby heat sources installed later can change that environment. Re-check derating assumptions if the burial context has visibly changed since original installation.

A Single Fault Can Masquerade as a Distributed Problem
Troubleshooting

An intermittent loop fault that seems to move between fixtures on different visits is often one degrading splice whose contact resistance varies with temperature or moisture, not multiple separate faults. Time-domain reflectometry to localise the actual fault point saves far more time than sequentially testing each fixture along the loop.

Repair Splices Are the Weakest Point in an Aging Loop
Reliability

Every emergency repair splice made under time pressure to restore service is a slightly weaker point than an original factory joint, even when done correctly. Track splice locations and ages across the loop so future fault-finding starts with the most likely candidates rather than the whole route.

Groundwater Table Changes Alter Cable Risk Over Time
Durability

A cable route that was well-drained at installation can end up seasonally waterlogged years later due to drainage changes, new pavement, or shifting site grading. Where insulation faults cluster in one section, check the current drainage situation before assuming the cable itself has simply reached end of life.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the AGL primary cable: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an AGL primary cable?

The AGL primary cable (also called the series cable or loop cable) is the main power distribution cable in airfield ground lighting. It carries constant current, typically 6.6 A or 20 A, from the constant current regulator (CCR) through a continuous loop to every isolating transformer and fixture on a series circuit.

Q2Where is the AGL primary cable used?

On all primary series circuits: runway lighting (edge, centreline, threshold, end and touchdown zone lights), taxiway lighting (edge and centreline lights, stop bars, clearance bars and guidance signs) and approach lighting systems such as ALSF-1, ALSF-2 and MALSR.

Q3How is the cable constructed?

A stranded annealed copper conductor (aluminium in some cases) is covered by thick XLPE or EPR insulation and an outer sheath of PVC, TPU or HDPE for direct burial. Steel wire or steel tape armour is optional for mechanical protection. It can be single-core or multi-core, though single-core is common.

Q4What conductor sizes are typical?

Typical cross-sections are 6 mm², 10 mm², 16 mm², 25 mm² and 35 mm² (roughly AWG 10 to 2). Resistance at 20°C is about 3.08 Ω/km for 6 mm², 1.83 Ω/km for 10 mm², 1.15 Ω/km for 16 mm² and 0.727 Ω/km for 25 mm².

Q5What voltage rating does the cable have?

The nominal rating is 5 kV per FAA L-824, or 3.6/6 kV to 3.8/6.6 kV under IEC and BS practice. The high rating matters because a series loop can present several kV under open-circuit conditions.

Q6What temperature limits apply?

The operating range is -40°C to +90°C for XLPE and -40°C to +70°C for PVC. Maximum conductor temperature under short circuit for 5 seconds is 250°C for XLPE and 160°C for PVC.

Q7What insulation resistance should the cable have?

At least 50 MΩ measured cable-to-ground with a megger. Insulation breakdown or a conductor fault registers at the CCR as a ground fault or open circuit.

Q8Which standards apply to AGL primary cable?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-7 (L-824 underground cable for airport lighting circuits), IEC 60502, BS 5467 and UL 1072. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should the primary cable be tested and inspected?

Typical annual checks cover insulation resistance, voltage drop and loop resistance, terminations, sheath condition, conductor loading, cable end and splice seals, rodent and mechanical damage, and route and marker verification. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with AGL primary cable?

Common problems are insulation damage during installation, moisture ingress at cable ends, excessive voltage drop on long runs, overheating from overloading, chemical attack on the sheath, rodent damage and incorrect termination. A circuit that goes dark, low insulation resistance readings or dim fixtures at the end of a long run are typical symptoms.
