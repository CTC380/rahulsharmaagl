Medium Intensity Obstruction Light

A versatile aviation warning light used on structures between 45 and 150 metres in height. Medium intensity obstruction lights operate during both day and night, providing a bright red flashing or steady‑burning signal that ensures obstacles are visible to pilots in all lighting conditions. They are the most common type of obstruction light for towers, wind turbines, and tall buildings.

Overview

What Is It?

A Medium Intensity Obstruction Light is an aviation warning light designed for structures that are between 45 and 150 metres (150 to 500 feet) in height, or as an intermediate light on taller structures. These lights provide a high‑intensity red signal that is visible during both daytime and nighttime operations, ensuring that obstacles are clearly marked for pilots at all times.

Medium intensity lights are characterised by their dual‑mode operation: they typically flash during the day (with higher intensity) and switch to a steady‑burning or slower‑flashing mode at night. This adaptive behaviour optimises visibility while minimising light pollution and power consumption. They are widely used on communication towers, wind turbines, chimneys, buildings, and transmission towers, making them the most common type of obstruction lighting for medium‑height structures.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Medium intensity lighting exists to fill the operational middle ground between structures too short to need aggressive warning and those tall enough to require the most powerful high-intensity systems: at 45–150 metres, a structure is tall enough to be a genuine hazard but not so tall that only maximum-intensity lighting would suffice. The day/night dual-mode behaviour — higher-intensity flashing by day, steady or slower flashing at night — exists for the same underlying reason as in high-intensity systems, matching output to the different visibility demands of daylight versus darkness, but calibrated to a level proportionate to a mid-height structure rather than the most extreme obstacles. Because this height band covers the largest share of towers, chimneys, buildings, and turbines that require any lighting at all, medium intensity's purpose is essentially to be the practical, most widely applicable option: enough warning to be effective without over-specifying a level of intensity the structure's height doesn't warrant.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Medium intensity obstruction lights are installed on a wide range of structures:

  • Communication towers – mobile phone masts, radio/TV broadcast towers (typically 45–150m).
  • Wind turbines – as top‑of‑nacelle lights (often required for turbines over 45m).
  • Chimneys and smokestacks – industrial and power plant structures.
  • High‑rise buildings – commercial and residential towers.
  • Transmission towers – electrical power line pylons.
  • As intermediate lights on tall structures – in combination with high intensity lights on structures over 150m.
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the top of the structure and, on taller structures used in combination with high-intensity lighting, at the intermediate levels specified by the applicable lighting scheme. Mounting brackets are matched to the structure type — pole or lattice-arm mounts on towers and masts, facade brackets on buildings, or dedicated plinths on chimneys — and sized for the wind and vibration loading at the installation height. Positioning keeps each light clear of the structure's own members, antennas, or adjacent equipment so the day/night dual-mode signal remains visible from all directions. Power and control cabling runs back to a photocell-equipped controller that switches the fixture between its higher-intensity daytime flash and lower-intensity or steady nighttime mode, with cabling and connections rated for continuous outdoor exposure at the structure's height.

Function

How Does It Work?

Medium intensity obstruction lights operate with adaptive intensity and flashing patterns to ensure visibility in all conditions:

  • Light Source: Modern systems use high‑brightness LED arrays, which offer superior energy efficiency and long life compared to traditional halogen lamps (100–200W). LED systems also allow precise control of intensity and flashing patterns.
  • Colour: The light emits a red beam (aviation red), which is the internationally recognised colour for obstruction marking. Red provides high contrast against the sky during both day and night.
  • Beam Pattern: Most medium intensity obstruction lights are omnidirectional (360° horizontal coverage) to ensure visibility from all approach angles.
  • Day/Night Operation: The light operates in two modes:
    • Daytime: Flashing mode with high intensity (typically 2,000–20,000 cd) to be visible against bright daylight.
    • Nighttime: Steady‑burning or reduced‑intensity flashing (typically 2,000 cd or less) to reduce light pollution and glare.
  • Control: A photocell (day/night sensor) automatically switches between modes. Some systems are remotely controlled via a central monitoring system.
  • Power: Typically powered from the local mains supply (110/230V AC) or via a dedicated solar‑powered system for remote locations.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The day/night photocell switching shown opposite sits inside a self-contained architecture at the fixture itself — there's no shared series circuit tying it to other lights on the structure or the airfield. Mains or solar/battery power feeds the light's own local controller, which reads the photocell and sets the flash mode and intensity entirely on-site, with no dependency on an external control system to function. Because each light runs its own photocell cycle independently, multiple medium intensity lights on the same structure aren't synchronised to one another the way high-intensity tiers are — each simply reacts to the ambient light it sees. Where a facility wants centralised oversight, the fixture can report status to a remote or central monitoring system, letting maintenance staff see lamp or power faults without a site visit; that link, however, is for monitoring only, and the local controller keeps switching modes on schedule even if the connection to the monitoring system drops.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is built around a weatherproof die-cast aluminium or high-strength polymer housing, sized for surface- or pole-mounting on structures such as masts, towers, and cranes at intermediate obstruction heights. A red, toughened lens covers the high-intensity LED array or halogen lamp, gasket-sealed to achieve an IP65/IP67, dustproof and weather-resistant rating suited to prolonged outdoor exposure. The housing's mounting base accommodates either direct surface fixing or a pole-clamp bracket, with the optical assembly oriented to deliver the specified omnidirectional beam coverage and vertical beam angle. Internal driver electronics regulating flash rate and day/night intensity switching are enclosed within the sealed housing, with a separate compartment or gland for the incoming power cable, whether mains AC, low-voltage DC, or a solar-charging connection. Corrosion-resistant fasteners and UV-stable housing finishes are used throughout to withstand sustained sun, rain, and temperature cycling at height.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Photocell Failure

The day/night sensor is critical for proper operation. Failure can result in the light operating at the wrong intensity (e.g., flashing brightly at night), causing light pollution and potential pilot distraction. Regular inspection and cleaning of the photocell are essential.

LED Driver Failure

Medium intensity LED obstruction lights rely on electronic drivers. These can fail due to thermal stress, power surges, or moisture ingress. Regular inspection and testing of the driver module are recommended.

Lens Contamination

Dust, dirt, bird droppings, and environmental pollutants can reduce light output and compromise visibility. Routine cleaning with appropriate materials is necessary to maintain performance.

Lightning and Surge Damage

Obstruction lights on tall structures are vulnerable to lightning strikes and power surges. Proper surge protection devices (SPDs) and lightning arrestors are essential to protect the electronics.

Flashing Synchronisation

When multiple medium intensity lights are installed on a single structure (e.g., intermediate levels), they must be synchronised to flash simultaneously. Synchronisation failure can create confusing visual patterns. Regular checks of the synchronisation system are recommended.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Light Fails to Operate
Likely Cause
Failed LED driver module, often from thermal stress, power surge, or moisture ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Check driver output voltage/current against spec; inspect the driver compartment for moisture staining or heat discoloration; megger the circuit for surge damage.
Corrective Action
Replace the driver module, confirm the compartment seal is intact, and verify SPDs are functioning before re-energising.
Wrong Intensity or Flash Pattern at Night
Likely Cause
Photocell failure is causing the controller to misjudge ambient conditions, running the wrong intensity step or flash mode.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare photocell output to a reference light meter; check controller mode/intensity-step settings against the site's operating plan.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the photocell and confirm the controller is set to the correct intensity step and flash pattern for day/night operation.
Loss of Synchronisation with Other Fixtures
Likely Cause
Controller clock drift or a failed sync signal link between fixtures on the same structure.
Diagnostic Steps
Time the flash pattern of each fixture against the others with a stopwatch or sync tester; check sync signal wiring/receiver status.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the controller's sync module and re-verify all fixtures flash in unison after the fix.
Surge or Lightning Damage
Likely Cause
A lightning strike or induced surge has damaged the driver or controller despite installed protection.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect SPDs for a blown/failed indicator; megger the circuit; check for visible strike damage at the mounting point.
Corrective Action
Replace failed SPDs and damaged electronics, and confirm the structure's grounding and lightning arrestor system meets resistance requirements.
Reduced Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from dust, dirt, or bird droppings, or gradual LED lumen depreciation.
Diagnostic Steps
Take a photometric reading against the rated intensity; visually inspect the lens for contamination.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with an approved non-abrasive cleaner; replace the LED module if output remains below spec after cleaning.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Day and Night Intensity Modes Need Separate Verification
Photocell

Because this fixture is designed to be visible in both daytime and nighttime operation at different intensity levels, a photometric check run only at one time of day can miss a fault confined to the other mode. Verify both day and night intensity settings during commissioning and periodic checks rather than testing whichever mode happens to be active at inspection time.

Solar-Backed Units Need Season-Aware Battery Checks
Reliability

Where this class of light is solar-powered, its daytime flash mode draws meaningfully more current than steady night-time operation, which can outpace panel charging during a run of short winter days even if the battery tested fine in summer. Re-check backup power adequacy around the low-daylight season, not only once at commissioning.

Flash Rate Verification Should Be Instrumented, Not Eyeballed
Diagnostics

A flash rate that's drifted slightly out of the ICAO/FAA-specified range is very difficult to judge reliably by eye against a stopwatch estimate, especially at the flash rates typical of this class of fixture. Use proper timing instrumentation for flash-rate verification during both commissioning and routine checks.

Lens Fouling Cuts Effective Intensity Before It's Visually Obvious
Maintenance

A lens with a thin, even film of dust or grime can look "clean enough" on casual visual inspection while still reducing measured intensity below the compliant threshold. Rely on the photometric reading rather than a visual assessment to decide whether lens cleaning is due.

Mounting Height Positioning Should Be Re-Checked After Structural Changes
Compliance

A structure that gets an antenna, added equipment, or a roof modification after the obstruction light was installed can shift the fixture's effective height relative to the structure's actual highest point, quietly invalidating the original marking scheme. Re-verify positioning whenever the host structure is modified, not only when the light itself is serviced.

Backup Power Verification Deserves an Actual Load Test
Testing

A backup supply reading correct standby voltage on a meter doesn't confirm it can actually carry the fixture through a real outage if the transfer mechanism or battery capacity has quietly degraded. Run a genuine load or transfer test on backup power rather than accepting a voltage reading alone as sufficient verification.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about medium intensity obstruction lights: when they are used, day and night modes, intensity, power options, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a medium intensity obstruction light?

A medium intensity obstruction light is an aviation warning light for structures between 45 and 150 m (150 to 500 ft) in height, or as an intermediate light on taller structures. It shows aviation red and typically flashes at higher intensity by day, then switches to a reduced steady or flashing mode at night.

Q2Which structures use medium intensity obstruction lights?

Communication towers typically 45–150 m tall, wind turbines (often as top-of-nacelle lights on turbines over 45 m), chimneys and smokestacks, high-rise buildings and transmission towers. They also serve as intermediate lights combined with high intensity lights on structures over 150 m.

Q3What is the intensity of a medium intensity obstruction light?

Day intensity is 2,000–20,000 cd flashing, and night intensity is reduced to about 2,000 cd, steady or flashing. Chromaticity must fall within the ICAO Annex 14 aviation red boundary.

Q4What is the flash rate of a medium intensity obstruction light?

The daytime flash rate is typically 20–40 flashes per minute. Night operation follows the configured pattern, steady or slower flashing, which reduces light pollution and power consumption.

Q5How does a medium intensity light switch between day and night modes?

A photocell or twilight sensor selects the day or night intensity step. If the photocell fails, the light can run at the wrong intensity, such as flashing brightly at night, so its switching should be checked regularly.

Q6What beam pattern does a medium intensity obstruction light have?

Omnidirectional with 360° horizontal coverage, and a vertical beam angle typically 10° to 30° above horizontal so it stays visible to approaching aircraft.

Q7How are medium intensity obstruction lights powered?

From 110–230 V AC mains, 12/24 V DC or a solar panel with battery storage, depending on site power availability. LED versions draw about 20–80 W, and solar variants use a charge controller and battery low-voltage disconnect.

Q8Which standards apply to medium intensity obstruction lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment), FAA AC 70/7460-1 (obstruction marking and lighting) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED performance. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q9How often should medium intensity obstruction lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for day/night photocell switching and flash rate verification, and annual for photometric intensity checks, seal and weatherproofing inspection and cable and power connection inspection. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does my medium intensity light fail to operate or run the wrong pattern at night?

A light that will not operate is often a failed LED driver module from thermal stress, a power surge or moisture ingress. A wrong intensity or flash pattern at night usually points to photocell failure. Check driver output, megger the circuit for surge damage, compare the photocell to a reference light meter and confirm the controller's intensity step.
