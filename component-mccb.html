Moulded Case Circuit Breaker (MCCB)

A high‑performance, adjustable circuit breaker designed for protection of AGL distribution circuits and feeder cables. MCCBs offer higher current ratings, adjustable trip settings, and greater breaking capacity than MCBs, making them ideal for protecting the main power distribution panels, CCR inputs, and large‑capacity series circuits in Airfield Ground Lighting infrastructure.

Overview

What Is It?

A Moulded Case Circuit Breaker (MCCB) is a type of circuit breaker that provides overcurrent protection for higher‑current circuits, typically from 16A up to 1600A or more. Unlike MCBs (Miniature Circuit Breakers), which are limited to lower ratings (up to 125A) and have fixed trip characteristics, MCCBs offer:

  • Adjustable Trip Settings: The overload (thermal) and short‑circuit (magnetic) trip settings can be adjusted to match the specific circuit requirements.
  • Higher Breaking Capacity: MCCBs are capable of interrupting fault currents up to 100kA or more, making them suitable for high‑fault‑current environments like main distribution boards and CCR inputs.
  • Higher Current Ratings: Available in a wide range of current ratings from 16A to 1600A, covering all AGL power distribution needs.
  • Electronic Trip Units: Many MCCBs are equipped with microprocessor‑based trip units that provide sophisticated protection curves (LSI – Long‑time, Short‑time, Instantaneous) and communication capabilities.
  • Robust Enclosure: The moulded case provides excellent insulation and mechanical protection, making MCCBs suitable for harsh environments.

In AGL systems, MCCBs are used as the main circuit protection devices in substations, CCR rooms, and distribution panels, protecting the incoming power supply and major feeder cables.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The MCCB's purpose is to provide the same fault-clearing protection as an MCB but at the higher currents and fault levels found at an airfield's main distribution and CCR input points, where a fault could otherwise release far more energy than a miniature breaker is designed to interrupt safely. Its adjustable trip settings let the protection be tuned precisely to the cable and equipment it protects rather than accepting a fixed, one-size-fits-all threshold, and its higher breaking capacity is what allows it to safely clear a short circuit on a high-current feeder without the breaker itself failing catastrophically in the process. In systems with electronic LSI trip units, this purpose extends to selective coordination — ensuring that when a fault does occur, only the breaker closest to it trips, rather than an upstream breaker unnecessarily taking out a much larger section of the airfield.

Application

Where Is It Used?

MCCBs are used in the higher‑power sections of AGL electrical infrastructure:

  • Incoming Main Distribution: Protecting the main power supply to the AGL substation from faults in the utility feed or internal distribution.
  • CCR Input Protection: Protecting the input side of each Constant Current Regulator from overloads or short circuits in the power supply.
  • Sub‑Distribution Panels: Protecting feeder cables that supply power to various airfield sectors (e.g., runway 09, runway 27, taxiway lighting, apron lighting).
  • Generator and UPS Changeover Panels: Protecting the connection between the backup power sources (generators, UPS) and the AGL distribution system.
  • Large‑Capacity Series Circuits: In some applications, MCCBs are used to protect high‑current series circuits (e.g., 20A isolating transformer circuits).
Placement

Installation & Location

MCCBs are panel-mounted at the higher-power points in the network — incoming main distribution, CCR input protection, sub-distribution feeders, and generator or UPS changeover panels — fixed to a rigid mounting plate or chassis rail rated for the breaker's frame size and fault current. Given their larger physical size and higher current ratings compared with MCBs, MCCBs are positioned with generous bus bar and cable bend clearances, and enclosures around them are sized to manage the heat generated under sustained load. Their location within a switchboard reflects the distribution hierarchy — mains incomers first, then feeder MCCBs to each sector of the airfield — so isolation for maintenance follows a clear, traceable sequence back to the main supply.

Function

How Does It Work?

An MCCB operates using a combination of thermal, magnetic, and (in electronic versions) microprocessor‑based trip mechanisms:

  • Thermal Overload Protection: A bimetallic element heats up and bends under sustained overcurrent, triggering the trip mechanism after a time delay. The delay is inversely proportional to the square of the current (I²t curve), providing true thermal protection for cables and equipment.
  • Magnetic Short‑Circuit Protection: An electromagnetic coil provides instantaneous tripping under severe fault currents (short circuits). The trip threshold is adjustable in many MCCBs.
  • Electronic Trip Units (LSI): Microprocessor‑based trip units offer three distinct protection functions:
    • L (Long‑time): Adjustable overload protection with inverse‑time characteristic.
    • S (Short‑time): Adjustable short‑time protection with definite‑time or inverse‑time characteristic, providing selective coordination with downstream devices.
    • I (Instantaneous): Adjustable instantaneous protection for high‑magnitude faults.
  • Arc Extinction: MCCBs use arc chutes with de‑ionizing plates to extinguish the arc quickly and efficiently, ensuring safe interruption of high fault currents.
  • Communication: Modern MCCBs can be equipped with communication modules (Modbus, Profibus, Ethernet) for remote monitoring, control, and integration with the ALCMS.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The MCCB typically sits one tier above the MCBs it protects, at the main incomer or a major sub-distribution point, and its role in the architecture is defined as much by coordination as by protection: its long-time, short-time, and instantaneous (LSI) settings are deliberately staggered against the trip curves of the MCBs downstream so that a fault on a final circuit is cleared by the MCB first, with the MCCB acting as backup protection only if the MCB fails to clear it. Where the MCCB is fitted with an electronic trip unit and a communication module (Modbus, Profibus, or Ethernet), its status, trip history, and even its protection settings can be read and adjusted remotely by the ALCMS or a SCADA system, turning it from a purely local protection device into a monitored node in the wider control network — a capability that basic thermal-magnetic MCCBs, which report nothing beyond the position of their handle, do not have.

Construction

Physical Construction

The MCCB's moulded case is formed from high-dielectric-strength, flame-retardant thermoset material engineered to contain internal arcing and withstand the mechanical and thermal stresses of interrupting large fault currents without case rupture. Heavy-duty silver-alloy contacts, sized for the breaker's higher current rating, are operated by a stored-energy spring mechanism that ensures rapid, contact-welding-resistant opening regardless of manual operating speed, backed by a larger arc chamber with multiple splitter plates to quench higher-energy arcs. The trip unit, whether a fixed thermal-magnetic assembly or a field-adjustable electronic (LSI) unit, is mounted in a separate compartment accessible for setting adjustment without exposing the main contact chamber. Line and load terminals are designed for larger cable lugs or bus bar connections and are shrouded per the enclosure rating, with the complete unit available in DIN-rail, panel-mount, or draw-out chassis configurations to suit switchgear integration.

Pre-Energisation

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off

Record insulation, contact resistance, and trip unit settings alongside the MCCB's rating and position in the commissioning dossier.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Trip Setting Adjustment

Electronic MCCBs offer adjustable LSI settings. Incorrect adjustment (e.g., setting the instantaneous threshold too low) can cause nuisance tripping or loss of selective coordination with downstream devices. Proper coordination studies and careful setting are essential.

Insufficient Breaking Capacity

If the available fault current exceeds the MCCB's breaking capacity (Icu), the breaker may fail to interrupt safely, leading to catastrophic failure. Verify the fault current at the installation point and select an appropriate MCCB with adequate Icu.

Loose Connections and Terminals

High‑current MCCBs are particularly sensitive to loose connections. Poor terminal tightening can cause heating, arcing, and eventual failure. Use proper torque tools and follow manufacturer torque specifications.

Electronic Trip Unit Battery Depletion

MCCBs with electronic trip units often have internal batteries to maintain settings and provide power for the display/communication during power loss. These batteries have a limited lifespan (typically 10 years) and need periodic replacement.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Nuisance Tripping or Loss of Coordination with Downstream Devices
Likely Cause
Electronic LSI trip settings (particularly the instantaneous threshold) have been set too low or without a proper coordination study.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the current LSI settings against the site's protection coordination study; check trip event logs to identify which protection function (long-time, short-time, or instantaneous) is triggering.
Corrective Action
Re-adjust LSI settings per a proper coordination study, and re-verify selective coordination with upstream and downstream devices before returning to service.
Breaker Fails to Clear a Fault Cleanly
Likely Cause
The available fault current at the installation point exceeds the MCCB's rated breaking capacity (Icu).
Diagnostic Steps
Calculate or verify the prospective fault current at the installation point; compare against the installed MCCB's Icu rating on its nameplate.
Corrective Action
Replace with an MCCB rated for the actual fault current level, and re-verify coordination with adjacent protective devices before returning to service.
Warm or Discoloured Breaker Body or Terminals
Likely Cause
Loose connections or terminal tightening below the manufacturer's torque specification, causing resistive heating and arcing at high current.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise and inspect terminal torque using a calibrated torque tool against the manufacturer's specification; check for discolouration, pitting, or arc marks at the terminals.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate to the correct torque, and replace the breaker if terminal damage (melting, pitting) is found; schedule a follow-up thermal check after re-energising.
Trip Unit Loses Settings or Display After Power Loss
Likely Cause
The electronic trip unit's internal backup battery has depleted, typically after around 10 years of service.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the trip unit's battery status indicator if available; note the installation or last battery-replacement date; verify whether settings and display return correctly once mains power is restored.
Corrective Action
Replace the internal battery per the manufacturer's procedure, and re-verify and re-program trip settings afterward to confirm nothing was lost.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Upstream Isolation Before Removal
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the upstream supply before removing or replacing an MCCB, and verify zero energy at its terminals before disconnecting busbars or cabling, even though the breaker's own contacts are open.

Correct Rating and Trip Settings on Replacement
Compliance

Fit a replacement MCCB with the same frame size, rated current, and breaking capacity as the original, and verify adjustable thermal and magnetic trip settings match the coordinated protection scheme before returning it to service.

Investigate Before Resetting a Tripped Breaker
Personnel Safety

Investigate the cause of a trip before resetting an MCCB, particularly on a main distribution board or CCR input feeding significant downstream load; repeatedly closing onto a persistent fault risks a violent failure at these higher fault currents.

Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any work downstream of an MCCB, verify insulation resistance and correct load connection before closing the breaker, since a residual fault at re-energisation at this current level can cause significant equipment damage.

Arc-Flash Awareness
Personnel Safety

Follow the site's arc-flash procedure, including appropriate PPE and safe working distance, when racking, operating, or working near an MCCB on a high-fault-current board, given the higher breaking capacities involved.

FOD Control at the Panel
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and breaker blanks accounted for at all times when a distribution board is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it.

Check for Thermal Damage on Replacement
Personnel Safety

Inspect the busbar and adjacent connections for discolouration or heat damage when replacing an MCCB that tripped on an overload or showed signs of overheating, since a hot connection at these current levels can damage neighbouring equipment.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a board with an incorrectly rated breaker, mismatched trip settings, or a loose termination after maintenance; verify correct settings, labelling, and torque before closing out the work order.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Adjustable Trip Settings Need a Documented Setting, Not Just a Capable Breaker
Commissioning

An MCCB's adjustable thermal and magnetic settings are only useful if the as-set values are recorded and match the coordination study; a breaker left at factory default settings after installation can defeat the discrimination the design engineer calculated on paper.

Re-Verify Discrimination After Any Downstream Change
Coordination

Adding or resizing a downstream breaker or feeder can quietly break a previously-verified discrimination study; treat any downstream distribution change as a trigger to re-check coordination against the MCCB's settings, not just confirm the new circuit trips correctly on its own.

Confirm Breaking Capacity Against Actual Prospective Fault Current
Design

A breaking capacity that was adequate at initial design can become marginal after upstream transformer upgrades or utility network changes increase available fault current; recheck prospective fault current at the MCCB's location whenever upstream supply capacity changes, not only at initial specification.

Contact Wear Shows Up as Heat Before It Shows Up as a Trip
Troubleshooting

Worn or pitted contacts inside an MCCB usually manifest first as localised heating at the terminals under load, well before the breaker mechanism itself misbehaves; periodic thermal imaging of main distribution boards catches this failure mode earlier than waiting for a nuisance trip or failure to close.

Exercise Main Breakers That Rarely Operate
Maintenance

MCCBs at the head of a distribution board or CCR input can go years without a full open-close cycle; a mechanism that has seized slightly from disuse may not close or trip cleanly when actually called on. Build a manual exercise cycle into the maintenance plan rather than assuming standby readiness.

Keep Trip Unit Firmware and Settings Documentation Current
Documentation

On electronic trip-unit MCCBs, a firmware update or trip-unit replacement can silently reset protection settings to default; always confirm and re-document the applied settings after any trip-unit servicing, rather than assuming the replacement preserved the prior configuration.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about moulded case circuit breakers in AGL distribution: LSI settings, ratings, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is an MCCB?

A Moulded Case Circuit Breaker gives overcurrent protection for higher-current circuits, typically 16 A up to 1600 A or more, above the range of MCBs.

Q2How is an MCCB different from an MCB?

An MCCB handles higher currents and fault levels, with breaking capacities of 10-100 kA, adjustable trip settings in many models and optional communication, whereas MCBs are limited to lower ratings (up to 125 A).

Q3Where are MCCBs used in AGL systems?

In higher-power sections such as incoming main distribution and CCR input protection.

Q4How does an MCCB trip?

Using thermal and magnetic mechanisms, or in electronic versions a microprocessor-based trip unit.

Q5What do LSI settings mean?

Long-time (Ir) covers sustained overload, short-time (Isd) covers short-circuit with a delay for coordination, and instantaneous (Ii) covers severe faults. Some units add ground-fault (Ig).

Q6What ratings are typical?

16-1600 A, 400/690/1000 V AC (up to 1500 V DC), breaking capacity of 10-100 kA, 3- or 4-pole, mounted on DIN rail, panel or draw-out.

Q7Can an MCCB connect to ALCMS?

Optional Modbus, Profibus or Ethernet communication provides remote status and trip alarms to ALCMS.

Q8Which standards apply?

IEC 60947-2, UL 489, IS/IEC 60947-2 and NFPA 70 (NEC).

Q9How often should an MCCB be checked?

Annually: verify trip settings, re-torque terminals, test and replace the trip unit backup battery, and review that breaking capacity still exceeds system fault levels.

Q10Why is there nuisance tripping or lost coordination?

Electronic LSI settings, particularly the instantaneous threshold, may be too low or set without a coordination study. Review the settings against the protection study.
