Weather Station

A comprehensive suite of meteorological sensors that provides real‑time weather data critical for airfield operations. Weather stations support ATC decision‑making, flight planning, low‑visibility procedures, and winter maintenance, delivering essential parameters including wind, visibility, temperature, pressure, and precipitation.

Overview

What Is It?

A Weather Station (also known as an AWOS – Automated Weather Observing System, or METAR station) is an integrated system of meteorological sensors and data processing equipment that measures, records, and disseminates weather conditions at an airfield. These systems provide critical environmental data for ATC, pilots, airport operations, and maintenance teams. A typical airfield weather station includes sensors for wind speed and direction, temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, precipitation, visibility (RVR – Runway Visual Range), cloud height (ceilometer), and present weather (rain, snow, fog). The data is transmitted to ATC, flight planning systems, airfield displays, and often broadcast via ATIS (Automatic Terminal Information Service). Weather stations are essential for safe aircraft operations, enabling low‑visibility procedures (CAT II/III), runway selection, and winter maintenance planning. They are typically installed at multiple locations across the airfield, with primary units near the runway threshold and secondary units at other key points.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The weather station's purpose is to turn a set of individually noisy sensor readings, wind, visibility, ceiling, pressure, into a single, consistent operational picture that ATC, pilots, and airport operations can all act on with confidence. Because low-visibility procedures such as CAT II/III activation, runway selection, and winter maintenance triggers depend on threshold values being crossed reliably rather than approximately, the station's role is as much about measurement integrity and consistent siting as it is about the sensors themselves; a reading taken at the wrong point on the airfield or with a miscalibrated instrument can trigger the wrong operational response. Distributing primary and secondary units across key locations, rather than relying on one central reading, is what lets the reported conditions actually reflect what a specific runway threshold or approach path is experiencing rather than an airfield-wide average.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Weather stations are deployed at various critical locations across the airfield:

  • Runway Thresholds: Primary weather stations located near runway ends, providing RVR, wind, and visibility data for approach and landing.
  • ATC Towers: Integrated weather displays for controllers, often with dedicated wind sensors on the tower itself.
  • Mid‑Runway: Additional wind and visibility sensors for cross‑wind and mid‑field conditions.
  • Apron Areas: Weather data for ground handling, gate operations, and de‑icing procedures.
  • Remote Locations: Stand‑alone weather stations for specific approach paths or outlying areas.
  • Maintenance Facilities: Weather data for winter maintenance planning and runway friction management.
  • Flight Planning & Briefing: Providing weather data to pilots for pre‑flight planning and dispatch.
  • ATIS Broadcast Systems: Automated weather reporting for pilot information.
Placement

Installation & Location

Individual sensor heads (wind, RVR transmissometer, ceilometer, temperature/humidity) are distributed at the specific locations their reading applies to — threshold, mid-runway, or apron — each mounted on its own mast or post at the height and setback specified for that sensor type, kept clear of turbulence-inducing obstacles and of the runway strip's frangibility requirements. Sensor cabling runs back to a local field enclosure and from there, generally over fibre or a dedicated data link, to the central weather processing system so RVR and wind readings reach ATC with minimal latency; mast foundations account for wind loading given the sensors' typically exposed siting, and each sensor's exact position is surveyed and documented since its reading is only valid for the specific location it represents.

Function

How Does It Work?

The weather station operates as an integrated data acquisition and dissemination system. Key functional aspects include:

  • Sensor Suite: The station includes multiple sensors, each measuring a specific parameter:
    • Wind Sensors: Ultrasonic or cup‑and‑vane anemometers for speed and direction, often with gust measurement.
    • Temperature & Humidity: Platinum resistance thermometers (RTD) and capacitive humidity sensors in a radiation shield.
    • Barometric Pressure: Silicon‑based or capacitive pressure transducers, providing QNH and QFE readings.
    • Visibility (RVR): Forward scatter meters or transmissometers measuring atmospheric transparency.
    • Ceilometer: Laser‑based instrument measuring cloud ceiling height (up to 15,000 ft).
    • Precipitation: Tipping‑bucket rain gauge or optical disdrometer for rain/snow measurement.
    • Present Weather: Optical sensors that detect fog, haze, rain, snow, and other obscurations.
  • Data Acquisition: A central data logger samples sensors at high frequency (typically 1‑10 Hz), processes raw data, and computes averaged and instant values.
  • Quality Control: The system applies algorithms to detect sensor failures, outliers, and consistency checks (e.g., comparing redundant sensors) to ensure data reliability.
  • Data Dissemination: Real‑time data is transmitted via Ethernet, serial (RS‑232/485), or wireless to ATC displays, A‑SMGCS, ATIS, and weather networks (e.g., World Meteorological Organization).
  • Data Logging: Historical weather data is stored locally and often transmitted to central servers for analysis, reporting, and regulatory compliance.
  • Self‑Diagnostics: The station monitors its own health (power, communication, sensor status) and provides alarms for maintenance.

Weather stations are designed for 24/7/365 operation in harsh environments, with heated sensors for icing conditions, redundant power supplies, and lightning protection.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The weather station's data has two separate downstream paths rather than a single control loop. Raw sensor readings are aggregated and quality-checked by the station's own data logger, then distributed over Ethernet or serial to ATC displays, ATIS, and A-SMGCS for direct human and system use — these paths carry information only, with no lighting action attached. Where RVR is used to drive airfield lighting intensity, a separate path feeds the RVR reading (via the ALCMS server or a dedicated interface) into the brightness-step logic that also considers ATC instruction and pilot-controlled lighting input, and it is that combined logic — not the weather station directly — that commands the CCR to change intensity. A sensor fault or data-quality flag is reported at the station level and, if it affects a value used for lighting control, is escalated to the ALCMS as a degraded-input condition rather than silently continuing to drive brightness decisions on unreliable data.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each sensor — anemometer, thermometer, hygrometer, barometer, precipitation gauge, ceilometer, or visibility sensor — is housed in its own sealed, weatherproof enclosure rated IP65/IP67, mounted at the height and orientation its measurement principle requires on a common mast, pole, or tripod, typically 10–30 m tall for wind sensors and lower for temperature and precipitation instruments. Sensors exposed to icing conditions incorporate internal heating elements to keep moving parts (anemometer cups or vanes) and measurement surfaces free of ice, drawing the higher end of the unit's power consumption range during cold-weather operation. All sensor cabling runs down the mast in a common duct or cable tray to a base-mounted data logger enclosure, itself sealed to IP65/IP67 and housing the signal-conditioning electronics, communication interfaces, and battery/solar backup where mains power is not available at the site. The mast or tripod structure is engineered for the local wind-loading design case, and where sited near the runway, mast height and guy-wire anchoring are coordinated with obstacle-limitation surface requirements.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Sensor Icing

Wind sensors, particularly cup‑and‑vane anemometers, can freeze in icing conditions, providing erroneous or no data. Heated sensors (thermostatically controlled) are essential for cold‑weather operations. Regular inspection of heating elements is required.

Visibility Sensor Contamination

Forward scatter meters and transmissometers are sensitive to dirt, dust, salt, and bird droppings on the optical windows. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft cloths is essential for accurate RVR readings.

Radiation Shield Degradation

Temperature and humidity sensors are housed in radiation shields (passive or aspirated). Over time, shields can become dirty or damaged, causing solar radiation heating errors. Regular cleaning and inspection of the shield are required.

Power Supply Instability

Weather stations, especially those with heating elements, draw significant power. Voltage fluctuations or power outages can cause data loss or sensor resets. Use UPS and redundant power sources.

Communication Dropout

Weather data is critical for ATC; any communication loss can disrupt operations. Use redundant communication paths (e.g., wired + wireless) and monitor link health.

Sensor Calibration Drift

All meteorological sensors drift over time, affecting accuracy. Regular calibration (typically annually) against certified reference standards is essential to maintain data integrity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

No or Erroneous Wind Data in Cold Weather
Likely Cause
Sensor icing on a cup-and-vane anemometer.
Diagnostic Steps
Check heater status and thermostatic control, and inspect for physical ice on the sensor.
Corrective Action
Repair or activate the heating element, clear ice from the sensor, and verify normal readings resume.
Inaccurate RVR / Visibility Readings
Likely Cause
Contamination of the forward scatter meter or transmissometer optical windows.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect optical windows for dirt, dust, salt, or bird droppings, and compare readings before and after cleaning.
Corrective Action
Clean optical windows with approved solvents and soft cloths, and schedule more frequent cleaning if contamination recurs quickly.
Temperature/Humidity Readings Show Solar Heating Error
Likely Cause
Radiation shield degradation (dirty or damaged, passive or aspirated).
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the radiation shield for dirt or damage and compare readings against a reference sensor in known-good shielding.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the radiation shield, and verify the aspirated shield fan (if fitted) is operating.
Data Loss or Sensor Resets During Power Events
Likely Cause
Power supply instability, particularly under the load of heating elements.
Diagnostic Steps
Log supply voltage over time and check UPS and redundant power source status.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the power supply, and ensure the UPS and redundant sources are functional and adequately sized for the heater load.
Loss of Weather Data at ATC Display
Likely Cause
Communication dropout on the link between the weather station and the display/ATC system.
Diagnostic Steps
Check communication link status (wired and wireless if redundant) and review link health monitoring logs.
Corrective Action
Repair the faulted communication path and confirm the redundant path (wired + wireless) takes over correctly.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Sensor Icing (Heater / Anti-Icing Failure)
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A failed or underpowered heating element on wind, visibility, or precipitation sensors during freezing conditions.
Effect
No or erroneous wind data in cold weather as ice physically blocks or damages the sensing element.
Detection Method
Heater/anti-icing function check ahead of and during the cold-weather season, verified functionally rather than just visually.
Visibility Sensor Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Dust, insects, salt spray, or precipitation residue accumulating on the visibility sensor's optical windows.
Effect
Inaccurate RVR/visibility readings that don't reflect true atmospheric conditions, potentially affecting low-visibility operations.
Detection Method
Scheduled visibility sensor lens cleaning combined with a sensor-by-sensor calibration check.
Radiation Shield Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure or physical damage to the temperature/humidity sensor's radiation shield reducing its ability to block solar heating.
Effect
Temperature and humidity readings show a solar heating error, particularly noticeable on clear, calm days.
Detection Method
Radiation shield inspection for damage or discolouration, and a redundant-sensor consistency check against a shielded reference.
Power Supply Instability
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing UPS batteries or a marginal power feed at the sensor suite or data logger.
Effect
Data loss or sensor resets during voltage events, creating gaps in the observation record right when conditions may be changing.
Detection Method
Power supply and UPS test under simulated load-shedding, and a review of sensor reset logs against known voltage events.
Communication Dropout
High Severity
Root Cause
Network cable damage, a failed data logger interface, or a fault in the dissemination path to ATC displays, ATIS, or A-SMGCS.
Effect
Loss of weather data at the ATC display, removing a safety-relevant input from controllers at the point it's most needed.
Detection Method
Communication link test along the full dissemination path, and a dissemination path test as part of scheduled maintenance.
Sensor Calibration Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Normal component ageing in individual sensors within the suite, uncorrected until the next scheduled calibration.
Effect
Gradual disagreement between sensors that should read similarly, surfacing as a redundant-sensor consistency failure.
Detection Method
Sensor-by-sensor calibration check against a traceable reference, and a redundant-sensor consistency check.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Anemometer Icing Silently Produces False-Calm Readings
Reliability

An anemometer with ice building up on its moving parts can freeze in place and report a false calm or steady reading exactly during the winter weather conditions operators most need accurate wind data for. Confirm heated anemometer elements are functioning as part of pre-winter and in-season checks, not just annual calibration.

Sensor Siting Representativeness Can Degrade as the Airfield Changes
Design

A weather station sited to be representative of runway touchdown conditions when installed can become less representative as new buildings, vegetation, or terrain changes develop nearby over the years. Periodically re-assess siting representativeness against current site conditions, not just the original siting study.

RVR and Ceilometer Calibration Drift Is Easy to Miss Without Independent Cross-Checks
Verification

Runway Visual Range and ceilometer readings can drift gradually due to lens contamination or optical component ageing in a way that isn't obvious from the sensor's own self-reported values. Cross-check readings against an independent reference or a second co-located sensor periodically, rather than trusting a single sensor's long-term trend in isolation.

Multi-Sensor Consistency Matters as Much as Individual Sensor Accuracy
Design

Where multiple weather sensors are installed across the airfield, each can individually pass calibration while disagreeing with each other by an amount that matters operationally, particularly for RVR at different runway ends. Check inter-sensor consistency as a specific verification step, not just each sensor's calibration in isolation.

Data Latency to ATC and ATIS Should Be Verified End-to-End
Operational

A sensor reading correctly at the mast doesn't guarantee that value reaches the ATC display or ATIS broadcast promptly; verify the full data path latency periodically, since a lag introduced anywhere in the processing or transmission chain can leave controllers or pilots working from stale weather data.

Lightning Protection Deserves Its Own Inspection Cycle
Electrical

A weather station mast is often the tallest nearby structure and a natural lightning target; grounding and surge protection condition should be inspected on its own schedule rather than folded into general electrical checks, since a degraded ground path here risks the whole sensor suite in a single strike.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the weather station: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an airfield weather station?

An airfield weather station, also known as an AWOS (Automated Weather Observing System) or METAR station, is an integrated system of meteorological sensors and data processing equipment that measures, records and disseminates weather conditions at an airfield.

Q2What sensors does an airfield weather station include?

A typical system includes wind sensors (ultrasonic or cup-and-vane anemometers), temperature and humidity sensors in a radiation shield, barometric pressure transducers giving QNH and QFE, visibility (RVR) sensors such as forward scatter meters or transmissometers, a ceilometer for cloud height, and present weather and precipitation sensors.

Q3Why are weather stations important at airports?

They provide environmental data to ATC, pilots, airport operations and maintenance teams, enabling low-visibility procedures (CAT II/III) and runway selection. Data is sent to ATC, flight planning systems and displays, and is often broadcast through ATIS.

Q4Where are weather stations deployed?

At runway thresholds (primary stations providing RVR, wind and visibility), on or near ATC towers for controller displays, mid-runway for crosswind and mid-field conditions, and in apron areas.

Q5What measurement ranges and accuracies are typical?

Wind speed 0-75 m/s (±0.2 m/s), wind direction 0-360° (±2°), temperature -50°C to +60°C (±0.2°C), pressure 600-1,100 hPa (±0.3 hPa), RVR 10 m to 10,000 m, and ceiling height 0 to 15,000 ft.

Q6How is a weather station powered?

By 24 V DC (18-32 V) or 120/230 V AC with backup battery or solar to keep it running during mains interruptions. Consumption is 5 W to 20 W without heating and 20 W to 100 W with heating. It is a standalone station independent of the series lighting circuit, with surge protection on sensor and communication lines.

Q7Which standards apply to weather stations?

The main references are ICAO Annex 3 (meteorological service for international air navigation), WMO Guide No. 8 (instruments and methods of observation) and FAA AC 150/5220-16 (AWOS). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a weather station be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for visibility sensor lens cleaning, semi-annual for communication link tests, and annual for sensor calibration, radiation shield inspection and a heater and anti-icing check before winter. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with weather stations?

Common problems are sensor icing, visibility sensor contamination, radiation shield degradation, power supply instability, communication dropout and sensor calibration drift. Symptoms include missing wind data in cold weather, inaccurate RVR readings and loss of data at the ATC display.
