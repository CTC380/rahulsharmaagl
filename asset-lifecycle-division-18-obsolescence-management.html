Obsolescence Management

The forward-looking technology, supportability and lifecycle-risk management layer of the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide. Division 18 identifies assets, components, software and technologies that are approaching or have reached a condition where continued support, spare availability, maintainability, reliability or future operation may be compromised — so that obsolescence is managed before it becomes an operational failure.

Initial Issue

The question this division answers — Which of our assets, components, software and technologies are approaching a point where support, spares, maintainability or reliability can no longer be guaranteed — and what should we do about it before that gap becomes an operational failure?

OBS-01 Obsolescence Identification
Purpose

The systematic process of identifying assets or components that may become difficult to support — before that difficulty turns into an unplanned failure.

Identify
  • Discontinued products
  • Aging equipment
  • Unsupported technology
  • End-of-life components
  • End-of-support software
  • Unavailable spare parts
  • Legacy control systems
  • Proprietary components
  • Manufacturer withdrawal
  • Declining supplier support
Information Sources
  • Manufacturer notifications
  • Supplier communication
  • Product lifecycle information
  • Procurement records
  • Spare-parts availability
  • Failure history
  • Maintenance records
  • Asset-register data
OBS-02 Product Discontinuation
Purpose

Product discontinuation occurs when a manufacturer stops producing a particular model or product.

Record
  • Manufacturer
  • Product
  • Model
  • Part number
  • Discontinuation date
  • Last order date, if available
  • Last support date, if available
  • Replacement product
  • Compatibility
  • Remaining installed population
Lifecycle Response
DiscontinuedAssess Installed AssetsAssess Spare RequirementIdentify AlternativeLast-Time Buy / Migration / Replacement
OBS-03 Manufacturer Discontinuation
Purpose

Manufacturer discontinuation is broader than individual product discontinuation. A manufacturer may exit a product line, stop supporting a technology, close a service facility, withdraw from a market, stop providing technical support or software updates, or reduce spare availability.

Strategic Concern

Even if the equipment itself is technically functional, loss of manufacturer support can increase lifecycle risk on its own — independent of the asset's physical condition.

OBS-04 Spare-Part Obsolescence
Purpose

Particularly important for AGL because an operational asset can become difficult to maintain even when the main equipment remains functional.

Identify
  • Discontinued parts
  • Long-lead parts
  • Proprietary parts
  • No-alternative components
  • Electronic components
  • Legacy control components
  • Special connectors
  • Specialized modules
Assess
Installed PopulationFailure RateCurrent StockSupplier AvailabilityLead TimeFuture Spare Requirement
OBS-05 Technology Obsolescence
Purpose

Occurs when an existing technology becomes outdated relative to available and supportable alternatives — legacy lighting technology, legacy CCR control, legacy communication, legacy monitoring, legacy control platforms, older electronic systems.

Assess
  • Performance
  • Reliability
  • Energy efficiency
  • Supportability
  • Spare availability
  • Integration capability
  • Future compatibility
Important Distinction

Technology should not be declared obsolete solely because a newer technology exists. The assessment should consider whether the existing technology remains fit for purpose and supportable.

OBS-06 Software Obsolescence
Purpose

Software can become obsolete independently of the physical equipment it runs on.

Assess
  • Software version
  • Operating platform
  • Licensing
  • Vendor support
  • Security/update support
  • Hardware compatibility
  • Database compatibility
  • Backup/restore capability
  • Availability of skilled support
Potential Consequence

A physically healthy system may become difficult to operate or maintain because its software platform is no longer supported.

OBS-07 Control-System Obsolescence
Purpose

Control systems require dedicated obsolescence management because they can have long operational lives while their electronics and software evolve rapidly.

Assess
  • PLC/controller
  • I/O modules
  • HMI
  • Communication modules
  • Power supplies
  • Software
  • Firmware
  • Programming tools
  • Network interfaces
  • Spare modules
  • OEM support
Strategic Options
ContinueStrategic Spare StockPartial ModernizationTechnology MigrationComplete Control-System Replacement
OBS-08 Legacy CCR Management
Purpose

Legacy CCRs should be managed as a specific asset population rather than assessed only at the point of individual failure.

Assess
  • CCR age
  • Manufacturer
  • Model
  • Control technology
  • Component availability
  • Output performance
  • Failure history
  • Spare availability
  • Manufacturer support
  • Interface compatibility
  • Monitoring capability
Management Options
  • Continue with monitoring
  • Procure strategic spares
  • Component modernization
  • Control upgrade
  • CCR refurbishment
  • CCR replacement
OBS-09 Legacy Fixture Management
Purpose

Legacy AGL fixtures may present obsolescence risks involving light source, LED modules, drivers, optical components, housing components, connectors, replacement parts and manufacturer support.

Assess
  • Installed quantity
  • Failure rate
  • Remaining useful life
  • Spare availability
  • Photometric performance
  • Technology availability
  • Retrofit possibility
  • Replacement alternatives
Portfolio View

A large installed population of a discontinued fixture should be treated as a portfolio-level obsolescence risk, not merely individual equipment failures.

Isolation transformers can remain physically serviceable for long periods, but their replacement availability and compatibility should still be monitored.

  • Supplier availability

OBS-11 Legacy Cable Management
Purpose

Cable systems generally present a different form of obsolescence risk compared with electronic equipment.

Assess
  • Cable type
  • Manufacturer
  • Availability
  • Compatible replacement
  • Jointing components
  • Termination components
  • Connector compatibility
  • Age
  • Failure history
  • Test results
  • Installed configuration
Important Distinction

For cables, physical deterioration and supportability should be assessed separately. A cable may be technically obsolete, physically degraded, supportable, or non-supportable — these are different lifecycle conditions.

OBS-13 Last-Time-Buy Strategy
Purpose

A last-time buy may be appropriate when a manufacturer announces product discontinuation but replacement migration cannot immediately be implemented.

Determine
  • Installed population
  • Annual consumption
  • Failure rate
  • Expected remaining life
  • Current stock
  • Supplier lead time
  • Discontinuation date
  • Storage life
  • Required safety stock
Decision

Required quantity vs. available quantity vs. expected future consumption. The last-time buy should be based on a documented forecast rather than simply purchasing the maximum quantity available.

OBS-14 Alternative-Product Evaluation
Purpose

When an existing product becomes obsolete, an alternative should be technically evaluated before adoption.

Assess
  • Functional compatibility
  • Electrical compatibility
  • Mechanical compatibility
  • Optical compatibility
  • Environmental suitability
  • Performance
  • Reliability
  • Interchangeability
  • Maintenance requirements
  • Spare availability
  • Manufacturer support
  • Lifecycle cost
Important

Form-factor similarity does not automatically mean technical interchangeability.

Most Important Design Principle

Obsolescence should be managed before it becomes an operational failure. Division 18 should remain forward-looking — it identifies technology, equipment, component and support risk while there is still time to plan a response, rather than reacting once a failure has already occurred and no spare or alternative exists.

Division 18 connects across the lifecycle: Division 05 — Procurement selects supportable products, Division 13 — Spare Parts monitors spare availability, Division 14 — Warranty & Supplier monitors manufacturer support, Division 16 — Refurbishment assesses life-extension options, and Division 17 — Technology Upgrade evaluates modern alternatives — all of which feed into Division 18's risk register and mitigation strategy.

Monitor → Identify → Confirm Manufacturer/Product Status
Assess Population, Criticality, Spares & Failure Risk
Record in Obsolescence Register — Select Mitigation
Budget & Plan → Implement → Verify → Close Obsolescence Risk

Mitigation options range from simple monitoring through strategic spares, last-time buy, alternative product, refurbishment or retrofit, up to full technology migration or replacement — selected by obsolescence status and asset criticality, not by default to the most expensive option.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Obsolescence Management in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 18 — Obsolescence Management in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 18 is the forward-looking technology, supportability and lifecycle-risk management layer. It covers 17 topics on obsolescence identification, discontinuation, legacy CCR/fixture/transformer/cable management, last-time-buy strategy, technology migration and the obsolescence register.

Q2What is obsolescence identification?

It is the systematic process of identifying assets or components that may become difficult to support before that difficulty turns into an unplanned failure. It looks at discontinued products, aging equipment and unsupported technology.

Q3What types of obsolescence does Division 18 cover?

It covers product discontinuation, manufacturer discontinuation, spare-part obsolescence, technology obsolescence, software obsolescence and control-system obsolescence. Software can become obsolete independently of the physical equipment it runs on.

Q4Why is spare-part obsolescence especially important for AGL?

An operational asset can become difficult to maintain even when the main equipment remains functional. Discontinued, long-lead, proprietary or no-alternative parts are the main concern.

Q5How are legacy CCRs, fixtures, transformers and cables managed?

Legacy CCRs should be managed as a specific asset population rather than assessed only when an individual unit fails. Fixtures, transformers and cables have their own topics, and cable systems generally present a different form of obsolescence risk than electronic equipment.

Q6How is obsolescence risk assessed?

Every identified obsolete or potentially obsolete asset should be assessed for lifecycle risk, considering criticality, failure probability and consequence, spare availability, lead time and manufacturer support.

Q7What is a last-time-buy strategy?

A last-time buy may be appropriate when a manufacturer announces discontinuation but migration to a replacement cannot immediately be implemented. It is sized using installed population, annual consumption, failure rate and expected remaining life.

Q8How does obsolescence feed replacement planning and technology migration?

Obsolescence should feed planned replacement rather than emergency replacement, and alternative products should be technically evaluated before adoption. Technology migration is the controlled transition from legacy technology to a supported platform, and the Obsolescence Register is the central management record for the division.
