AEI-681 5-Why Analysis for AGL Failure

Purpose — A structured, iterative questioning technique that traces a failure from its immediate symptom back through successive causes to a systemic root cause, by repeatedly asking "why" against each answer until further questioning stops yielding new, actionable information.

Applicable Scope

Applicable to single, relatively well-bounded AGL faults (a specific fixture, transformer, or connector failure) where the causal chain is expected to be linear rather than branching; less suited on its own to complex, multi-factor system-level failures.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighStopping too early — accepting the first plausible answer at "why #2 or #3" instead of continuing until a true systemic cause (process, design, or training) is reached
HighFollowing a single linear chain when the real causal structure branches, so a genuine contributing factor on a parallel path is never asked about
MediumConducting the analysis solo from a desk rather than with the people who did the work and can supply the actual facts at each step
MediumTreating "human error" or "operator forgot" as a terminal answer instead of continuing to ask why the error was possible or likely
LowNo documentation of the answer at each "why," making the analysis unreviewable and its logic unverifiable later
LowUsing 5-Why on a genuinely complex, multi-cause system failure where a fishbone or fault tree would represent the problem more accurately
Required Tools
5-Why worksheet or whiteboard templateThe specific work order / fault record being analysedAccess to the technician(s) or data involved at each causal stepFishbone diagram as a cross-check for branching causesFacilitator experienced in root-cause interviewing technique
Safety Precautions
  • Conduct any interview involved in the analysis in a non-punitive, just-culture manner.
  • Apply the standard LOTO/access precautions for any physical verification performed as part of the analysis.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1State the problem precisely and factually — the specific fault, location, and date — rather than a vague or generalised description.
  2. Step 2Ask "why did this happen?" and record the direct, evidence-based answer, not a guess or assumption.
  3. Step 3For each answer, ask "why" again, continuing to peel back the causal layer until the answer points to a process, design, training, or systemic condition rather than a further physical symptom.
  4. Step 4Typically continue for five iterations as a guideline, but stop earlier if a clear systemic cause is reached, or continue further if the chain has not yet reached one.
  5. Step 5Cross-check the final answer against the evidence gathered at each step to confirm the logic holds end-to-end, not just that five "whys" were asked.
  6. Step 6Convert the confirmed root cause into a specific, assignable corrective action rather than leaving it as a diagnostic statement alone.
Key Outputs
A documented chain of question-and-answer pairs from symptom to root cause
Evidence supporting each answer in the chain, not assumption alone
The final systemic (process/design/training) cause identified
A specific, assignable corrective action tied to the root cause
Confirmation the chain did not stop prematurely at a symptom
Validation Criteria

Read the completed chain back from the final root cause forward to the original symptom and confirm each "why" logically and factually follows from the one before it, with no unsupported leaps, before accepting the analysis as complete.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Where the chain reveals a procedural gap, route the finding into the relevant SOP/checklist update.
  • Where the chain reveals a training gap, route the finding into the training programme.
  • Where the chain reveals the failure is more complex or branching than a single line supports, escalate to a fishbone or fault tree analysis instead.
Programme Integration
  • Use 5-Why as the default first-pass technique for single, well-bounded faults, reserving fishbone/FTA for more complex ones.
  • Retain completed 5-Why worksheets in the CMMS/RCA record for future reference and pattern-checking.
  • Train investigators to recognise when a linear 5-Why is inadequate and a branching technique is needed instead.
  • Periodically audit completed 5-Why analyses for premature stopping at a symptom rather than a systemic cause.
Related References

AEI-682 Fishbone Analysis for AGL Failure

Purpose — A structured cause-and-effect diagramming technique (Ishikawa diagram) that maps every plausible contributing cause of a failure across standard categories — typically Equipment, Process, People, Materials, Environment, and Measurement — to ensure a broad, systematic search rather than following only the first hypothesis that comes to mind.

Applicable Scope

Applicable to failures with multiple, potentially interacting contributing factors — repeated component failures, circuit-level degradation, or any fault where the cause is not obviously a single linear chain.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighPopulating only the categories the investigator is personally familiar with (e.g., only Equipment causes) and neglecting People, Process, or Environment branches
HighListing symptoms or effects on the bone rather than genuine contributing causes, conflating the diagram with a simple fault list
MediumStopping after the first pass without a facilitated brainstorm involving people from different roles (technician, engineer, planner) who would surface different branches
MediumFailing to prioritise or weight the identified causes afterward, leaving the diagram as an unranked list rather than a basis for action
LowUsing generic, non-specific category labels that do not map well to AGL-specific causal categories
LowTreating the completed diagram as the root cause itself, rather than as an input to further verification of the leading candidate causes
Required Tools
Fishbone/Ishikawa diagram template (6M or similar category structure)Whiteboard or diagramming softwareFacilitated brainstorming session with cross-functional participantsThe specific fault/failure history data being analysedCategory definitions adapted to AGL causes (Equipment, Process, People, Materials, Environment, Measurement)
Safety Precautions
  • Conduct the brainstorming session in a non-punitive environment so People-category causes are surfaced honestly.
  • Apply standard LOTO/access precautions for any physical verification of a candidate cause identified on the diagram.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1State the effect (the specific failure) clearly at the head of the diagram, as precisely as the 5-Why problem statement.
  2. Step 2Establish the major cause categories on the main bones — typically Equipment, Process, People, Materials, Environment, and Measurement for AGL applications.
  3. Step 3Facilitate a structured brainstorm with cross-functional participants, populating each category with candidate contributing causes, not effects.
  4. Step 4Use sub-branches to break down a broad candidate cause into more specific, testable factors (e.g., under Equipment: "transformer" → "wattage mismatch").
  5. Step 5Review the completed diagram as a group to identify the most plausible candidate causes for further investigation, based on available evidence rather than opinion alone.
  6. Step 6Carry the leading candidates forward into targeted testing/verification (physical inspection, data review) rather than concluding the RCA from the diagram alone.
Key Outputs
Coverage across all relevant cause categories, not just one
Causes (not effects/symptoms) populated on each bone
Cross-functional participation in the brainstorm
Leading candidate causes identified for further verification
Sub-branch specificity sufficient to support targeted testing
Validation Criteria

Confirm the diagram was populated with genuine causes across multiple categories by a cross-functional group, and that the leading candidates identified were subsequently tested against physical or data evidence before the RCA was closed.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Carry the leading candidate cause(s) forward into physical inspection or data verification to confirm or rule out each before selecting a corrective action.
  • Re-run the brainstorm with broader participation if the initial diagram shows clear category imbalance (e.g., nothing under People or Environment).
Programme Integration
  • Use fishbone analysis as the standard technique for any failure with more than one plausible contributing factor.
  • Retain completed fishbone diagrams in the CMMS/RCA record for future reference and cross-division pattern-checking.
  • Train facilitators in structured brainstorming technique to avoid one-sided category population.
  • Periodically review closed fishbone RCAs for category coverage as a quality check on the technique's application.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure)

Standards: ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 10.2

AEI-683 Fault Tree Analysis for CCR Failure

Purpose — A deductive, top-down logic-diagram technique that starts from a defined CCR-level failure (the "top event") and systematically breaks it down through AND/OR logic gates into the combinations of lower-level equipment, protection, and human-factor events that could cause it.

Applicable Scope

Constant Current Regulators (transformer-type and TSCR/inverter-type), their protection relays, power supply, and the human/procedural events that interact with them (e.g., an unresolved trip being reset without investigation).

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighDefining the top event too broadly (e.g., "CCR fails") rather than a specific, well-bounded failure mode (e.g., "CCR trips on overcurrent and will not reset"), making the tree unmanageably large
HighIncorrect gate logic — using an OR gate where an AND relationship actually exists between contributing events, overstating the failure probability
MediumStopping the tree decomposition at an intermediate event without breaking it down to a basic, independently verifiable event
MediumOmitting human-factor or procedural basic events (e.g., "trip reset without investigation") and modelling only hardware failure modes
LowBuilding the tree without input from the technicians who actually operate and maintain the CCR, missing practical failure paths
LowTreating the completed tree as static, rather than updating it as new failure modes are discovered through field experience
Required Tools
Fault tree diagramming software or template with standard AND/OR gate symbolsCCR single-line diagram and protection coordination studyCCR trip/failure history for calibrating basic event likelihoodCross-functional review team (electrical engineer, technician, protection specialist)Basic event probability/frequency data where available from Division 23/27 records
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the CCR before any physical verification of a basic event identified in the tree.
  • Apply standard safety precautions relevant to the specific basic event being field-verified.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Define the top event precisely — a specific, well-bounded CCR failure mode rather than a generic "CCR fails" statement.
  2. Step 2Identify the immediate, necessary and/or sufficient contributing events directly beneath the top event, connected by the correct AND/OR gate logic.
  3. Step 3Continue decomposing each intermediate event downward until reaching basic events — independently verifiable hardware, environmental, or human-factor causes.
  4. Step 4Include human-factor and procedural basic events (e.g., incorrect relay setting, trip reset without investigation) alongside hardware events, not hardware alone.
  5. Step 5Where data is available, estimate the relative likelihood of each basic event using CCR failure history to identify the dominant cut sets (combinations most likely to produce the top event).
  6. Step 6Review the completed tree with a cross-functional team to validate the gate logic and confirm no plausible basic event has been omitted.
Key Outputs
Top event definition — specific and well-bounded
Gate logic (AND/OR) correctness at each level
Decomposition depth — basic events, not stopped at intermediate events
Human-factor/procedural basic events included
Dominant cut sets identified from available failure data
Validation Criteria

Cross-check the completed tree against actual CCR trip/failure history to confirm the identified dominant cut sets are consistent with observed failure patterns, and have the tree reviewed and signed off by a cross-functional team before use.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Where a dominant cut set points to a specific hardware weakness, route the finding into corrective replacement/upgrade.
  • Where a dominant cut set points to a procedural gap (e.g., trip reset without investigation), route the finding into SOP revision.
  • Use the tree to prioritise which basic events warrant the most rigorous field verification during an active investigation.
Programme Integration
  • Maintain the CCR fault tree as a living document, updated as new failure modes are discovered through Division 23 RCA findings.
  • Use the tree during design review for new CCR installations or protection setting changes to anticipate failure paths proactively.
  • Train protection and maintenance engineers in fault tree construction and gate logic to avoid the common pitfalls above.
  • Cross-reference the tree's basic events against the PM checklist to ensure each is actually inspected/tested periodically.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-CCR-04 (CCR LOTO Procedure)

Standards: IEC 61025 (fault tree analysis), ICAO Annex 14 Vol I

AEI-684 Fault Tree Analysis for Series Circuit Failure

Purpose — A top-down logic-diagram technique applied to a series-circuit-level failure (e.g., "multiple lights dark on Circuit X"), decomposing the top event through AND/OR gates into the cable, transformer, connector, CCR, and earthing basic events that could combine to produce it.

Applicable Scope

Complete series circuits — CCR output, primary series cable loop, all transformers and fixtures on the loop, and the earthing system supporting it.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighModelling only a single fault path (e.g., cable break) when a series circuit's topology often allows multiple distinct basic events to produce the same top-level symptom (dark section)
HighIncorrect gate logic between a genuine circuit-wide cause (e.g., CCR output failure, which is a single event affecting everything downstream) and a localised cause (e.g., one cable segment), which have fundamentally different tree structures
MediumNot accounting for circuit topology changes (added fixtures, extended loop) in the tree, so it no longer reflects the as-built circuit
MediumTreating "cable fault" as a single basic event rather than decomposing it by segment, obscuring which specific section is implicated
LowBuilding the tree without reference to the as-built circuit layout drawing, missing physical segment boundaries
LowFailing to distinguish a series-circuit-specific topology (one break affects everything downstream) from a parallel or radial topology in the gate logic
Required Tools
As-built circuit design calculation and topology drawingFault tree diagramming software or templateCircuit-level fault history from Division 23 recordsInsulation resistance test history by segmentCross-functional review team familiar with the specific circuit
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the CCR before any physical verification of a basic event on the affected circuit.
  • Coordinate airside access for any field verification through ATC/airfield operations.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Define the top event precisely — the specific symptom (e.g., "Circuit X, Section 4-9 dark, other sections normal") rather than a generic circuit failure statement.
  2. Step 2Distinguish circuit-wide basic events (CCR output failure, upstream cable break) from localised basic events (a specific segment fault) using the correct gate logic for series topology.
  3. Step 3Decompose the cable-fault branch by physical segment, referencing the as-built layout drawing, rather than treating "cable" as a single undifferentiated basic event.
  4. Step 4Include transformer, connector, and earthing basic events at each fixture position within the affected section.
  5. Step 5Where circuit-level fault history exists (Division 23), estimate relative likelihood of each basic event/segment to identify the dominant cut set for the specific symptom observed.
  6. Step 6Validate the tree against the current as-built topology, updating it if fixtures have been added or the loop extended since the tree was last built.
Key Outputs
Top event definition — specific symptom and section
Circuit-wide vs. localised basic event gate logic
Cable-fault decomposition by physical segment
Transformer/connector/earthing basic events included per position
Tree currency against as-built topology
Validation Criteria

Cross-check the dominant cut set identified by the tree against the actual insulation resistance test pattern and fault-location result from the current investigation, confirming the tree correctly predicted where the fault would be found.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Use the dominant cut set to prioritise fault-location effort (which segment or component to test first) during an active investigation.
  • Where the tree reveals a topology not reflected in current records, update the as-built drawing and design calculation.
Programme Integration
  • Maintain circuit fault trees for high-criticality or historically fault-prone circuits as living documents.
  • Update the tree whenever a change-control record shows fixtures added or the loop extended.
  • Use the tree during design review to identify single points of failure (e.g., an upstream basic event that would affect an unacceptably large section) for design mitigation.
  • Cross-reference dominant cut sets against the PM programme to ensure the most likely basic events receive proportionate inspection attention.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-RCL-02 (Series Circuit Fault Location Procedure)

Standards: IEC 61025, ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5340-30

AEI-685 Fault Tree Analysis for Transformer Failure

Purpose — A top-down logic-diagram technique applied to isolation transformer failure, decomposing the top event (e.g., "transformer open/short circuit") through AND/OR gates into the design, material, environmental, and workmanship basic events that could combine to produce it.

Applicable Scope

L-830 series-circuit isolation transformers and their base-can environment, wattage/tap specification, and installation/maintenance history.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighConflating "insulation failure" as a single basic event rather than decomposing it into its distinct sub-causes — moisture, thermal, mechanical, and age-related degradation — which each have different corrective paths
HighTreating wattage/tap mismatch (a specification/workmanship event) and manufacturing defect (a material event) as the same basic event when they require entirely different corrective actions
MediumNot including base-can drainage/moisture events as basic events feeding into the insulation-failure branch
MediumOmitting batch/lot traceability as a basic event, preventing the tree from being used to investigate a suspected manufacturing-batch issue
LowBuilding a generic transformer fault tree not calibrated to the specific transformer type/rating in use on the affected circuit
LowFailing to update the tree with basic event likelihood data as Division 23 RCA findings accumulate over time
Required Tools
Fault tree diagramming software or templateTransformer specification and wattage/tap design referenceTransformer failure history by location and batch (Division 23 records)Base-can moisture/drainage inspection dataCross-functional review team (electrical engineer, technician, procurement/quality)
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before removing any transformer for basic-event verification.
  • Treat a suspect transformer as potentially still hazardous from stored energy or induced voltage until confirmed isolated.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Define the top event precisely — the specific failure mode (e.g., "transformer primary-to-secondary short") rather than a generic "transformer fails" statement.
  2. Step 2Decompose the top event into its major branches — insulation failure, thermal failure, mechanical damage, and manufacturing defect — using appropriate gate logic.
  3. Step 3Further decompose the insulation-failure branch into moisture, thermal, mechanical, and age-related basic events, referencing base-can and loading data.
  4. Step 4Include wattage/tap mismatch and installation workmanship as distinct basic events feeding the thermal-failure branch, separate from a genuine material/manufacturing defect.
  5. Step 5Include batch/lot traceability as a basic event to support investigation of a suspected manufacturing-batch pattern.
  6. Step 6Where Division 23 failure history is available, weight the basic events by observed frequency to identify the dominant cut set for the specific population being analysed.
Key Outputs
Top event definition — specific failure mode
Insulation-failure branch decomposition (moisture/thermal/mechanical/age)
Wattage/tap mismatch modelled as distinct from manufacturing defect
Batch/lot traceability included as a basic event
Basic event likelihood weighting from failure history, where available
Validation Criteria

Cross-check the dominant cut set identified by the tree against the physical teardown findings from the current failed unit, confirming the tree correctly anticipated the observed failure signature.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Use the dominant cut set to prioritise which basic events to verify first during a repeated-failure investigation (see RCA-546).
  • Where the tree points to a batch-level cut set, escalate to supplier quality engagement.
  • Where the tree points to a wattage/tap specification gap, correct the design/verification process.
Programme Integration
  • Maintain the transformer fault tree as a living document, recalibrated as Division 23 RCA findings accumulate.
  • Use the tree during procurement/specification review to anticipate failure paths for new transformer types before large-scale deployment.
  • Cross-reference dominant cut sets against the PM programme (wattage verification, insulation trending) to ensure proportionate coverage.
Related References

AEI-686 Fault Tree Analysis for Cable Failure

Purpose — A top-down logic-diagram technique applied to primary or secondary cable failure, decomposing the top event (e.g., "cable insulation breakdown at a specific segment") through AND/OR gates into the environmental, mechanical, electrical, and workmanship basic events that could combine to produce it.

Applicable Scope

L-824 primary series cable and secondary cable, including duct banks, pull boxes, and field splices along the route.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighTreating the entire cable route as a single basic event rather than decomposing it by segment/joint, which prevents the tree from being used for actual fault-location prioritisation
HighOmitting splice/joint quality as a distinct basic event separate from the cable itself, understating how often the repair, not the original cable, is the true failure point
MediumNot including civil works/mechanical damage as a time-varying basic event whose likelihood should be re-weighted when nearby excavation or resurfacing is known to be occurring
MediumConflating overvoltage/harmonic electrical stress with age-related insulation degradation as a single "insulation failure" event, when they call for different corrective actions
LowBuilding the tree without reference to the as-built route drawing, losing the physical segment structure needed for practical use
LowNot updating basic event likelihood as insulation resistance trend data accumulates over successive PM cycles
Required Tools
As-built duct and cable route drawingFault tree diagramming software or templateInsulation resistance trend data by segment (Division 23/27 records)Civil works/vehicle-loading history for the routeSplice/joint installation and inspection records
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the CCR before any cable work or basic-event verification.
  • Obtain excavation clearance and utility locates before any physical verification requiring pavement or verge opening.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Define the top event precisely — the specific segment or symptom (e.g., "insulation failure, Segment 12-15") rather than a generic cable-failure statement.
  2. Step 2Decompose by physical segment/joint using the as-built route drawing, so each basic event corresponds to a specific, locatable point.
  3. Step 3For each segment, include distinct basic events for moisture ingress, mechanical damage, splice/joint quality, and electrical overstress.
  4. Step 4Weight civil works/vehicle-loading basic events higher for segments currently under or recently subject to nearby works.
  5. Step 5Use accumulated insulation resistance trend data to identify segments already showing degradation, raising their basic event likelihood in the tree.
  6. Step 6Validate the tree against the current investigation's fault-location result to confirm the dominant cut set correctly anticipated the failure location.
Key Outputs
Top event definition — specific segment/symptom
Segment-level decomposition against the route drawing
Splice/joint quality modelled as distinct from the cable itself
Civil works/vehicle-loading basic events time-weighted appropriately
Insulation resistance trend data incorporated into basic event likelihood
Validation Criteria

Cross-check the dominant cut set identified by the tree against the actual TDR/fault-location result from the current investigation, confirming the segment predicted matches the segment found.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Use the dominant cut set to prioritise which segment to test first during an active fault-location investigation.
  • Where the tree points to a splice/joint-quality cut set, escalate to splice technique/training review (see PRV-635).
  • Where civil works basic events dominate, coordinate protective measures with the civil works team.
Programme Integration
  • Maintain segment-level fault trees for chronically fault-prone routes as living documents, updated with each PM cycle's insulation data.
  • Use the tree during route planning for new circuits to identify segments at elevated inherent risk (e.g., planned proximity to future civil works).
  • Cross-reference dominant cut sets against the PM programme to ensure the highest-likelihood segments receive proportionate insulation testing frequency.

AEI-687 Fault Tree Analysis for Fixture Failure

Purpose — A top-down logic-diagram technique applied to complete fixture failure, decomposing the top event (e.g., "fixture fails to illuminate") through AND/OR gates into the transformer, connector, lamp/LED, housing/seal, and physical-damage basic events that could combine to produce it.

Applicable Scope

Complete inset or elevated AGL fixture assemblies — housing, optics, lamp/LED module, transformer, connector, and base can.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighBuilding a generic "fixture fails" tree that does not distinguish between the several independent subsystems (electrical supply, light source, housing integrity) that can each independently cause the same top-level symptom
HighTreating "fixture failure" and "transformer failure" (AEI-685) as separate, unrelated trees rather than recognising the transformer branch as a sub-tree feeding into the fixture-level top event
MediumNot weighting basic events by the specific location's known stress profile (jet-blast zone, chronically wet base can), understating the dominant cut set for that specific fixture population
MediumOmitting physical/mechanical damage (impact, jet blast, snow plough) as a basic event distinct from a purely electrical or material cause
LowBuilding the tree without reference to fixture batch/model-specific failure history, missing a design or manufacturing basic event
LowNot distinguishing photometric/optical degradation (a gradual basic event) from an abrupt "not working" failure mode in the same tree
Required Tools
Fault tree diagramming software or template, incorporating the transformer sub-tree (AEI-685) and connector considerationsFixture-level failure history by position and batchHigh-stress zone mapping (jet blast, snow-plough routes, traffic)Photometric survey trend dataCross-functional review team (electrical, civil, quality)
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before fixture removal for basic-event verification.
  • Coordinate airside access through ATC/airfield operations.
  • Use FOD-safe tools and account for every item before leaving the movement area.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Define the top event precisely — the specific symptom (e.g., "fixture completely dark" vs. "fixture dim/out of photometric tolerance") since these have different dominant branches.
  2. Step 2Structure the tree around the fixture's independent subsystems — electrical supply (linking to the transformer sub-tree), light source, housing/seal integrity, and physical structure.
  3. Step 3Incorporate the transformer fault tree (AEI-685) as a sub-tree feeding into the fixture-level electrical-supply branch, rather than duplicating its logic.
  4. Step 4Include physical/mechanical damage as a distinct basic event branch, weighted by the specific location's known stress profile.
  5. Step 5Include fixture batch/model as a basic event to support investigation of a suspected design or manufacturing pattern.
  6. Step 6Where fixture-level failure history is available, weight basic events by observed frequency for the specific position/batch to identify the dominant cut set.
Key Outputs
Top event definition distinguishing failure symptom types
Subsystem structure with transformer sub-tree properly incorporated
Physical/mechanical damage weighted by location stress profile
Fixture batch/model included as a basic event
Basic event likelihood weighting from position-specific failure history
Validation Criteria

Cross-check the dominant cut set identified by the tree against the physical inspection findings from the current failed fixture, confirming the tree correctly anticipated the observed failure mode.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Use the dominant cut set to prioritise inspection sequence (transformer, connector, lamp/LED, housing) during an active fixture investigation.
  • Where the tree points to a location-driven cut set, consider fixture-type upgrade or physical protection for that zone.
  • Where the tree points to a batch-level cut set, escalate to supplier quality engagement.
Programme Integration
  • Maintain the fixture fault tree as a living document, incorporating updated transformer and LED sub-trees as those are revised.
  • Use the tree during fixture specification review for new installations, particularly in known high-stress zones.
  • Cross-reference dominant cut sets against the PM/photometric survey programme to ensure proportionate inspection coverage.

AEI-688 Fault Tree Analysis for Sign Failure

Purpose — A top-down logic-diagram technique applied to illuminated sign failure, decomposing the top event (e.g., "sign panel dark or internal fault") through AND/OR gates into the light source/driver, housing/seal, ventilation/cooling, and structural basic events that could combine to produce it.

Applicable Scope

L-858 illuminated taxiway/runway guidance signs — housing, door seal, internal light source/driver, panel assembly, and ventilation provision.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighBuilding a generic "sign fails" tree that does not distinguish an internal electrical fault (light source/driver) from an environmental fault (moisture, heat) with entirely different corrective paths
HighOmitting ballast/driver-to-light-source compatibility as a distinct basic event, missing a common specification-mismatch failure path
MediumNot including cooling-fan function as a basic event feeding the thermal-failure branch where fans are fitted
MediumTreating sign structural instability (vibration, impact) as unrelated to internal component failure rather than recognising it as a basic event that fatigues internal connections over time
LowBuilding the tree without reference to sign-specific batch/wiring-harness records, missing a manufacturing-defect basic event

  3. Step 3Plot the trend and visually and statistically assess its shape — stable, linear decline, or accelerating (non-linear) decline.
  4. Step 4Normalise or annotate the trend for known cyclical factors (season, temperature) to distinguish genuine directional decline from expected cyclical variation.
  5. Step 5Compare the trend's trajectory against a defined proactive action threshold, set comfortably ahead of the hard failure threshold.
  6. Step 6Trigger proactive investigation or preventive action when the trend crosses the action threshold, rather than waiting for the hard failure threshold to be reached.
Key Outputs
Full historical series compiled, not single-point readings
Test methodology consistency confirmed across the series
Trend shape assessed (stable/linear/accelerating)
Seasonal/cyclical normalisation applied where relevant
Defined proactive action threshold distinct from hard failure threshold
Validation Criteria

Confirm that recent trend reviews have correctly identified any parameter approaching its proactive action threshold, and that the corresponding preventive action was triggered before the hard failure threshold was reached.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Trigger proactive investigation/preventive action for any parameter confirmed to have crossed its action threshold.
  • Correct test methodology inconsistency identified in the historical series to improve the reliability of future trend analysis.
Programme Integration
  • Standardise test methodology across all technicians and equipment for every trended parameter.
  • Set and document a proactive action threshold, distinct from the hard failure threshold, for every parameter trended in the PM programme.
  • Review trend data on a defined periodic cycle as a standing PM/quality activity, not only reactively.

AEI-697 Failure Pareto Analysis

Purpose — The application of Pareto (80/20) analysis to the classified AGL failure mode inventory — ranking failure categories by frequency or cumulative risk contribution to identify the small number of categories responsible for the large majority of field failures, focusing engineering and preventive resources accordingly.

Applicable Scope

Applicable to the field-wide or division-wide classified failure mode data (from AEI-690), typically compiled from CMMS/FRACAS records over a defined analysis period.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighRanking by raw failure count alone without considering severity, so a high-frequency but low-consequence category dominates the chart at the expense of a lower-frequency but high-severity one that deserves more attention
HighUsing an analysis period too short or too long for the actual failure rate, either producing a noisy chart from too little data or masking a recent emerging pattern by diluting it with old data
MediumNot re-running the Pareto analysis periodically, so resource allocation continues to follow an outdated pattern after a previously dominant category has been successfully addressed
MediumClassifying at too coarse a level (e.g., "electrical failure" as one bar) which obscures which specific sub-category is actually driving the count
LowPresenting the Pareto chart without the underlying data table, making it harder for stakeholders to interrogate the classification behind each bar
LowTreating the "vital few" categories in isolation without also tracking whether the "trivial many" collectively remain trivial or are quietly growing
Required Tools
Classified failure mode data from AEI-690CMMS/FRACAS export for the defined analysis periodSpreadsheet or statistical/charting tool for Pareto chart generationCombined risk data (from AEI-691–694) for a risk-weighted Pareto variantDefined, documented analysis period and re-run cadence
Safety Precautions
  • Not directly safety-critical as a desk/data exercise.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Define the analysis scope and period — field-wide or division-specific, and a time window appropriate to the actual failure rate of the categories involved.
  2. Step 2Compile the classified failure count (or combined risk score) by category from the CMMS/FRACAS data for that scope and period.
  3. Step 3Rank categories in descending order and calculate the cumulative percentage to identify the "vital few" categories responsible for the majority of failures or risk.
  4. Step 4Where severity varies significantly across categories, produce a risk-weighted Pareto (using combined S×O×D or similar) alongside the simple frequency-based chart.
  5. Step 5Present the chart with its underlying data table so the classification behind each bar can be interrogated by stakeholders.
  6. Step 6Re-run the analysis on a defined periodic cycle to confirm whether previously dominant categories have declined and whether a new category is emerging.
Key Outputs
Analysis scope and period explicitly defined
Classified data correctly compiled from the CMMS/FRACAS source
Cumulative percentage/vital-few categories correctly identified
Risk-weighted variant produced where severity varies significantly
Underlying data table available alongside the chart
Validation Criteria

Cross-check the top-ranked category in the Pareto chart against a manual review of a sample of the underlying fault records to confirm the classification and count are accurate before the chart is used to justify resource allocation.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Direct RCA and preventive-action resources toward the "vital few" categories identified, consistent with the risk-based prioritisation approach in AEI-694.
Programme Integration
  • Re-run the Pareto analysis on a defined periodic cycle (e.g., quarterly) as a standing input to the cross-division RCA review (see PRV-640).
  • Track whether previously addressed "vital few" categories have genuinely declined in subsequent Pareto cycles, confirming corrective/preventive action effectiveness (see AEI-699, AEI-700).
  • Watch for a category migrating from the "trivial many" into the "vital few" over successive cycles as an early warning of an emerging pattern.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-PRV-01 (Preventive Maintenance Procedure)

Standards: ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 9.1 (monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation), ISO 55001

AEI-698 Maintenance History Analysis

Purpose — The systematic review of a component's or circuit's complete maintenance record — every PM visit, repair, part replacement, and inspection finding over its full service life — to extract patterns, verify maintenance adequacy, and inform investigation, distinct from looking only at the immediate fault at hand.

Applicable Scope

Applicable to any AGL component, fixture, circuit, or CCR with an accumulated maintenance record in the CMMS, used both during active RCA investigations and as a standalone periodic review activity.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighReviewing only the most recent one or two work orders rather than the full service-life history, missing an earlier finding that is directly relevant to the current investigation
HighTreating the maintenance record as complete and accurate without cross-checking for gaps — missed PM visits, incomplete checklists, or undocumented informal repairs
MediumNot distinguishing between a genuine repeat fault and a series of unrelated, coincidental faults when reviewing the history, leading to a false pattern conclusion
MediumReviewing the history in isolation from the equipment's actual operating conditions (load, environment, traffic) over the same period, missing a contextual explanation for the pattern
LowReviewing history from a single data source (e.g., CMMS only) without cross-referencing paper records, photographs, or informal notes that may hold additional context
LowExtracting the history but not converting the findings into a structured input for further Pareto, trend, or recurrence analysis
Required Tools
Full CMMS/FRACAS work order history export for the itemCross-reference to paper records, photographs, or informal maintenance notesEquipment operating condition data (load, environment) for the same periodMaintenance history review checklist/templateAccess to prior RCA findings referencing the same item, where they exist
Safety Precautions
  • Not directly safety-critical as a desk/record-review exercise.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1Compile the complete maintenance history for the item across its full service life, not only the most recent entries.
  2. Step 2Cross-check the CMMS record against any available paper records, photographs, or informal notes for completeness and consistency.
  3. Step 3Identify gaps in the record — missed PM visits, incomplete checklists — and note them as a limitation on the analysis rather than assuming full coverage.
  4. Step 4Distinguish genuine repeat findings (same fault type/location recurring) from a series of unrelated events across the history.
  5. Step 5Cross-reference the maintenance timeline against known operating condition changes (traffic increase, nearby civil works, seasonal patterns) for contextual explanation.
  6. Step 6Feed the structured findings into the relevant Pareto (AEI-697), trend (AEI-696), or recurrence (AEI-695) analysis, or directly into an active RCA investigation.
Key Outputs
Full service-life history compiled, not recent entries only
Cross-referenced against paper/photographic records where available
Record gaps identified and noted as a limitation
Genuine repeat findings distinguished from coincidental events
Operating condition context cross-referenced against the timeline
Validation Criteria

Confirm the compiled history against the CMMS source data for completeness, and confirm any pattern conclusion drawn is supported by genuinely comparable, correctly classified events rather than coincidental unrelated ones.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Route any genuine repeat pattern identified into the appropriate Division 23 RCA topic for full investigation.
  • Address any identified maintenance record gap (missed PM, incomplete checklist) as its own corrective finding.
Programme Integration
  • Make full-history review, not just recent-record review, a standard step in any RCA investigation procedure.
  • Improve CMMS data entry discipline to close the specific gaps identified during history reviews.
  • Retain paper/photographic records in a way that is cross-referenceable with the CMMS for future history analysis.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-QA-01 (Work Order Closure and Verification Procedure)

Standards: ISO 55001 (asset management), ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 7.5 (documented information)

AEI-699 Corrective Action Effectiveness

Purpose — The structured follow-up review conducted after a corrective action has been implemented, to confirm the action actually eliminated or reduced the specific fault it targeted, rather than assuming effectiveness from the work order being closed.

Applicable Scope

Applicable to any corrective action taken from a Division 23 RCA, Division 27 preventive topic, or any other fault-response work order, reviewed against the specific fault/failure mode it was intended to address.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighClosing the review at "action was implemented" rather than "action was verified effective," conflating completion with effectiveness
HighReviewing effectiveness too soon after implementation, before enough time/exposure has passed for the fault to plausibly recur if the action was inadequate
MediumReviewing effectiveness only at the exact location of the original fault, missing whether the same action needs to be (or was) applied to other units with the same underlying cause
MediumNot defining, at the time the corrective action is decided, what specific evidence would constitute proof of effectiveness
LowAttributing an apparent improvement to the corrective action without ruling out a coincidental factor (season change, reduced traffic) that could explain the same result
LowNot tracking effectiveness review as a formal, closeable step in the CMMS, so it is informally skipped under workload pressure
Required Tools
The original RCA/corrective action record with defined success criteriaCMMS fault history for the specific item/location post-implementationDefined review interval appropriate to the fault type's expected recurrence timescaleComparable baseline data (pre-action fault rate) for before/after comparisonEffectiveness review checklist/template
Safety Precautions
  • Not directly safety-critical as a desk/data review, though any physical re-inspection performed as part of the review should follow standard LOTO and access precautions.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1At the time the corrective action is decided, explicitly define what evidence (e.g., "zero recurrence over 12 months" or "insulation resistance trend stabilises") would constitute proof of effectiveness.
  2. Step 2Allow an appropriate review interval to pass — long enough for the fault to plausibly recur if the action was inadequate, based on the fault type's expected timescale.
  3. Step 3Pull the post-implementation fault history for the specific item/location and compare it against the defined success criteria and the pre-action baseline.
  4. Step 4Consider whether any coincidental factor (season, traffic change) could explain an apparent improvement independent of the corrective action itself.
  5. Step 5Where the action was intended to apply beyond the single original location (e.g., a batch-wide replacement), confirm it was actually implemented across the full intended population.
  6. Step 6Formally close the effectiveness review as its own tracked step in the CMMS, distinct from the original work order closure.
Key Outputs
Success criteria defined at the time the action was decided, not retrospectively
Review interval appropriate to the fault type's recurrence timescale
Post-implementation data compared against pre-action baseline
Coincidental explanatory factors considered and ruled out
Action population coverage confirmed where broader than a single location
Validation Criteria

Confirm the effectiveness review was completed as a distinct, documented step with a clear conclusion (effective / not effective / inconclusive, pending further time) against the originally defined success criteria.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Where the action is found not effective, re-open the original RCA for further investigation rather than assuming the case is closed.
  • Where the action is found only partially effective, extend or supplement the corrective action rather than accepting a partial improvement as success.
Programme Integration
  • Make effectiveness review a mandatory, tracked step in the CMMS for every corrective action arising from a Division 23 RCA.
  • Define success criteria and review interval as a standard part of every corrective action decision, not an afterthought.
  • Feed effectiveness review outcomes into the periodic cross-division RCA review (see PRV-640) to identify any systemic pattern of ineffective actions.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-QA-01 (Work Order Closure and Verification Procedure)

Standards: ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 10.2 (corrective action effectiveness review is an explicit requirement)

AEI-700 Preventive Action Effectiveness

Purpose — The structured follow-up review conducted after a preventive action (Division 27) has been implemented, to confirm it actually reduced the field-wide incidence of the failure mode it targeted, distinct from corrective action effectiveness (AEI-699) which reviews a single fault response rather than a proactive, population-wide measure.

Applicable Scope

Applicable to any preventive action from Division 27 or any other proactive programme measure (PM checklist change, training update, design standard revision), reviewed against the field-wide failure mode frequency it was intended to reduce.

Common Pitfalls / Misapplication
HighReviewing only whether the preventive action was implemented (e.g., "checklist was updated") rather than whether field-wide failure frequency for the targeted mode actually declined
HighReviewing too soon relative to the PM cycle length, before the new checklist/procedure has actually been applied across a representative sample of the population
MediumNot maintaining a clean pre-action baseline frequency for comparison, making it difficult to quantify the actual improvement
MediumAttributing an observed decline to the preventive action without considering a coincidental factor (fewer new installations, milder weather season) that could explain part of the change
LowReviewing field-wide frequency without also checking whether the preventive action was actually adopted consistently across all relevant teams/locations
LowNot distinguishing a genuine reduction in occurrence from a reduction in reported occurrence, where reporting/detection practices may have also changed
Required Tools
The Division 27 preventive topic or programme measure with its defined intent/targetPre-action baseline field-wide frequency data (from AEI-692/AEI-696/AEI-697)Post-action field-wide frequency data over an appropriate review periodAdoption/compliance check across relevant teams/locationsEffectiveness review checklist/template
Safety Precautions
  • Not directly safety-critical as a desk/data review.
Methodology Steps
  1. Step 1At the time the preventive action is implemented, capture or confirm the pre-action baseline field-wide frequency for the targeted failure mode.
  2. Step 2Allow a review period appropriate to the PM cycle length and the failure mode's expected timescale, so the action has genuinely had a chance to take effect across the population.
  3. Step 3Pull the post-action field-wide frequency data (via Pareto/trend analysis, AEI-696/AEI-697) for the same failure mode and compare against the baseline.
  4. Step 4Check adoption/compliance — confirm the preventive measure (checklist item, training, standard) was actually applied consistently across relevant teams and locations, not only in isolated instances.
  5. Step 5Consider whether any coincidental factor could explain part of an observed change, and whether reporting/detection practice itself changed over the same period.
  6. Step 6Formally document the effectiveness conclusion and feed it into the periodic cross-division RCA/preventive review (see PRV-640).
Key Outputs
Pre-action baseline frequency captured and retained
Review period appropriate to PM cycle and failure mode timescale
Post-action frequency compared against baseline using consistent methodology
Adoption/compliance verified across relevant teams/locations
Coincidental and reporting-practice factors considered
Validation Criteria

Confirm the effectiveness conclusion is supported by a genuine before/after comparison using consistent data sources and methodology, with adoption verified across the intended population, before the preventive action is considered validated.

Typical Findings & Actions
  • Where the preventive action is found ineffective, revisit the underlying Division 23 RCA and Division 27 topic for a more fundamental review rather than assuming the measure simply needs more time.
  • Where adoption is found inconsistent, address the compliance gap directly (retraining, process reinforcement) before concluding the measure itself is ineffective.
Programme Integration
  • Make preventive action effectiveness review a standing, periodic item in the cross-division RCA review (see PRV-640), not a one-off check.
  • Maintain clean, consistent baseline and post-action frequency data as a standard output of the Pareto/trend analysis programme (AEI-696, AEI-697).
  • Use validated-effective preventive actions as reference case studies when designing future preventive measures for comparable failure modes.
Related References

SOP: SOP-PRV-01 (Preventive Maintenance Procedure), SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-QA-01 (Work Order Closure and Verification Procedure)

Standards: ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 9.1/10.2, ISO 55001 (asset management)

Quick answers about Division 30 — Advanced RCA / Engineering Investigation in the AGL Troubleshooting Atlas.

Q1What does Division 30 of the AGL Troubleshooting Atlas cover?

Division 30 is the Advanced RCA / Engineering Investigation reference. It publishes 20 diagnostic topics, AEI-681 to AEI-700, in a single subsection.

Q2What information does each topic include?

Every topic follows the same diagnostic record: the symptom, equipment/system, possible causes ranked by likelihood (high, medium or low), required tools, safety precautions, diagnostic procedure, test points, root cause verification, corrective action, preventive action and related references (SOPs, standards, diagrams and case studies).

Q3Which topics are in Division 30?

Q4How does a diagnostic procedure begin in Division 30?

Each procedure starts with observation before any intervention. For example, AEI-681 (5-Why Analysis for AGL Failure) begins: State the problem precisely and factually — the specific fault, location, and date — rather than a vague or generalised description.

Q5What safety precautions apply in Division 30?

Each topic lists its own precautions. For example, AEI-681 (5-Why Analysis for AGL Failure) starts with: Conduct any interview involved in the analysis in a non-punitive, just-culture manner.
