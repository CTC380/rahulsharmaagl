What is lifecycle engineering in airfield lighting?

Engineering an asset for its whole life, not just its purchase. Design, installation, upkeep and renewal are decided together, with failures expected early, steady in the middle and rising at wear-out.

The Fact Engineering concept

Lifecycle engineering means engineering an AGL asset for its whole life, not just for the day it is bought. Design, product choice, installation, commissioning, operation, maintenance and renewal are treated as one chain of decisions, so that the lighting gives the required service at an acceptable total cost and risk. The Asset Lifecycle entry shows the stages and the records; this entry covers the engineering choices that shape them.

1Design life is a decision

Before choosing equipment, decide the life the asset must give and the conditions it will face: operating hours, temperature, water, de-icing chemicals, traffic loads and the risk of impact. Those choices drive the materials, sealing, mounting and light source. Changing the light source changes the pattern of lamp failures, but it does not change how cables, connectors, transformers and seals age, so each part needs thinking about on its own timescale.

Design life

The period the asset is designed and specified to serve, under stated conditions.

Service life

How long it actually serves, shaped by installation, environment, duty and upkeep.

2Early faults, steady failures, then wear-out

A repairable asset tends to follow the bathtub curve. Early failures come mostly from installation and manufacturing faults, which is why careful jointing, sealing and commissioning tests pay off. In the useful life failures are random and the rate is steady, which is where MTBF and MTTR describe the asset. Then wear-out pushes the rate up, and the aim is to renew on evidence of condition before the failures arrive in numbers. LED light sources tend to lose output gradually rather than fail suddenly, so condition evidence must include measured light output, not only a count of failed lamps.

PhaseWhat drives failuresEngineering response
Early lifePoor joints and seals, installation mistakes, manufacturing defectsQuality control, torque and sealing checks, commissioning tests
Useful lifeRandom events: lightning, water ingress, impact, a lamp failingSurge protection, redundancy, planned maintenance, spares
Wear-outAgeing of insulation, seals, connectors and optics; corrosionTrack condition, such as insulation-resistance trends, and renew before failures climb

3Count the whole-life cost

The purchase price is only one part of the cost. Whole-life cost adds installation, energy, upkeep, spares, downtime and renewal, so an option that is cheaper to buy but harder to repair, or that wears out sooner, can cost more over the same period. Compare options across the same period and on the same required service, and include the cost of lost availability.

  1. Define the required life. The service needed, the duty and the conditions.
  2. Estimate whole-life cost. Compare options over the same period.
  3. Design for uptime and repair. Fewer weak links, spares, repair access and records.
  4. Commission and prove. Test so early faults are found before handover.
  5. Track condition. Inspection, testing and failure records through the useful life.
  6. Renew and feed back. Replace on evidence, then use the history in the next design.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is lifecycle engineering in airfield lighting?

Engineering an asset for its whole life, not only its purchase: design, procurement, installation, commissioning, operation, maintenance and renewal, so that it gives the required service at an acceptable total cost and risk.

Q2How is it different from the asset lifecycle entry?

The asset lifecycle entry shows the management view: the stages an asset passes through and the records kept. Lifecycle engineering covers the engineering choices that shape each stage, such as design life, reliability, repairability and when to renew.

Q3What is the bathtub curve?

A picture of how the failure rate of a repairable asset changes with time. It is high early on from manufacturing and installation faults, roughly steady through the useful life, and rising again at wear-out. MTBF is meaningful mainly in the steady phase.

Q4What is the difference between design life and service life?

Design life is the period an asset is designed and specified to serve under stated conditions. Service life is how long it actually serves in the field, which depends on installation quality, the environment, how hard it is worked and how well it is maintained.

Q5Why use whole-life cost instead of purchase price?

Purchase price is only one part. Installation, energy, maintenance, spares, downtime and renewal often add up to more over the life, so a cheaper unit that is harder to repair or wears out sooner can cost more overall.

Q6How do MTBF and MTTR fit into lifecycle engineering?

MTBF describes how often failures occur in the steady phase and MTTR how long each takes to put right, so together they set availability. Lifecycle engineering uses them at design time, to choose equipment and spares, and in service, to spot when failures begin to climb.
