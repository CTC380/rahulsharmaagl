Rapid Exit Taxiway Indicator

A set of fixed, unidirectional yellow lights installed on the runway itself that count down the distance remaining to an upcoming rapid exit taxiway. Arranged in a three-two-one sequence 100 metres apart, RETIL gives a landing pilot a clear, low-visibility-proof cue of how much runway is left before the exit, improving runway occupancy time and overall airport efficiency.

Overview

What Is It?

A Rapid Exit Taxiway Indicator Light (RETIL) system is a set of fixed, unidirectional yellow lights installed on the runway itself, on the same side of the centreline as an upcoming rapid exit taxiway, that count down the distance remaining to that exit. RETIL consists of six yellow lights arranged in a three-two-one sequence spaced 100 metres apart, with the single light positioned 100 metres before the start of the exit turn. Rapid exit taxiways are typically angled at 25° to 45° to the runway centreline, allowing aircraft to exit the runway at higher speeds than standard 90° exits. RETIL gives a landing pilot an unambiguous, low-visibility-proof cue of how much runway remains before the exit, improving runway occupancy time and overall airport capacity.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

RETIL exists to give a landing pilot a precise, count-down cue of the distance remaining to a rapid exit, without asking them to judge the position of an angled, hard-to-see taxiway junction visually during rollout. Because the shallow exit angle is geometrically harder to spot in time than a perpendicular one, especially at night or in low-visibility conditions (RETIL is specifically recommended where RVR is below 350 m or traffic density is high), the three-two-one light sequence lets the pilot judge exactly how much runway is left before deciding whether — and at what deceleration rate — to make the turn. This directly shortens the time an aircraft occupies the runway after landing, which is the practical purpose behind the system: reducing runway occupancy time increases the number of landings an airport can safely handle in a given period, making RETIL as much a capacity tool as a safety one.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Rapid exit taxiway indicators are installed on precision approach runways at busy airports where efficient runway clearance is critical. They are used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) with high traffic volumes
  • Major international airports where runway capacity is a constraint
  • Rapid exit taxiway intersections where high-speed exits are provided
  • Runways with multiple rapid exit points for efficient aircraft flow
  • Low visibility operations where clear guidance is essential
Placement

Installation & Location

RETIL fixtures are installed on the runway pavement, not on the taxiway — on the same side of the runway centreline as the associated rapid exit, upstream of the physical divergence point. The six lights are set out in the fixed three-two-one arrangement, spaced 100 metres apart, with the single light 100 metres before the start of the exit turn, so the array reads the same way at every exit regardless of that exit's own curve radius or divergence angle. Cabling runs in pavement ducting back to the runway lighting series circuit that already serves that section of runway (typically the runway centreline or edge circuit), sharing its isolating transformers and CCR rather than needing a dedicated feed. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline survey and the exit's start-of-turn point, since the whole point of the fixed 100 m spacing is that it stays consistent airport-wide rather than varying with the individual exit's geometry.

Function

How Does It Work?

RETIL operates as a fixed-pattern, distance-to-go cue rather than a continuously guiding system:

  • Identification: Six fixed, unidirectional yellow lights on the runway pavement, aligned to be visible to a landing aircraft, mark the approach to a specific rapid exit.
  • Count-down Indication: The lights appear in groups of three, two, and one as the aircraft moves down the runway — 300 m, 200 m, and 100 m before the start of the exit turn respectively — giving the pilot a simple visual count-down.
  • Decision Cue: Seeing the reducing groups lets the pilot judge deceleration and confirm they will reach taxiing speed in time to make the turn onto the rapid exit.
  • Hand-off: Once the aircraft turns off the runway, guidance continues from the taxiway centreline lights and lead-off lights on the exit taxiway itself, which RETIL does not control.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

RETIL is not a stand-alone controlled system — electrically, each of the six lights is simply another branch fixture on whichever runway series circuit already covers that section of runway (normally the runway centreline or edge lighting circuit), stepped in brightness by the same CCR and, where fitted, reported through the same ALCMS loop. There is no separate RETIL controller, no independent intensity schedule, and no sign component: all six lights share the runway circuit's current step, so they brighten and dim together with the rest of that circuit's fixtures under low-visibility procedures. This matters for fault-finding: a RETIL light going dark while the rest of the runway circuit stays lit points to a fixture-level fault (LED array, isolating transformer, or connector) rather than a loop-wide problem, while a full-loop fault takes down RETIL along with every other fixture on that circuit. Downstream of the exit itself, guidance responsibility passes to the taxiway centreline and lead-off lights, which are separate fixtures on the taxiway's own circuit.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each RETIL light is built as a low-profile, inset (flush) fixture that seats into an L-868 base can recessed into the runway pavement, with the top surface load-rated to withstand repeated aircraft wheel loading during landing rollout. The housing is a die-cast aluminium alloy body, anodised or powder-coated to resist de-icing fluid, fuel spillage, and abrasion from tyre contact. A high-strength polycarbonate or borosilicate glass lens covers the LED array, gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating against water and dust ingress. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors, which mate to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer housed in an adjacent base can. Stainless-steel fasteners are used throughout, and the base can assembly includes a drainage provision so water does not pool around the transformer and cable entry points beneath the pavement surface.

Installation Requirements

Base Can Excavation & Grade
Civil

Excavate and compact the sub-grade to design depth before setting the L-868 base can, holding the can top flush with the runway surface so the light sits within tolerance for repeated wheel loading.

Drainage Provision
Civil

Provide a weep hole or drainage path at the base can so water does not pool around the transformer and cable entry beneath the pavement surface.

Duct & Cable Route
Electrical

Lay the circuit duct/cable route to design depth and separation from other services, avoiding sharp bends that could stress the L-823 connector at final termination.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal the primary connector per manufacturer procedure, keeping the splice fully submersible-rated before backfill or reinstatement of the base can.

Isolating Transformer / LED Driver Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer or LED driver rating matches the fixture and the circuit's rated current step before connection.

Base Can Fixing & Sealing
Mechanical

Torque the fixture's fixing bolts to the specified value and confirm the housing gasket seats correctly before the base can is set into the pavement.

Aim & Chainage Positioning
Mechanical

Set each light's unidirectional aim down the runway toward approaching landing traffic, and confirm its chainage (100 m, 200 m, or 300 m before the exit turn) against survey before backfill, since a light in the wrong position gives the pilot a false distance-to-go.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record base can position, cable route, connector seal, and exit-angle alignment as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination

Rapid exit indicators are exposed to rubber deposits from aircraft tyres and de-icing fluids. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and visibility.

Misalignment

RETIL lights can become misaligned due to pavement settlement, tilting the beam away from square with the runway direction. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are essential.

LED Driver Failure

LED drivers in rapid exit indicators can fail due to thermal stress or power surges. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers is essential to maintain system reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED driver or LED array, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity is unbroken through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED driver/array if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop (severed cable, corroded L-823 splice, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger cable runs to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Poor Visibility Despite Fixture Being Lit
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits, de-icing fluid residue, or grime built up on the lens is scattering or blocking light output.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for contamination; run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds to quantify the loss.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush; re-verify intensity with a follow-up photometric reading.
Light Aim Drifting Off the Runway Direction
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement or an impact has shifted the fixture's aim away from being square down the runway toward approaching traffic.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture aim against the runway centreline using a digital level or alignment jig; inspect the base can and surrounding pavement for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Re-align the fixture square to the runway direction and address any base can or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Fixture Flickering or Intermittent
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded primary/secondary crimp connections, or an LED driver cycling under thermal stress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque all cable connections at the fixture base can; check for corrosion on crimps; monitor driver output and temperature once the CCR has warmed up.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded connections with new crimps and sealant, or replace the LED driver if thermal cycling is confirmed under load.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Driver Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling in the base can, a transient power surge on the supply, or ordinary end-of-life component degradation within the driver electronics.
Effect
Indicator goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the remainder of the taxiway lighting loop continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and driver output verification at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single RETIL fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire runway circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at the fixture base can during maintenance.
Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the pavement ducting.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire taxiway series loop, including the exit indicator.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Lens Contamination / Obstruction
Low Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits from tyre contact, de-icing fluid residue, and general grime accumulating on the outer lens surface.
Effect
Reduced or scattered light output that makes the exit cue harder to see despite the fixture being electrically healthy.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity testing and visual inspection of the lens during quarterly cleaning.
Fixture Aim Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Pavement settlement, heave, or impact loading around the base can that shifts the fixture's aim off square with the runway direction.
Effect
Distance-to-go cue becomes less visible or reads inconsistently to an approaching pilot, weakening the count-down effect of the 3-2-1 pattern.
Detection Method
Semi-annual alignment verification with a digital level or alignment jig, and inspection of the surrounding pavement for settlement.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the runway loop, or nuisance tripping on healthy circuits.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Base Can / Foundation Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Poor sub-grade compaction at installation, frost heave, or drainage failure allowing water to pool and erode the surrounding fill.
Effect
Fixture tilts out of aim tolerance, cable connections come under mechanical stress, and water can pool around the base can.
Detection Method
Visual and level inspection of the base can and surrounding pavement during annual maintenance, and a check of drainage paths at each RETIL light's position on the runway.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The inset indicator lights sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling connectors.

High-Speed Exit Traffic
Operational

Aircraft use this exit at up to 50–60 knots; work here requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM where the runway or exit is affected, since the exit's higher speed leaves less margin than a standard 90° taxiway exit.

Work Is On the Runway, Not the Taxiway
Operational

All six RETIL lights sit on the live runway surface, not on the exit taxiway; treat any base-can work here as a runway closure requiring full ATC coordination, not the lighter coordination typical of taxiway-side maintenance.

Fixed 3-2-1 Spacing, Not Exit-Specific
Compliance

RETIL spacing is fixed at 100 m between the three-two-one groups regardless of that exit's own divergence angle or curve radius; after any replacement, confirm each light's position against the runway centreline survey and the exit's start-of-turn point, not a locally judged interval.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken the exit indication.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave any of the six RETIL lights dark, misaligned, or at the wrong chainage after maintenance; verify the 3-2-1 pattern reads correctly before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
One Dark RETIL Light Is a Fixture Fault, Not a Loop Fault
Troubleshooting

Because all six RETIL lights share the runway circuit with every other fixture on that section, a single dark light while the rest of the runway and the other five RETIL lights stay lit almost always points to that fixture's own transformer, connector, or LED array — not the CCR or the loop. Pulling loop test gear before checking the one fixture wastes a shift.

Re-Verify Chainage Against the Runway Survey, Not the Last Light
Alignment

Every RETIL light's position is fixed by the runway centreline survey and that exit's start-of-turn point — 100 m, 200 m, or 300 m upstream — not by the exit's own divergence angle or curve radius. After pavement work or fixture replacement, check chainage and aim against the original survey mark, not against the neighbouring light or a "typical" spacing figure. A light that looks fine sitting next to its group can still be at the wrong distance-to-go if it was set out from the wrong reference.

Match LED Step Curve to the Runway Circuit, Not a Bench Reference
Retrofit

When retrofitting RETIL lights to LED, confirm the brightness-step curve tracks the runway CCR's steps the same way the original lamp did. Because these fixtures share a loop with the runway centreline or edge lights, a mismatched curve shows up as the RETIL group looking a shade off from the runway lights right next to it — easy to miss on a single-fixture bench test.

Confirm the RVR/Density Trigger for RETIL Provision, Not Just the Fixture
Commissioning

RETIL is recommended where RVR is below roughly 350 m or traffic density is high, and it's easy to commission the six lights correctly while overlooking whether the airport's low-visibility procedures actually call up the associated runway circuit's higher steps in time. Verify the operational trigger, not just the photometric output, during commissioning.

Rollout Tyre Contact Shortens the Lens-Cleaning Cycle Here
Maintenance

RETIL lights sit in the runway's landing rollout path, where tyre contact and rubber deposition build up faster than on a lightly-trafficked taxiway fixture. Treat these six lights' cleaning interval as shorter than a typical taxiway circuit's rather than applying one blanket schedule airport-wide.

Log Base-Can Settlement Trends on the Runway
Inspection

Base cans in the runway's landing rollout zone take more repeated wheel-load stress than fixtures on a lightly-used taxiway run. Track level/alignment readings over successive inspections rather than relying on a single pass/fail check — a slow tilt trend catches foundation settlement before it pushes the light out of aim tolerance.
