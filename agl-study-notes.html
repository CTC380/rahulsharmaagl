01

INTRODUCTION TO AIRFIELD GROUND LIGHTING (AGL)

Airfield Ground Lighting, commonly called AGL, is the network of lighting and visual guidance systems used on an aerodrome to help pilots identify, approach, land on, taxi along and operate around the airfield. It becomes especially important during darkness, restricted visibility and instrument operations.

It is not simply a collection of lights. An AGL installation combines lighting equipment + electrical circuits + control systems + installation + testing + maintenance + operational requirements.

Why is AGL required?

Without appropriate visual guidance, runway and taxiway geometry is harder to identify, particularly at night or in reduced visibility. AGL helps pilots identify:

  • Runway edges
  • Runway threshold
  • Runway end
  • Runway centreline
  • Touchdown zone
  • Taxiway routes
  • Holding positions
  • Runway entrances
  • Approach alignment
  • Vertical approach guidance

Main AGL areas

Runway

  • Edge, threshold, end lights
  • Centreline, touchdown zone
  • Runway end identifier lights
  • Rapid exit taxiway indicator lights, where provided

Taxiway

  • Edge and centreline lights
  • Lead-on and lead-off lights
  • Stop bars
  • Runway guard lights

Approach area

  • Approach Lighting Systems
  • PAPI, APAPI
  • Other visual guidance systems

Airfield signs

  • Location and direction
  • Mandatory instructions
  • Runway and taxiway identification

AGL as a complete system

A common beginner mistake is studying every light in isolation. Understand the whole chain:

CCRPrimary CablePrimary ConnectorIsolating TransformerSecondary ConnectorLight Fixture

If one part fails, the visible symptom may appear somewhere else. That is why AGL people need both component knowledge and system knowledge. FAA specifications include dedicated requirements for constant-current regulators, L-823 connectors, isolating transformers and airport lighting cable, showing how these form distinct parts of the system.

Key points for exams
  • AGL = Airfield Ground Lighting
  • It provides visual guidance for aerodrome operations
  • It includes runway, taxiway, approach and other visual guidance applications
  • It is both a lighting system and an electrical infrastructure system
  • Understand the complete system, not only the fixture
02

RUNWAY LIGHTING SYSTEMS

Runway lighting provides visual guidance during take-off and landing. Study each light by connecting Location → Colour → Function → Operational meaning.

1. Runway edge lights

Define the lateral limits of the runway. FAA guidance classifies them by intensity: HIRL (High), MIRL (Medium), LIRL (Low). They are normally white, with yellow replacing white on the last 2,000 ft or half the runway length, whichever is less, on instrument runways. ICAO Annex 14 uses yellow on the last 600 m or one-third of the runway length, whichever is less; check the applicable AIP.

2. Threshold lights

Identify the beginning of the landing portion of the runway. In FAA configurations they are green when viewed from the approach side.

3. Runway end lights

Identify the end of the usable runway. FAA bidirectional fixtures show red when viewed from the runway and green when viewed from the approach side. Red = runway end; Green = threshold.

4. Runway centreline lights

Provide accurate alignment, especially on precision approach runways and in reduced visibility. FAA AIM describes 50 ft spacing. The FAA colour sequence seen from the landing threshold is White → Alternating White/Red → Red:

Distance remainingCentreline colour
More than 3,000 ftWhite
Last 3,000 to 1,000 ftAlternating white/red
Last 1,000 ftRed
Indian AGL exam note. This is an FAA reference, not a universal ICAO/Indian configuration. Indian AIP entries follow the ICAO pattern: white up to 900 m from the runway end, alternating white/red from 900 m to 300 m, and red for the final 300 m (on runways shorter than 1,800 m the alternating section starts at the midpoint), with 30 m or 15 m spacing depending on runway/category. So the colour changes occur at: FAA = 3,000 ft and 1,000 ft remaining; ICAO/Indian AIP = 900 m and 300 m remaining. Always identify which standard, AIP, airport specification or project requirement the question refers to.

5. Touchdown zone lights (TDZL)

Identify the touchdown zone on applicable precision approach runways. FAA describes rows of transverse light bars, symmetrically placed about the centreline, steady-burning white.

6. REIL

Runway End Identifier Lights: two synchronised flashing lights placed laterally near the threshold for rapid, positive identification of the approach end of a runway.

7. RETIL

Rapid Exit Taxiway Indicator Lights give distance-to-go information to the nearest rapid exit taxiway and improve situational awareness during landing and rollout. ICAO-based provisions describe fixed, unidirectional yellow lights in a 3-2-1 pattern. Check the aerodrome AIP for the runways that have RETILs. REIL identifies the runway end; RETIL indicates distance toward a rapid exit.

03

TAXIWAY LIGHTING SYSTEMS

Taxiways guide aircraft between runways, aprons, terminals, maintenance areas and other locations. Colours and configurations communicate location and movement guidance.

Lighting systemColourBasic meaning
Taxiway edgeBlueDefines taxiway edge
Taxiway centrelineGreenTaxiway guidance
Lead-on / lead-offGreen and yellowRunway-environment transition
Stop barRedStopping position
Runway guard lightsFlashing yellowRunway holding position

FAA guidance confirms blue edge, green centreline and alternating green/yellow lead-on and lead-off lighting in applicable configurations, and describes runway guard lights as flashing yellow.

1–2. Edge and centreline

Edge lights outline the taxiway (Blue). Centreline lights guide along the route (Green).

3. Lead-off lights

Guide aircraft exiting a runway onto a taxiway. FAA describes alternating green and yellow, beginning with green, from the runway centreline toward the holding position/critical-area boundary. Leaving runway → Lead-Off.

4. Lead-on lights

Guide aircraft entering the runway from a taxiway, using the same alternating green/yellow pattern as lead-off lights, in bidirectional fixtures where provided. In ICAO practice, lead-on lights are green and work with the stop bar: lit when the stop bar is off. Going toward runway → Lead-On.

5–6. Stop bars and runway guard lights

Stop bars are red and mark a stopping position. Runway guard lights are flashing yellow, in elevated or in-pavement arrangements, and identify a runway holding position. Don't confuse Stop Bar → Red with Runway Guard → Flashing Yellow. A lit stop bar must not be crossed. ATC switches it off when it clears the aircraft or vehicle to proceed; contingency procedures apply only if it cannot be switched off.

7. Runway entrance lights (REL)

Part of the FAA Runway Status Lights system: red in-pavement fixtures along the taxiway centreline near a holding position. When activated they indicate runway traffic or an aircraft on final approach within the activation area. Runway Guard = flashing yellow; REL = red. They are different systems.

Key points for exams
  • Blue = Taxiway Edge · Green = Taxiway Centreline
  • Red = Stop Bar · Flashing Yellow = Runway Guard
  • Green/Yellow = Lead-On / Lead-Off
  • Red REL = Runway Entrance Lights in FAA RWSL
04

APPROACH LIGHTING SYSTEMS & PRECISION APPROACH AIDS

Approach lighting helps pilots transition from instrument conditions to visual references for landing. It is installed in the approach area beyond the threshold. FAA describes ALS as providing information on runway alignment, height perception, roll guidance and horizontal references.

  • Runway alignment
  • Longitudinal perspective
  • Approach path reference
  • Threshold identification
  • Lateral reference
  • Instrument-to-visual transition

Main FAA approach lighting terms

ALSF-1

Approach Lighting System with Sequenced Flashing Lights, Category I configuration; high intensity.

ALSF-2

High-intensity system supporting Category II/III instrument approaches.

SSALR

Simplified Short Approach Lighting System with Runway Alignment Indicator Lights. Medium intensity, associated with Category I approaches; also a mode of ALSF-2/SSALR where applicable.

MALSR

Medium Intensity Approach Lighting System with Runway Alignment Indicator Lights, along the extended centreline; steady-burning bars plus sequenced flashers.

Don't memorise names blindly. ALSF = sequenced flashing lights; MALSR = medium intensity + RAIL; SSALR = simplified short + RAIL. Indian AIP data shows CAT I and CAT II approach systems, PAPI and TDZ lighting varying by aerodrome, so never assume every airport uses the same ALS configuration.

ICAO / India: Annex 14 describes simple approach lighting (centreline about 420 m with a crossbar at 300 m) and precision approach lighting CAT I (centreline 900 m, crossbar at 300 m) and CAT II/III (900 m, extra crossbar at 150 m and red side rows over the inner 270 m). FAA also uses MALS, MALSF, SALS and ODALS. Always confirm against the applicable AIP.

PAPI: Precision Approach Path Indicator

Visual approach slope guidance. FAA describes two or four lights in a single row, normally to the left of the runway. Common four-light interpretation:

Pilot seesMeaning
4 whiteToo high
3 white + 1 redSlightly high
2 white + 2 redOn slope
1 white + 3 redSlightly low
4 redToo low

More white = high. More red = low. 2 white + 2 red = on path.

VASI: Visual Approach Slope Indicator

Common two-bar interpretation: White/White = above path; Red/White = on path; Red/Red = below path. Three-bar VASI can give two visual glide paths.

  • Common 4-light PAPI on path = 2 White + 2 Red
  • More White = Above Path · More Red = Below Path
  • ALSF-2 = high-intensity, FAA CAT II/III applications
  • MALSR = medium intensity with runway alignment indicator lights

AIRFIELD ELECTRICAL SYSTEMS FOR AGL

The fixture is only one part of an AGL system. Behind every light is an electrical system delivering controlled power. The series circuit is one of the most important concepts for electrical and airport engineering students.

POWER SUPPLY → CCR → PRIMARY CABLE → PRIMARY CONNECTOR
   → ISOLATING TRANSFORMER → SECONDARY CONNECTOR → AGL LIGHT

1. Constant Current Regulator (CCR)

Supplies controlled current to the series circuit, provides intensity levels in applicable systems and interfaces with airport lighting control. FAA lists AC 150/5345-10H for CCRs and regulator monitors. Why a CCR? Because series-circuit lighting is designed to operate with controlled constant current. Typical FAA values (check the specification): 6.6 A maximum; 3-step 4.8 / 5.5 / 6.6 A; 5-step 2.8 / 3.4 / 4.1 / 5.2 / 6.6 A.

2. Series circuit

CCR out ─► [TX 1] ─► [TX 2] ─► [TX 3] ─► … ─► [TX N] ─┐
  ▲           │         │         │              │    │
  │        Light 1   Light 2   Light 3        Light N │
  │                                                   │
  └─────  return to CCR: the loop is continuous  ─────┘

The circuit is one continuous loop: the same current leaves the CCR, passes through every isolating transformer primary and returns to the CCR.

Never open an energised series circuit. The CCR holds current constant, so an open loop can produce dangerous voltage. Isolate, lock out and follow permit-to-work before breaking the primary circuit.

3. Isolating transformer

Provides electrical isolation between the primary series circuit and the fixture, transfers power to the secondary side, and lets individual fixtures connect to the series circuit. FAA AC 150/5345-47C covers series-to-series isolation transformers.

4–5. Primary cable and connector

The primary cable is part of the high-voltage series circuit; FAA AC 150/5345-7F specifies L-824 underground cable. Primary connectors join cable lengths and connect the cable to the transformer primary; in FAA systems they are normally L-823. A poor connection can look like a circuit or fixture fault.

6–7. L-823 and secondary connection

L-823 connectors are used for underground power cables, isolation transformer leads and fixture leads (FAA AC 150/5345-26E), covering both primary and secondary connectors. The transformer secondary supplies the fixture through the secondary connection. L-823 = airport lighting cable connector.

AGL fault-finding logic

Safety first. Treat every primary circuit as hazardous. Isolate at the CCR, lock out and tag out, prove dead and earth as your procedures require before opening any connector or cable.

Don't immediately replace the light. Ask, in order:

  1. Is the CCR operating correctly?
  2. Is the series circuit energised?
  3. Is the primary circuit continuous?
  4. Are the connectors satisfactory?
  5. Is the isolating transformer healthy?
  6. Is the secondary connection satisfactory?
  7. Is the light fixture working?

One runway light not operating

  • Fixture fault
  • Secondary connector problem
  • Transformer fault
  • Primary connector problem
  • Local cable issue

Don't assume the lamp or LED is at fault.

Several lights not operating

  • CCR output
  • Primary circuit and cable continuity
  • Connector condition
  • Multiple component failures

The more fixtures affected, the more you should investigate the common part. If the whole circuit is out, suspect an open primary loop or a CCR trip.

  • Transformer = isolation between primary series circuit and fixture
  • L-823 = connector spec · L-824 = underground cable spec (FAA)
  • Understand the complete circuit before troubleshooting
