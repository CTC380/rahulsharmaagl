Spectrometer

A high‑precision optical instrument that measures the spectral power distribution (SPD) of light emitted by AGL fixtures. Provides comprehensive colour and intensity data across the visible spectrum, enabling detailed analysis of colour quality, colour rendering, and compliance with ICAO/FAA chromaticity standards. Essential for type testing, research, and troubleshooting complex colour issues.

Overview

What Is It?

A Spectrometer (specifically a spectroradiometer) is an advanced optoelectronic instrument that measures the spectral power distribution (SPD) of a light source — the amount of optical power emitted at each wavelength across the visible spectrum (typically 380 nm to 780 nm). Unlike a colorimeter, which uses filters to mimic the human eye's response, a spectrometer captures the full spectral fingerprint of the light. This enables:

  • Precise chromaticity calculation — deriving CIE (x,y) or (u',v') coordinates with high accuracy
  • Colour rendering index (CRI) — assessing how accurately colours appear under the light
  • Correlated colour temperature (CCT) — quantifying the warmth or coolness of white light
  • Spectral analysis — identifying unexpected spectral spikes or dips caused by LED binning, filter degradation, or optical contamination
  • Metamerism detection — understanding when two lights appear to match in colour but have different SPDs, which can cause visual discrepancies under different lighting conditions

In AGL applications, spectrometers are primarily used in laboratory settings for type testing, research, and advanced troubleshooting, though portable field spectrometers are increasingly available for on‑site diagnostic work.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The spectrometer's purpose is to see past the single colour coordinate a colorimeter reports and reveal the full spectral "fingerprint" behind it, which matters because two light sources can register the same nominal colour while having very different underlying spectra — a distinction that becomes critical when diagnosing a colour that is marginally out of tolerance or verifying that the LEDs actually installed in a fixture are from the correct manufacturing bin. Its detailed spectral data is what allows engineers to trace a colour shift back to its physical cause, such as a filter that has faded in a specific wavelength band, rather than simply confirming that a problem exists. Because of this diagnostic depth, the spectrometer also serves as the primary reference used to calibrate simpler field-grade colorimeters and photometers, keeping the whole measurement chain traceable back to a single spectral truth.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Spectrometers are deployed in specialised scenarios where detailed spectral data is required:

  • Type Testing and Certification: Independent testing laboratories use spectrometers to verify that new AGL fixture models meet the full chromaticity and colour quality requirements specified in ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5345‑46.
  • LED Bin Verification: Manufacturers use spectrometers to ensure that the LED chips used in AGL fixtures are from the correct chromaticity bin, as required by the design specification.
  • Research and Development: When developing new AGL lighting technologies (e.g., novel LED phosphor blends), spectrometers provide the detailed spectral feedback needed for optimisation.
  • Advanced Troubleshooting: When colourimeter readings show marginal compliance or indicate a colour shift, a spectrometer can reveal the underlying spectral cause — for example, a degraded filter that attenuates a specific wavelength range.
  • Calibration of Colorimeters and Photometers: Reference spectrometers are used as primary standards to calibrate field‑grade photometers and colorimeters, ensuring traceability to national metrology institutes.
  • Quality Assurance in Manufacturing: Some high‑end AGL manufacturers use spectrometers on production lines to perform 100% spectral inspection of critical components like optical filters and LED modules.
Placement

Installation & Location

The spectrometer has no fixed field location — it is a bench or portable instrument kept in a calibration or photometric laboratory and brought to the measurement point as needed. Laboratory measurements are made on an optical bench in a darkened room, with the fixture or sample under test mounted on a fixed stand and the instrument's fibre-optic probe or cosine-corrected input positioned at the specified distance and angle. For field or production-line use, a portable unit with a handheld probe is positioned directly against or in front of the light source being sampled. Like other photometric reference instruments, the spectrometer is periodically sent to an accredited calibration laboratory to maintain traceability, and is itself sometimes used in that role to calibrate field-grade photometers and colorimeters.

Function

How Does It Work?

A spectrometer typically consists of the following key components:

  • Entrance Optics: A lens or fibre optic assembly that collects the light from the fixture under test and couples it into the instrument.
  • Wavelength Dispersion Element: A diffraction grating or prism that separates the incoming light into its constituent wavelengths (similar to a rainbow).
  • Linear Array Detector: A silicon photodiode array (e.g., CCD or CMOS) that captures the intensity of light at each wavelength simultaneously. The number of pixels determines the spectral resolution.
  • Signal Processing and ADC: The detector signals are amplified, digitised, and converted into spectral data (intensity vs. wavelength).
  • Microprocessor and Software: The instrument calibrates the raw data using a traceable reference source (e.g., a standard lamp with known SPD). It then calculates photometric and colorimetric quantities (luminous flux, CIE coordinates, CCT, CRI, etc.) from the SPD.

For AGL measurements, the spectrometer is typically placed at a defined distance and angle relative to the fixture (matching the geometry used for intensity measurements). The fixture is operated at the required current step, and the spectrometer captures the SPD. The software then compares the chromaticity coordinates against the relevant standard's colour boundary table to determine pass/fail. The full SPD also allows detection of spectral artefacts (e.g., spikes due to reflected ambient light or artefacts from the fixture's optics).

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Like the photometer, a spectrometer used for airfield lighting or paint verification is a standalone measurement instrument rather than a networked control system, but its output flows through a defined signal chain. Light from the sample or fixture enters through the input optics, is dispersed by a grating or prism across the detector array, and the resulting per-wavelength intensity signal is digitized and processed against the instrument's stored wavelength and intensity calibration, traceable to certified reference sources, to produce a spectral output such as chromaticity coordinates or dominant wavelength. The result is compared against the applicable specification to generate a pass/fail determination, and as with any calibrated optical instrument, periodic recalibration against a certified reference source is essential since a drifted instrument corrupts every downstream pass/fail decision.

Input Optics → Dispersive Element → Detector Array → Calibration → Spectral Output

Construction

Physical Construction

The instrument is built around a fixed or holographic diffraction grating that disperses incoming light onto a linear CCD or CMOS detector array, all mounted on a rigid optical bench inside a light-tight enclosure to prevent stray light and mechanical drift from affecting wavelength calibration. Light enters through a cosine-corrected diffuser or integrating sphere port, and where the instrument is used for luminaire testing rather than ambient light measurement, a fibre-optic input probe is used instead so the detector head can be kept away from the heat and electrical noise of the light source under test. Laboratory-grade units are housed in a benchtop enclosure suited to a temperature-controlled environment, while field-hardened versions add a sealed IP54 housing, shock-mounted optics, and a rechargeable battery pack so the instrument can be carried to a fixture under test and operated without a bench setup. Data and power connections use standard USB, Ethernet, or Wi-Fi interfaces rather than industrial connectors, reflecting the instrument's role as a portable or laboratory measurement tool rather than a permanently installed field device.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Calibration Drift and Traceability

Spectrometers require rigorous calibration using a traceable standard lamp (e.g., a halogen lamp with known spectral irradiance). Calibration can drift due to lamp ageing, changes in detector sensitivity, or optical misalignment. Annual recalibration by an accredited laboratory is essential. Field‑grade instruments should be checked against a portable reference source before each use.

Stray Light and Flare

Stray light within the spectrometer (light scattering inside the optical path) can cause spectral distortion, especially in the presence of strong spectral lines or when measuring low‑intensity signals. High‑quality instruments use baffles and order‑sorting filters to minimise stray light. In the field, ensure that the instrument is not exposed to direct sunlight or strong ambient light sources.

Temperature Sensitivity

Both the spectrometer's optical components (grating, detector) and the fixture under test are temperature‑sensitive. Changes in temperature can shift the wavelength calibration and affect the detector's quantum efficiency. For accurate results, allow both the instrument and the fixture to stabilise at the operating temperature (typically 25°C) before measurements.

Fibre Optic Coupling Losses

Many spectrometers use a fibre optic cable to collect light. Variations in fibre alignment, bending, or connector contamination can introduce losses that affect the measured spectral shape. Use a reference measurement to normalise the signal, and keep the fibre path clean and free from sharp bends.

Integration Time and Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio

Selecting the correct integration time is critical — too short, and the signal is noisy; too long, and the detector saturates. The optimal setting depends on the fixture's intensity and the ambient light level. Many spectrometers offer auto‑integration, but manual verification is recommended for critical measurements.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Measured Values Drift from Known Reference
Likely Cause
Calibration drift from lamp ageing, detector sensitivity change, or optical misalignment.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the instrument against a portable reference source, review the calibration history and due date, and inspect optical alignment.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate against a traceable standard lamp at an accredited laboratory, or realign optics if the drift is mechanical.
Distorted Spectral Readings in Bright Conditions
Likely Cause
Stray light entering the optical path from direct sunlight or strong ambient sources.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the measurement conditions for ambient light exposure and compare readings taken shielded versus unshielded.
Corrective Action
Shield the instrument from direct sunlight/ambient light, and use baffles or an order-sorting filter if available.
Wavelength Calibration Shift Between Measurements
Likely Cause
Temperature sensitivity of the optical components (grating, detector) or the fixture under test.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the instrument and fixture temperature against the specified operating point and compare readings taken before and after stabilisation.
Corrective Action
Allow the instrument and fixture to stabilise at the specified operating temperature before measuring.
Inconsistent Spectral Shape Between Tests
Likely Cause
Fibre optic coupling losses from misalignment, bending, or connector contamination.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the fibre for sharp bends or contamination, check connector cleanliness, and take a reference measurement to normalise the signal.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the fibre/connector, correct routing to avoid sharp bends, and re-normalise against a reference.
Noisy or Saturated Signal
Likely Cause
Integration time set incorrectly for the fixture's intensity or ambient light level.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the current integration time setting against the signal level, check for detector saturation flags, and compare against the auto-integration recommendation.
Corrective Action
Adjust the integration time manually, or verify and apply the auto-integration setting; re-measure to confirm signal-to-noise is acceptable.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Wavelength Calibration Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Thermal expansion of the optical bench, mechanical shock, or grating/detector ageing shifting the wavelength-to-pixel mapping.
Effect
Measured values drift from the known reference, and repeat measurements of the same source show a wavelength calibration shift between sessions.
Detection Method
Wavelength calibration check against a certified reference source at the start of each measurement session.
Intensity / Radiometric Calibration Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Detector sensitivity ageing or degradation of the calibration standard used to set the intensity scale.
Effect
Absolute intensity and derived photometric values read consistently high or low against a known-good source.
Detection Method
Intensity calibration check against a traceable calibration standard, tracked over time for drift.
Stray Light and Flare
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Internal reflections, a contaminated optical path, or ambient light entering through a compromised light seal.
Effect
Distorted spectral readings in bright conditions, and elevated dark-current baseline even with no source present.
Detection Method
Dark current/stray light check with the input blocked, and a repeat under bright ambient conditions to isolate the source.
Fibre Optic Coupling Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Fibre connector wear, misalignment, or contamination at the coupling interface between the input optics and the spectrometer.
Effect
Inconsistent spectral shape or reduced signal-to-noise ratio between otherwise identical test setups.
Detection Method
Fibre coupling inspection and a repeatability check comparing successive measurements of the same known source.
Temperature Sensitivity / Thermal Drift
Low Severity
Root Cause
Detector dark-current and electronic gain both vary with ambient temperature if the unit lacks active thermal stabilisation.
Effect
Measurement results shift over the course of a session as the instrument warms up or as ambient conditions change.
Detection Method
Temperature stability check comparing readings at the start and after thermal equilibration.
Detector Saturation / Noisy Signal
Low Severity
Root Cause
Integration time set too long for a bright source, or too short for a dim one, pushing the detector outside its optimal signal-to-noise range.
Effect
Noisy or saturated signal that produces unreliable spectral data regardless of the instrument's underlying calibration health.
Detection Method
Integration time and signal-to-noise ratio check against the manufacturer's recommended operating range for the source intensity.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Bright Light / Eye Exposure During Measurement
Personnel Safety

Avoid direct, close-range viewing into a high-intensity fixture while taking a spectral reading; use the instrument's input optics or diffuser at the manufacturer's specified stand-off distance rather than positioning your eyes in the beam path.

Battery Charging Safety
Personnel Safety

Charge the instrument's internal Li-ion battery only with the supplied AC adapter or USB source, and avoid charging or storing it in extreme heat, to prevent battery damage or thermal events.

Hot Fixture / Housing Contact
Personnel Safety

Fixtures under test may be hot after a sustained operating period; allow adequate cooling time before positioning the instrument in close contact with the housing or lens.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Taking spectral readings on runway, taxiway, or approach fixtures requires ATC/airside coordination where the measurement involves accessing an active fixture or keeping it illuminated outside normal traffic patterns.

Calibration Reference Integrity
Operational

Handle the certified reference light source or calibration standard carefully and keep its own calibration traceable, since a drifted reference undermines every reading taken with the instrument.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm chromaticity and intensity readings fall within the applicable photometric standard before closing out the measurement task, and log the reference used for traceability.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Let Both Instrument and Fixture Thermally Settle Before Trusting a Reading
Measurement Accuracy

Taking a reading immediately after unpacking a cold spectrometer from a vehicle, or measuring a fixture that hasn't reached thermal equilibrium after being switched on, introduces a wavelength and intensity error that has nothing to do with either device's calibration. Build a stabilisation period into the field test procedure itself, not just into the lab calibration protocol.

Ambient Light Contamination is Easy to Miss on a Bright Runway
Field Technique

Daylight or nearby fixture spill entering the measurement path produces a spectral distortion that can look like a legitimate feature of the light source under test rather than an obvious error. Shield the measurement path and, where possible, take readings during low-ambient conditions, and always compare against a known reference to catch contamination that isn't visually obvious.

Fibre Bend Radius Affects Results, Not Just Cable Life
Calibration

A collection fibre coiled tighter than its minimum bend radius during field use can introduce loss that shifts the measured spectral shape, not just the overall signal level, which is easy to mistake for a genuine change in the light source. Route and store the fibre to respect its bend radius during every measurement, and re-zero against a reference if the fibre routing has changed since the last calibration check.

Integration Time Needs Field Verification, Not Just Auto-Range Trust
Signal Quality

Auto-integration algorithms are tuned for typical conditions and can under- or over-expose in edge cases, such as a very low-intensity LED fixture at end of life or a saturating close-range measurement. Manually verify the signal isn't clipped or excessively noisy for critical acceptance or dispute measurements rather than relying solely on the auto setting.

A Portable Reference Check is Not a Substitute for Lab Recalibration
Calibration

Checking a field instrument against a portable reference source before use is valuable for catching gross drift, but it doesn't have the traceability or precision of an accredited lab calibration and shouldn't be used to extend the recalibration interval. Keep the annual accredited calibration on schedule regardless of how well the field checks have been trending.

Log Environmental Conditions Alongside Every Compliance Measurement
Documentation

A chromaticity or intensity measurement disputed later is far easier to defend or diagnose if the ambient temperature, warm-up time, and instrument calibration date were recorded alongside the reading itself. Build these fields into the standard test record rather than treating them as optional metadata.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL spectrometers: spectral measurement, accuracy, standards, calibration and field technique.

Q1What is a spectrometer in AGL testing?

A spectrometer, specifically a spectroradiometer, measures the spectral power distribution of a light source, meaning the optical power emitted at each wavelength across the visible spectrum, typically 380-780 nm.

Q2How is a spectrometer different from a colorimeter?

A colorimeter reports a single colour coordinate, while a spectrometer reveals the full spectral fingerprint behind it. Two sources can register the same nominal colour yet have very different spectra, which matters for verifying colour compliance.

Q3Where are spectrometers used in AGL work?

In specialised scenarios needing detailed spectral data, such as independent laboratory type testing and certification of new fixture models against chromaticity and colour-quality requirements.

Q4How does a spectrometer work?

Entrance optics (a lens or fibre) collect light from the fixture, a diffraction grating disperses it by wavelength, and a linear array detector records the spectrum.

Q5What accuracy can be expected?

Spectral resolution is typically under 5 nm for laboratory grade and under 10 nm for field grade, wavelength accuracy ±0.5 nm and ±1 nm respectively, and chromaticity accuracy ±0.0005 (x, y) for laboratory grade or ±0.0015 for field grade.

Q6What are the operating conditions?

Typically +10°C to +40°C in a temperature-controlled environment, with IP20 for laboratory units and IP54 for field-hardened versions. Scans take under one second.

Q7How is a spectrometer powered?

By an internal rechargeable battery (typically Li-ion) or a mains AC adapter, with battery protection and low-battery and charging indication on the display.

Q8Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14 (spectral requirements for AGL colours), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (Specification for Runway, Taxiway, Heliport, and Vertiport Light Fixtures), IEC 61827 and CIE colorimetry methods (CIE 15 and CIE S014).

Q9How often should a spectrometer be calibrated and checked?

Annually for calibration against a traceable reference source and for temperature stability, and semi-annually for stray light. Fibre optic connectors should also be inspected annually for contamination or misalignment.

Q10Why are readings drifting from a known reference?

Common causes are calibration drift from lamp ageing, detector sensitivity change or optical misalignment. Check the instrument against a portable reference source and review its calibration history.
