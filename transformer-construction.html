How is a transformer built?

A series isolation transformer is a sealed, layered unit: a core with two windings, insulation around them, a potting compound, a housing and leads with connectors. Nothing inside is meant to be serviced.

The Fact Circuit practice

A series isolation transformer is a sealed unit built in layers. At the centre is a magnetic core with a primary winding and a secondary winding around it, each wrapped in an insulation system. A potting compound fills the space around them, a moulded housing encloses the lot, and leads with connectors leave at each end.

The construction is designed to stay sealed and rigid for years in a damp pit, so there are no serviceable parts inside. When a unit is faulty it is tested and replaced under site procedures, not opened. The lead exits and connectors are the usual weak points, which is why they are checked first. Whatever the build, the primary leads are part of a high-voltage loop: treat every lead as live until the circuit is isolated and proved dead under site procedures.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is a series isolation transformer made of?

A magnetic core, a primary winding and a secondary winding wound around it, an insulation system separating them, a potting compound filling the space around them, an outer housing, leads with connectors, and markings that identify the unit.

Q2Why is the transformer potted and sealed?

It sits in a pit or base can where damp and dirt are normal. Potting and a sealed housing keep moisture away from the windings and hold the parts rigid against vibration, so the insulation stays intact for the life of the unit.

Q3Can a transformer be opened and repaired?

Not as a normal practice. The construction is sealed and the potting bonds the parts together. A faulty transformer is tested and replaced under site procedures rather than repaired in the field.

Q4Which parts are most likely to be damaged in service?

The lead exits, boots and connectors take the most handling and are the most exposed to water, so they are the usual weak points. Cracks or cuts in the housing are the next thing to look for.

Q5Does the construction change what the nameplate shows?

Yes. The core, windings and insulation decide the power, current, frequency and insulation ratings that the nameplate states, so the plate is the quickest way to know what the unit inside is built for.
