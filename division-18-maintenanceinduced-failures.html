Maintenance-Induced Failures

The dedicated failure classification for failures caused, introduced or left unresolved as a result of inspection, preventive maintenance, corrective maintenance, replacement, adjustment, cleaning, calibration, repair or post-maintenance activities. Kept strictly separate from the underlying equipment-failure divisions: the final fixture or component failure belongs to its own equipment division, while the maintenance-induced cause is recorded here.

Initial Issue

Key principle — Connector was originally healthy → maintenance activity damages connector → poor contact → fixture failure. The final fixture failure is classified under the relevant equipment/component division; the maintenance-induced cause is classified here under Division 18. This dual classification — primary technical failure + maintenance-induced cause — is what turns “equipment failed” into “equipment was damaged or left defective because of maintenance,” and is essential for MTBF analysis, technician competency review, SOP improvement and spare-part quality analysis.

MIF-01 Incorrect Maintenance Procedure
Failure Definition

A maintenance activity is performed using a procedure that is incorrect, unsuitable, outdated, incomplete or not applicable to the equipment.

Possible Causes
  • Wrong SOP used
  • Incorrect maintenance sequence
  • Procedure not followed
  • Outdated procedure
  • Equipment-specific procedure not available
  • Technician misunderstanding
Possible Consequences
  • Equipment damage
  • Incorrect adjustment
  • Incomplete maintenance
  • New failure introduced
  • Safety risk
Investigation

Verify:

  • Applicable SOP
  • SOP revision
  • Maintenance activity performed
  • Actual sequence followed
  • Technician competency
  • Maintenance record

MIF-15 Incomplete Testing After Maintenance
Failure Definition

Required post-maintenance testing is not performed completely or is omitted.

Examples
  • No functional test
  • No continuity test
  • No insulation test where required
  • No earth verification where applicable
  • No photometric verification where required
  • No control-system test
  • No PAPI alignment verification
Possible Consequences
  • A maintenance-induced defect may remain undetected before return to service.
Critical Maintenance Principle

No maintenance activity should be considered fully closed until the required post-maintenance verification has been completed and recorded. Cross-reference with Division 17 — Testing & Commissioning Failures.

MIF-18 Maintenance-Induced Equipment Damage
Failure Definition

Equipment is physically, electrically, mechanically or optically damaged as a direct or reasonably established consequence of maintenance activity.

Examples
  • Fixture damaged during removal
  • Connector damaged during replacement
  • Cable damaged during excavation
  • Lens damaged during cleaning
  • Housing damaged during handling
  • Terminal damaged during tightening
Scope Note

This is the broad consequence category. Where the specific mechanism is known, record the more specific category as the primary failure and use maintenance-induced equipment damage as the causal classification.

D. Dual Classification Example

Every Division 18 event should be recorded alongside its primary technical failure:

Primary failure: Connector high contact resistance

Causal classification: Damaged connector during maintenance

Root cause: Incorrect connector handling procedure

Corrective action: Connector replacement + technician retraining + SOP revision

E. Failure Severity

Every Division 18 event receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging:

Critical / Safety-Critical — immediate operational or safety risk

Major / Operationally Critical — significant availability impact

Moderate — reduced performance, not yet critical

Minor / Non-Critical — limited or no operational effect

Degradation / Intermittent / Latent — developing or hidden fault

Failure Database vs. Troubleshooting Atlas

The Troubleshooting Atlas answers “I have a fault now — how do I diagnose and fix it?” The Failure Database answers “this failure happened — what happened, why, what evidence proves it, what was done, and how do we stop it recurring?” Division 18 is the layer that keeps this honest: it separates “equipment failed” from “equipment was damaged or left defective because of maintenance,” which is what makes MTBF analysis, repeat-failure analysis, technician competency review, SOP improvement, spare-part quality analysis and maintenance-audit activities meaningful across the wider AGL Failure Intelligence Engine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about maintenance-induced failures, as covered in Division 18 of the AGL Failure Database.

Q1What is Division 18 — Maintenance-Induced Failures in the AGL Failure Database?

Division 18 covers failures caused by maintenance activity itself. It contains 18 published topics (MIF-01 to MIF-18), including incorrect procedure, incorrect component replacement, incorrect torque, loose connections after maintenance, damaged connectors and cables, incorrect sealing, alignment and calibration, incomplete inspection, wrong or poor-quality spares, incomplete testing and documentation failure.

Q2How are the 18 maintenance-induced topics grouped?

They fall into seven groups: Procedure & Workmanship Failures (3 topics), Maintenance-Induced Damage (3), Sealing, Alignment & Calibration (3), Inspection & Cleaning Failures (2), Spare-Part & Component Failures (2), Post-Repair Installation & Testing (2) and Maintenance Quality & Reliability (3).

Q3Does “failure repaired” mean maintenance was done correctly?

Not necessarily. Division 18 supports a post-maintenance quality gate with seven checks: asset condition recorded before maintenance, approved SOP followed during work, correct spare verified, torque, sealing and alignment verified at assembly, maintenance checklist completed, required post-maintenance tests completed and records updated.

Q4What maintenance failure chains does Division 18 capture?

Division 18 records the full chain, not just the final symptom. In the connector chain, a maintenance activity damages the connector, causing poor contact, high contact resistance and a fixture failure. In the testing chain, incomplete testing lets a defect go undetected, the equipment returns to service, and an operational failure and unplanned corrective maintenance follow. Procedure, cable, sealing, alignment, spare-part and recurring-defect chains are also captured.

Q5Why is every maintenance-induced event recorded alongside a primary technical failure?

Dual classification separates what failed from why. For example, the primary failure is connector high contact resistance, the causal classification is a connector damaged during maintenance, and the root cause is an incorrect connector-handling procedure. The corrective action then covers connector replacement, technician retraining and an SOP revision.

Q6How does Division 18 relate to the other failure divisions?

Each Division 18 failure is cross-referenced to the technical divisions it affects. For example, loose connections after maintenance relate to Divisions 02, 05 and 15, damaged connectors to Division 05, incorrect sealing to Divisions 01, 05 and 16, incorrect alignment to Divisions 01, 11, 13 and 14, and incorrect calibration to Divisions 03 and 17.

Q7How are failure severity and operational impact classified?

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging. The classes are Critical / Safety-Critical (immediate operational or safety risk), Major / Operationally Critical (significant availability impact), Moderate (reduced performance, not yet critical), Minor / Non-Critical (limited or no operational effect), and Degradation / Intermittent / Latent (a developing or hidden fault).

Q8How does the Failure Database differ from the Troubleshooting Atlas?

The Troubleshooting Atlas answers “I have a fault now — how do I diagnose and fix it?” The Failure Database answers “this failure happened — what happened, why, what evidence proves it, what was done, and how do we stop it recurring?” Kept strictly separate, then linked end to end, the two form an AGL Failure Intelligence Engine rather than a simple fault list.
