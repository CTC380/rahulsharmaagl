What are Medium-intensity Obstruction Lights?

Brighter flashing or fixed lights for masts, chimneys, wind turbines and tall buildings, from about 45 m up to about 150 m.

flashing white (Type A)
red (Type B flashing, Type C fixed)
height bands (guide)
The Fact ICAO Annex 14

Medium-intensity lights are the middle class of obstacle light. They are used where a low-intensity light would not be conspicuous enough, on objects typically from about 45 m to about 150 m above ground.

There are three types. Type A is flashing white, at about 20,000 cd by day and at twilight and about 2,000 cd at night. Type B is flashing red and Type C is fixed red, both about 2,000 cd and both for night.

Lights are placed at the top and at intermediate tiers so the object is shown from top to base and from every direction. Type A is stepped down by a light sensor as the daylight fades, so it does not dazzle people on the ground at night.

How the Lights Are Applied
1
Confirm the class

Height and extent put the object in the medium band, between low- and high-intensity.

2
Choose the type

Type A for day and night conspicuity; Type B or C where red night lights and day marking are used.

3
Set the tiers

Place lights at the top and at intermediate levels so every approach direction sees the object.

4
Control and monitor

Use a light sensor to step intensity, keep flashing in step on groups, and monitor for lamp or sensor failure.

  Important Distinction

Medium-intensity does not mean one light. The three types behave differently by day and night, so say which type and when it works:

  • Type A (Flashing White)→ works day, twilight and night; intensity steps down at night
  • Type B / C (Red)→ night lights; by day the object relies on its marking
  • High-intensity→ flashing white, far brighter, for objects above about 150 m

Also keep it apart from low-intensity: medium-intensity lights flash (or are brighter fixed red) and carry far more light, so they are never a drop-in swap for a Type A or B low-intensity light.

Key Points

ICAO Medium-intensity Obstacle Lights

ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, Chapter 6, provides medium-intensity obstacle lights Type A, Type B and Type C. Type A is flashing white, with a higher peak intensity by day and at twilight than at night. Type B is flashing red and Type C is fixed red, both for night use. They are used on objects typically from about 45 m to about 150 m, placed at the top and at intermediate levels so the object is visible from every direction in azimuth. Where a light of this class would not be adequate, high-intensity lights are used. Take the type, intensities, flash rate and layout from the regulator's determination and the current edition of the standard.

FAA Medium-intensity Obstruction Lights

The FAA covers this in AC 70/7460-1, Obstruction Marking and Lighting. Its medium-intensity lights are the L-865 (flashing white, used day and night) and the L-864 (flashing red, used at night). Which lighting style applies, and whether it is used with day marking, depends on the structure and the FAA determination. Confirm the current edition of the advisory circular.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What are medium-intensity obstruction lights?

Medium-intensity obstruction lights are the middle class of obstacle light. They are used on taller fixed objects such as masts, chimneys, wind turbines and tall buildings. They come as flashing white (Type A), flashing red (Type B) and fixed red (Type C).

Q2What is the difference between Type A, B and C?

Type A is flashing white, with a peak intensity of about 20,000 cd by day and at twilight and about 2,000 cd at night. Type B is flashing red and Type C is fixed red, both about 2,000 cd and both night lights. By day, an object lit with Type B or C relies on its marking.

Q3Why is Type A white by day and dimmer at night?

White flashing light is very conspicuous by day, so the light is run at full intensity then. At night the same intensity would dazzle and disturb people on the ground, so a light sensor steps it down. Many installations also pair a white day light with a red night light.

Q4How tall an object do medium-intensity lights normally cover?

Typically objects from about 45 m to about 150 m above ground level. Below that, low-intensity lights are normally enough. Above it, high-intensity lights are normally needed. The height band is a guide, and the regulator's determination for the object governs.

Q5Do medium-intensity lights need to flash together?

On groups of structures, such as a wind farm, flashing in step is normally required or strongly preferred, so the group reads as one hazard and does not disturb people on the ground. How this is done is covered in a later entry on flash synchronisation.
