Product Overview

Low Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B)

What It Is

Low-intensity obstruction lights provide visual indication of structures that may present an aviation obstacle. They are used on buildings, towers, masts, small structures and other installations where the approved aeronautical study requires low-intensity lighting.

Under ICAO Annex 14 both Type A and Type B are fixed (steady-burning) red lights. They are night-time identification lights; day marking, where required, is by paint or markers, and flashing or higher-intensity lighting is covered by the Medium and High Intensity pages.

How We Supply It

Precision AGL Solutions supplies low-intensity obstruction lighting for new installations, replacement projects, obstruction-lighting upgrades and aviation safety applications.

The light provides steady-burning red output and may be installed standalone or as one level of a coordinated obstruction-lighting system. Optical, electrical and mechanical configuration are selected to the project.

Steady, Not Flashing

Low-intensity Type A and Type B lights are fixed lights and should not flash. A flashing red light, for example on medium-intensity Type B, is a different product category.

Where a structure needs flashing or higher-intensity lighting, select from the Medium or High Intensity obstruction light pages and do not mix lighting types on one level without the approved lighting plan.

Optical Coverage

Correct installation is essential to maintain the intended visibility and reliability of obstruction lighting.

Lights should be installed so that structural elements do not unnecessarily obstruct the intended optical coverage. The top light should be as close to the highest point as practicable, with intermediate levels arranged as required by the approved lighting plan.

FAQ

Low Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B) — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a low-intensity obstruction light?

Low-intensity obstruction lights are steady-burning red lights that mark less extensive structures at night, typically where the height above surrounding ground is below about 45 m (150 ft). They are used on buildings, towers, masts, small structures and other installations where the approved aeronautical study requires low-intensity lighting. They are night-time identification lights, and any day marking is by paint or markers.

Where are low-intensity obstruction lights used?

Typical uses are buildings and rooftop structures, communication and transmission towers, antenna and telecommunications masts, utility and infrastructure structures, small towers and poles, industrial structures, and temporary or permanent aviation-obstruction installations. They can be installed standalone or as one level of a coordinated obstruction-lighting system, and the final arrangement should come from the approved aviation lighting assessment and applicable regulatory requirements.

What is the difference between Type A and Type B?

Under ICAO Annex 14 both are fixed (steady-burning) red lights and differ in minimum peak intensity: Type A is 10 cd and Type B is 32 cd. Type A is for less extensive objects below about 45 m, while Type B is used where Type A would be inadequate or an earlier warning is needed. The FAA L-810 steady-burning red light has a 32.5 cd minimum. These are minimums for orientation, so confirm them against the edition and authority that apply.

Do Type A and Type B lights flash?

No. They are fixed lights and should not flash. A flashing red light, for example on medium-intensity Type B, is a different product category, and low-intensity Type C and Type D lights are flashing lights for vehicles and are not covered here. Where a structure needs flashing or higher-intensity lighting, select from the medium or high intensity pages and do not mix lighting types on one level without the approved lighting plan.

How do I choose between Type A and Type B?

The choice follows structure height and extent, location and surroundings, the required minimum intensity, the need for earlier special warning, the number and location of lighting levels, visibility from all directions and the applicable obstruction-lighting criteria. Obstruction-light classifications depend on the aviation authority and the structure, so confirm the configuration against the approved project specification.

Where should the lights be placed on a structure?

Follow the approved obstruction-lighting layout, including the required lighting levels and the spacing of intermediate levels. The top light should be as close to the highest point as practicable, and the number of lights per level should let the structure be seen from every direction. Install so that structural elements do not unnecessarily obstruct the optical coverage.

What electrical and control options are available?

The electrical configuration is selected to suit the site supply and control arrangement, covering supply voltage, AC or DC operation, surge protection, cable entry, connector arrangement, local switching, photocell or automatic day/night control, and remote monitoring or alarm interfaces where required. The final configuration should be coordinated with the obstruction-lighting control system and site power infrastructure.

How is a low-intensity light commissioned?

Verify the mounting position and orientation, electrical connections and supply voltage, and confirm steady-burning operation with no flicker or flash. Check the light colour and optical output, photocell or control operation where fitted, and alarm or monitoring interfaces where applicable, then record the results. Where photometric verification is required, use calibrated equipment and follow the project procedure.

What maintenance and troubleshooting does it need?

Routine work includes cleaning the optical window, checking that red colour and output have not degraded, and inspecting mounting hardware, cable entries and enclosure seals, with periodic functional testing. A light that is not operating points to a supply, protection or control fault, reduced visibility to a dirty optical window, flicker to a loose connection or wrong configuration, and moisture inside the enclosure to a seal or cable-entry problem.

Which standards apply to low-intensity obstruction lights?

References include ICAO Annex 14 Volume I Chapter 6 (obstacle lighting, Tables 6-1 and 6-2) and Appendix 1 for colours, ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4, FAA AC 70/7460-1 and AC 150/5345-43J (L-810), and DGCA India CAR Section 4 with AAI and NOC conditions. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, structure and authority, and specific national regulations take precedence in final selection.

What should be provided when requesting low-intensity obstruction lights?

Provide the structure type, height and location, the applicable aviation authority or project specification, the required Type A or Type B classification, the operating mode and colour, the quantity, the mounting arrangement, the available power supply, the existing control system, the environmental conditions and whether it is a new or replacement installation. For replacements, do not select by physical dimensions alone, because the optical and operational requirements of the approved arrangement must be maintained.
