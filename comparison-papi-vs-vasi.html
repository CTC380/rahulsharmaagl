Visual Approach Slope Guidance Comparison

PAPI VS VASI

Two visual glidepath systems, two very different indication logics. PAPI's four-light row gives the pilot finer graduation of vertical position; VASI's traditional two-bar geometry gives a simpler colour read. Both exist to answer one operational question — is the aircraft on the correct visual approach slope?

02 · Definitions

What Is PAPI & What Is VASI?

PAPI

PAPI consists of four light units installed in a horizontal row, normally on the left side of the runway. Each unit produces a white indication when the aircraft is above the designed approach path and a red indication when it is below it.

Pilot interpretation

The pilot reads position from the number of white and red lights across the row — more white means higher, more red means lower.

VASI

VASI traditionally uses two light bars — a near bar and a far bar. The pilot sees different colour combinations between the two bars depending on the aircraft's position relative to the approach path. Installations use 2, 4, 6, 12 or 16 light units; three-bar versions add a second, slightly higher glide path.

Pilot interpretation

The pilot reads position from the colour relationship between the two bars — white over white, red over white, or red over red, read as the far bar over the near bar.

03 · Visual Indication Logic

How Each System Signals Position

Both systems mark high, on-path and low — but PAPI does it with a light count, VASI does it with a bar-colour relationship.

PAPI

Read across the row — white = high, red = low
4 White — too high
2 White + 2 Red — on path
4 Red — too low

VASI

Read the far bar (top) over the near bar (bottom) — “red over white” is on path
White / White — too high
Red / White — on path
Red / Red — too low

Colours are read far bar over near bar — the familiar “red over white” is the on-path indication. Three states versus PAPI's five means VASI gives the pilot a coarser read of vertical position.

05 · The Main Difference

Number of Lights vs Colour Relationship

PAPI

The pilot determines position from the number of white and red lights across a single row — four units, more graduation.

VASI

The pilot determines position from the colour relationship between the near and far bars — simpler, coarser read.

Both systems answer the same question: is the aircraft on the correct visual approach slope? PAPI just gives a more finely graded answer.

06 · Scope of Guidance

What PAPI & VASI Do Not Provide

Neither system provides lateral navigation like a localizer, range information like DME, full precision guidance like an ILS, runway centreline guidance, or horizontal course deviation. They primarily provide vertical approach-path information.

PAPI and ILS can be installed on the same runway. The ILS localizer gives lateral guidance and the glideslope gives vertical guidance; PAPI provides an important visual cross-check once the runway environment becomes visible.

07 · Electrical System

Common Circuit Architecture

Both systems are relatively simple compared with an ALS or ILS, and share a similar simplified electrical arrangement: power supply → CCR / series circuit → isolation transformers → PAPI or VASI light units. This is the series-circuit (CCR-fed) form; voltage-powered variants also exist — for example, FAA PAPI Style A uses a power and control unit instead of a CCR, while Style B is the series-circuit version.

  • Intensity control
  • Monitoring and fault indication
  • Remote control
  • ALCMS interface, depending on the installation
08 · Maintenance

Keeping Each System On-Path

PAPI Maintenance

  • Optical cleanliness and lens condition
  • Light-source condition
  • Unit alignment, elevation and azimuth
  • Structural condition
  • Electrical connections, transformer/cable condition

Alignment is particularly important — even with all four units illuminated, incorrect alignment can produce an incorrect approach-path indication.

VASI Maintenance

  • Light-source condition and lens cleanliness
  • Optical and bar alignment
  • Elevation angle
  • Structural condition
  • Electrical connections, transformer/cable condition

The two-bar geometry must remain correctly aligned to the designed visual approach path.

09 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: PAPI provides lateral guidance
Fact

No. PAPI primarily provides vertical approach-path guidance, not lateral or centreline guidance.

Myth: PAPI is an optical version of ILS
Fact

No. PAPI is a visual aid; ILS is a radio-navigation system with its own localizer and glideslope.

Myth: Four red PAPI lights means correctly positioned
Fact

No — four red means the aircraft is below the designed approach path.

Myth: 2 white + 2 red means exactly on runway centreline
Fact

No. It indicates the designed vertical approach path. Lateral alignment is a separate consideration.

Myth: VASI and PAPI are interchangeable in every installation
Fact

No. They have different configurations, visual indications, design criteria and installation requirements.

11 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI vs VASI

Engineering Verdict

PAPI is not simply a modern replacement for VASI — it is a finer-graduation visual glidepath system. Both answer the same operational question, but PAPI's four-unit row gives the pilot more discrimination of vertical position than VASI's two-bar colour read.

Neither system replaces an ILS, provides lateral guidance, or confirms centreline alignment — both are purely vertical visual references, meant to be read together with the rest of the approach environment.

PAPI4-light row + white/red count + finer vertical discrimination.
VASI2-bar geometry + colour relationship + simpler, coarser read.

PAPI has largely superseded VASI in new installations, but VASI installations remain in service at many airports — always verify the actual system installed at a given runway rather than assuming. ICAO Annex 14 no longer carries a specification for the two-/three-bar VASIS.

12 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest difference between PAPI and VASI?

PAPI uses a single row of four light units read as a white/red count, giving five possible indications. VASI uses two bars read as a colour relationship, giving three possible indications — so PAPI provides finer vertical discrimination.

Does PAPI provide lateral or centreline guidance?

No. Both PAPI and VASI provide vertical approach-path guidance only — neither gives lateral navigation, range information or centreline guidance.

Can PAPI and ILS be installed on the same runway?

Yes. They perform different functions — the ILS gives radio-based lateral and vertical guidance, while PAPI gives the pilot a visual cross-check once the runway environment is in sight.

What does 2 white + 2 red mean on a PAPI?

It means the aircraft is on the designed approach path — the standard on-path indication for a typical PAPI installation.

Why is alignment so critical for both systems?

Because the indication depends entirely on the optical setting of each unit or bar. Even with every light illuminated, incorrect alignment produces an incorrect approach-path indication to the pilot.

Is VASI still used at airports today?

PAPI has largely become the standard for new installations, but VASI systems remain in service at a number of airports — the applicable system should always be verified for the specific runway.

What is the difference between PAPI and VASI?

PAPI uses a single wing bar of two or four light units, each showing red or white depending on the pilot's angle, with a sharp transition. VASI uses two or more bars of lights placed at different distances from the threshold and is read by the red and white pattern between the bars. PAPI is more compact, gives a more precise indication and is the standard choice for new installations.

13 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations, §5.3.5 — visual approach slope indicator systems: PAPI/APAPI configuration, siting and photometric requirements. The two-/three-bar VASIS specification is no longer carried in Annex 14.

DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I

India's aerodrome design and operations requirements, implementing ICAO Annex 14 — the primary regulatory reference for visual aids on Indian airport projects. Read alongside ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4 (Visual Aids) for design guidance.

FAA AC 150/5345-28 & AC 150/5340-30

PAPI equipment specification (L-880 four-box, L-881 two-box; Style A voltage-powered, Style B series-circuit) and airport visual-aid installation details including PAPI siting. For existing VASI installations, rely on the original installation documents and the airport's approved design.

IS 12290 & IS 7785 (BIS)

IS 12290 covers isolating transformers for airport lighting systems; IS 7785 is the multi-part series for elevated-type aerodrome lighting fittings. Neither is an equipment specification for PAPI or VASI light units — for those units, follow ICAO Annex 14 / Doc 9157 Part 4 or the FAA PAPI specification, as the project requires.

Final Engineering Note

Do not design a PAPI or VASI installation from a generic web diagram. The final siting and alignment must always be developed from the current governing standard, approved airport design and project-specific requirements.

PAPI is not a "better VASI" in every sense — it is a different optical logic that happens to give finer graduation. Either system, correctly aligned and maintained, exists to answer the same question for the pilot on approach.
