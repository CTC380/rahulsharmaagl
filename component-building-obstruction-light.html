Building Obstruction Light

An aviation warning light system for tall buildings and skyscrapers, typically featuring medium‑intensity red lights at the top and intermediate levels to indicate the building's height and shape. Designed for urban environments with aesthetic considerations, reliable 24/7 operation, and compliance with local aviation regulations.

Overview

What Is It?

A Building Obstruction Light is an aviation warning light system installed on tall buildings, skyscrapers, and high‑rise structures to alert aircraft of their presence. These structures often exceed 45 metres (150 feet) and are located in urban areas near airports or flight paths. The lighting system typically uses medium‑intensity red steady‑burning or flashing lights installed at the building's rooftop and at intermediate levels (every 45 metres) to clearly indicate the building's height and shape. Modern systems are designed with aesthetic considerations—low profile, neutral colours, and minimal visual impact during daytime—while providing reliable 24/7 obstruction marking. They are engineered to withstand urban environmental challenges: wind, rain, pollution, and temperature extremes.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The purpose of obstruction lighting on tall buildings is to make a structure's true height and outline visible to a pilot who may otherwise judge clearance from the surrounding skyline rather than the specific building that actually presents the hazard, particularly in dense urban areas near airports or flight paths where many structures compete for visual attention. Marking the rooftop alone isn't sufficient on very tall buildings, which is why lights are also placed at intermediate levels — this lets a pilot perceive the building's full vertical extent and shape rather than just its highest point, avoiding a false sense of the structure's true profile. The aesthetic design considerations built into modern systems — low profile, neutral daytime colours — exist because obstruction lighting on occupied buildings has to satisfy the aviation warning function continuously without imposing an unacceptable visual or quality-of-life cost on the people who live and work around the structure, a constraint that towers in unpopulated areas don't face to the same degree.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on tall buildings, skyscrapers, communication towers on buildings, and any high‑rise structure that exceeds 45 metres (150 feet) AGL or is located within an aviation protection zone. Used extensively in urban centres, business districts, and residential high‑rises near airports. Multiple lights are installed at the roof level and at intermediate levels (typically every 45 metres) on very tall buildings to provide a clear outline. The lights must be visible from all directions, requiring omnidirectional or 360° coverage. Special attention is given to mounting hardware that integrates with the building's architecture and does not compromise waterproofing or structural integrity.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the building's highest point and at intermediate levels down the facade or structure at the specified vertical interval (typically every 45 m), positioned so each light is visible from all azimuths without being shadowed by parapets, antennas, or adjacent architectural features. Rooftop units are mounted on a dedicated bracket or plinth above mechanical penthouses and other rooftop equipment, while intermediate-level lights are fixed to the facade, a ledge, or a dedicated bracket engineered into the building's structure. All penetrations through the building envelope are flashed and sealed to preserve waterproofing, and mounting hardware is selected to match the facade material and finish so the installation integrates with the building's architecture. Power and control cabling is routed through existing building risers back to a central photocell and controller, with each level typically monitored so a failed light can be identified and reported without a site visit.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each building obstruction light is powered from the building's main electrical supply, typically with a dedicated circuit and backup battery or UPS to ensure operation during power outages. The light source is usually a high‑efficiency LED array with a red filter, providing medium intensity (~2,000 cd) for nighttime and low‑visibility conditions. Some installations use flashing lights (20–40 fpm) for enhanced visibility. The lights are controlled by a photocell to switch on at dusk and off at dawn, or by a time‑based controller. The housing is constructed from weather‑resistant materials (aluminium or stainless steel) with IP66/IP67 protection, and features surge protection and EMI shielding to prevent interference with building systems. Aesthetic considerations include low‑profile designs and neutral colours to blend with the building's exterior.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Building obstruction lighting is controlled as a single lighting group rather than at the level of an individual fixture. A photocell or a time-based controller makes the dusk/dawn (or fixed-schedule) decision and switches a dedicated circuit that feeds every fixture on the building — the roof-top light and each intermediate-level light every ~45m down the facade — so the full outline appears and disappears together. That circuit is tapped from the building's main electrical supply but is backed by a battery or UPS specifically so the obstruction lights keep working through a building-wide power outage, which is exactly when they matter most. Surge protection and EMI shielding sit between the lighting circuit and the building's own electrical and communications systems to stop switching transients on the lighting circuit from coupling into other building systems, and vice versa. Because these lights are a regulatory requirement rather than a discretionary feature, most installations also feed fixture-level status back to the building management system or a remote monitoring service, so an outage can trigger maintenance action (and, where required, a NOTAM) rather than going unnoticed until a visual check.

Photocell / Timer → Dedicated Circuit + Backup Power → Roof & Intermediate Fixtures (with status feedback)

Day/Night Character

Invisible by Day, Outlines the Building by Night

Daytime — Off

Photocell keeps fixtures off. Low-profile, neutral-colour housings blend with the facade — no visual clutter on the skyline.

Night — On

Photocell switches on at dusk. Red steady or slow flash (~2,000 cd) at roof and every ~45m below traces the building's full height for approaching aircraft.

Unlike the wind turbine and mast obstruction lights, there's no white daytime flash mode here — the emphasis is architectural: a single red, dusk-to-dawn signal that stays visually unobtrusive against the building's exterior in daylight while still tracing its outline at night.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is a low-profile, weatherproof LED unit in a die-cast aluminium or corrosion-resistant polymer housing, designed to mount unobtrusively on the rooftop and at intermediate levels along the building facade or structure without disrupting the building's daytime appearance. A red, toughened lens caps the LED array, gasket-sealed with an O-ring or silicone seal to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating against wind-driven rain and dust. The housing incorporates a bracket or mounting plate suited to rooftop parapets, structural steel, or facade mullions, with adjustable orientation to aim the beam pattern for optimal omnidirectional coverage. Wiring enters through a sealed cable gland connected to the building's electrical supply, typically backed by an uninterruptible power supply to maintain illumination through building power interruptions. Corrosion-resistant fasteners and finishes are used throughout given the fixture's prolonged outdoor exposure at height.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Wind‑Induced Vibration and Fatigue

Tall buildings sway and vibrate, which can cause mechanical fatigue in mounting brackets and electrical connections. Use of vibration‑damping mounts, flexible cable conduits, and regular bolt torque checks is essential.

Light Pollution and Aesthetic Concerns

Bright red lights can be intrusive in urban environments. Using shielded optics, directional beams (where possible), and dimming controls (during low traffic periods) can help mitigate light pollution while maintaining aviation safety.

Moisture Ingress via Rooftop Penetrations

Mounting lights on rooftops requires waterproof penetrations. Failed seals can lead to water ingress into the building. Use of proper flashing, sealants, and regular inspection of rooftop mounts is critical.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Light Fails to Illuminate
Likely Cause
Blown supply fuse, tripped breaker, or a failed photocell stuck in the "day" position at the rooftop distribution point.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the local breaker/fuse and supply voltage at the fixture; bridge or bypass the photocell to confirm whether it is holding the circuit open.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the fuse/breaker; replace a failed photocell and re-torque all rooftop connections before closing up the enclosure.
Excessive Vibration-Induced Flicker or Intermittent Operation
Likely Cause
Wind-induced building sway has loosened mounting bolts or fatigued a crimp connection at the fixture base.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect mounting bracket torque against spec; visually check cable strain relief and connector crimps for fretting or cracking.
Corrective Action
Re-torque fasteners to spec, fit vibration-damping washers, and re-terminate any fatigued connections with new crimps and strain relief.
Water Ingress at Rooftop Penetration
Likely Cause
Failed roof flashing or a degraded cable gland seal has allowed water to track into the fixture housing or junction box.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect flashing and gland seals during a wet-weather walk or hose test; check for corrosion or condensation inside the housing.
Corrective Action
Re-seal the penetration with approved flashing and sealant, replace the cable gland, and dry out and megger the circuit before re-energising.
Light Pollution Complaints / Wrong Operating Mode
Likely Cause
Photocell threshold has drifted or dimming control has failed, leaving the light at full intensity during permitted low-traffic dimming periods.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify photocell switching point against ambient light level; check dimming controller settings and schedule.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the photocell and confirm the dimming controller schedule matches the site's operating requirements.
Reduced Light Output
Likely Cause
Lens or fixture surface contamination from urban dust, pollution film, or bird droppings is attenuating the beam.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for grime buildup; take a photometric reading and compare against rated intensity.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with an approved non-abrasive cleaner and re-check intensity; replace the lens if pitted or UV-degraded.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Roof-Mounted Access Restrictions Drive the Maintenance Plan
Maintenance

Unlike a purpose-built mast, a building rooftop often has restricted access windows, tenant coordination requirements, or its own permit-to-work process that can add weeks to scheduling a routine lamp or lens replacement. Build that lead time into the maintenance plan rather than treating rooftop obstruction lights as equivalent in accessibility to ground-level fixtures.

Intermediate-Level Coverage Gets Missed When Floors Get Renovated
Coordination

Facade renovations, added signage, or new rooftop equipment on intermediate floors can obstruct a light's beam years after commissioning without anyone reviewing obstruction lighting as part of the building works. Flag obstruction-light sightlines whenever facade or rooftop modification work is planned, not only during scheduled lighting inspections.

UPS Backup Deserves a Real Transfer Test
Testing

A UPS reporting full charge can still fail to pick up the obstruction-light load if the transfer switch has degraded, and building electrical rooms rarely get the same rigorous backup-power testing regime as dedicated airfield equipment. Schedule an actual mains-loss transfer test rather than accepting a battery-voltage reading as sufficient proof.

Photocell Siting on a Building Face Skews Easily
Calibration

A photocell mounted on one face of a tall building can be shaded by an adjacent structure or the building's own massing at certain times of day, causing the light to switch on or off out of step with true ambient conditions elsewhere on the roof. Re-check photocell threshold calibration against actual dusk/dawn switching rather than assuming factory calibration holds regardless of siting.

Wind-Induced Vibration Is Worse at the Roofline
Structural

Fixtures mounted at a rooftop parapet or corner see amplified wind-induced vibration compared with the same light lower on the facade, which can loosen bracket fixings faster than the standard inspection interval anticipates. Prioritise mounting hardware checks at the highest, most exposed mounting points on the structure.

Document Which Lights Cover Which Obstacle Evaluation Surface
Compliance

On a building with multiple wings or roof levels, it's easy to lose track of which obstruction light was installed to satisfy which specific point on the original obstacle evaluation. Keep as-built records tying each fixture to its designated marking point, so a later renovation doesn't inadvertently remove or relocate a light that's still required.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about building obstruction lights: when they are required, colour, spacing, power, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a building obstruction light?

A building obstruction light is an aviation warning light fitted to tall buildings and high-rise structures so pilots can see their presence, height and shape. It is typically a medium-intensity red LED light, steady-burning or flashing, and is designed for reliable 24/7 operation.

Q2Which buildings need obstruction lights?

Lights are installed on buildings and skyscrapers that exceed about 45 m (150 ft) above ground level or that lie within an aviation protection zone. They are common in urban centres, business districts and residential high-rises near airports or flight paths.

Q3What colour and intensity are building obstruction lights?

They use aviation red, either steady or flashing. Night intensity is typically about 2,000 cd, and the standards range for medium intensity is 2,000–20,000 cd depending on day or night mode. Chromaticity must fall within the ICAO Annex 14 red boundary.

Q4How many obstruction lights does a tall building need?

Lights are placed at the rooftop and, on very tall buildings, at intermediate levels typically every 45 m so the full height and shape are shown. Each light must be visible from all directions without being shadowed by parapets, antennas or architectural features.

Q5Do building obstruction lights flash?

They can be steady or flashing. Where flashing is used the rate is typically 20–40 flashes per minute, synchronised between the rooftop and intermediate-level units through cascaded timing.

Q6How are building obstruction lights powered and controlled?

They run from the building's mains supply, typically 230 V AC on a dedicated circuit routed via the electrical riser, with UPS backup so the light continues through a mains loss. A photocell or time-based controller switches them on at dusk and off at dawn, and individual fusing lets a unit be maintained without de-energising the whole string.

Q7Which standards apply to building obstruction lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 70/7460-1 (obstruction marking and lighting), FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment) and IEC 62305 for lightning protection and bonding. Always verify the current edition and the requirements of your national authority.

Q8How can obstruction lights reduce light pollution in cities?

Shielded optics, directional beams where permitted and reduced night intensity through photocell switching all limit dazzle for nearby residents. Low-profile, neutral-coloured housings also reduce daytime visual impact, while the lights still meet the required photometric output.

Q9How often should building obstruction lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for flash controller and UPS backup tests, and annual for photometric intensity checks and seal and weatherproofing inspections. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is a building obstruction light not working or flickering?

A light that stays dark is often a blown fuse, tripped breaker or a photocell stuck in the day position. Flicker or intermittent operation is commonly caused by wind-induced building sway loosening mounting bolts or fatiguing a crimp connection. Re-torque fasteners, fit vibration-damping washers and re-terminate any fretted connections.
