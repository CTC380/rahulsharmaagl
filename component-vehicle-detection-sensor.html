Vehicle Detection Sensor

A specialised detection system that monitors the presence and movement of ground vehicles on the airfield surface. Providing critical input to runway incursion prevention systems, A‑SMGCS, and ATC surveillance, these sensors enhance safety by alerting controllers to unauthorised vehicle movements on runways and taxiways.

Overview

What Is It?

A Vehicle Detection Sensor is a specialised surveillance device designed to detect and track ground vehicles operating on the airfield surface—including runway sweeper trucks, maintenance vehicles, fuel trucks, baggage tugs, and other support vehicles. These sensors provide real‑time vehicle presence and movement data to Air Traffic Control, Airport Operations Centres, and runway incursion prevention systems. Unlike aircraft detection sensors that are optimised for larger targets, vehicle detection sensors are engineered to reliably detect smaller, slower-moving targets (typically 1‑5 tonnes) with high sensitivity and low false‑alarm rates. They are deployed at critical locations such as runway crossings, taxiway intersections, vehicle access points, and runway ends. The sensor data is integrated with A‑SMGCS (Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control Systems), RWSL (Runway Status Light) systems, and ATC displays to provide comprehensive surface surveillance, enabling controllers to manage both aircraft and vehicle movements safely and efficiently, particularly during low‑visibility operations and complex ground movements.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The vehicle detection sensor exists to close a specific blind spot in surface surveillance: aircraft detection systems are tuned for large, predictable targets, while service vehicles are smaller, move less predictably, and are exactly the kind of traffic a controller can lose track of during a busy ground movement sequence, particularly in low visibility. By giving ATC and A-SMGCS the same real-time presence and movement data for vehicles that they already have for aircraft, the sensor lets vehicle and aircraft movements be managed within a single coherent picture rather than the vehicle side being managed by memory, radio position reports, or visual observation from the tower alone. This matters most at the exact locations, runway crossings and taxiway intersections, where an undetected vehicle would otherwise pose the greatest incursion risk.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Vehicle Detection Sensors are deployed at critical locations across the airfield where ground vehicle movements pose a potential conflict with aircraft operations:

  • Runway Crossings: Detecting vehicles approaching or crossing active runways to prevent incursions and trigger warning systems.
  • Taxiway Intersections: Monitoring vehicle movements at busy taxiway intersections to enhance situational awareness.
  • Vehicle Access Points: Detecting vehicles entering the airside from landside access gates.
  • Runway Ends (Threshold Areas): Monitoring vehicle activity near runway thresholds during maintenance or inspection operations.
  • Apron Areas: Tracking ground support vehicles (fuel trucks, baggage tugs, catering vehicles) for improved apron management.
  • Runway Status Light (RWSL) Systems: Providing vehicle detection input for Runway Entrance Lights (REL) and Takeoff Hold Lights (THL).
  • A‑SMGCS (Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control Systems): Integrating with A‑SMGCS for comprehensive surface surveillance and conflict detection.
  • ATC Towers: Providing controllers with real‑time vehicle location and tracking data, enhancing situational awareness.
  • Airport Operations Centers: Monitoring fleet vehicle movements for operational planning and resource allocation.
Placement

Installation & Location

Sensors are positioned at the runway crossing, taxiway intersection, or access gate they monitor, mounted either as an in-pavement inductive loop (cut and sealed into the surface) or as a pole-mounted microwave/infrared unit set back clear of vehicle and aircraft clearance envelopes. Mounting height and detection-zone aim are set out so the zone covers the full width of the crossing or gate without extending into an adjacent lane where it would generate false triggers. Detector cabling runs on its own screened pair back to the local controller or Remote I/O rather than sharing conduit with unrelated lighting circuits, since the output typically drives a warning light or gate interlock directly.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Vehicle Detection Sensor uses advanced detection technologies optimised for smaller, slower targets. Key functional aspects include:

  • Detection Technologies: Sensors typically use one or a combination of:
    • FMCW Radar (24 GHz or 77 GHz): Detects moving vehicles with high sensitivity, providing range, speed, and position information, immune to weather and lighting.
    • Infrared (active or passive): Uses thermal signatures or reflected IR to detect vehicles, effective in darkness, fog, and smoke.
    • Inductive Loops: Buried loops detect metallic vehicles passing over; simple and reliable, but limited to fixed points.
    • Video Analytics (AI/CCTV): Uses camera footage with machine learning algorithms to detect and classify vehicles, providing visual confirmation.
    • Fusion Sensors: Combine multiple technologies (e.g., radar + IR + video) for improved detection reliability and false‑alarm rejection.
  • Target Classification: Advanced sensors can differentiate between vehicles and aircraft, or between different vehicle types (e.g., fuel truck vs. baggage tug), to provide more meaningful data to controllers.
  • Coverage Zone: The sensor is positioned to cover a specific area—typically 50‑300 metres for radar/IR, or a precise detection point for inductive loops.
  • Speed and Direction Detection: Radar sensors can determine vehicle speed and direction of travel, providing valuable information for conflict prediction.
  • Output Signal: The sensor provides a clean, isolated output—typically a dry contact, transistor output, or digital message (Ethernet/Modbus)—that indicates vehicle presence, speed, and direction. The output is connected to the RWSL processor or A‑SMGCS.
  • Self‑Diagnostics: The sensor continuously monitors its own health (power, communication, signal quality) and provides diagnostic outputs for proactive maintenance.

Vehicle detection sensors are typically mounted on poles adjacent to the monitored area, on overhead gantries, or integrated into existing airfield infrastructure. Their position and sensitivity are carefully calibrated to detect vehicles while ignoring small objects (birds, debris) and reducing false alarms.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Like the Stop Bar Sensor it is closely related to, the Vehicle Detection Sensor is a detection input rather than a decision-maker — it reports the presence of a smaller, slower-moving target (a service vehicle or piece of ground equipment) to a Remote I/O unit or local PLC, and the access-control or ALCMS logic upstream decides what to do with that information: hold a gate or barrier, trigger a warning light, or log the crossing. It does not itself energise any actuator or lighting circuit. Because it is tuned to detect targets smaller and slower than the aircraft the Stop Bar Sensor is designed around, it is typically deployed at vehicle crossing points and service road junctions rather than on the runway itself, and a fault is reported and handled the same conservative way — as a loss of detection at that location, not as an assumption that the crossing is clear.

Construction

Physical Construction

The sensor head is housed in a sealed, weatherproof enclosure — die-cast aluminium or glass-reinforced polymer — rated IP65/IP67, with a radome or optical window covering the radar, IR, or camera aperture to protect it from rain, dust, and de-icing overspray while remaining transparent to the sensing wavelength. Pole-mounted units bolt to an adjustable bracket that allows azimuth and elevation to be set and locked during commissioning, and where video analytics are used, the camera module is a separate sealed unit connected to the processing electronics by a short weatherproof cable run. Internal electronics are conformally coated and connected through sealed M12 or similar circular connectors rather than open terminal strips, keeping moisture out of the detection and output circuitry. Built-in transient voltage suppression is mounted near the cable entry point to protect against lightning-induced surges, and the enclosure includes a grounding lug for bonding to the site's lightning protection system.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

False Alarms from Non‑Vehicle Targets

Radar and IR sensors may detect pedestrians, animals, or debris, causing false alarms. Advanced filtering and target classification algorithms are required to differentiate vehicles from other objects. Regular calibration and software updates are essential.

Sensor Obscuration (Radar/IR/Camera)

Snow, ice, heavy rain, dirt, or bird droppings on the sensor window or camera lens can significantly reduce detection performance. Regular cleaning and the use of heated housings, wipers, or air purges can prevent obscuration.

Inductive Loop Damage

Buried inductive loops can be damaged by pavement resurfacing, ground movement, or heavy vehicle loads. Periodic loop testing (using a loop tester) is required to detect open or short circuits.

Video Analytics Performance in Low Light

Camera‑based detection systems may struggle in low‑light conditions (night, fog, heavy rain) without specialised low‑light cameras or IR illumination. Use thermal cameras or integrated IR illuminators for reliable performance.

Alignment Drift

Over time, sensor mounts can shift due to vibration, thermal expansion, or ground movement, altering the detection zone. Regular site surveys and alignment checks using calibration targets are recommended.

Power Supply Instability

Voltage fluctuations or spikes can cause sensor resets or damage. Use a regulated power supply with surge protection and UPS backup to ensure reliable operation.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
False-Alarm Tuning Trades Directly Against Missed-Detection Risk
Design

Tightening sensitivity to cut nuisance false alarms on a busy movement area inevitably raises the risk of missing a genuine slow-moving vehicle at the detection threshold; tune sensor sensitivity against the specific traffic mix and false-alarm tolerance at that location rather than applying one blanket setting airfield-wide.

Low-Visibility Performance Needs Separate Verification From Clear-Day Testing
Verification

A sensor that reliably detects vehicles in clear daylight can behave differently in fog, heavy rain, or snow depending on its underlying technology. Verify detection performance under degraded-visibility conditions specifically, since that's when the system matters most operationally.

Integration Latency With A-SMGCS/RWSL Matters as Much as Detection Accuracy
Design

A sensor can detect a vehicle correctly and still contribute to a near-miss if the data takes too long to propagate through to the A-SMGCS display or RWSL logic; verify end-to-end latency from detection to displayed or actuated response, not just standalone sensor accuracy.

Coverage Overlap at Intersections Should Be Deliberate, Not Assumed
Design

Runway crossings and taxiway intersections often sit at the edge of two adjacent sensors' coverage zones; confirm there's deliberate overlap covering the full intersection rather than assuming adjacent sensor footprints meet cleanly, since a coverage gap at exactly the highest-risk point defeats the system's purpose.

Alignment Drift Is Gradual and Easy to Miss Between Scheduled Calibrations
Maintenance

Small mounting or aiming drift from wind, thermal cycling, or incidental contact can shift a sensor's detection zone gradually without triggering an obvious fault. Include a target-based alignment check in routine maintenance rather than relying solely on the sensor's own self-reported status.

Treat Detection Data as Safety-Critical for Change Control
Operational

Because vehicle detection feeds directly into runway incursion prevention logic, any change to sensor configuration, firmware, or mounting should go through the same change-control rigor as a change to the lighting or signage it protects, not be treated as a routine IT update.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about vehicle detection sensors: technologies, coverage, outputs, standards and common problems.

Q1What is a vehicle detection sensor in AGL?

A vehicle detection sensor is a surveillance device that detects and tracks ground vehicles on the airfield surface, such as sweeper trucks, maintenance vehicles, fuel trucks and baggage tugs. It gives real-time presence and movement data to ATC, airport operations centres and runway incursion prevention systems.

Q2Why are dedicated vehicle sensors needed?

Aircraft detection systems are tuned for large, predictable targets. Service vehicles are smaller (typically 1 to 5 tonnes), move less predictably and are easy to lose track of during busy ground movements or low visibility. Vehicle sensors give ATC and A-SMGCS the same data for vehicles as for aircraft.

Q3What detection technologies are used?

FMCW radar (24 GHz or 77 GHz), infrared (active or passive), inductive loops, video analytics (AI/CCTV), and fusion sensors that combine several technologies for better reliability and false-alarm rejection. Radar is immune to weather and lighting, infrared works in darkness, fog and smoke, and loops are simple but limited to fixed points.

Q4What is the detection range?

Typically 50 to 300 m for radar, 50 to 150 m for infrared, 50 to 100 m for video, and 5 to 20 m for inductive loops. Radar sensors can also measure vehicle speed (typically ±2 km/h) and direction of travel.

Q5Where are vehicle detection sensors installed?

At runway crossings, taxiway intersections, vehicle access points from landside gates, runway thresholds during maintenance operations and apron areas. They also feed Runway Status Light (RWSL) systems, A-SMGCS, ATC towers and airport operations centres.

Q6How does the sensor connect to other systems?

It has a clean, isolated output: typically a dry contact relay, opto-isolated transistor output, Ethernet (Modbus TCP), RS-485 serial or an IP video stream. Output indicates presence, speed and direction and connects to the RWSL processor or A-SMGCS. Response time is typically 150 ms or less for radar.

Q7What are the typical electrical and environmental ratings?

Power is 24 VDC nominal (18 to 32 VDC) or 120/230 VAC for video units, with 5 to 50 W consumption depending on type. Protection class is IP65/IP67, operating temperature is -30°C to +70°C, and built-in TVS surge protection is rated up to 6 kV.

Q8Which standards apply to vehicle detection sensors?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (covering the REL and THL in-pavement fixtures that RWSL drives), EUROCAE ED-87E (A-SMGCS MASPS) and EUROCAE ED-116 (MOPS for surface movement radar sensor systems). Always verify the current edition.

Q9How can false alarms be reduced?

Radar and IR sensors may detect pedestrians, animals or debris, so advanced filtering and target classification algorithms are needed. Position and sensitivity are calibrated to detect vehicles while ignoring small objects such as birds and debris, and the typical false alarm rate target is no more than 1 per 24 hours with proper calibration.

Q10What are the common vehicle detection sensor problems?

The common issues are false alarms from non-vehicle targets, obscuration of the sensor window or lens by snow, ice, rain, dirt or bird droppings, inductive loop damage from resurfacing or ground movement, poor video analytics performance in low light, alignment drift, and power supply instability.
