High Intensity Obstruction Light

A powerful aviation warning light used on structures exceeding 150 metres in height. High intensity obstruction lights produce an extremely bright white flashing signal during the day and switch to red at night, ensuring that tall obstacles are visible to pilots from great distances and in all weather conditions. They are essential for skyscrapers, tall transmission towers, and large wind farms.

Overview

What Is It?

A High Intensity Obstruction Light is a powerful aviation warning light designed for structures that exceed 150 metres (500 feet) in height. These lights produce an extremely bright white flashing signal during daytime and switch to a red flashing or steady‑burning signal at night, ensuring that the obstacle is visible to pilots from distances exceeding 50 kilometres in clear conditions.

High intensity obstruction lights are the most powerful type of obstruction lighting, with daytime intensities reaching up to 200,000 candela. They are typically installed at the top of very tall structures such as skyscrapers, tall communication towers (over 150m), large chimneys, and wind turbines in offshore wind farms. These lights operate with a dual‑mode system: white flashing during the day (for maximum contrast against bright skies) and red at night (to reduce glare and light pollution). They are often installed in multiple tiers on the tallest structures, providing a continuous visual warning at different heights.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

High intensity lighting exists because the tallest structures — those exceeding 150 metres — pose a hazard at distances and altitudes where a medium or low intensity light would simply not be bright enough to register, particularly during daytime when a red light of ordinary intensity is easily lost against a bright sky. The dual-mode design, extremely bright white by day and switching to red at night, exists to solve two different visibility problems with the same fixture: daytime demands maximum contrast against a bright background, while nighttime demands a lower-glare, less light-polluting signal that's still unmistakably a warning. Multi-tier installation on the tallest structures serves the broader purpose shared across all obstruction lighting — conveying the structure's full vertical profile rather than just its summit — but at a scale where the sheer height makes a single light point insufficient to communicate that profile accurately from a distance.

Application

Where Is It Used?

High intensity obstruction lights are installed on the tallest structures:

  • Skyscrapers and high‑rise buildings – over 150m in height.
  • Tall communication towers – TV/radio broadcast masts exceeding 150m.
  • Large chimneys and cooling towers – power plants and industrial facilities.
  • Offshore wind turbines – in wind farms, where maritime visibility is critical.
  • Tall transmission towers – electrical pylons over 150m.
  • As intermediate lights on extremely tall structures – providing multiple levels of warning.
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the top of the structure and, where the structure requires multiple tiers, at intermediate levels set out per the applicable lighting scheme so the full height is represented. Mounting brackets are engineered for the specific structure type — rooftop plinths on buildings, dedicated arms on lattice masts and chimneys, or nacelle-top brackets on wind turbines — and rated for the wind, ice, and vibration loading at the installation height. Because high-intensity units draw significant power and generate heat, mounting positions allow for ventilation and provide safe, code-compliant access for lamp or module replacement, often via a maintenance platform or lift built into the structure. Power and synchronization cabling connects each tier back to a central controller so day/white and night/red modes switch in unison across every light on the structure, and surge protection is fitted given the fixture's exposed position at the structure's most lightning-prone points.

Function

How Does It Work?

High intensity obstruction lights operate with a sophisticated dual‑colour, dual‑intensity system:

  • Light Source: High‑power LED arrays or xenon flash tubes are used to achieve the extremely high luminous intensities required. LED systems are increasingly common due to their efficiency, instant‑on capability, and precise control.
  • Colour and Mode: The light operates in two distinct modes controlled by a photoelectric sensor or timer:
    • Daytime (white flashing): Emits a bright white flash (typically 40–60 flashes per minute) with an intensity of 20,000–200,000 cd. White provides maximum contrast against the bright sky.
    • Nighttime (red flashing or steady): Switches to a red signal (flashing or steady) with a reduced intensity (typically 2,000–20,000 cd) to avoid glare and light pollution.
  • Beam Pattern: The lights are typically omnidirectional (360° horizontal coverage) to ensure visibility from all approach angles. Some systems have directional optics for specific applications.
  • Control and Synchronisation: High intensity lights are often synchronised across multiple tiers on a single structure, flashing simultaneously to create a coherent warning signal. This is managed by a central controller.
  • Power: These systems require substantial power (often 200–500W or more) and are typically mains‑powered (110/230V AC) with backup power for reliability.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A high intensity obstruction light is a freestanding unit rather than part of a constant-current series circuit, so its control architecture sits entirely within the structure it protects. Mains power (with battery or generator-backed UPS standing in automatically if it fails) feeds a central controller, which reads a photoelectric sensor to decide whether the site is in day or night conditions and sets the corresponding flash intensity and colour. That same controller is what generates the synchronised flash-timing signal distributed to every tier on the structure (covered opposite), so the apex, mid, and lower tiers all fire as one coherent signal rather than as independently timed lights. Fault supervision happens locally too: the controller monitors LED driver or flash-tube health and the status of the mains/backup power path, and where a remote monitoring system is connected, it reports status and alarms upstream for maintenance visibility. That reporting link is strictly one-way for safety purposes — the day/night and synchronisation logic keeps running locally even if the connection to the monitoring system is lost, since the light's warning function can't depend on a live network link.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is a rugged die-cast aluminium or corrosion-resistant alloy housing, engineered to support the high-power LED array or xenon flash tube and its associated driver electronics at surface- or pole-mounted locations on tall structures such as towers, masts, and turbines. A toughened, weather-sealed lens or dome, typically clear for the high-intensity white day flash and switchable or paired with a red filter for the reduced-intensity night mode, is gasket-sealed to the housing to achieve an IP65/IP67 rating against wind, rain, and dust at height. The internal driver and flash-control electronics are potted or conformal-coated and mounted on vibration-damped standoffs to withstand the mechanical loading imposed by wind-induced structural sway. A separate weatherproof junction box or integrated terminal compartment provides sealed cable entry for the mains AC supply and, where fitted, backup battery or generator connections. Lightning protection bonding and surge suppression are standard given the fixture's typically exposed, elevated mounting position, and corrosion-resistant fasteners and finishes are used throughout for extended outdoor service life.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

High Power Consumption and Cooling

High intensity lights generate significant heat, especially in LED systems. Inadequate heat sinking or ventilation can lead to premature LED failure or reduced light output. Proper thermal management is critical for reliability.

Lightning and Surge Protection

These lights are typically installed at the highest points of structures, making them extremely vulnerable to lightning strikes. Robust surge protection devices (SPDs) and lightning arrestors are essential to protect the electronics.

Photocell and Timing Failure

The day/night switching is critical. If the photocell fails, the light may operate in the wrong mode (e.g., white flashing at night), causing severe light pollution and potential pilot confusion. Regular inspection and backup control systems are recommended.

Lens Contamination and Cleaning

At high altitudes, lenses can accumulate dirt, ice, or bird droppings, which significantly reduce light output. Regular cleaning and de‑icing measures are necessary, especially in cold climates.

Synchronisation Failure

When multiple high intensity lights are installed on a structure (e.g., intermediate tiers), they must be synchronised. Failure can result in confusing or misleading flash patterns. Regular checks of the synchronisation system are essential.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Light Fails to Flash or Stays Dark
Likely Cause
Tripped thermal protection from inadequate heat sinking, or a failed driver module following a surge event.
Diagnostic Steps
Check driver fault indicators and internal temperature logs if available; inspect heat sink fins for dust blockage or airflow obstruction; megger the circuit for surge damage.
Corrective Action
Clear the heat sink and confirm ventilation is unobstructed; replace the driver module if surge-damaged and confirm SPDs are intact.
Incorrect Day/Night Mode or Erratic Switching
Likely Cause
Photocell failure or contamination is causing the controller to misread ambient light, triggering white flash mode at night or no operation by day.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare photocell output to a reference light meter; inspect the sensor lens for dirt or condensation; check backup control logic if fitted.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the photocell, verify calibration against ambient thresholds, and confirm the backup/override control operates correctly.
Lightning or Surge Damage to Electronics
Likely Cause
A direct or induced lightning strike has damaged the driver, controller, or synchronisation module despite installed protection.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect SPDs for indicated failure (blown indicator or open circuit); megger all circuits; check for physical strike damage at the mounting point.
Corrective Action
Replace failed SPDs and any damaged electronic modules; verify the lightning arrestor and grounding system meet resistance requirements before re-energising.
Loss of Synchronisation Between Tiers
Likely Cause
A GPS sync receiver fault or a controller clock drift has desynchronised flash timing between intermediate and top-tier lights.
Diagnostic Steps
Check GPS sync receiver lock status and signal strength; compare flash timing between tiers with a stopwatch or sync test tool.
Corrective Action
Reposition or replace the GPS receiver if it cannot achieve lock, or reset/replace the controller clock module, then re-verify synchronisation across all tiers.
Reduced Intensity or Uneven Flash Output
Likely Cause
Lens icing, bird droppings, or dirt accumulation at height is attenuating output, or gradual LED lumen depreciation has occurred.
Diagnostic Steps
Take a photometric reading and compare to the required minimum intensity; visually inspect the lens for contamination or ice buildup.
Corrective Action
Clean or de-ice the lens per the site's cold-weather procedure; if output remains low after cleaning, replace the LED array and re-test.

Safety & Operational Notes

Photobiological Hazard
Personnel Safety

Never look directly at the fixture at close range while energised; daytime output can reach 200,000 candela and cause temporary flash-blindness or discomfort well beyond a momentary glance.

Extreme-Height Access
Fall Protection

These units are typically mounted at 150 metres or higher; use certified climbing/rigging equipment and follow the tower owner's permit-to-work before ascending.

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the fixture supply and verify zero energy before opening the housing; the flash-tube or LED driver stage can retain stored energy briefly after power is removed.

Lightning / EMF Exposure
Electrical Safety

Treat the structure as lightning-prone at this height and avoid work during an active storm; be aware of co-located RF antennas and their EMF exposure limits.

Day/Night Mode Verification
Functional Check

After any maintenance, verify both the white daytime flash mode and the red or steady night mode switch correctly with the photocell/timer before leaving the site.

Aviation Authority Coordination
Operational

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Tier Sync Faults Are Easy to Miss From the Ground
Diagnostics

On a multi-tier structure, one tier flashing slightly out of synchronisation with the others is often invisible to a ground observer during the day but reads as a strobing, disorganised hazard marking at night. Verify multi-tier flash synchronisation with instrumentation at each service rather than relying on a visual check from below.

Day/Night Switching Failures Are Worse in This Mode Than Any Other
Photocell

A high-intensity light stuck in white daytime mode after dusk is not just non-compliant, it's genuinely disorienting to a pilot at night; conversely a unit stuck in red night mode during the day undersells the marking to a degree a medium-intensity light's failure doesn't. Treat photocell threshold drift on this fixture as a higher-priority fault than the same symptom on a lower-intensity light.

Lightning Bonding Needs Its Own Continuity Check, Not Just a Visual
Electrical

A lightning-protection bond that looks intact can have lost continuity internally from repeated strike current or corrosion at the connection, on a structure tall enough to be a preferred strike point. Test bonding continuity with a meter at scheduled intervals rather than relying on a visual inspection of the bracket alone.

Vibration-Damped Fixings Loosen Gradually, Not Suddenly
Mechanical

The damping designed to protect the fixture from structural sway also masks the early feel of a loosening fixing, so a mounting fault can progress a long way before it's obvious by hand-check alone. Torque-check fixings against the as-built spec on a fixed schedule rather than waiting for a visibly loose fitting.

The Central Controller Is a Single Point of Failure for the Whole Structure
Design

Where one central controller drives every tier's flash timing and mode switching, a controller fault can take the entire structure's marking dark or out of sync at once, unlike a fixture-by-fixture failure elsewhere in the site. Prioritise controller redundancy or a documented rapid-swap spare for structures where a single controller serves multiple tiers.

UPS Transfer Test Should Cover the Full Tier, Not Just One Light
Testing

Backup power on a tall structure typically feeds an entire tier or the whole marking system, so a transfer test that only confirms one fixture picks up backup power can miss a partial failure elsewhere on the same circuit. Verify the backup transfer restores every tier the UPS is meant to cover, not a single sample fixture.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about high intensity obstruction lights: when they are required, day and night modes, intensity, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a high intensity obstruction light?

A high intensity obstruction light is the most powerful type of aviation obstruction light, designed for structures taller than about 150 m (500 ft). It flashes bright white by day and switches to red at night so the obstacle can be seen from long distances.

Q2Which structures need high intensity obstruction lights?

The tallest structures, including skyscrapers over 150 m, broadcast masts, large chimneys and cooling towers, offshore wind turbines and transmission towers over 150 m. On extremely tall structures they are also used as intermediate lights.

Q3How bright is a high intensity obstruction light?

Daytime white intensity is typically 20,000–200,000 cd and night red intensity is 2,000–20,000 cd. In clear conditions the signal can be seen from more than 50 km away.

Q4Why does the light change from white to red?

White gives maximum contrast against a bright daytime sky, while red at a reduced night intensity avoids dazzling people on the ground in the dark. A photocell or twilight sensor switches between day, twilight and night modes, often GPS-synchronised in multi-unit installations.

Q5What is the flash rate of a high intensity obstruction light?

Typically 40–60 flashes per minute in both day and night modes, synchronised by GPS or photocell across multi-unit installations.

Q6What light sources and power do these lights use?

High-power LED arrays or xenon flash tubes, running from 110–230 V AC mains with battery or generator backup. Power consumption is roughly 200–500 W for LED versions, and LED life is typically 50,000–100,000 hours.

Q7How is the beam distributed?

The beam is omnidirectional with 360° horizontal coverage. The vertical beam angle is typically 3° to 10° above horizontal, wide enough to stay visible across typical approach descent angles.

Q8Which standards apply to high intensity obstruction lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment), FAA AC 70/7460-1 (obstruction marking and lighting) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED performance. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q9How often should a high intensity obstruction light be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for day/night photocell switching, flash rate and backup power tests, and annual for photometric intensity and seal and weatherproofing inspections. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does a high intensity obstruction light fail to flash or stay dark?

Common causes are tripped thermal protection from poor heat sinking, or a driver module damaged by a surge. Check driver fault indicators and heat sink fins for dust blockage, megger the circuit for surge damage, and confirm surge protection devices are intact.
