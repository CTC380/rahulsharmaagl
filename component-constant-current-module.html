Constant Current Module

A precision electronic module that provides a stable, regulated current to LED arrays or other current‑sensitive loads in AGL fixtures. Designed to maintain consistent light output despite variations in input voltage or temperature, ensuring reliable performance in demanding airfield environments.

Overview

What Is It?

A Constant Current Module is a dedicated electronic circuit that regulates the current flowing through an LED load to a predetermined, stable value, regardless of variations in the input voltage or changes in the load's characteristics (such as temperature‑induced resistance changes). Unlike a full LED driver which may include AC‑DC conversion and other functions, a constant current module is often a more compact, specialised sub‑circuit that can be integrated into a larger driver or LED PCB.

Key functions of a constant current module include:

  • Current Regulation: Maintaining a constant output current (e.g., 350 mA, 700 mA, 1 A) despite input voltage fluctuations (e.g., from a CCR or DC supply).
  • Thermal Compensation: Adjusting the current to prevent LED overheating or to compensate for temperature‑induced changes in LED forward voltage.
  • Protection: Incorporating over‑current, over‑voltage, and short‑circuit protection to safeguard the LEDs and the module itself.
  • Dimming Capability: In some designs, the module supports PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) or analogue dimming to adjust brightness in response to control signals.
  • High Efficiency: Minimising power loss to reduce heat generation and improve overall system efficiency.

Constant current modules are typically used in AGL fixtures where space is limited, or where a modular design approach is preferred, allowing for easy replacement or upgrade without redesigning the entire fixture.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The constant current module's purpose is to give an LED load exactly the current it needs to produce stable, predictable light output regardless of the swings in input voltage or temperature that would otherwise cause an LED's brightness to drift or its life to shorten. Because LEDs are current-driven devices whose forward voltage changes with temperature, a fixed voltage supply alone cannot reliably control their brightness — the constant current module's feedback loop is what makes it possible to compact this regulation function into a small sub-circuit that can be embedded directly on an LED PCB or integrated into a larger driver, enabling the space-constrained, multi-channel, or custom LED arrays used in AGL fixtures without requiring a full standalone driver for every LED string.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Constant current modules are used in a wide range of LED‑based AGL applications:

  • Runway and Taxiway Lights: In inset and elevated LED fixtures where space is at a premium, providing stable current to high‑power LEDs.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: In sequenced flashing lights and barrettes, where precise current control is needed for consistent intensity.
  • Obstruction Lights: In red or white aviation warning beacons, where reliability and long life are critical.
  • PAPI and VASI Units: In visual approach slope indicators, where the current must be matched to maintain colour‑transition accuracy.
  • Apron and Floodlights: In high‑power LED area lighting, where multiple modules may be used in parallel.
  • Stop Bars and Guard Lights: In linear LED arrays for visual guidance.

They are also found in:

  • Retrofit LED Kits: Replacing halogen lamps with LED modules that include a constant current module.
  • Custom LED Arrays: In prototype or specialised fixtures where off‑the‑shelf drivers are not suitable.
  • Multi‑Channel Lighting: In fixtures with independent colour channels (e.g., RGB or tunable white), each with its own constant current module.
Placement

Installation & Location

The module is mounted as a compact PCB or potted assembly inside the fixture housing (for an LED runway/taxiway light) or inside the CCR/driver cabinet (for larger installations), positioned close to the LED array or lamp it feeds so the current-carrying leads stay short. Where used inside a sealed fixture, the module is mounted on or near the fixture's heat sink so its own heat dissipation is managed alongside the LED's; where used in a control cabinet, it's DIN-rail or panel-mounted with its input and output terminals kept clearly separated to avoid confusing the regulated LED-side current with the incoming supply. No separate field cabling is involved beyond the fixture or cabinet's existing power leads.

Function

How Does It Work?

The constant current module operates through a feedback control loop:

  • Input Stage: The module accepts a DC input voltage (typically from a power supply or a rectified AC source). The input voltage range is usually wide (e.g., 12–48 V DC) to accommodate various power sources.
  • Current Sensing: A low‑value sense resistor (shunt resistor) is placed in series with the LED load. The voltage drop across this resistor is proportional to the load current.
  • Control IC: A dedicated current‑regulating IC (often a switching regulator or linear regulator) monitors the voltage across the sense resistor and adjusts the output to maintain a constant current. The IC may use a pulse‑width modulation (PWM) or linear control scheme.
  • Switching or Linear Regulation: In switching regulators (buck, boost, or buck‑boost), the IC rapidly switches a transistor on and off to control the average current through the inductor and load. Linear regulators dissipate excess power as heat, which is less efficient but can offer lower noise.
  • Protection Circuits: The module typically includes:
    • Over‑Current Protection (OCP): Limits the current to a safe level in case of a short circuit or component failure.
    • Over‑Voltage Protection (OVP): Prevents damage if the input voltage exceeds a safe threshold.
    • Thermal Shutdown: Reduces or shuts off the current if the module's temperature exceeds a safe limit.
  • Dimming Interface: Many constant current modules have a dimming input (PWM or 0–10 V analogue) that allows the output current to be adjusted, typically to match the ALCMS intensity steps.

The module is often potted or conformally coated to protect against moisture and vibration, and its compact design allows it to be placed close to the LEDs, minimising wiring losses.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The constant current module sits at the bottom of the airfield's control hierarchy, one level below the series-circuit CCR rather than in place of it. On a conventional series-circuit fixture, the CCR regulates the whole loop's current and the module has no role at all; the module appears instead in LED fixtures fed by a DC bus or a local AC/DC supply, where it is the component that actually turns a commanded intensity step into a precise, regulated current at the LED array. Its dimming interface — PWM or 0–10 V analogue — is what receives the ALCMS's intensity step once that step has been translated into a local signal, either by an upstream driver stage or by a smart fixture's own controller; the module itself has no visibility into RVR, ATC instructions, or loop-level fault reporting; it only ever sees a dimming command and a current it must hold constant. Its own feedback loop (current sense resistor → control IC → regulator) is entirely local and fast, closing in microseconds to hold the LED current steady regardless of input voltage variation — a much tighter and faster loop than the CCR's loop-level voltage monitoring, and one that never leaves the fixture or driver cabinet it's mounted in. Protection circuits (OCP, OVP, thermal shutdown) act autonomously at this local level too, so the module can shed load or shut down to protect itself without waiting for, or reporting through, the ALCMS.

Construction

Physical Construction

The module is a small surface-mount or through-hole PCB assembly, typically 20–60mm on its longest side, built around a switching or linear regulator IC with its inductor, sense resistor, and feedback network laid out to keep the current-sense path short and low-noise. Once assembled, the board is coated — conformal acrylic, silicone, or urethane spray for indoor/dry applications, or fully potted in epoxy resin for outdoor and submersible fixtures — to protect the solder joints and components from moisture, vibration, and thermal cycling across its -40°C to +85°C operating range. Mounting holes or clips fix it inside the fixture housing near the LED array it drives, close enough to keep the DC output leads short but positioned to avoid trapping heat against the LED's own thermal path.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Current Drift and Regulation Errors

Over time, the current regulation accuracy can drift due to ageing of components (e.g., sense resistor, control IC). This can cause light output to change, affecting system performance. Regular photometric checks and, if necessary, recalibration or replacement of the module are recommended.

Thermal Management Failure

Constant current modules generate heat, especially linear types. Inadequate heat sinking or poor thermal contact can cause overheating, leading to reduced efficiency or shutdown. Ensure the module is properly mounted with thermal interface material and that the fixture provides adequate cooling.

Voltage Spike Damage

Input voltage transients from the power supply (e.g., from a CCR or switching power supply) can damage the module's input stage. Installing a transient voltage suppressor (TVS) or surge protection diode at the module's input can help protect against these spikes.

Incorrect Dimming Signal

If the module supports dimming, applying an incorrect PWM frequency or voltage level can cause flicker or unstable light output. Ensure the dimming signal matches the module's specifications and that the wiring is properly shielded to prevent noise pickup.

Mechanical Damage and Moisture Ingress

The module is vulnerable to physical damage during handling or installation. Additionally, moisture can ingress through the potting or coating, causing corrosion. Handle with care and inspect for signs of damage or corrosion regularly.

Diagnostic Steps
Check for recent switching events, lightning activity, or supply anomalies logged by the CCR; inspect for visible damage on the module's input components.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged module and install a transient voltage suppressor or surge protection diode at its input to prevent recurrence.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Treat the Fixture as Live Until the Series Loop Is Confirmed Dead
Electrical Safety

Where the module sits inside a fixture fed from a CCR-derived DC bus, isolating the module's own low-voltage leads is not enough; lock out and verify zero energy on the parent series circuit first, since the fixture body and upstream wiring can still be at several kV relative to ground.

Cabinet Capacitor Discharge
Electrical Safety

Where the module is panel-mounted inside a CCR or driver cabinet, input and output capacitors can retain charge after the cabinet supply is switched off; follow the manufacturer's discharge procedure and confirm terminals are de-energised before handling.

ESD Precautions
Personnel Safety

The module is a compact PCB or potted assembly sensitive to electrostatic discharge; use standard ESD handling precautions (grounded wrist strap, antistatic mat) when removing or replacing it, particularly in dry conditions.

Heat Sink Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the module and its heat sink to cool before handling after an active period; the module is mounted to share heat dissipation with the LED array or driver, and both can remain hot well after power is removed.

Isolation Topology Awareness
Electrical Safety

Confirm whether the module is a non-isolated (buck) or isolated (flyback) design before connecting test equipment; a non-isolated module can share a common reference with the incoming supply, creating a shock or equipment-damage risk if test leads are grounded assuming isolation.

Protection Circuits Not to Be Bypassed
Compliance

Never bypass or defeat the module's over-current, over-voltage, or thermal-shutdown protection to work around a fault; doing so risks overdriving the LED array and can create a fire or component-failure hazard.

Verify Output Current Before Closing Out
Compliance

After replacement or repair, measure output current against the sense-resistor design value before returning the fixture to service; an incorrectly set current under-drives photometric output or over-drives and shortens LED life.

Loop-Level Monitoring Can't See Inside This Module
Troubleshooting

Because the module's feedback loop is entirely local, the ALCMS and CCR have no visibility into what it's actually doing — a fixture the loop reports as healthy can still have its module quietly current-limiting or thermally throttling. Verify actual LED output at the fixture rather than trusting loop-level status alone when brightness looks inconsistent between otherwise identical fixtures.

Repeated Protection-Circuit Trips Are Data, Not Just an Inconvenience
Reliability

If a module's OCP, OVP, or thermal shutdown keeps triggering, treat it as a symptom of an upstream problem — a marginal supply, poor heat sinking, a degrading LED array — rather than simply resetting it and moving on. The protection circuit is telling you something before it fails hard.

Know the Regulation Topology Before Judging Housing Temperature
Design

A linear-regulation module dissipates excess power as heat by design, while a switching (buck/boost) module runs cooler at the same output. Confirm which topology you're dealing with before treating a warm housing as evidence of a fault.

Confirm the Dimming Signal Type Matches What the Fixture Expects
Compatibility

PWM and 0–10V analogue dimming interfaces aren't interchangeable. A module swapped in with the wrong interface can appear to power the LED array fine at full brightness while failing to respond correctly at intermediate ALCMS steps.

Potting Protects Against Moisture, Not Against Stored Charge
Electrical Safety

A potted or conformally coated module can still hold input or output capacitance after power is removed. Treat it as live until confirmed otherwise, regardless of how sealed the housing looks.

Verify Output Current at More Than One Dimming Step
Commissioning

Because the module's job is to hold current constant across a dimming range, confirm regulation at a low intensity step as well as full brightness after replacement — a module that regulates well at 100% can still misbehave at the bottom of its range.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about constant current modules: how they work, how they differ from a CCR, dimming, standards, testing, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a constant current module in airfield ground lighting?

A constant current module is a compact electronic circuit that regulates the current flowing through an LED load to a set, stable value, regardless of variations in input voltage or temperature-induced changes in the load. Unlike a full LED driver, it usually does not include AC-DC conversion, and it is often integrated into a larger driver or onto the LED PCB of an AGL fixture.

Q2What is the difference between a constant current module and a CCR?

A Constant Current Regulator (CCR) regulates the current of the whole series circuit (loop), and on a conventional series-circuit fixture the module has no role at all. The module appears in LED fixtures fed from a DC bus or a local AC/DC supply, where it turns a commanded intensity step into a precise, regulated current at the LED array. It sits one level below the CCR rather than in place of it.

Q3How does a constant current module work?

It operates through a feedback control loop. A low-value sense (shunt) resistor in series with the LED load produces a voltage drop proportional to the load current, and a control IC monitors that voltage and adjusts a switching (buck, boost or buck-boost) or linear regulator to hold the current constant. Switching types are typically 85% efficient or better and run cooler, while linear types dissipate excess power as heat (typically 70% or better) but can offer lower noise. Built-in over-current, over-voltage and thermal-shutdown protection act locally.

Q4Where are constant current modules used in AGL?

They are used in LED-based AGL fixtures where space is limited or a modular design is preferred: runway and taxiway lights, approach lighting, obstruction lights, PAPI and VASI units, apron floodlights, and stop bars and guard lights. They also appear in retrofit LED kits, custom LED arrays and multi-channel fixtures where each channel has its own module.

Q5What are the typical specifications of a constant current module?

Typical values are an input of 12-48 V DC (or wider, such as 10-60 V DC), a regulated output current of 350 mA, 700 mA, 1 A or 1.5 A, and regulation accuracy of about ±2% to ±5% depending on design. Efficiency is 85% or better for switching types and 70% or better for linear types, with an ambient operating range of -40°C to +85°C. Dimming is by PWM (100 Hz to 1 kHz) or 0-10 V, and the board is conformally coated or potted.

Q6How is a constant current module dimmed, and does it talk to the ALCMS?

Many modules have a dimming input, either PWM or 0-10 V analogue, which receives the ALCMS intensity step once an upstream driver stage or the fixture's own controller has translated it into a local signal. The module itself has no direct ALCMS link: it only sees a dimming command and a current it must hold constant, and protection events such as OCP, OVP or thermal shutdown are handled locally. PWM and 0-10 V interfaces are not interchangeable.

Q7Which standards apply to constant current modules?

The main references are IEC 61347 (lamp controlgear, general and safety requirements), IEC 62384 (performance of DC or AC supplied electronic control gear for LED modules) and IPC-6012 (rigid PCB qualification, for modules built on PCBs). Always follow the manufacturer's isolation rating and wiring diagram, and verify the current edition of each standard.

Q8How should a constant current module be tested at commissioning?

Confirm correct regulation across the full rated DC input range, then measure output current against the sense-resistor design value and check the protection circuits (OCP, OVP and thermal shutdown) by simulating fault conditions. Verify the PWM or 0-10 V dimming response against the ALCMS intensity steps, measure output ripple and noise with an oscilloscope, run a thermal burn-in at rated load, and inspect the potting or conformal coating for voids or cracks. Check regulation at a low dimming step as well as full brightness, and record the results in the commissioning dossier.

Q9How often should a constant current module be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for output current verification, dimming signal verification, surge protection checks and enclosure and seal inspection, and semi-annual for thermal performance checks. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is the light output of an LED fixture slowly changing over time?

The usual cause is current regulation drift from ageing of the sense resistor or control IC. Measure the output current against the module's rated setpoint and compare the photometric output with the baseline commissioning data. Recalibrate the module if the drift is still within tolerance, or replace it if regulation accuracy has moved outside the acceptable band.

Q11Why does a constant current module run hot or shut down under load?

The most common cause is inadequate heat sinking or poor thermal contact between the module and the fixture body. Check the condition of the thermal interface material and the mounting torque, and measure the case temperature under normal load against the rated maximum. Re-apply the thermal interface material, correct the mounting, and improve fixture-side cooling if temperatures remain high. Also confirm whether the module is a linear type, which runs warm by design.

Q12Is it safe to work on a constant current module if its own leads are isolated?

No. Where the module sits inside a fixture fed from a CCR-derived DC bus, lock out and verify zero energy on the parent series circuit first, because the fixture body and upstream wiring can still be at several kV relative to ground. Cabinet-mounted modules can retain charge in their input and output capacitors, and potting does not prevent that. Confirm whether the module is isolated or non-isolated before connecting test equipment, use ESD precautions, and never bypass the over-current, over-voltage or thermal-shutdown protection.
