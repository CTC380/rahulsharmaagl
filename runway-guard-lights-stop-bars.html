Runway Guard Lights & Stop Bars

Runway Guard Lights and Stop Bars are critical visual safeguards used at runway/taxiway interfaces. They do not perform the same function: Runway Guard Lights provide a conspicuous visual warning of the presence of a runway, while Stop Bars provide a visual indication of a holding position and are integrated into the control of aircraft movement across that position. Their effectiveness depends on the complete system, not any single component.

Overview

What Are Runway Guard Lights and Stop Bars?

Runway Guard Lights and Stop Bars are airport visual aids associated with runway/taxiway intersections and runway holding positions. They support the prevention of unintended runway entry by providing additional visual information to pilots, and should be engineered as separate but coordinated safety functions.

Runway Guard Lights

Provide a conspicuous visual warning to pilots approaching a runway.

Stop Bars

Provide a controlled visual indication associated with a runway holding position.

Not the Same System

Different function, different behaviour — but part of one coordinated runway-safety architecture.

Their effectiveness depends on the complete system: Location → Configuration → Colour → Visibility → Electrical Supply → Control → Interlocking → Monitoring → Operational Response.

Why They Matter

Runway Incursion Prevention

Runway incursions can occur when an aircraft, vehicle or person enters or occupies a runway protected area without appropriate authorization. Visual safeguards provide an additional layer of protection — the engineering objective is Detect / Authorize / Warn / Hold / Control / Verify.

Lighting alone cannot replace ATC procedures, pilot situational awareness, vehicle driver procedures, runway markings, aerodrome operational controls, A-SMGCS where provided, or safety management procedures. Instead, these systems form part of a layered runway-safety architecture.

Colour & Function

Runway Guard Lights vs Stop Bars

Runway Guard Lights — Amber/Yellow

Use a conspicuous yellow/amber visual warning characteristic as specified by the applicable aerodrome standard. Colour verification matters because source degradation, optical ageing, incorrect replacement components and contamination can all affect colour.

Stop Bars — Red

A stop bar consists of a row of red lights associated with a runway holding position. Red provides a strong visual indication associated with the stop/hold function, and colour performance must be maintained throughout the equipment lifecycle.

Configuration

Runway Guard Light Configuration

Runway Guard Lights may be provided in different configurations depending on the runway/taxiway arrangement and applicable standard. The installed configuration must match the approved design.

Elevated Runway Guard Lights

May be installed where required by the design. The fixture must remain conspicuous without creating an unacceptable aircraft clearance hazard.

  • Mounting height & frangibility
  • Visibility & clearance
  • Orientation & foundation
  • Wind loading & mechanical stability
  • Cable connection

Inset Runway Guard Lights

Used where an elevated fixture is unsuitable or pavement installation is required. Inset fixtures require particular attention to pavement condition and water management.

  • Pavement installation & loading
  • Drainage & water ingress
  • Optical orientation & beam direction
  • Cable routing & transformer arrangement
  • Maintenance access

Flashing characteristics — where flashing is part of the approved configuration, verify flashing operation, sequence, synchronization where required, visibility, control response and failure indication. Do not treat a flashing light as acceptable merely because it produces intermittent illumination; the actual characteristic must conform to the applicable requirement.

Elevated vs Inset — Cross-Section

Elevated fixtures sit above the pavement on a frangible mount; inset fixtures are flush with the surface and depend on the pavement structure for support and drainage

Location & Layout

Stop-Bar Location & Configuration

Stop bars are installed at designated holding positions and may consist of multiple inset fixtures arranged transversely across the relevant taxiway. The configuration must provide sufficient visual recognition from the intended approach direction, and the exact location must follow the approved aerodrome design.

Location Engineering

  • Runway & taxiway geometry
  • Holding position marking
  • Aircraft clearance
  • Pilot eye position

Fixture Arrangement

  • Number of fixtures & spacing
  • Alignment & centreline relationship
  • Lateral position
  • Light beam orientation

Visibility Factors

  • Intensity & colour
  • Viewing angle
  • Pavement condition
  • Atmospheric & background lighting

Therefore: Stop Bar ON ≠ Stop Bar Visually Effective. A stop bar must be readily identifiable from the intended aircraft or vehicle approach direction — being commanded on is not the same as being seen correctly.

Interlocking

Interlocking & the Stop-Bar / Centreline Relationship

Interlocking is one of the most important engineering aspects of stop-bar systems. The control philosophy must prevent an unsafe combination of commands where the system design requires such protection — coordinating stop-bar status, taxiway centreline lighting, clearance to proceed, ATC command, associated routing and A-SMGCS or other operational systems.

Electrical Chain

Electrical Architecture

A typical AGL electrical chain for these fixtures runs from the CCR through the primary series cable, isolation transformer and secondary connector to the fixture itself. The exact configuration depends on the equipment and approved design, but every interface in the chain must be electrically compatible.

CCR Behaviour

Verify correct circuit current, correct intensity setting, stable operation, open-circuit response and fault response for both Runway Guard Lights and stop bars connected to a series circuit.

Isolation Transformers

Selection should consider fixture load, circuit characteristics, secondary requirements, environmental conditions, connector configuration and manufacturer requirements, coordinated with the fixture.

Cable & Connectors

Inspect the complete field connection — primary cable, primary connector, transformer, secondary connector and fixture connection. A stop-bar fault may originate in the field connection rather than the light fixture.

RGL / Stop-Bar Fixtures on the Series Circuit

RGL and stop-bar fixtures sit on the same constant-current loop as any other AGL fixture — each isolated through its own transformer and secondary connector

Inset Installation

Inset Fixture Installation & Water Ingress

Inset stop-bar and runway guard light installations require particular attention to pavement integration. Incorrect installation can affect both optical performance and pavement safety.

Pavement Interface

Inspect fixture elevation, seating, fasteners, joint condition, seal condition, pavement damage, drainage and foreign material. Poor pavement integration can cause mechanical damage and water ingress.

Water Ingress

Potential entry points include fixture seals, cable connections, transformer connections, pavement joints and damaged housings — leading to insulation deterioration, corrosion, driver failure, connector failure, intermittent lighting and earth faults.

Fixture alignment is critical: Approved Direction → Fixture Orientation → Optical Beam → Pilot Viewing Position. A fixture may be electrically healthy while producing insufficient visual performance because of incorrect alignment.

Verification

Electrical Testing vs Photometry

These are different verification activities, and both may be required depending on the applicable requirement.

ActivityWhat It Determines
Electrical TestingWhether the electrical system is functioning correctly — continuity, insulation resistance, circuit current, transformer testing, connector inspection, supply verification, functional and control testing.
PhotometryWhether the lighting system is producing the required optical performance — intensity, colour, beam alignment, distribution and uniformity where applicable, against the applicable current standard.
Electrical PASS ≠ Photometric PASS, and Photometric PASS ≠ Complete Electrical PASS. Both may be required for full verification.
Functional Testing

Stop-Bar & Runway Guard Light Functional Testing

Stop-Bar Functional Test

Command ON → Stop Bar Illuminates → Correct Red Output → Monitoring Indicates ON → Command OFF → Stop Bar Extinguishes → Monitoring Indicates OFF → Fault Simulation → Alarm/Status Verification.

Runway Guard Light Functional Test

Verify correct command, correct illumination, correct flashing characteristic where applicable, correct colour, correct intensity, correct monitoring and correct alarm response — from both electrical and operational perspectives.

Troubleshooting Philosophy

Three Layers of Investigation

A stop-bar fault should be investigated through three layers — this prevents a control-system indication from being mistaken for physical lighting performance.

Learn From the Field

Common Engineering Mistakes

01

Treating Them as One System

Runway Guard Lights and Stop Bars have different functions and should be engineered accordingly.

02

Illumination-Only Check

Checking only whether the fixtures illuminate, without verifying colour, alignment or control.

03

Ignoring Control Logic

Overlooking interlocking and control logic during inspection or troubleshooting.

04

Trusting ALCMS Alone

Assuming ALCMS status proves physical lighting performance without field verification.

05

Replace-First Habit

Replacing fixtures before checking the common circuit for shared-cause failures.

06

Skipping Operational Verification

Ignoring operational verification after electrical testing is complete.

07

Uncontrolled Logic Changes

Changing control logic without reviewing the approved operational philosophy.

08

Poor Documentation

Failing to document physical or control/software modifications.

Keyword Reference

Stop Bar Lights at an Airport

Stop bar lights airport

Stop bar lights at an airport are red lights across a taxiway at a holding position. When they are lit, the aircraft or vehicle must not cross onto the runway. They are switched with the controller's clearance and, where installed, work together with the green taxiway centreline lights beyond the bar. See the stop-bar configuration, control and interlocking sections of this page for how they are laid out and checked.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What are runway guard lights and stop bars?

They are visual aids associated with runway and taxiway intersections and runway holding positions, supporting the prevention of unintended runway entry. Runway guard lights provide a conspicuous visual warning of the presence of a runway, while stop bars provide a controlled visual indication of a holding position.

Q2What is the difference between runway guard lights and stop bars?

They are not the same system. Runway guard lights are a warning layer using a yellow or amber characteristic as specified by the applicable aerodrome standard. Stop bars are a red controlled holding layer, integrated with control of aircraft movement across the holding position. They are separate but coordinated safety functions.

Q3What are elevated and inset runway guard lights?

Elevated fixtures sit above the pavement on a frangible mount and must stay conspicuous without creating an unacceptable clearance hazard, considering mounting height, frangibility, visibility, orientation, foundation and wind loading. Inset fixtures are flush with the pavement and need particular attention to pavement condition, loading, drainage and water ingress. The installed configuration must match the approved design.

Q4How are stop bars located and configured?

Stop bars are installed at designated holding positions and may consist of multiple inset fixtures arranged transversely across the taxiway. The configuration must give sufficient visual recognition from the intended approach direction, and the exact location must follow the approved aerodrome design.

Q5Does a stop bar being ON mean it is effective?

No. A stop bar must be readily identifiable from the intended aircraft or vehicle approach direction, so being commanded on is not the same as being seen correctly. Intensity, colour, viewing angle, pavement condition and background lighting all affect visibility.

Q6How are stop bars controlled and monitored?

Control runs from ATC or operational command through the control system and interlocking logic to the stop-bar control and fixtures, with a reverse monitoring path from fixture or circuit status through monitoring and ALCMS to the operator. The design should let operators distinguish Commanded OFF from Failed OFF where the system supports it.

Q7Does ALCMS status prove a stop bar is compliant?

No. Monitoring confirms circuit and control state but does not replace optical or field verification.

Q8Why is interlocking important for stop bars?

Interlocking is one of the most important engineering aspects. The control philosophy must prevent an unsafe combination of commands where the design requires such protection, coordinating stop-bar status with taxiway centreline lighting and related control.

Q9Can lighting alone prevent runway incursions?

No. Lighting cannot replace ATC procedures, pilot situational awareness, vehicle driver procedures, runway markings, aerodrome operational controls, A-SMGCS where provided, or safety management procedures. These systems form part of a layered runway-safety architecture.

Q10What should be verified for flashing runway guard lights?

Where flashing is part of the approved configuration, verify flashing operation, sequence, synchronization where required, visibility, control response and failure indication. A light is not acceptable merely because it produces intermittent illumination; the characteristic must conform to the applicable requirement.
