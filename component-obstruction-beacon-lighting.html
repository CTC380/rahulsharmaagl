Obstruction & Beacon Lighting

The red warning lights that mark tall structures and the rotating airport beacon that identifies the field — two visual aids that operate outside the runway and taxiway lighting systems but are essential for airspace awareness and airport acquisition.

Overview

What Are They?

Obstruction lighting marks fixed structures that pose a hazard to aircraft — chimneys, broadcast masts, cooling towers, high-rise buildings, and even wind turbines. They are classified by intensity — low, medium and high — with the class chosen mainly by the height of the structure: low intensity up to about 45 m, medium intensity from 45 m to 150 m, and high intensity above 150 m or wherever an aeronautical study calls for it. Low-intensity units are red and usually steady-burning; medium intensity is either flashing white by day or flashing/steady red by night; high intensity is flashing white, stepped down sharply at twilight and again at night.

Airport beacons are the rotating or flashing lights that identify the airport itself. The standard civil land airport beacon produces alternating white and green flashes (white-green-white-green). A water aerodrome uses alternating white and yellow; a heliport uses a green–yellow–white sequence; a military airfield uses two quick white flashes between green flashes. Flash rate carries information too: beacons marking airports, landmarks and points on federal airways run at 24–30 flashes per minute, while heliport beacons run faster, at 30–45. The beacon is usually mounted on the control tower or a dedicated structure, visible from long range.

Unlike runway or taxiway lighting, obstruction and beacon systems are usually independently powered and locally controlled — mains-fed, with solar and battery options for remote sites — but they are still part of the overall visual aids suite and must meet the photometric and colour requirements of ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 6, or the corresponding FAA or national specifications. Note that obstruction lighting frequently sits outside the aerodrome boundary and outside the aerodrome operator’s control: the structure owner is normally responsible for it, which is what makes the reporting and notification arrangements around a failed light as important as the hardware.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Runway and taxiway lighting guides an aircraft that is already following a known, planned path; obstruction and beacon lighting exists for the opposite case — warning aircraft that are not necessarily headed anywhere near a runway about a fixed hazard, or an airport's presence, that they might otherwise not see in time. Obstruction lights on masts, towers, and tall structures have to be effective for any aircraft passing nearby regardless of its intended route, which is why they are designed for all-round, long-range visibility rather than aimed toward a specific approach direction. The airport beacon serves a related but distinct purpose: it lets a pilot identify and locate the airport itself from a distance, well before the runway's own lighting or markings are visible, using an internationally recognised colour pattern (white-green for civil land airports) so the signal is unambiguous regardless of which country or airfield the pilot is approaching. Running these systems independently of the runway/taxiway circuits — sometimes on solar power at remote sites — ensures that this hazard-warning and identification function keeps working even if the airfield's main lighting circuits are down.

Application

Where Are They Used?

Obstruction lighting is installed on any structure that penetrates the obstacle-limitation surfaces or is tall enough to be a hazard:

  • Low-intensity (red): Structures up to about 45 m, and as the intermediate levels of a layered scheme on taller structures. ICAO Type A is a minimum of 10 cd fixed red; Type B a minimum of 32 cd fixed red; Type E a flashing 32 cd red, which flashes in step with the other lights on the structure.
  • Medium-intensity (white or red): Structures from 45 m to 150 m. ICAO Type A is flashing white at 20,000 cd by day, reducing to 2,000 cd at night, 20–60 fpm; Type B is flashing red at 2,000 cd, 20–60 fpm; Type C is steady red at 2,000 cd. Dual A/B and A/C units switch white by day to red by night on a photocell.
  • High-intensity (white): Structures above 150 m, and cases where an aeronautical study requires it. ICAO Type A: 200,000 cd by day, 20,000 cd at twilight, 2,000 cd at night, flashing at 40–60 fpm. Type B (used for tower and catenary marking) is 100,000 cd by day with the same twilight and night steps.
  • FAA equivalents: L-810 low-intensity red; L-864 flashing red (20–40 fpm); L-865 medium-intensity flashing white (40 fpm); L-856/L-857 high-intensity flashing white (40 and 60 fpm respectively).
  • Aerodrome beacon: Mounted at the highest practical unobstructed point — control tower roof, dedicated mast or tip-down pole — alternating white and green for a lighted land aerodrome, 24–30 flashes per minute.
  • Heliport and water aerodrome beacons: A heliport beacon shows a green–yellow–white sequence at 30–45 flashes per minute; a water aerodrome shows alternating white and yellow; a military airfield shows two quick white flashes between green flashes.

Where a structure carries several flashing lights they are synchronised to flash together, so the outline and height of the obstacle read as one object rather than as unrelated lights. Intermediate levels matter as much as the top light: ICAO caps the spacing between levels at 105 m where medium-intensity Type A lights are used, and at 52 m where Type B lights alternate with low-intensity Type B.

Placement

Installation & Location

Obstruction lights are mounted at the top of the structure and, on taller masts and towers, at intermediate levels as well, so the outline and height of the hazard remains visible from any direction rather than just marked at its highest point; on structures using a layered intensity scheme, the level of each light is set by the applicable obstacle-marking standard rather than convenience of access. Fixtures are fixed directly to the structure's own steelwork or a dedicated mounting bracket, with power and control cabling run inside or alongside the structure back to a control unit that may be independently powered — sometimes with a solar panel and battery at remote, hard-to-wire sites — rather than tied into the airfield's own AGL circuits. The airport beacon itself is installed at the highest practical, unobstructed point on the airfield, typically the control tower roof or a dedicated mast built for the purpose, mounted so its rotating or flashing beam has a clear 360° sweep with nothing on the structure itself blocking the light in any direction. Because these systems are frequently on independent power and control, their installation includes commissioning the local control unit's day/night switching (typically a photocell) separately from the main AGL circuit switching used for runway and taxiway lighting.

Function

How Do They Work?

Obstruction lights use incandescent or halogen lamps, xenon discharge tubes for high-intensity strobes, or — now the default for new and retrofit work — high-brightness LEDs. The flash pattern is produced by a control unit that either switches the lamp supply or fires a high-voltage trigger pulse into a discharge tube. Colour comes from a red filter on a white source, or directly from monochromatic red emitters in an LED unit, which is why LED units avoid the filter-fade and filter-cracking failures that older filtered fixtures suffer.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Colour & intensity classification: ICAO and the FAA both define low, medium and high intensity classes, each with its own type variants, photometric minima and beam spread requirements. Class selection follows structure height and the finding of any aeronautical study, not the installer’s preference.
  • Flash rate: Medium-intensity units flash at 20–60 fpm under ICAO (FAA L-864 red is 20–40 fpm, L-865 white is 40 fpm); high-intensity units at 40–60 fpm. All flashing lights on one structure, and normally across a group of structures, flash in unison.
  • Day / twilight / night switching: Switching is three-state, not two. A photocell facing the northern (polar) sky drives the step change: full intensity by day, a reduced twilight step, and a much lower night step — 200,000 / 20,000 / 2,000 cd for a high-intensity Type A unit. Dual units also change colour, white by day to red by night. Getting the twilight step wrong is a common commissioning miss.
  • Power supply: Normally a dedicated mains supply at the structure rather than the airfield series circuit; solar LED units with battery storage are used where cabling a remote mast is impractical. A series-circuit feed, where used, goes through an isolation transformer and a circuit adapter.
  • Beacon rotation: A rotating beacon carries a two-sided optical assembly — for a civil land aerodrome, one clear lens and one green lens 180° apart — turning at constant speed so an observer sees alternating white and green flashes at 24–30 per minute. Strobe and LED beacons produce the same apparent flash rate and colour sequence electronically, with no moving parts.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

An obstruction light or beacon's control logic is local and self-contained rather than networked back to ALCMS in most installations. A control unit generates the required flash pattern — steady-burning for low-intensity red, or a timed flash sequence for medium/high-intensity units, firing a high-voltage pulse for xenon strobes — and where multiple units share a structure, a synchronisation signal keeps their flashes aligned so aircraft see a single coordinated pattern rather than random flickering. A photoelectric cell, sighted at the northern (polar) sky so it is not fooled by direct sun or by nearby lighting, feeds ambient light level back into the control unit, which steps the fixture through its day, twilight and night levels — and, on dual units, from white to red — without operator intervention. Published switching thresholds exist and should be verified rather than assumed: a single L-810 type unit, for instance, turns on when ambient light falls to no less than 35 foot-candles and off when it rises to no more than 60, and many modern units include a self-monitoring circuit that detects lamp or LED failure and reports it — via a dry contact, a wireless link, or a visible local indicator — to maintenance or the ALCMS where such an interface exists.

Construction

Physical Construction

Obstruction beacon fixtures are built around a lamp or LED source housed within a weatherproof, typically die-cast aluminium or polycarbonate enclosure, with a hardened glass or polycarbonate dome or lens sealed to the housing to achieve an IP65/IP66 rating against rain, dust, and UV exposure at the beacon's typically elevated, exposed mounting location. High-intensity strobe-type beacons use a xenon flash tube or high-output LED array driven by a capacitor discharge or solid-state driver circuit housed in a separate weatherproof enclosure at or near the beacon head, connected by heavy-gauge cable rated for the beacon's peak power demand. Rotating beacon designs incorporate a motor-driven optical assembly within a sealed housing — for a civil land aerodrome, one clear and one green lens 180° apart — with a low-friction bearing supporting the rotating element and slip rings carrying current to the rotating light source. The optical system is designed to put its useful beam roughly 1–10° above the horizon, though the flash remains visible well above and below that band. The mounting bracket or pole attaching the beacon to the structure it marks, whether a chimney, mast, or building rooftop, is corrosion-resistant steel or aluminium engineered to withstand the wind loading and vibration typical of an elevated structure-top installation, with cabling routed through weatherproof conduit down to the power supply and any photocell or timer control gear at the base.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Structure-Top Mounting
Mechanical

Fix the bracket or pole to the structure (chimney, mast, or rooftop) with fasteners rated for the site's wind loading and vibration, since obstruction beacons are typically mounted at exposed, elevated locations.

Cable Route Down-Structure
Electrical

Route cabling through weatherproof conduit from the beacon head down to the power supply and control gear, using heavy-gauge cable rated for the beacon's peak power demand on strobe-type units.

Power Feed Configuration
Electrical

Confirm whether the unit is fed from the AGL series circuit (6.6A), independent 230V AC mains, or solar/battery, since obstruction and beacon systems are often independently powered rather than tied to the runway circuit.

Photocell/Timer Control Wiring
Electrical

Wire the photocell or timer control gear at the base so day/night intensity switching operates correctly once energised.

Flash Synchronisation Setup
Configuration

Where multiple units mark the same structure or airport, configure GPS or wired flash synchronisation so all units flash in unison rather than independently.

Enclosure & Dome Sealing
Mechanical

Confirm the lens/dome seal and driver enclosure gaskets are intact before closing up, since the beacon's elevated location leaves it fully exposed to rain, dust, and UV.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record mounting position, power feed configuration, and sync setup as-built before the unit is first energised.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the beacon's supply circuit before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of first switch-on.

Photocell / Timer Day-Night Switching Verification
Energisation

Cover and uncover the photocell to confirm the unit steps correctly through all of its modes — day, twilight and night, and white-to-red on dual fixtures. Test all three states: a unit that switches day-to-night correctly can still sit at the wrong twilight level, which is the step most often missed at commissioning.

Flash Rate & Synchronisation Verification
Energisation

Measure flash rate against the range specified for that light type (medium intensity 20–60 fpm under ICAO, 20–40 fpm for FAA L-864; high intensity 40–60 fpm; aerodrome beacon 24–30 fpm) and, for multi-unit installations, confirm GPS or wired synchronisation holds every unit in phase.

Beam Pattern & Rotation Verification
Final Acceptance

For rotating beacons, confirm smooth constant-speed rotation, the correct colour sequence, and that the beam clears every surrounding obstruction through the full 360° with its useful spread around 1–10° above the horizon; for fixed and strobe units, confirm the beam covers the required sector and vertical spread.

Photometric Intensity Test
Final Acceptance

Measure candela output with a calibrated photometer against the applicable ICAO/FAA low-, medium-, or high-intensity threshold for the beacon's classification.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, switching, flash-rate, and photometric readings alongside as-built beacon position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Intensity / Candela Output Check
Annual

Verify output against the rated intensity for the unit's class (low, medium, or high) using a calibrated photometer, since degraded output reduces obstacle visibility.

Flash Rate & Synchronisation Check
Semi-annual

Confirm flash rate stays within the range specified for that light type — 20–60 fpm for medium intensity under ICAO, 40–60 fpm for high intensity, 24–30 fpm for an aerodrome beacon — and that GPS or wired sync keeps multiple units in phase where required.

Lens / Dome Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean the lens or dome of dust, insect debris, and film build-up so light output and colour stay within spec.

Photocell / Timer Verification
Annual

Test day/night intensity switching against ambient light levels to confirm the photocell or timer transitions correctly.

Rotating Mechanism Check
Annual

On rotating beacon units, inspect the drive motor, bearings, and gearing for wear or noise that could indicate developing mechanical failure.

Seal & Enclosure Inspection
Annual

Check housing seals and gaskets for cracking or compression set to keep the unit weatherproof, particularly on elevated structure-mounted beacons.

Power Supply Check
Annual

Verify supply voltage and the integrity of the dedicated supply on mains-fed units, or battery state of health, panel output and charge controller condition on solar-powered 12/24 V DC units. Confirm the supply is still genuinely independent of the airfield lighting circuits if the design intended it to be.

Mounting & Structural Check
Annual

Inspect the mounting bracket or mast interface for corrosion or loosened fasteners, especially on beacons mounted atop tall structures exposed to wind loading.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lamp / LED Failure

Obstruction lights operate continuously and are often in harsh environments. Lamp burnout or LED driver failure is the most common issue. Regular visual inspection is the primary maintenance strategy.

Solar System Battery Degradation

Solar-powered units are common on remote towers. Battery life is typically 3–5 years; degraded batteries can cause intermittent operation or failure to maintain night-time flash patterns.

Lens Soiling / Bird Droppings

Obstruction lights attract birds and are exposed to dust and pollution. Soiled lenses can reduce intensity below the required standard, often without being obvious to ground observers.

Flash Pattern Drift / Asynchrony

When multiple units on a tall structure are not synchronised, the obstacle's shape is less visible. GPS sync modules can fail, requiring replacement or reconfiguration.

Beacon Motor Failure

Traditional rotating beacons have mechanical motors and belts that can seize, causing the beacon to stop rotating. This is a critical failure requiring urgent attention.

Photocell Misadjustment

If the day/night switching photocell is misaligned, dirty, or sited where it sees artificial light, the system may sit in the wrong mode — full white intensity at night, which produces serious glare complaints from residents and can prompt an enforced shutdown, or the night step during the day, which leaves the obstacle effectively unmarked. A unit stuck at the twilight step in either direction is the version of this fault most likely to go unreported.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Beacon Dark or Not Flashing
Likely Cause
Lamp burnout or LED driver failure, the most common failure mode given continuous operation in harsh environments.
Diagnostic Steps
Check lamp continuity or LED driver output voltage, inspect for a visibly broken filament or driver fault indicator, and confirm supply voltage is present at the fixture.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp or LED driver/module promptly given the critical safety function, and log the failure for trending against expected service life.
Intermittent Operation or No Flash at Night (Solar Units)
Likely Cause
Solar system battery degradation — batteries typically last 3–5 years and lose capacity to sustain night-time flash patterns as they age.
Diagnostic Steps
Check battery voltage and capacity against specification, review the battery's age against the expected 3–5 year service life, and inspect the solar panel and charge controller for correct operation.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded battery, and inspect/clean the solar panel to ensure full charging capacity is restored.
Beacon Intensity Below Required Standard Despite Working Lamp
Likely Cause
Lens soiling from dust, pollution, or bird droppings reducing light output without being obvious from the ground.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a close inspection or photometric check of the lens, and compare against the last cleaning date in the maintenance log.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with appropriate non-abrasive materials, and increase cleaning frequency in high-soiling locations (e.g., near bird roosts or industrial pollution).
Obstacle Shape Not Clearly Defined at Night (Multi-Unit Structures)
Likely Cause
Flash pattern drift or asynchrony between units on a tall structure, often from a failed GPS sync module.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the flash sequence across all units on the structure, check the GPS sync module's lock status and signal reception at each unit.
Corrective Action
Replace or reconfigure the failed GPS sync module, and re-verify synchronised flashing across all units on the structure.
Beacon Stops Rotating (Traditional Rotating Types)
Likely Cause
Beacon motor or drive belt seizure — a critical failure requiring urgent attention.
Diagnostic Steps
Manually check for free rotation, inspect the motor for seizure or overheating, and check the drive belt for wear, slippage, or breakage.
Corrective Action
Replace the seized motor or worn/broken belt, and treat as an urgent repair given the loss of the obstruction warning function.
Beacon Operates in the Wrong Mode (Glare at Night or Weak Signal by Day)
Likely Cause
Photocell misadjustment, misalignment, contamination, or siting where it sees artificial light, causing the unit to select the wrong day/twilight/night step.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the photocell for dirt or physical misalignment, and test day/night switching against actual ambient light levels.
Corrective Action
Clean and realign the photocell, or replace it if it fails to switch correctly, and re-verify correct mode operation at dawn and dusk.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Lamp or LED Driver Burnout
High Severity
Root Cause
Continuous operation in harsh environments accelerates normal end-of-life wear on the lamp filament or LED driver electronics.
Effect
Total loss of the obstruction warning signal, removing a critical safety indication to aircraft in the vicinity of the structure.
Detection Method
Lamp continuity or LED driver output voltage checks, and confirmation that supply voltage is present at the fixture during scheduled inspection.
Solar Battery Capacity Loss
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Normal battery ageing over the typical 3–5 year service life, sometimes accelerated by a soiled solar panel reducing charge input.
Effect
Beacon fails to sustain its night-time flash pattern through a full cycle, weakening or losing the warning signal after dark.
Detection Method
Battery voltage and capacity checks against specification, tracked against battery age and cross-checked with solar panel and charge controller condition.
Lens Soiling
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Accumulated dust, pollution, or bird droppings on the lens, often in locations near bird roosts or industrial sources.
Effect
Reduced light output that is not obvious from the ground, quietly narrowing the beacon's effective visibility range.
Detection Method
Close visual inspection or photometric check of the lens, compared against the last recorded cleaning date.
GPS Sync Module Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
GPS sync module loses lock or fails on a structure fitted with multiple beacon units requiring synchronised flashing.
Effect
Flash pattern drifts out of sync across units on the structure, degrading the clarity of the obstruction warning presented to pilots.
Detection Method
Visual observation of the flash sequence across all units, and a check of the GPS sync module's lock status and signal reception.
Beacon Motor or Drive Belt Seizure
High Severity
Root Cause
Motor bearing wear or belt fatigue over time, sometimes accelerated by lack of lubrication or exposure to the elements.
Effect
Rotating beacon stops sweeping entirely, an urgent loss of the obstruction warning function.
Detection Method
Manual check for free rotation, inspection of the motor for seizure or overheating, and a check of the drive belt for wear, slippage, or breakage.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Working at Height
Personnel Safety

Use fall protection and follow the structure owner's climb/access procedure when servicing a beacon mounted on a tower, mast, or building; obstruction beacons are typically located at the highest and least accessible points of the structure they mark.

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the beacon's supply circuit before opening the housing or replacing a lamp/driver, and where a battery is fitted, isolate or disconnect it as well, since the battery can continue to power the fixture even with mains supply removed.

Lock Out Rotating Beacons
Mechanical

On rotating beacon units, isolate and lock out the drive motor before reaching into the mechanism, and never attempt to manually stop or free a rotating beam by hand; a seized motor or belt can release suddenly once obstruction is cleared.

Notify Before Taking a Beacon Out of Service
Operational

Notify the relevant aviation authority and issue a NOTAM before de-energising an obstruction beacon for maintenance, since the beacon is the primary warning to aircraft that the structure exists; restore and verify the light before cancelling the notice.

Solar Battery Handling
Electrical Safety

Handle the sealed battery pack per the manufacturer's guidance and check for swelling, heat, or leakage before servicing; replace a battery nearing the end of its typical 3–5 year service life proactively rather than waiting for a night-time failure.

Verify Sync Across All Units After GPS Work
Compliance

After replacing or resetting a GPS sync module, observe the flash sequence across every beacon on the structure to confirm they remain synchronised, since a drifted unit degrades the clarity of the warning even while each individual light appears to function normally.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on power or battery connections, and confirm the fixture and mounting structure are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting supply.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a beacon at reduced intensity, out of sync, or otherwise non-compliant after maintenance; verify light output and, where applicable, rotation and synchronisation before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Flash Synchronisation Drift Compounds Across a Chain of Towers
Design

On a chain of obstruction beacons along a ridge or power line, small timing drift in each unit's controller can accumulate into a visibly ragged, unsynchronised flash pattern across the whole chain, even though each beacon meets its individual flash-rate tolerance. Verify synchronisation as a system, not unit by unit.

Photocell Calibration Drifts With Lens Ageing, Not Just Dirt
Maintenance

A photocell that once reliably switched between day and night modes can start switching early or late as its own sensing lens yellows with age, separate from any dust or dirt accumulation. Recalibrate or replace the photocell on a service interval rather than assuming a cleaning will restore accurate switching.

Structural Vibration Fatigues LED Modules Faster Than Static Mounting Suggests
Mechanical

Beacons mounted on tall lattice towers or guyed masts experience continuous low-amplitude vibration from wind that a bench-tested fixture never sees. Solder joints and connector crimps can fatigue and fail well ahead of a module's rated life; factor mounting location into replacement interval planning, not just hours of operation.

Solar Sizing Needs a Worst-Case Cloud-Cover Run, Not Average Insolation
Electrical

Standalone solar-powered beacons sized against average annual insolation can run down during an extended overcast stretch in the worst season at that latitude. Size battery capacity and panel area against a worst-case consecutive low-light period, not an annual average.

GPS-Synced Units Need a Documented Fallback Behaviour
Reliability

Where beacon synchronisation depends on GPS timing, define and verify what each unit does on GPS signal loss — free-run at last known rate, or a documented fallback pattern — rather than discovering the failure behaviour for the first time during an actual outage.

Colour and Flash-Rate Standards Differ by Jurisdiction — Confirm, Don't Assume
Compliance

Required beacon colour and flash characteristics can differ between ICAO Annex 14 guidance and a given state's civil aviation authority rules, and between day and night requirements at the same site. Confirm the applicable standard for the specific jurisdiction and structure height rather than carrying over a spec from a different project.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the Obstruction & Beacon Lighting: what it is, how it works, typical specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is Obstruction & Beacon Lighting?

Obstruction lighting marks fixed structures that pose a hazard to aircraft — chimneys, broadcast masts, cooling towers, high-rise buildings, and even wind turbines. They are classified by intensity — low, medium and high — with the class chosen mainly by the height of the structure: low intensity up to about 45 m, medium intensity from 45 m to 150 m, and high intensity above 150 m or wherever an aeronautical study calls for it.

Q2What is the purpose of Obstruction & Beacon Lighting?

Runway and taxiway lighting guides an aircraft that is already following a known, planned path; obstruction and beacon lighting exists for the opposite case — warning aircraft that are not necessarily headed anywhere near a runway about a fixed hazard, or an airport's presence, that they might otherwise not see in time.

Q3Where is Obstruction & Beacon Lighting used and installed?

Obstruction lighting is installed on any structure that penetrates the obstacle-limitation surfaces or is tall enough to be a hazard: Low-intensity (red): Structures up to about 45 m, and as the intermediate levels of a layered scheme on taller structures. ICAO Type A is a minimum of 10 cd fixed red; Type B a minimum of 32 cd fixed red; Type E a flashing 32 cd red, which flashes in step with the other lights on the structure.

Q4How does Obstruction & Beacon Lighting work?

Obstruction lights use incandescent or halogen lamps, xenon discharge tubes for high-intensity strobes, or — now the default for new and retrofit work — high-brightness LEDs. The flash pattern is produced by a control unit that either switches the lamp supply or fires a high-voltage trigger pulse into a discharge tube.

Q5What are the typical technical parameters of Obstruction & Beacon Lighting?

Typical values are — Low-intensity, ICAO Type A / B: ≥ 10 cd / ≥ 32 cd, fixed red. Type E: ≥ 32 cd flashing red, in step with other lights on the structure; Medium-intensity, ICAO Type A: Flashing white — 20,000 cd day and twilight, 2,000 cd night; 20–60 fpm; Medium-intensity, ICAO Type B / C: Type B: flashing red 2,000 cd, 20–60 fpm. Type C: steady red 2,000 cd; High-intensity, ICAO Type A: Flashing white — 200,000 cd day / 20,000 cd twilight / 2,000 cd night; 40–60 fpm; High-intensity, ICAO Type B: Flashing white — 100,000 cd day / 20,000 cd twilight / 2,000 cd night; 40–60 fpm (towers, catenary marking).

Q6What are the electrical requirements for Obstruction & Beacon Lighting?

Typical requirements are — Power Input: Dedicated mains supply (typically 120/230 V AC) at the structure, or 12/24 V DC from a solar-battery set at remote sites. A series-circuit feed is the exception rather than the norm and requires an isolation transformer plus circuit adapter; Supply Independence: Kept off the airfield AGL circuits so the obstacle warning survives a runway/taxiway lighting outage; verify which board and which changeover arrangement actually feeds it rather than assuming; Cable Type: Direct-burial or riser mains cable in weatherproof conduit up the structure (L-824 series cable only where series-loop fed).

Q7What photometric requirements apply to Obstruction & Beacon Lighting?

Typical requirements are — Measured Quantity: Effective intensity for flashing lights — the intensity of a steady light of the same colour giving the same visual range under identical conditions — determined by the Blondel–Rey relationship, not by instantaneous peak candela; Low-intensity (red): ≥ 10 cd (ICAO Type A) or ≥ 32 cd (Type B / E); fixed, or flashing in step with the structure’s other lights; Medium-intensity (white, Type A): 20,000 cd day / 20,000 cd twilight / 2,000 cd night, flashing 20–60 fpm; Medium-intensity (red, Type B / C): 2,000 cd flashing (Type B, 20–60 fpm) or 2,000 cd steady (Type C).

Q8Which standards apply to Obstruction & Beacon Lighting?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, Vol I — Ch. 6 — Visual aids for denoting obstacles: light types, intensities, flash rates and placement, with characteristics tabulated in Appendix 2 and colours in Appendix 1. Aerodrome beacons are covered in Chapter 5; ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4 — Aerodrome Design Manual, Visual Aids — design and siting guidance for obstacle lighting and beacons; FAA AC 70/7460-1 — Obstruction Marking and Lighting — the U.S. siting standard: which structures are lit, with what, and at which levels; FAA AC 150/5345-43 — Specification for Obstruction Lighting Equipment — L-810, L-864, L-865, L-856/L-857 photometrics, flash rates and qualification; FAA AC 150/5345-12 — Specification for Airport and Heliport Beacons — L-801A/H/S and L-802A/H/M/S types and classes; FAA AC 150/5340-30 — Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids — beacon siting, mounting and installation; EASA CS-ADR-DSN — Certification specifications for aerodrome design — obstacle lighting and aerodrome beacon requirements for EASA-regulated aerodromes; DGCA CAR Section 4 — Indian requirements for obstacle marking and lighting (Aerodrome Standards and Air Traffic Services), applied alongside the relevant AAI and BIS specifications. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should Obstruction & Beacon Lighting be inspected and maintained?

Typical maintenance tasks and intervals are: Intensity / Candela Output Check (annual); Flash Rate & Synchronisation Check (semi-annual); Lens / Dome Cleaning (quarterly); Photocell / Timer Verification (annual); Rotating Mechanism Check (annual); Seal & Enclosure Inspection (annual); Power Supply Check (annual); Mounting & Structural Check (annual). Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10How do I troubleshoot common Obstruction & Beacon Lighting faults?

Common symptoms covered are: Beacon Dark or Not Flashing; Intermittent Operation or No Flash at Night (Solar Units); Beacon Intensity Below Required Standard Despite Working Lamp; Obstacle Shape Not Clearly Defined at Night (Multi-Unit Structures); Beacon Stops Rotating (Traditional Rotating Types); Beacon Operates in the Wrong Mode (Glare at Night or Weak Signal by Day). For example, for "Beacon Dark or Not Flashing": Likely Cause: Lamp burnout or LED driver failure, the most common failure mode given continuous operation in harsh environments. Diagnostic Steps: Check lamp continuity or LED driver output voltage, inspect for a visibly broken filament or driver fault indicator, and confirm supply voltage is present at the fixture. Corrective Action: Replace the failed lamp or LED driver/module promptly given the critical safety function, and log the failure for trending against expected service life.
