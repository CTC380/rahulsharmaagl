Wireless Sensor

A compact, battery-powered or energy-harvesting device that monitors critical parameters of AGL equipment and transmits data wirelessly to an IoT Gateway or cloud platform. Wireless sensors enable cost-effective, scalable condition monitoring of airfield lighting systems without the need for extensive cabling.

Overview

What Is It?

A Wireless Sensor is a self-contained monitoring device that measures physical or electrical parameters (current, voltage, temperature, vibration, humidity, etc.) and transmits the data over a wireless network (LoRa, Zigbee, NB-IoT, Wi-Fi, or Bluetooth). In AGL applications, these sensors are deployed on:

  • Lighting Fixtures: Monitoring current, temperature, and lamp status to detect failures early.
  • Isolating Transformers: Measuring primary and secondary current, and winding temperature.
  • CCRs and Power Equipment: Monitoring load current, ambient temperature, and vibration.
  • Cable Systems: Detecting current imbalance or earth leakage using clamp-on current sensors.
  • Environmental Conditions: Tracking ambient temperature, humidity, and precipitation near critical equipment.

Wireless sensors reduce installation costs, enable retrofitting of existing infrastructure, and provide real-time visibility into the health of AGL systems, supporting predictive maintenance and operational efficiency.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The wireless sensor's purpose is to extend the same current, temperature, or vibration monitoring available on smart fixtures and transformers to equipment and locations where running new cabling would be impractical or prohibitively expensive — making retrofit monitoring of existing airfield infrastructure genuinely feasible rather than something that only applies to newly installed equipment. Its long battery life and low-power wireless transmission exist specifically so it can be installed and largely forgotten for years, providing the same early-warning visibility into equipment health as a wired sensor without requiring trenching, conduit, or a hard-wired power supply. This is what allows an airport to add condition monitoring incrementally across its existing lighting circuits rather than needing a wholesale infrastructure upgrade to gain that visibility.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Wireless sensors are deployed across the airfield where monitoring is needed but cabling is impractical:

  • Runway and Taxiway Lighting: Monitoring individual edge, centreline, and threshold lights for current and temperature anomalies.
  • Isolating Transformers: Monitoring transformer health to predict failures before they cause circuit outages.
  • CCR Rooms: Environmental monitoring (temperature, humidity) of equipment shelters.
  • Approach Lighting: Monitoring barrette and flashing light status for approach systems.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Ensuring beacons and obstruction markers are operational.
  • PAPI and VASI Systems: Verifying correct lamp current and tilt angle.
  • Remote Equipment: Monitoring wind cones, VDGS, and other standalone systems.

Sensors are typically mounted inside or adjacent to the equipment being monitored, with antennas positioned for optimal wireless coverage.

Placement

Installation & Location

The sensor is fixed to (or placed directly inside) the fixture, transformer, or shelter it monitors, using adhesive, a magnetic base, or a small bracket, positioned so its measurement point (current clamp, temperature probe, or humidity element) sits in direct contact with or close proximity to what it's sensing. Because there's no cabling to a control room, siting is driven mainly by radio performance: the unit is placed to keep line-of-sight or minimal obstruction to the nearest gateway, clear of dense metal enclosures that would attenuate the signal, and a coverage check is carried out at installation before the sensor is sealed into its final position. Battery-powered units are sited for reasonable access at replacement intervals, and the sensor's location and parent asset are registered on the monitoring platform so its readings map to the correct piece of equipment.

Function

How Does It Work?

A wireless sensor operates through a combination of sensing, processing, and communication. The device includes:

  • Transducer/Sensor Element: Converts the physical parameter (current, temperature, vibration) into an electrical signal (e.g., Hall effect for current, thermistor for temperature).
  • Signal Conditioning: Amplifies, filters, and digitizes the signal using an analog-to-digital converter (ADC).
  • Microcontroller: Processes the data, performs local calculations, and determines if alerts need to be triggered.
  • Wireless Transceiver: Transmits the data using a low-power protocol (LoRa, Zigbee, NB-IoT, etc.) to a nearby IoT Gateway or directly to the cloud.
  • Power Source: Typically a long-life battery (3-10 years) or energy harvesting (solar, inductive) to ensure maintenance-free operation.
  • Enclosure: Rugged, weatherproof housing (IP67/IP68) to withstand airfield environments.

The sensor typically operates in a low-duty cycle mode, waking up periodically (e.g., every 5 minutes) to take a measurement, transmit data, and then returning to deep sleep to conserve battery. Some sensors also support event-driven transmission when a threshold is exceeded.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Battery life over years, not raw performance, is what shapes this device's control logic. For most of its life the MCU sits in a deep-sleep state drawing microamps, woken only by one of two triggers: a real-time-clock interrupt on a fixed schedule (e.g., every 5 minutes) or an interrupt from the sensing element itself when a configured threshold is crossed. On waking, the MCU powers up the sensor and analog front end just long enough to take and condition a reading, then powers up the radio transceiver, forms a small data packet, and transmits it to the nearest gateway using whatever low-power protocol the sensor is built around (LoRa, Zigbee, or NB-IoT) — the radio is the single largest power draw in the system, so the firmware minimises its on-time rather than the sensing itself. If the gateway does not acknowledge receipt, the MCU can buffer the reading and retry on the next cycle rather than holding the radio on to wait. Once transmission (or buffering) completes, every subsystem is powered back down and the MCU returns to sleep until the next scheduled or threshold-triggered wake, a pattern that is what allows a coin-cell or small battery pack to last several years in the field.

Every stage but sleep is powered on only for the seconds it takes to complete its job — the wake-measure-transmit-sleep cycle, not the sensor itself, is what determines multi-year battery life.

Construction

Physical Construction

The sensor node is built into a compact, sealed polymer or die-cast enclosure rated IP67/IP68, sized to house the sensing element (current transformer, thermocouple, accelerometer, or humidity/pressure element), signal-conditioning circuitry, wireless radio module, and battery pack within a single weatherproof shell. The enclosure incorporates a moulded antenna window or external whip antenna positioned to maximise radio range while remaining protected from physical damage, and cable glands or potted cable entries seal the connection to the sensed conductor or mounting point against moisture ingress. Mounting features, such as DIN-rail clips, magnetic bases, or bolt-on brackets, are integrated into the housing to suit installation on cables, conduits, or fixture bodies without additional hardware. The battery compartment is designed for field replacement without breaking the main enclosure seal, and the overall unit is built to withstand the wide operating temperature range and vibration typical of outdoor, unattended AGL monitoring points.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

RF Interference and Signal Attenuation

Airfield structures, metal enclosures, and electromagnetic noise can degrade wireless signal strength. Proper antenna placement and using a gateway with multiple antennas or repeaters is essential for reliable coverage.

Battery Life Degradation

Extreme temperatures (especially high heat) can reduce battery life. Use of industrial-grade batteries and temperature-compensated power management is recommended. Some sensors support energy harvesting (solar, thermal) to extend lifetime.

Sensor Drift and Calibration

Over time, sensor elements can drift, affecting measurement accuracy. Periodic calibration or verification against known references is required, especially for critical parameters like current and temperature.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Sensor Not Reporting / Dropped from Network
Likely Cause
Depleted battery, sensor out of gateway range, or RF interference from nearby structures or equipment.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the sensor's reported battery level and last-seen timestamp on the gateway; review signal strength history; check for new obstructions or interference sources near the sensor path.
Corrective Action
Replace the battery, relocate the sensor or gateway antenna for better line of sight, or add a repeater to close the coverage gap.
Weak or Intermittent Signal Strength
Likely Cause
Marginal antenna placement, metal enclosures or airfield structures attenuating the signal, or electromagnetic noise from nearby equipment.
Diagnostic Steps
Log signal strength over time to identify patterns tied to weather or equipment operation; check antenna orientation and mounting; survey the path for new obstructions.
Corrective Action
Reposition the antenna, add a gateway with multiple antennas or a repeater, and re-verify signal strength meets the reliability margin.
Battery Draining Faster Than Expected
Likely Cause
Extreme temperatures reducing battery capacity, or a reporting interval set more frequently than needed.
Diagnostic Steps
Review battery discharge history against ambient temperature logs; check the configured reporting/transmission interval.
Corrective Action
Switch to an industrial-grade or temperature-compensated battery, adjust the reporting interval, or add energy harvesting where supported.
Reported Values Drift from Known-Good Reference
Likely Cause
Sensor element drift accumulating over time, particularly for current and temperature measurements.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare sensor output against a calibrated reference instrument under the same conditions; review calibration history for a drift trend.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the sensor, and re-verify accuracy against the reference before returning it to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Battery Handling & Disposal
Personnel Safety

Handle and dispose of the sensor's battery according to the manufacturer's safety data sheet, and never puncture, crush, or expose it to excessive heat when replacing it in the field.

RF Interference Awareness
Operational

Site and orient the sensor to avoid known sources of RF interference where practical, and be aware that dense metal structures or other wireless equipment nearby can degrade its link reliability.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate placement or servicing of sensors mounted within the movement area with ATC/airside operations, particularly where a ladder, vehicle, or extended presence near active pavement is required.

Weatherproof Housing Care
Operational

Confirm the housing seal is correctly reinstated after opening a sensor for battery replacement or servicing, since moisture ingress is a common cause of sensor element drift and premature failure.

PPE for Field Servicing
Personnel Safety

Wear appropriate PPE for the mounting location, including fall protection for elevated sensors and hi-vis clothing for sensors within the movement area.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Battery Life Is a Reporting-Interval Decision
Design

The single biggest lever on service life is the reporting interval, not the battery chemistry. Tightening the interval "just to be safe" during commissioning quietly halves the field life and turns into an unplanned battery-swap campaign a year or two later than expected.

A Marginal Link Fails Intermittently, Not Cleanly
Troubleshooting

A sensor near the edge of usable signal strength tends to drop packets sporadically rather than going fully offline, which can look like a flaky sensor rather than an RF path problem. Log signal strength over time before swapping hardware; a declining trend usually points to a new obstruction, not a failing unit.

Stagger Battery Replacement, Don't Wait for Failure
Maintenance

Letting every sensor in a batch run to depletion means they tend to fail in a cluster, since they were commissioned around the same time. Track installation dates and proactively rotate batteries on a schedule rather than reacting to a wave of "sensor offline" alerts.

Cold Weather Compounds Two Problems at Once
Reliability

Low temperature both reduces usable battery capacity and is often when winter operations most need reliable sensor data. Budget extra margin into replacement schedules for sensors in cold-climate deployments rather than using a single battery-life figure across all sites.

Gateway Placement Matters as Much as Sensor Placement
Design

Chasing sensor-side fixes for a poor link often overlooks that the gateway or repeater location is the real constraint. A mid-field repeater can resolve link issues across several sensors at once more effectively than relocating each one individually.

Weatherproof Seals Degrade Faster With Frequent Access
Durability

Every battery swap is a chance to compromise the housing seal if the gasket isn't inspected and reseated properly. A sensor that needs frequent access due to a short battery life will see more seal-related failures over its life than one serviced rarely, which is worth factoring into the interval-vs-battery-life trade-off.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about wireless sensors for AGL monitoring: protocols, battery life, standards, maintenance and troubleshooting.

Q1What is a wireless sensor in AGL monitoring?

A self-contained device that measures parameters such as current, voltage, temperature, vibration or humidity and transmits the data over a wireless network such as LoRa, Zigbee, NB-IoT, Wi-Fi or Bluetooth.

Q2Why use wireless sensors on an airfield?

They extend current, temperature and vibration monitoring to equipment and locations where running new cabling would be impractical or expensive, making retrofit monitoring feasible.

Q3Where are wireless sensors deployed?

Wherever monitoring is needed but cabling is impractical, including runway and taxiway lighting (current and temperature anomalies) and isolating transformers.

Q4Which wireless protocols and ranges are typical?

LoRaWAN, Zigbee, NB-IoT, LTE-M, Wi-Fi or Bluetooth 5.0 on regional bands of 868 MHz, 915 MHz or 2.4 GHz, with LoRa reaching up to about 10 km line of sight.

Q5What can they measure and how accurately?

Current ranges of 0-10 A, 0-100 A or 0-1000 A depending on model, temperature from -40°C to +125°C, and typical accuracy of ±1% to ±3% of full scale.

Q6How long does the battery last?

Typically 3-10 years depending on transmission interval (1 minute to 24 hours) and protocol, using a lithium primary cell or a rechargeable cell with energy harvesting.

Q7Is the sensor electrically isolated from the monitored circuit?

Battery operation and CT-based current sensing keep the unit galvanically isolated from the monitored circuit.

Q8Which standards apply?

FCC Part 15 (USA radio frequency devices), ETSI EN 300 220 (short range devices), the LoRaWAN specification and IEC 60529 (IP code), with IP67 or IP68 enclosures typical.

Q9How often should wireless sensors be maintained?

Semi-annually for battery and RF signal strength surveys, annually for calibration verification, gateway and repeater inspection and enclosure and seal checks, and quarterly for firmware update review.

Q10Why has a sensor stopped reporting?

Likely causes are a depleted battery, the sensor being out of gateway range, or RF interference from nearby structures. Check the reported battery level and last-seen timestamp on the gateway.
