Mounting Flange

The structural interface that securely attaches the AGL fixture to its mounting infrastructure — whether a base plate, base can, frangible pole, or direct pavement mounting. Engineered for precise alignment, load distribution, and vibration resistance.

Overview

What Is It?

A Mounting Flange is a flat, ring-like or plate-like structural component that provides the mechanical interface between an AGL fixture and its supporting structure. Typically cast or machined from aluminium, steel, or stainless steel, the flange is integrated into the fixture's bottom housing or base assembly. It features precisely located bolt holes or threaded studs for secure attachment to a base plate, base can, frangible pole, or pavement-mounted anchor system. The mounting flange ensures proper alignment of the fixture's optical axis, distributes mechanical loads (wind, vibration, impact) evenly across the mounting interface, and provides a stable foundation for reliable long-term operation. Many flanges also incorporate alignment keys or locating pins to ensure correct orientation during installation.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

A fixture that has to be periodically removed for lamp replacement or maintenance still has to go back exactly where it was — any drift in its mounting position changes its beam angle relative to the runway. The mounting flange's purpose is to give the fixture a single, repeatable mechanical reference to its supporting structure, so reinstallation restores the same alignment without a technician needing to re-aim it in the field. Because the flange also spreads wind, vibration, and impact loads evenly across the mounting interface rather than concentrating them at a few bolt points, it protects the fixture's housing from the stress cracking or fatigue that an unevenly loaded connection would eventually cause — making the flange as much a structural safeguard for the fixture as it is a mounting convenience.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Mounting flanges are used in every AGL fixture that requires a secure, detachable mechanical connection to its mounting infrastructure. Common applications include:

  • Elevated Runway & Taxiway Lights: Flanged base for frangible pole or base plate mounting.
  • Inset Runway & Taxiway Lights: Flanged base ring for flush mounting in pavement base cans.
  • Threshold & End Lights: Flanges for both elevated and inset configurations.
  • Approach Lighting: Flanges for barrette and mast-mounted approach light units.
  • PAPI & VASI: Precision flanges with adjustment mechanisms for optical alignment.
  • Obstruction Lights: Flanges for pole-mounted beacon systems.
  • Wind Cones: Flanges for mast-mounted windsock assemblies.
  • Signage Structures: Flanges for mandatory and information signs.
Placement

Installation & Location

The flange is fitted at the fixture's own base, bolted either to the top of a frangible pole (elevated installations) or into the rim of a base can set into the pavement (inset installations), with bolt torque set to the manufacturer's value so the mechanical joint stays secure under vibration and wheel-load cycling without over-stressing the flange material. Gasket seating between the flange and its mating surface is checked during installation, since this joint is usually also the fixture's primary water-ingress barrier; flange orientation is keyed where the fixture's optical assembly is directional, so the flange can only be bolted on in the position that keeps the beam correctly aimed.

Function

How Does It Work?

The mounting flange serves several critical functions in the mechanical installation of an AGL fixture:

  • Mechanical Attachment: Provides the bolted interface that secures the fixture to its support structure, ensuring the fixture remains fixed in position under all operational conditions.
  • Load Distribution: Spreads the forces from wind, vibration, and accidental impact across a wide area of the mounting surface, preventing stress concentrations that could cause damage.
  • Optical Alignment: Ensures that the fixture is positioned at the correct height and orientation (azimuth and tilt) to project the required beam pattern, via precision-machined surfaces and locating features.
  • Service Access: Allows for quick removal and replacement of the fixture for maintenance, using accessible fasteners.
  • Frangible Interface (Elevated Fixtures): Often serves as the mounting point for the frangible coupling or breakaway mechanism, providing a clean separation point in case of impact.

The flange is typically bolted to the support structure using stainless steel fasteners (bolts, washers, nuts) with thread-locking compound to prevent loosening due to vibration. Proper torque application is essential to ensure secure attachment without damaging the flange or the support structure.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The mounting flange is a purely mechanical bolt-circle interface with no control-logic role — it joins a fixture or pole base to the base plate or mounting surface beneath it, using a bolt pattern (hole count, diameter, and spacing) specific to that fixture family. Because the pattern is specific rather than standardised across all AGL hardware, a flange is not generally interchangeable between different fixture types even where the overall footprint looks similar, and matching the flange to the correct mounting surface is a fit-up check made during installation rather than anything monitored afterward.

Construction

Physical Construction

The flange is a machined disc or plate of die-cast aluminium, stainless steel, or zinc-plated carbon steel, with a circular bolt-hole pattern around its perimeter sized to match the fixture's mounting boss and the receiving pole, mast, or base structure. A raised or recessed spigot, dowel pins, or a keyway are incorporated to provide rotational alignment, ensuring the fixture is seated in the correct orientation every time it's remounted after maintenance. The flange face is machined flat to seat evenly against its mating surface, with a gasket groove or O-ring channel where a weatherproof seal is required at the joint. Surface finishing, powder-coating, anodising, or nickel-plating, protects the flange from corrosion in outdoor service, and the bolt pattern and material thickness are sized to carry the specified wind and vibration loads without fatigue over the fixture's service life.

Bolt Torque Setting
Mechanical

Torque the mounting bolts to the manufacturer's specified value so the joint stays secure under vibration and wheel-load cycling without over-stressing the flange material.

Gasket Seating Check
Mechanical

Check gasket seating between the flange and its mating surface, since this joint usually also serves as the fixture's primary water-ingress barrier.

Mating Surface Condition
Mechanical

Fitment Sign-off

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Bolt Torque Verification
Annual

Re-torque flange bolts to spec so the fixture stays securely seated and able to resist wind loads up to 200 km/h without loosening.

Alignment Feature Inspection
Annual

Check dowel pins, locating rings, or keyways for wear or damage that could allow rotational misalignment of the mounted fixture.

Corrosion / Coating Check
Annual

Inspect powder-coated, anodized, or nickel-plated surfaces for coating breakdown, and cast aluminium flanges for pitting or galvanic corrosion at bolt contact points.

Bolt Hole Wear Check
Annual

Inspect bolt holes for elongation or cracking under repeated vibration loading, which can gradually loosen the fixture over time.

Flange-to-Base Seal Check
Annual

Where a gasket or sealant is used at the flange interface, confirm it remains intact to prevent moisture tracking into the mounting cavity.

Vibration Looseness Check
Semi-annual

Check for play or rocking between the flange and mounted fixture, a common early sign of bolt or hole wear before failure occurs.

Crack Inspection (Die-Cast Flanges)
Annual

Inspect die-cast aluminium flanges closely for hairline casting cracks, which can propagate under repeated thermal cycling and vibration.

Thread Condition Check
Annual

Inspect threaded bolt holes for galling or stripping, especially where fasteners have been repeatedly removed for maintenance access.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Fasteners and Flange

In coastal or high-humidity environments, galvanic corrosion can occur between the aluminium flange and steel bolts, or between dissimilar metals. Use stainless steel fasteners with anti-seize compound and ensure the flange coating is intact.

Bolts Loosening from Vibration

Continuous vibration from aircraft and ground vehicles can cause mounting bolts to loosen over time. This can lead to fixture misalignment or complete detachment. Use thread-locking compound (Loctite) and conduct regular torque checks.

Misalignment from Installation Errors

If the flange is not correctly aligned during installation, the fixture's optical beam will be misdirected. This is a common issue that affects runway and taxiway light performance. Always use alignment jigs and check azimuth/tilt angles during installation.

Flange Cracking from Over-Torque

Applying excessive torque to the mounting bolts can crack the flange, especially in cast aluminium fixtures. Always use a calibrated torque wrench and follow the manufacturer's specified torque values.

Damage from Snowploughs or Ground Vehicles

In elevated fixtures, the flange can be damaged by snowploughs or ground vehicles. Regular visual inspection for bending, cracking, or deformation is essential, especially after winter operations.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Corrosion Found Between Flange and Fasteners
Likely Cause
Galvanic corrosion has developed between the aluminium flange and steel bolts, or another dissimilar metal pairing, in a coastal or high-humidity environment.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the flange and fastener interface for corrosion products, and confirm whether stainless steel fasteners and anti-seize compound were used at installation.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded fasteners with stainless steel and anti-seize compound, and touch up or replace any damaged flange coating.
Fixture Misaligned or Loose at the Flange
Likely Cause
Continuous vibration from aircraft and ground vehicles has loosened the mounting bolts over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Check bolt torque against the manufacturer's specification, and inspect for signs of thread-locking compound having been used originally.
Corrective Action
Re-torque bolts to specification with thread-locking compound applied, and add the flange to the routine torque-check schedule.
Fixture's Optical Beam Is Misdirected
Likely Cause
The flange was not correctly aligned during installation, misdirecting the fixture's beam.
Diagnostic Steps
Check azimuth and tilt angle against the design specification using an alignment jig, and run a photometric check on the beam pattern.
Corrective Action
Re-align the flange using the alignment jig to the correct azimuth/tilt, and re-verify with a photometric test.
Flange Found Cracked Near the Mounting Bolts
Likely Cause
Excessive torque was applied to the mounting bolts, cracking the cast flange material.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the flange for cracking around bolt holes, and check the torque wrench calibration and technique used during the last installation.
Corrective Action
Replace the cracked flange, and re-install using a calibrated torque wrench set to the manufacturer's specified value.
Flange Bent or Deformed Without a Clear Installation Cause
Likely Cause
A snowplough or ground vehicle has struck the elevated fixture, damaging the flange.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the flange for bending, cracking, or deformation, particularly after winter operations.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged flange, and consider additional visibility markers or protective measures if the location is a repeat contact point.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Galvanic Corrosion at the Fastener Interface
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Dissimilar metals (aluminium flange with steel fasteners) in contact without isolation, compounded by coastal salt air or persistent moisture at the joint.
Effect
Fasteners seize or weaken, and corrosion products build up between the flange and base, degrading the mechanical seal over time.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the flange-fastener interface during routine maintenance, checking specifically for corrosion staining or pitting.
Bolt Loosening from Vibration
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Sustained vibration from aircraft and ground traffic acting on mounting bolts installed without thread-locking compound.
Effect
Fixture aim drifts out of tolerance, connections come under mechanical stress, and in severe cases the fixture can loosen from its base entirely.
Detection Method
Scheduled torque checks against manufacturer specification, and periodic aim/level verification during maintenance rounds.
Flange Cracking Near Bolt Holes
High Severity
Root Cause
Over-torqued mounting bolts during installation or reinstallation, or a casting defect in the flange material stressed by normal loading.
Effect
Structural weakening around the bolt holes that can propagate to a full fracture, releasing the fixture from secure mounting.
Detection Method
Close visual inspection for hairline cracking around bolt holes, and verification of torque wrench calibration used at installation.
Impact Damage from Ground Equipment
High Severity
Root Cause
Direct contact from a snowplough, mower, or other ground vehicle against an elevated fixture, particularly during winter operations or low-visibility conditions.
Effect
Flange bends or deforms, misdirecting the fixture's beam or taking it out of service entirely.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for bending, cracking, or deformation, with added checks scheduled after winter operations or known contact events.
Installation Misalignment
Low Severity
Root Cause
Flange was not set to the correct azimuth or tilt angle during installation, or an alignment jig was not used or was miscalibrated.
Effect
Fixture's optical beam is misdirected, reducing effective intensity in the intended viewing direction without any visible fault at the fixture itself.
Detection Method
Azimuth/tilt check against design specification using an alignment jig, confirmed with a photometric check of the beam pattern.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Verify Isolation Before Removal
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the circuit and verify zero energy before unbolting the mounting flange from its base plate, base can, or frangible pole.

Respect Alignment Keys
Compliance

Seat the flange on its locating pins or alignment keys before tightening; forcing it into a rotated position can misalign the fixture's optical axis and put its beam pattern out of tolerance.

Correct Torque
Mechanical

Torque flange bolts evenly and to specification; uneven or insufficient torque can leave the fixture loose under wind and vibration loading, while over-torquing can distort the flange or an underlying seal.

Frangibility Not to Be Compromised
Mechanical

Where the flange sits within a frangible assembly, use only the specified hardware and torque; substituting stronger fasteners or a stiffer flange can raise the effective breakaway force.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside and raise a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance during the work.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for all fasteners removed from the flange, and clear the site before reinstating the fixture.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Alignment Keys Only Help if They're Actually Engaged
Alignment

A flange installed without its locating pins or keys properly seated can appear correctly bolted while sitting slightly rotated from its intended optical alignment; verify keys or pins are fully engaged during installation, not just that the bolts are tight.

Torque Sequence Prevents Flange Warp on Larger Assemblies
Mechanical

Tightening flange bolts in the wrong order can induce a slight warp that's difficult to detect visually but throws off optical alignment; use a cross-pattern tightening sequence, particularly on flanges with more than four fixing points.

Check the Flange-to-Housing Gasket Face for Pitting, Not Just the Gasket
Corrosion

A degraded gasket is the obvious failure point, but the metal sealing face it seats against can also pit or corrode over time, preventing a proper seal even with a brand-new gasket installed. Inspect the mating face condition, not just the gasket itself.

Confirm Bonding Continuity Through the Flange After Reassembly
Electrical

Where the flange is part of the fixture's earthing path, paint or coating disturbed during installation or maintenance can leave a mechanically sound but electrically discontinuous joint; verify with a meter after any flange is removed and reinstalled.

Dissimilar Metal Contact at the Flange Interface Accelerates Corrosion
Durability

A flange material that doesn't match its mating surface (aluminium flange on a steel base, for example) sets up galvanic corrosion at the interface faster than either material would degrade alone; verify compatibility or use proper isolation when materials differ.

Record Flange Orientation at Installation for Future Reference
Documentation

Where a flange's keying only allows one correct orientation but multiple physically possible mounting positions, documenting the as-installed orientation prevents a technician reassembling it incorrectly after future maintenance.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about mounting flanges: function, materials, alignment, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a mounting flange in airfield ground lighting?

A mounting flange is a flat ring-like or plate-like structural component that forms the mechanical interface between an AGL fixture and its supporting structure. It is typically cast or machined from aluminium, steel or stainless steel.

Q2What does a mounting flange do?

It bolts the fixture to its support, spreads wind, vibration and impact loads over a wide area, and sets the fixture's height and orientation through precision-machined surfaces and locating features.

Q3Where are mounting flanges used?

On elevated and inset runway and taxiway lights, threshold and end lights, approach light units, and PAPI and VASI units, where precision flanges have adjustment mechanisms for optical alignment.

Q4What are typical mounting flange dimensions and materials?

Diameters of about 80 to 250 mm, thicknesses of 6 to 20 mm, and 3 to 8 bolt holes (M8 to M12 or 3/8"-16 UNC). Materials are die-cast aluminium (A380), 316L stainless steel or zinc-plated carbon steel.

Q5How is fixture alignment maintained on a flange?

Dowel pins, locating rings or keyways provide rotational alignment. These should be inspected for wear because damage can let the fixture rotate out of alignment.

Q6Which standards apply to mounting flanges?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-46 (light fixture specification), ICAO Annex 14 and ASTM B117 for salt spray corrosion testing.

Q7How often should mounting flanges be inspected?

Annually. Re-torque the flange bolts, check dowel pins, locating rings and keyways, and inspect the coating for breakdown and cast aluminium flanges for pitting.

Q8What are the common problems with mounting flanges?

Galvanic corrosion between the flange and fasteners in coastal or humid sites, bolts loosening from vibration, misalignment from installation errors, cracking from over-torque and damage from snowploughs or vehicles.
