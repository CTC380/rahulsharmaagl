Remote Control Panel

A decentralised operator interface that provides local control and monitoring of airfield lighting circuits from remote locations. Typically installed in substations, maintenance shops, or satellite equipment rooms, the Remote Control Panel enables manual override, circuit isolation, and status verification without requiring access to the main control room.

Overview

What Is It?

A Remote Control Panel is a compact, ruggedised control console that replicates a subset of the main AGL control functions in a distributed location. It is designed to give local operators—such as maintenance crews, substation attendants, or ground staff—the ability to manually control lighting circuits, change brightness levels, and view status indications without relying on the central ALCMS or the main control room.

Typically, the Remote Control Panel includes:

  • Circuit Control Switches: Toggle or selector switches for turning individual circuits or groups on/off.
  • Brightness Step Selection: Rotary switches or pushbuttons to adjust the intensity level (e.g., 3‑step or 5‑step) for local circuits.
  • Status Indicators: LED lamps showing the real‑time status of circuits (ON/OFF, brightness step, fault conditions).
  • Alarm Annunciation: Visual (flashing light) and audible (buzzer) alerts for local faults, such as circuit breaker trips or communication loss.
  • Local/Remote Selector: A key‑operated switch that determines whether the panel is in "Local" mode (allowing manual control) or "Remote" mode (giving priority to ALCMS commands).
  • Emergency Stop / Override: A prominent pushbutton to immediately de‑energise selected circuits in an emergency.

The panel is typically wall‑mounted in a weatherproof enclosure (IP54 or higher) for outdoor use, or in a standard indoor panel for equipment rooms. It communicates with the main control system via hardwired discrete I/O or a serial/ Ethernet link, and includes interlock logic to prevent conflicting commands when in Remote mode.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The remote control panel exists to give operators working away from the central control room — a substation attendant, a maintenance crew in the field — the ability to control and check the circuits directly in front of them without needing a radio call back to the tower for every routine action or fault response. This matters most during active maintenance or a developing local fault, where the fastest safe response is often the one taken right where the problem is, not relayed through a central operator.

The local/remote selector switch exists specifically to prevent two operators from issuing conflicting commands to the same circuit at the same time: putting the panel in local mode deliberately takes priority away from the ALCMS for as long as field work requires direct control, and switching back to remote hands that priority cleanly back to the central system once the work is done.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Remote Control Panels are deployed in various locations across the airfield where local access to lighting control is beneficial, including:

  • Remote CCR Substations: Providing local operators with the ability to manually control CCRs and lighting circuits during maintenance or ALCMS outages.
  • Runway Edge Cabinets: Allowing maintenance crews to test and isolate individual runway lighting segments without coordination with the control tower.
  • Taxiway Intersection Control Boxes: Enabling ground staff to manage taxiway lighting at complex intersections or during construction work.
  • Approach Lighting Control Shelters: Providing local control of approach light bars and flasher units for testing and troubleshooting.
  • Apron & Gate Control Rooms: Giving apron managers the ability to control apron floodlights and docking guidance lights independent of the main ALCMS.
  • Maintenance Shops: Allowing technicians to simulate circuit operations and verify repairs without affecting operational lighting.
  • Emergency Backup Control: Serving as a fallback control point in the event of a failure of the primary control room or network, ensuring continued lighting control.
Placement

Installation & Location

Remote control panels are installed at the field location they give local authority over — inside a CCR substation, mounted in a runway edge or taxiway intersection cabinet, fixed to the wall of an approach lighting control shelter, or set into an apron or gate control room console. Panel-mounted units are wired directly into the CCR or relay cabinet they control, sitting alongside or inside the same enclosure so local operation doesn't depend on a separate long cable run, while console-mounted panels in operations rooms connect back to their associated equipment via the ALCMS network. Positioning always keeps the panel accessible to the staff who need it during maintenance, testing, or an ALCMS outage, without requiring them to enter a restricted or hazardous area to reach it.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Remote Control Panel operates as a local interface, receiving commands from the operator and translating them into hardwired or digital control signals. Key functional aspects include:

  • Mode Selection: A key‑operated "Local/Remote" switch determines the panel's operational mode. In "Remote" mode, the panel's controls are disabled, and the circuit states follow the ALCMS commands. In "Local" mode, the operator's manual inputs override the ALCMS, allowing direct control.
  • Manual Control: When in Local mode, switches and pushbuttons directly energise or de‑energise control relays, which then switch the corresponding lighting circuits via contactors or CCR taps. This provides a simple, fail‑safe mechanism that does not rely on network communication.
  • Status Feedback: Indicator lamps are driven by auxiliary contacts from the field devices (contactors, circuit breakers) or from the ALCMS, providing positive confirmation of the circuit state. This feedback is essential for the operator to verify that commands have been executed.
  • Brightness Selection: Brightness step selectors send signals to the CCR or to a step‑switching module, changing the current level in the series circuit. The selected step is also displayed on the panel.
  • Alarm and Fault Detection: The panel monitors for local faults, such as loss of control power, circuit breaker trips, or contactor failures. These are annunciated via a flashing red light and an audible buzzer. Some panels also forward these alarms to the ALCMS for central logging.
  • Communication Interface: For panels with digital communication, status and control data are exchanged with the ALCMS via Modbus, Profibus, or Ethernet. This allows the ALCMS to know the state of the panel and to detect if the panel is in Local mode, preventing remote commands from being sent.
  • Interlock Logic: Hardwired or programmed interlocks prevent unsafe operations, such as switching a circuit off while it is required for an active approach, or changing brightness steps too rapidly.

The panel is designed to be fail‑safe: in the event of power loss, the panel's contacts return to a de‑energised state, and the panel must be manually reset when power is restored. The Local/Remote selector ensures that when the panel is in Remote mode, all local controls are locked out, preventing accidental manual intervention.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The remote control panel's architecture is built around a single interlock: the key-operated Local/Remote switch, which determines whether a circuit takes its commands from the central ALCMS or from the operator standing in front of the panel. In Remote mode, the panel's own switches are locked out and the circuit simply follows whatever the ALCMS commands, exactly as it would with no panel present. In Local mode, the same switches instead energise control relays directly, driving the same contactors or CCR taps the ALCMS would otherwise use, but without any dependency on the network link back to the control room.

This design deliberately keeps the two control paths mutually exclusive rather than layered — a circuit is never being commanded by both the ALCMS and a local operator at once — and on panels with digital communication, the ALCMS is told when a panel has been put into Local mode, so a remote command sent during field work is rejected rather than silently ignored or contested at the relay.

Local/Remote Switch → (ALCMS Command | Local Relay) → Contactor/CCR Tap → Circuit (status feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The panel is built into a ruggedised steel or GRP enclosure rated for the environment it sits in — a wall- or desk-mounted IP54/IP65 cabinet for a substation or equipment room, or a more heavily sealed unit where it stands closer to outdoor exposure. The front face carries the operator controls (illuminated pushbuttons, a rotary selector, or a small HMI touchscreen) and status indicators, laid out to mirror the main ALCMS control functions it replicates, with a lockable door or key-switch to restrict unauthorised use. Internally, a terminal strip or connector block brings in the control-cable pairs back to the main control room, along with a local power feed, and a small internal PLC, relay logic board, or communication gateway translates local operator actions into the same control-cable protocol the ALCMS uses, so the panel is a genuine parallel input rather than a separate control layer.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Local/Remote Selector Malfunction

The key‑operated selector switch can wear out or become damaged, potentially leaving the panel in an unintended mode. This can lead to loss of control or unexpected manual overrides. Regularly test the selector switch and replace it if it shows signs of wear.

Communication Loss with ALCMS

If the panel uses digital communication, a loss of link can prevent status updates and alarm forwarding. Ensure redundant communication paths are available, and consider a "communication lost" alarm on the panel itself.

Switch Contact Oxidation

In humid or coastal environments, switch contacts can oxidise, leading to intermittent or high‑resistance connections. Use gold‑plated or sealed switches and perform periodic contact cleaning or replacement.

Indicator Lamp Failure

LED indicators are long‑lived, but can fail due to surges or manufacturing defects. Use high‑quality LED lamps and include a lamp test feature to verify all indicators are functional during routine checks.

Incorrect Wiring After Modification

Field modifications to the panel wiring (e.g., adding new circuits) can lead to errors if not properly documented. Maintain up‑to‑date schematics and label all wires clearly. Conduct a full functional test after any wiring changes.

Environmental Damage

Outdoor panels are exposed to rain, dust, and temperature extremes. Ensure the enclosure is properly sealed and that any cable glands are tight. Regularly inspect for moisture ingress, corrosion, and gasket degradation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Panel Stuck in Unexpected Local/Remote Mode
Likely Cause
The key-operated selector switch has worn or been damaged, leaving the panel in an unintended mode.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the selector switch through each position and confirm the panel responds correctly; inspect the switch mechanism for wear or damage.
Corrective Action
Replace the selector switch, and confirm correct mode transfer with a full functional test before returning to service.
Status Updates or Alarms Not Reaching ALCMS
Likely Cause
Loss of the digital communication link between the panel and ALCMS.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the panel's own "communication lost" alarm if fitted; verify the communication link and any redundant path.
Corrective Action
Restore the communication link or failover to the redundant path, and confirm status and alarm forwarding are working before closing out.
Switch Feels Intermittent or Sticky
Likely Cause
Switch contact oxidation from humid or coastal site conditions, causing high-resistance or intermittent connections.
Diagnostic Steps
Operate the switch and check for intermittent response; measure contact resistance if accessible.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the affected switch, and consider gold-plated or sealed switches for future replacements in this environment.
Indicator Lamp Not Lighting
Likely Cause
LED indicator failure from a surge event or manufacturing defect.
Diagnostic Steps
Run the panel's lamp test feature if fitted to check all indicators at once; verify the actual field state to confirm the lamp, not the underlying function, has failed.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed indicator lamp with a high-quality LED replacement, and confirm it responds correctly on the next lamp test.
Unexpected Behaviour After a Field Wiring Change
Likely Cause
A field modification to the panel wiring was not correctly documented or wired.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the wiring against the current schematics and wire labelling; trace the modified circuit to confirm correct termination.
Corrective Action
Correct the wiring, update schematics and labels to match, and conduct a full functional test of the panel before returning to service.
Corrosion or Moisture Found Inside an Outdoor Panel
Likely Cause
A degraded enclosure gasket or loose cable gland has allowed moisture ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the enclosure gasket and cable glands for damage or looseness; check for visible corrosion or moisture inside the panel.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded gasket, tighten or replace cable glands, dry out the enclosure, and re-verify the seal before re-energising.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Before Internal Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the panel's supply before working inside its enclosure or behind the front fascia, and verify zero energy before disconnecting wiring, even though the panel's own switches may appear off.

Confirm Control Authority Before Switching
Operational

Confirm with the central ALCMS or control room which system has control authority before operating switches on the panel, since an uncoordinated local override can conflict with commands issued centrally.

Announce Local Overrides
Operational

Notify the control room before and after using the panel to override or take manual control of a circuit, so status displayed centrally is understood correctly and not mistaken for a fault.

PPE for Panel Work
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working inside the panel enclosure, and be aware of adjacent live circuits feeding indicator lamps, switches, or communication interfaces.

FOD Control in the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools and fasteners accounted for at all times when the panel is open, and remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent a dropped item bridging adjacent terminals.

Verify Status Indication After Maintenance
Compliance

After maintenance, confirm the panel's indicator lamps and switch positions correctly reflect actual circuit status before returning it to service, since a mismatched indication can lead a local operator to make an incorrect switching decision.

Return Control to Automatic
Compliance

Return the panel to its normal automatic/remote mode after maintenance is complete, and do not leave it in local override unless that is the intended operating state.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the panel in a forced, overridden, or fault-indicating state after maintenance; confirm the control system reports correct status before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Confirm Control Authority Before Every Session, Not Just Once
Operational

Control authority can shift between local and central control during a shift without every technician being aware; re-confirm authority at the start of each work session on the panel, not just when first arriving on site.

Indicator Lamps Can Fail Independently of the Circuit They Represent
Compliance

A burnt-out or stuck indicator lamp can mislead an operator into believing a circuit is in a different state than it actually is; verify lamp function against actual circuit state periodically, not just when a discrepancy is already suspected.

Local Override Logic Should Fail Safe, Not Fail Available
Design

Where a panel's override logic has a fault, confirm the intended failure mode leaves the circuit in its safer state rather than defaulting to local control being granted; verify this behaviour during commissioning and after any control-logic firmware update.

A 'Stuck' Switch May Be a Latching Relay, Not the Switch Itself
Troubleshooting

A control panel switch that seems to stick in one position is sometimes actually a latching relay or interposing contactor downstream holding its state; check the switching logic behind the panel before assuming the front-panel hardware itself has failed.

Panel Wiring Often Carries Multiple Voltage Classes
Electrical

Indicator lamp circuits, switch logic, and communication interfaces inside one panel enclosure can operate at different voltages; verify which specific terminals are isolated before assuming the whole panel is de-energised from a single lockout point.

Log Every Local Override, Not Just Ones That Caused an Issue
Compliance

A habit of logging only overrides that led to a problem makes it hard to spot a pattern of increasingly frequent manual intervention at one panel, which often signals an underlying automatic-control fault worth investigating.
