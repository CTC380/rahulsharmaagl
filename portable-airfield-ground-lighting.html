Portable Airfield Ground Lighting (PAGL)

A practical engineering guide to temporary, emergency and rapid-deployment airfield lighting. PAGL is a temporary lighting arrangement used to provide essential visual guidance when the permanent Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) system is unavailable, damaged, under maintenance, displaced by construction, or not yet operational. A portable system is not simply a collection of battery-powered lamps — it is a temporary aviation visual-aid system that must be correctly positioned, oriented, powered, controlled, maintained and verified for the operational task for which it is being used.

Overview

What Is Portable Airfield Ground Lighting?

Portable Airfield Ground Lighting is a temporary visual-lighting system consisting of transportable light units, power supplies, controls, mounting or anchoring arrangements and associated equipment.

It may be deployed on runways, taxiways, temporary runway configurations, displaced thresholds, emergency landing areas, remote or expeditionary airfields, construction areas, damaged lighting systems, temporary operational surfaces, or heliport/special-use applications where specifically engineered. The exact configuration depends on the intended aircraft operation and the approved operational arrangement.

The FAA currently lists AC 150/5345-50B, Specification for Portable Runway and Taxiway Lights, as active. It covers portable runway and taxiway lights and runway end identifier lights intended for temporary use when all or part of a runway lighting system is inoperative. (Federal Aviation Administration)

Not Just Battery Lamps

A portable system is a temporary aviation visual-aid system that must be correctly positioned, oriented, powered, controlled, maintained and verified — not simply switched on.

Temporary, Not Permanent

Portable AGL is normally a temporary operational solution used while the permanent system is restored, not a substitute for permanent AGL design.

FAA AC 150/5345-50B

The principal FAA equipment reference for portable runway/taxiway lights and runway end identifier lights intended for temporary use.

Purpose

Why Portable AGL Is Required

A permanent AGL system can become unavailable for many reasons. Portable lighting provides a temporary visual reference while the permanent system is restored.

CCR failure Series circuit faults Cable damage Flooding Substation failure Electrical fire Construction activities Runway rehabilitation Lighting fixture damage Airfield pavement works Control-system failure Emergency operational requirements
Core Principle

Portable Lighting Is a Temporary System

Portable AGL is normally a temporary operational solution, not a substitute for permanent AGL design.

FAA AC 150/5345-50B explicitly states that portable lights are intended only for temporary use and are not suitable for permanent installation. The FAA guidance primarily addresses VFR applications. (Federal Aviation Administration) Therefore, a portable system should not automatically be treated as equivalent to a permanent CAT I, CAT II or CAT III lighting installation.

Components

Main Components of a Portable AGL System

A portable system may contain some or all of the following. Not every portable installation needs every component.

Light Units

Portable runway lights, taxiway lights, threshold lights, runway end lights, runway end identifier lights, approach lights where specifically required, and portable PAPI or temporary visual guidance equipment where specifically engineered.

Power & Control

Battery units, solar charging systems, generator sets, temporary power distribution units, radio controllers and remote-control receivers.

Mechanical & Support Equipment

Cabling, connectors, mounting bases, stakes or anchors, wind-resistant supports, transport cases and charging equipment.

Light Types

Portable Light Types & Their Function

Portable Runway Edge Lighting

Defines the lateral limits of the usable runway. Primary functions: lateral guidance, runway boundary definition, runway orientation and visual continuity during night operations. The number and spacing of portable edge lights must follow the approved temporary runway layout and applicable requirements.

Portable Taxiway Lighting

May be used where temporary taxi routes are required. The arrangement must clearly communicate taxiway limits, direction, intersections, holding positions and safe aircraft movement routes — and must never create ambiguity between an active taxi route and an unavailable surface.

Portable Threshold Lighting

Identifies the beginning of the usable landing surface. Where portable edge lights are used to define a threshold, FAA AC 150/5345-50B states they should be supplemented by portable REIL to improve runway-end definition. (Federal Aviation Administration) The temporary layout should be treated as an integrated visual system rather than individual lamps.

Portable Runway End Lighting

Defines the far end of the usable runway. The engineer must consider colour, directionality, position, spacing, orientation, visibility and the relationship with threshold lighting — the installation must avoid creating confusing visual cues.

Portable REIL

Portable Runway End Identifier Lights may be used where an approved temporary arrangement requires stronger runway-end identification. Treat REIL as a distinct visual aid — verify its flashing characteristics, intensity, orientation and control arrangement against the applicable specification.

Portable Approach Lighting

More demanding than a simple runway-edge arrangement: precisely located lights, correct centreline geometry, correct crossbar arrangement, accurate orientation, controlled spacing, known photometric performance and structural stability. Must be specifically engineered for the applicable runway and approach operation.

Portable PAPI

Requires particularly careful engineering — a PAPI is not simply positioned beside the runway by eye. Performance depends on longitudinal and lateral location, elevation, horizontal alignment, vertical aiming, unit-to-unit geometry, approach profile, obstacle environment and photometric characteristics. A proper survey and aiming process is required.

Battery Performance Is Not Just Nominal Ampere-Hours

The system must consider lamp load, LED efficiency, flashing duty cycle, ambient temperature, battery age, state of charge, voltage characteristics, recharge time, required operating duration and reserve capacity. A system that works for several hours under laboratory conditions may behave differently during actual field deployment.

FAA AC 150/5345-50B specifies battery-related requirements for L-863 portable lights, including minimum operation after recharge and battery conditions intended to preserve required photometric performance, for both non-rechargeable and rechargeable batteries. (Federal Aviation Administration) Battery endurance must be evaluated together with photometric performance, not independently from it.

Critical Rule

Do Not Connect a Portable Light Directly to a CCR

A conventional CCR supplies regulated series-circuit current. A portable light designed for battery supply, low-voltage DC, solar power, or conventional AC may be electrically incompatible with a CCR circuit.

The portable equipment specification must define the permissible supply arrangement before any connection to airport electrical infrastructure is attempted.

Mechanical & Fail-Safe Design

Anchoring, Jet-Blast Resistance & Mechanical Design

Portable lights are exposed to wind, jet blast, propeller wash, wake vortices, vehicle movement, maintenance activity, rain and surface vibration. FAA AC 150/5345-50B requires portable lights to be securely anchored against wind, wake vortices and propeller or jet blast. (Federal Aviation Administration) The mechanical installation is as important as the electrical installation.

Position Stability

A light can remain electrically healthy but become operationally incorrect if it moves — after wind, a light displaced 150 mm creates incorrect geometry, and repeated aircraft operations can change fixture orientation and beam direction. Physical stability must be checked during operation.

Jet-Blast Resistance

Jet blast can tilt fixtures, move lightweight housings, damage cables, disconnect connectors, accumulate debris, change aiming and damage solar panels. Temporary systems must be evaluated according to their location and aircraft exposure.

Mechanical Design Features

Rugged housing, UV resistance, water resistance, corrosion resistance, impact resistance, stable base, carrying handle, secure connector, anchor points, accessible battery compartment and appropriate low profile where required.

Frangibility

Frangibility requirements depend on the specific equipment, location and applicable standard. A portable light must not be assumed to meet a permanent fixture's frangibility requirements simply because it is lightweight.

Waterproofing

Outdoor equipment can experience heavy rain, surface water, mud, condensation, water spray, humidity and dust. Ingress may cause electrical leakage, corrosion, LED failure, battery degradation, connector failure or optical contamination.

Connectors & Cable Management

Check mating, locking mechanism, contact condition, sealing, strain relief, insulation, polarity and contamination protection. Cables should be routed safely, protected from traffic and aircraft operations, secured, identified and kept clear of drainage paths.

Forces Acting on a Portable Fixture

A portable light must resist wind, jet/propeller blast and wake vortices while remaining correctly positioned and oriented

A fixture can remain electrically healthy after displacement — the failure is geometric, not electrical, which is why position and orientation must be re-checked after wind, rain or jet-blast exposure.
Photometry

Portable AGL Photometry

Photometry remains a critical part of portable lighting. The engineer should verify the four practical elements of portable AGL optical verification.

Colour
Candela
Coverage
Configuration

FAA AC 150/5345-50B contains explicit photometric requirements for L-863 portable lights, including minimum intensity by colour and elevation angle and aviation colour chromaticity requirements. (Federal Aviation Administration)

Colour Verification

Possible aviation light colours include white, yellow, red, green and blue. The correct colour depends on the visual-aid function and should be assessed using an appropriate photometric or colour-measurement method rather than visual appearance alone.

Intensity Verification

The FAA L-863 specification includes minimum intensity values for defined colours and vertical angular zones. A portable LED can appear bright to the eye and still have an unsuitable angular intensity distribution.

Beam Coverage

Depends on horizontal and vertical distribution, fixture orientation, aircraft approach direction, runway centreline and mounting height. A misaligned light can produce adequate candela in the wrong direction.

Configuration

Light output, orientation, spacing, position and colour together. A technically compliant individual lamp can still produce an incorrect temporary visual system if deployed incorrectly.

Geometry & Layout

Alignment, Runway Geometry & Temporary Layout

Before deploying portable lights, establish the temporary runway geometry: runway centreline, threshold, runway end, edge positions, usable runway length, displaced threshold if applicable, taxi access, closed portions and approach side. Portable lighting follows the approved operational geometry — it should not define that geometry arbitrarily.

Every deployment should verify: lateral position, longitudinal position, azimuth, vertical orientation, height, spacing and reference coordinates — measured or controlled using appropriate engineering methods.

Portable PAPI — Approach Guidance Geometry

Each PAPI unit projects a white sector above and a red sector below a precisely aimed transition angle; the pilot reads the ratio of white to red across the wing bar

Portable PAPI performance depends on longitudinal/lateral location, elevation, horizontal alignment, vertical aiming and unit-to-unit geometry — a proper survey and aiming process is required before use, not visual placement by eye.

Operational Context

Construction, Displaced Thresholds & Runway Incursion Risk

During Construction

Typical situations include runway resurfacing, pavement replacement, drainage work, electrical duct works, fixture replacement, threshold relocation, centreline rehabilitation and taxiway construction. The temporary arrangement must reflect the active work zone and the usable aircraft movement area.

Displaced Threshold

The installation must clearly distinguish usable runway from unavailable runway surface. Associated markings, signs, lighting and operational procedures must remain fully consistent with one another.

Portable Stop Bars & Runway Guard Lights

Portable stop-bar arrangements require special consideration because stop bars are part of runway-incursion prevention — evaluate correct location, red visual indication, control, fail-safe response, interlocking where applicable, monitoring and operational procedures. It should not be treated as a simple replacement lamp. Portable runway guard-light arrangements must be evaluated as part of the runway holding-position safety arrangement.

Runway Incursion Risk

Temporary configurations can increase risk because pilots and vehicle operators may be unfamiliar with new runway limits, temporary thresholds, temporary taxi routes, construction zones or changed holding positions. Lighting should be integrated with the complete temporary aerodrome configuration.

Operational Boundaries

What Portable AGL Does Not Automatically Do

Does Not Replace a NOTAM

The deployment of portable AGL does not automatically mean a runway can resume normal operations. A NOTAM or other operational action may be required depending on the nature and duration of the failure, runway condition, applicable regulations, temporary configuration and operational limitations. The correct operational status is determined by the responsible airport and aeronautical authorities.

Does Not Automatically Restore CAT Capability

Installing portable runway lights does not automatically mean CAT I, CAT II or CAT III operations are restored, or that instrument procedures remain unchanged. RVR, ILS, ALCMS, secondary power, stop bars, taxiway/approach lighting, monitoring and operational procedures may all be involved.

Is Not Automatically Suitable for Low Visibility

Required visual aids, monitoring, redundancy, electrical supply, operational procedures and equipment performance become more demanding as operating minima decrease. The temporary configuration must be assessed as an integrated system.

Is Not the Same as RVR

RVR measures the distance at which runway markings or lights can be seen under specified conditions. Photometry measures the lighting system's optical performance. An RVR reading cannot replace a lighting performance test.

A permanent ALCMS can indicate circuit, equipment, alarm and control status, but a simple portable light may not be connected to the airport's existing ALCMS. No ALCMS indication does not automatically mean the portable system has failed — and a portable light being switched ON does not prove it is optically correctly positioned or performing correctly.

Standards

Standards & Regulatory References

FAA

AC 150/5345-50B, Specification for Portable Runway and Taxiway Lights — issued September 20, 2007, currently listed as active. Covers portable runway and taxiway lights and runway end identifier lights for temporary use. FAA's L-863 classification and 2026 certification-program addendum reference this AC. (Federal Aviation Administration)

ICAO

Annex 14 provides the broader international framework for aerodrome visual aids. The FAA portable-light specification itself refers to ICAO Annex 14 Volume I for aviation colour chromaticity boundaries. (Federal Aviation Administration)

EASA

CS-ADR-DSN Issue 7, incorporated into the March 2026 Easy Access Rules for Aerodromes. (EASA) A temporary arrangement should not be described as "EASA compliant" without establishing its complete regulatory and operational basis.

Equipment Identification & Battery Management

Every portable unit should ideally have a unique identification, e.g. PAGL-RWY-001, PAGL-RWY-002, PAGL-TWY-001 — this helps with maintenance, battery tracking, deployment records, fault investigation, replacement and test history.

Battery ID → installation date → charge state → test duration → performance → replacement date. Battery deterioration can become a hidden cause of field failures.

Emergency Readiness

Emergency Deployment Kit, Team & Planning

Emergency Deployment Kit

Lighting units, battery packs, charging system, controller, spare connectors, anchoring equipment, layout tools, survey/alignment equipment, test equipment and documentation. The objective is a complete deployment capability, not merely stored lights.

Deployment Team

AGL engineer, electrical technician, survey/layout technician, maintenance personnel, airport operations representative, ATC representative where applicable, and a safety/construction representative where required. Roles should be established before an emergency occurs.

Rapid Deployment Planning

Pre-plan storage, transport, personnel, layout, power, control, verification and removal so the response is fast and repeatable rather than improvised.

The technical team does not independently decide the operational status of a runway. Its function is to establish what equipment is installed, what is functional, what measurements were obtained and what limitations exist. Operational authorities determine the appropriate action within the applicable regulatory framework — and ATC needs clear, consistent information on lighting availability, configuration, stop-bar status, restrictions and faults.

Storage Conditions

Equipment should be stored to minimize moisture, corrosion, battery degradation, connector contamination, mechanical damage, excessive heat and deep battery discharge.

Storage readiness directly affects emergency deployment time. A professional inventory system tracking equipment ID, type, colour, battery, controller, condition, last inspection, last photometric test, calibration status, location and deployment status converts an emergency kit into a managed engineering asset.

Engineering Model

The Portable AGL Engineering Model

A useful way to understand PAGL is as seven interacting dimensions — testing should answer whether a unit is ON, electrically healthy, optically correct, and physically correctly installed. All four matter.

A portable airfield light is not merely a lamp with a battery — it is one element of a temporary visual guidance system. The correct questions go beyond "is the light ON?" to: is it the correct light, in the correct position, correctly oriented, the correct colour, the correct intensity? Will the power last for the required operation? Can it withstand the environment? Can its status be controlled and verified? Does the complete temporary configuration support the authorized operation?

That is the difference between portable lighting equipment and a properly engineered Portable Airfield Ground Lighting system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is portable airfield ground lighting (PAGL)?

PAGL is a temporary lighting arrangement providing essential visual guidance when the permanent AGL system is unavailable, damaged, under maintenance, displaced by construction or not yet operational. It consists of transportable light units, power supplies, controls, mounting or anchoring arrangements and associated equipment.

Q2Is portable AGL just battery-powered lamps?

No. It is a temporary aviation visual-aid system that must be correctly positioned, oriented, powered, controlled, maintained and verified for the operational task it serves.

Q3Which FAA specification covers portable runway and taxiway lights?

The page states that the FAA lists AC 150/5345-50B, Specification for Portable Runway and Taxiway Lights, as active. It covers portable runway and taxiway lights and runway end identifier lights intended for temporary use when all or part of a runway lighting system is inoperative.

Q4Can portable AGL replace a permanent lighting system?

No. AC 150/5345-50B states that portable lights are intended only for temporary use and are not suitable for permanent installation, and the guidance primarily addresses VFR applications. A portable system should not automatically be treated as equivalent to a permanent CAT I, II or III lighting installation.

Q5When is portable AGL required?

Situations include CCR failure, series circuit faults, cable damage, flooding, substation failure, electrical fire, construction activities, runway rehabilitation, fixture damage, pavement works, control-system failure and emergency operational requirements.

Q6How does portable AGL differ from permanent AGL?

Portable AGL is temporary and mobile rather than fixed, is powered by battery, solar, generator or external supply, and is controlled locally, by wire or remotely. It commonly uses individual units or temporary circuits, is positioned and checked for each deployment, needs frequent deployment checks, and is certified on specific portable equipment and an approved temporary arrangement.

Q7What are the main components of a portable AGL system?

Light units (portable runway, taxiway, threshold and end lights, REIL, and approach lights or portable PAPI where specifically engineered), power and control (batteries, solar charging, generators, temporary distribution, radio controllers and receivers), and mechanical support (cabling, connectors, bases, stakes or anchors, wind-resistant supports, cases and chargers). Not every installation needs every component.

Q8What are typical applications of portable AGL?

Emergency lighting when permanent runway lighting is unavailable, construction and runway rehabilitation, temporary runway configurations, remote operations, and controlled training and testing or rapid expeditionary deployment where authorized.

Q9How should portable threshold lighting be arranged?

Where portable edge lights define a threshold, FAA AC 150/5345-50B states they should be supplemented by portable REIL to improve runway-end definition. The temporary layout should be treated as an integrated visual system rather than individual lamps.

Q10What must portable taxiway lighting avoid?

The arrangement must clearly communicate taxiway limits, direction, intersections, holding positions and safe movement routes, and must never create ambiguity between an active taxi route and an unavailable surface.
