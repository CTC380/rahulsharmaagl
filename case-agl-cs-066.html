AGL-CS-066 — PAPI Photometry Failure

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A PAPI photometric investigation where optical degradation and beam‑angle misalignment caused unstable glide‑path indication — from electrical verification through photometric measurement, beam‑angle calibration, and optical restoration.

Primary Skill

1Case Study Overview

During a scheduled PAPI calibration and photometric inspection, pilots reported that the PAPI indication appeared inconsistent during final approach, with unstable red/white transitions and reduced brightness in wet‑weather conditions.

The PAPI electrical system was fully operational, all four units were illuminated, and no power‑supply abnormalities were detected.

Because the PAPI units were operating electrically but the glide‑path indication was unreliable, a detailed photometric investigation was initiated covering intensity, beam angle, transition sharpness, chromaticity, optical contamination, and alignment accuracy.

The investigation identified optical degradation combined with beam‑angle misalignment and lens contamination, resulting in failure to meet ICAO Annex 14 PAPI photometric and angular transition requirements.

This case demonstrates that PAPI systems can remain electrically operational while providing incorrect visual glide‑path information to approaching aircraft.

3System Background

A PAPI system consists of:

  • Four precision optical units.
  • High‑intensity light source.
  • Optical lens assembly.
  • Red filter system.
  • White optical path.
  • Precision beam‑angle adjustment mechanism.
  • Power supply and control system.

PAPI performance depends on:

  • Light intensity.
  • Beam angle.
  • Transition sharpness.
  • Red/white chromaticity.
  • Optical cleanliness.
  • Lens transmission.
  • Mechanical alignment.
  • Unit‑to‑unit consistency.

Common photometric failure causes include:

  • Lens contamination.
  • Filter degradation.
  • Optical aging.
  • Mechanical misalignment.
  • Foundation settlement.
  • LED/lamp lumen depreciation.
  • Incorrect calibration.
  • Environmental contamination.

7PAPI Photometric Failure Principle

Correct intensity → Correct beam angle → Sharp red/white transition → Correct chromaticity → Stable glide‑path indication → Reliable approach guidance
Photometric failure → Reduced intensity → Blurred transition → Angular error → Incorrect glide‑path indication → ICAO non‑compliance

PAPI performance depends on both photometry and precision angular alignment.

10Root Cause

Training‑Case Finding

The PAPI photometry failure was caused by combined optical degradation and beam‑angle misalignment, including:

  • Lens contamination.
  • Optical aging.
  • Filter deterioration.
  • Mechanical settlement.
  • Calibration drift.

The electrical system remained healthy, but the PAPI units no longer produced ICAO‑compliant intensity, transition sharpness, chromaticity, and glide‑path indication.

11Corrective Action

The maintenance team:

  1. Cleaned the optical assemblies.
  2. Replaced degraded optical components.
  3. Replaced aged filters.
  4. Recalibrated beam angles.
  5. Re‑leveled the PAPI units.
  6. Verified transition sharpness.
  7. Performed photometric testing.
  8. Confirmed chromaticity compliance.
  9. Conducted operational flight‑path verification.
  10. Updated PAPI calibration records.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: All PAPI units ON. Pilots report unstable glide‑path indications. Electrical system healthy. Reduced brightness visible. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace the power supply.
B. Increase the light intensity setting.
C. Measure photometric intensity, verify beam‑angle calibration, inspect optical assemblies, and check mechanical alignment.
D. Replace all PAPI units.
Correct answer: C. Unstable glide‑path indications with healthy electrical operation point to a photometric, optical, or angular problem. Photometric measurement, beam‑angle calibration, optical inspection, and mechanical alignment checking will identify the cause without unnecessary equipment replacement.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about PAPI photometry failure, beam angle and transition sharpness.

Q1What is AGL-CS-066?

AGL-CS-066 is an engineering training case study. It covers a PAPI system that was electrically healthy but gave an unreliable glide-path indication because of optical degradation and beam-angle misalignment. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2Can a PAPI be electrically healthy but still give wrong glide-path information?

Yes. All four units were illuminated with no power abnormalities, but degraded optics and beam-angle error produced unstable red/white transitions and an unreliable indication.

Q3What did the measurements show in AGL-CS-066?

Intensity was 14,000 cd against a representative training requirement of at least 24,000 cd. The beam angle was 3.18° against 3.00°, an error of +0.18°, and the red/white transition was broad and inconsistent between units.

Q4Why does PAPI depend on both photometry and angular alignment?

A PAPI signals glide path through sharp red/white transitions at precise angles. Low intensity, filter aging or a beam-angle error blurs or shifts those transitions, even when the units are lit.

Q5What caused the failure in AGL-CS-066?

Combined optical degradation and beam-angle misalignment: lens contamination, optical aging, filter deterioration, mechanical settlement and calibration drift. The electrical system stayed healthy.

Q6How did foundation settlement contribute?

Minor foundation settlement caused unit tilt, beam-axis displacement and inconsistent unit elevation, so mechanical alignment had changed over time.

Q7What was found during optical inspection?

Dust contamination, insect deposits, surface scratching and lens hazing, plus reduced red saturation and a white colour shift from filter aging.

Q8How was the PAPI restored?

The team cleaned the optical assemblies, replaced degraded components and aged filters, recalibrated beam angles and re-leveled the units. They then verified transition sharpness, photometry and chromaticity, conducted operational flight-path verification and updated calibration records.

Q9What should you investigate first when PAPI indications are unstable but the electrical system is healthy?

Measure photometric intensity, verify beam-angle calibration, inspect the optical assemblies and check mechanical alignment. Replacing the power supply or all PAPI units would not address the cause.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

PAPI photometry failure is a combined optical and geometric alignment problem caused by lens contamination, optical aging, filter degradation, mechanical settlement, and beam‑angle calibration errors, and a PAPI system can be electrically operational while providing incorrect glide‑path information to approaching aircraft.

Systematic photometric measurement, beam‑angle verification, transition‑sharpness assessment, chromaticity testing, optical inspection, and mechanical recalibration can identify PAPI performance degradation and restore ICAO‑compliant visual approach guidance.

In AGL engineering, a PAPI system is not judged by whether all four lights are illuminated, but by whether every pilot receives the correct glide‑path indication at the correct angle, with the correct color transition, every time.
