Overview

PAPI Maintenance Checklist

PAPI maintenance has to cover much more than confirming that all four units are lit. A proper programme examines the optical units, lenses, colour performance, aiming angles, elevation, alignment, electrical connections, isolation transformers, foundations and overall operation. A PAPI can stay electrically operational while developing an optical or alignment problem.

A unit can be ON but

DirtyIncorrectly aimedMechanically displacedProducing reduced intensityShowing abnormal colourAffected by moisture or optical deterioration

The process combines visual inspection + mechanical inspection + electrical testing + aiming verification + photometry + functional testing + documentation.

AAI maintenance specifications give practical examples: PAPI serviceability, damage or displacement, angle, lens condition, calibration, earthing, cable insulation and transformer connections. These are airport- and contract-specific schedules, not a universal maintenance frequency.

Visual

1. Visual Inspection

Start every job with a visual inspection to find obvious problems before detailed measurements. Inspect all four units, lenses, housing, mounting, foundation, cables, connectors, transformers and the surrounding area. Identify each unit individually so maintenance and retesting stay traceable.

Unit and housing

Look for physical damage, cracks, loose components, deformation, displaced housing, damaged adjustment mechanisms, missing hardware, corrosion, loose covers, damaged seals and missing fasteners. The housing protects both optics and electrics.

Lens

Inspect for cracks, scratches, clouding, discoloration, contamination, surface damage, moisture and deposits. A damaged lens can change intensity, beam shape, colour and coverage, so document the condition rather than writing “OK”.

Dirt and contamination

Dust, oil, insects, environmental deposits, construction dust and pollution. AAI schedules call for lens cleaning and condition checks. Use methods and materials compatible with the manufacturer’s requirements, and avoid abrasives that can damage optical surfaces. A cracked or degraded lens needs replacement, not cleaning.

Water ingress and corrosion

Check for condensation, standing moisture, water marks, corroded internals, wet connectors and damaged seals. Causes include a damaged gasket, poor sealing, cracked housing, blocked drainage or poor installation. Do not just remove water and return the unit to service. Find the cause. Corrosion on housing, brackets, fasteners, adjustment hardware, cable entries, transformer housing and supports can eventually affect alignment and connections.

Foundation

The aiming reference depends on the unit’s physical position. Look for cracking, settlement, movement, loose anchor bolts, damaged concrete, drainage problems and pavement deterioration around the foundation. A PAPI can be electrically healthy while its foundation has shifted.

Alignment

2. Alignment, Aiming and Elevation

Alignment is one of the most critical areas. Check it against the approved PAPI design, not an arbitrary generic angle.

Horizontal alignment

Verify that units keep the required relationship to the runway reference, the row stays correctly oriented and the optical axes point at the approach area. Causes of displacement: foundation movement, impact, loose mounting, maintenance activity, nearby construction, pavement works.

Vertical aiming

Each unit is aimed at a controlled elevation angle, and a small change moves the white/red transition. Use proper aiming and calibration equipment, not visual estimation. AAI documentation includes PAPI angle checks and calibration with instruments such as sighting sticks, clinometers and spirit levels.

Unit-to-unit relationship

Do not stop at “Unit 1 good, Unit 2 good…”. Verify unit 1 ↔ 2 ↔ 3 ↔ 4. The relative aiming relationship is critical to the indication.

Elevation

Verify reference, foundation and unit elevation and the approved aiming settings. Changes to runway profile, pavement level, threshold position or PAPI foundation can alter the relationship between PAPI and approach path.

Approach slope

The slope is runway-specific. Many AAI aerodrome publications show a 3.00° PAPI angle, but always take the value from the applicable runway design and current aeronautical information. Record the approved PAPI angle and the measured / verified angle, not just “3°”.

MEHT

Minimum Eye Height over Threshold is a runway-specific parameter, not a generic equipment value. Make sure no physical change has altered the PAPI’s relationship with the threshold, runway elevation, approach path or approved visual guidance geometry.

Electrical

3. Electrical Maintenance

An apparent optical problem can start as an electrical one. Check supply, cable, connectors, transformer, insulation, earthing and the control interface. Take exact electrical values from the PAPI manufacturer and the approved project documents. Do not use a generic voltage as a universal acceptance criterion.

Supply, cable and connectors

Check availability and correct circuit operation, abnormal fluctuations, brightness stages and control response. Inspect primary cable and secondary leads for insulation, damage, moisture and terminations. Cable faults cause intermittent operation, low output, complete failure and insulation problems. Inspect plugs and sockets for damage, contamination, moisture, loose connection and damaged sealing. AAI schedules include the plug/socket connection to the isolation-transformer lead.

Isolation transformer and earthing

Inspect the transformer for damage, overheating, moisture, cable-entry and connector condition and insulation. Test it with the circuit as the procedure requires. Do not blame the light unit before checking the transformer and secondary circuit. For earthing, review the connection, conductor, terminals, corrosion, continuity and recorded test values. AAI’s schedule has dedicated earthing checks.

Insulation testing

Where the approved procedure specifies it, test PAPI-associated cabling and record the result. AAI examples include periodic megohmmeter testing with recorded results. The test voltage and minimum acceptance value come from the applicable airport procedure and equipment specification. A value from one contract is not a universal PAPI requirement.

Photometry

4. Photometric Maintenance

Photometry gives objective evidence of optical condition, and it matters most when visual inspection cannot explain an abnormal indication.

Intensity

Check whether each unit gives the required output under the prescribed test condition. Low readings can come from a dirty lens, optical deterioration, damaged optics, LED or driver degradation, electrical problems, the wrong operating condition or the measurement setup. First confirm the measurement.

Colour

Verify the required white/red indication. Problems can come from filters, optical components, LED degradation, contamination or incorrect replacement parts. Where formal acceptance needs it, use objective chromaticity measurement.

Beam coverage

The beam must give the required angular coverage. A fitting can have adequate peak intensity but abnormal distribution, so peak intensity ≠ complete beam compliance.

Beam alignment

Alignment determines where the useful output goes. Problems cause an incorrect indication, reduced effective guidance, abnormal transition or apparent intensity loss. Read aiming verification and photometry together.

Final Check

5. Final Functional Test, Pilot Indication and Calibration

After physical, electrical and optical maintenance, run a complete functional test: all four units, the correct sequence of indications, correct white/red appearance, local and remote control, brightness stages and alarms where available. The final question: does the PAPI give the intended visual indication from the approach direction? This does not replace any required formal flight inspection or authority-approved calibration.

Maintenance

Routine work to preserve serviceability.

Ground calibration / aiming verification

Verification and adjustment of the installed physical and optical settings.

Formal flight inspection

Where the procedure or authority requires it, an airborne verification of the operational relationship with the approach path and related facilities.

An AAI operational circular describes ground calibration before flight calibration and checks such as range, setting angles, brilliancy control and compatibility with the ILS glide path. Confirm current requirements through the airport’s approved procedures and the competent authority.

      7. Maintenance Frequency

      No single frequency applies to every airport. Intervals can come from the approved airport maintenance programme, operational category, authority requirements, equipment manufacturer, contract, local environment and failure history. AAI contracts show daily, weekly, monthly and half-yearly activities. One current AAI schedule includes daily serviceability and physical checks, weekly angle and lens checks and half-yearly calibration, earthing, insulation and transformer-connection checks. Another combines routine lens and equipment checks with angle calibration, electrical checks and periodic earthing and wiring inspections. The airport’s approved schedule takes precedence.

      Triggers

      When to Check Immediately, and After Changes

      Check immediately after

      • Vehicle impact
      • Construction near the PAPI
      • Runway rehabilitation
      • Threshold relocation
      • Pavement work
      • Severe weather
      • Foundation disturbance
      • Equipment replacement
      • Major electrical work
      • An abnormal pilot report

      After runway changes

      Relocation, extension, resurfacing, pavement-level changes, PAPI relocation or a change in approach geometry need an engineering review. Do not just check that the PAPI still turns on. Decide whether its relationship to the approach path remains correct.

      After major repair or lens replacement

      Repeat the relevant electrical, mechanical, aiming, photometric and functional checks and retest. After a lens or optical component change, inspect the installation, verify optical alignment, check colour, run photometry where applicable and confirm operation.

      After foundation work

      Foundation work can change unit height, horizontal alignment, vertical aiming and the relationship between the four units. Recheck the complete PAPI geometry.

      Records

      Records, Trends and the Defect Register

      Photometric and condition records

      Track intensity, colour, aiming, alignment, defects, repairs and replacement dates. History shows normal → degradation → intervention → recovery instead of isolated inspections. Condition indicators can include optical trend (is intensity declining?), alignment trend (is the setting moving?), failure trend (does one unit keep failing?), electrical trend and environmental trend (moisture or corrosion). This supports predictive rather than reactive maintenance.

      A maintenance report should hold

      • Asset information: airport, runway, PAPI designation, unit numbers, model and serial numbers
      • Physical condition
      • Electrical results
      • Optical results and aiming
      • Functional results
      • Defects, corrective actions, replacements, adjustments
      • Retest date, result and status

      Wider System

      PAPI Within the Wider AGL System

      Electrical testing first

      A functional failure can be a transformer, cable or connector problem. Sequence: PAPI failure → AGL electrical testing → transformer / cable / connector → optical test → repair → retest. This avoids needless replacement of the light unit.

      Lightning and surge

      Repeated electronic or transformer failures, especially after severe weather, justify checking lightning protection, grounding, bonding, surge protection and counterpoise where applicable.

      Emergency power

      Where PAPI relies on secondary power, verify normal power, backup supply, UPS and DG where used, changeover and control status. Assess the complete chain, not an isolated fitting.

      CAT II / CAT III airports

      PAPI maintenance matters more where it forms part of a precision approach system. Coordinate with runway visual aids, approach lighting, centreline, touchdown-zone lighting, stop bars, AGL secondary power, low-visibility procedures and airport requirements. Serviceability criteria depend on the runway category and airport procedure.

      Airport operations

      Coordinate with ATC, airport operations, electrical maintenance, AGL control-room staff, flight inspection authorities where applicable and airside safety teams. Never switch, adjust or make a PAPI unavailable without the airport’s approved operational procedure.

      FAQ

      PAPI Maintenance FAQ

      Q1What should a PAPI maintenance checklist include?

      Visual condition, lenses, contamination, housing, moisture, corrosion, foundation, horizontal alignment, vertical aiming, elevation, electrical supply, cables, connectors, isolation transformer, earthing, insulation, photometry, colour, beam coverage, functional operation and documentation.

      Q2How often should PAPI be maintained?

      It depends on the approved airport maintenance programme, regulations, equipment requirements and contract. AAI schedules show that different activities can have different frequencies.

      Q3Does PAPI need aiming checks?

      Yes. Aiming is fundamental to PAPI operation and should be verified with appropriate survey or calibration equipment, not visual estimation alone.

      Q4What causes PAPI misalignment?

      Foundation movement, physical impact, loose mounting, construction activity, pavement changes and incorrect maintenance adjustment.

      Q5Does cleaning PAPI lenses affect performance?

      Yes. Contamination can reduce optical performance, and AAI maintenance schedules include routine lens cleaning.

      Q6Does PAPI require photometric testing?

      Optical performance can need objective measurement in commissioning, maintenance or acceptance, depending on the programme. Photometry can assess intensity, colour, coverage and beam characteristics.

      Q7Can a PAPI be ON and still be defective?

      Yes. Electrical operation does not prove that intensity, colour, aiming or beam characteristics are correct.

      Q8What electrical components should be checked?

      Depending on the installation: supply, CCR or circuit, primary and secondary cable, connector, isolation transformer, insulation and earthing.

      Q9Should PAPI be recalibrated after replacement?

      Check the replacement for correct installation, alignment and aiming, then apply the functional and photometric verification the system and airport require.

      Q10What should be done after major PAPI maintenance?

      Perform the applicable mechanical, electrical, aiming, photometric and functional checks, and complete any required retesting or formal calibration.

      Q11Does runway resurfacing affect PAPI?

      It can. Changes in runway elevation or threshold geometry can alter the relationship between PAPI and the intended approach path, so an engineering review may be needed.

      Q12Is PAPI maintenance different for LED systems?

      Some lamp-specific tasks change, but LED PAPI still needs inspection of optical surfaces, aiming, alignment, mechanical condition, electrical systems and optical performance.

      Q13What should happen after a vehicle strikes a PAPI unit?

      Remove it from service as the airport procedure requires, inspect for structural and optical damage, check it electrically, realign it and test it before return to service.

      Q14What records should be maintained?

      Asset identification, inspection dates, aiming results, photometric data, electrical test results, defects, corrective actions, replacement history and retest results.
