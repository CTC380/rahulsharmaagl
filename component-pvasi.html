PVASI

Pulsating Visual Approach Slope Indicator – a compact visual glide slope system that uses a single pulsating light unit to indicate the aircraft's position relative to the correct approach path. A steady light indicates the correct glide slope, while pulsating (flashing) lights indicate above or below the path. PVASI is an economical, low‑power system ideal for smaller airfields, heliports, and military forward operating bases.

Overview

What Is It?

The Pulsating Visual Approach Slope Indicator (PVASI) is a compact and economical visual glide slope guidance system that uses a single light unit with a pulsating (flashing) beam instead of the fixed red/white colour transitions used in PAPI or VASI. The system projects a beam that appears steady when the aircraft is on the correct glide path, and pulsates (flashes) at varying rates when the aircraft is above or below the path.

PVASI is one of the simplest and most cost‑effective visual glide slope systems available. It typically consists of a single elevated light unit with a precision optical system and a pulsating mechanism. The system is easy to install, requires minimal electrical power, and provides an intuitive indication that is easily understood by pilots. It is particularly well‑suited for smaller airfields, heliports, and military forward operating bases where a full PAPI or VASI is not justified.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

PVASI exists to give smaller airfields, heliports, and austere operating locations a working glide slope reference without the cost, complexity, and power demands of a multi-unit PAPI or VASI array. Its single-unit, pulsating design condenses the same essential message — on path, above path, or below path — into one fixture that a pilot reads by whether the beam is steady or flashing, rather than by comparing the colour pattern across several separated lights.

This simplicity is the point: at locations where traffic volume or budget doesn't support a full system, PVASI still gives approaching pilots a real, trustworthy glide slope cue rather than leaving them with no visual glide guidance at all, which matters most during night operations or marginal visibility when judging descent angle by the runway alone becomes unreliable.

Application

Where Is It Used?

PVASI is deployed on a variety of smaller and specialised landing sites:

  • General aviation airports – with low traffic volumes and limited budgets.
  • Heliports – where a compact, lightweight system is needed.
  • Remote and regional airstrips – with limited electrical infrastructure and space.
  • Military forward operating bases – requiring rapid deployment and simplicity.
  • Temporary or emergency runways – where a portable, lightweight system is advantageous.
  • As a backup or supplement to ILS – on larger runways for additional visual guidance.
Placement

Installation & Location

PVASI uses a single light unit mounted on one side of the runway, set back from the threshold at the distance needed to place the on-slope indication over the threshold at the correct height, and tied into the runway centreline and threshold survey. The unit sits on a frangible mount at a fixed lateral offset from the runway edge, clear of the obstacle-limitation surface, and is individually aimed and levelled during commissioning so the pulse-rate transitions occur at the correct angular deviations either side of the design glide path. Cabling runs underground back to its regulator and pulse-control unit, and the single-light footprint makes PVASI suited to sites where a full multi-unit bar system isn't warranted by traffic volume or budget.

Function

How Does It Work?

PVASI operates on a simple but effective principle using pulsating light to convey glide path information:

  • Optical Design: The system consists of a single light unit (or occasionally a small array) that projects a narrow beam along the desired glide path. The optical system is designed to produce a beam with a specific vertical angular coverage.
  • Pulsating Indication: Unlike colour‑based systems (PAPI, VASI), PVASI uses the rate of pulsation (flashing) to indicate the aircraft's position:
    • Steady (no pulsation): The aircraft is on the correct glide path.
    • Slow pulsation (e.g., 1–2 flashes per second): The aircraft is slightly above or below the glide path (depending on system configuration).
    • Fast pulsation (e.g., 4–6 flashes per second): The aircraft is significantly above or below the glide path, indicating a need for immediate correction.
    The specific pulsing pattern (steady vs. pulsating, and the rate of pulsation) provides an intuitive indication that does not rely on colour recognition, making it suitable for colour‑blind pilots.
  • Glide Path Angle: PVASI is typically set to a standard 3° glide path angle, but can be adjusted to suit specific approach requirements (2.5°–4°).
  • Power and Control: PVASI units are powered from the constant current series circuit via isolating transformers, with intensity controlled by the CCR (typically 3‑step). The pulsating mechanism is typically controlled by an internal electronic driver or an external control unit.
  • Integration: PVASI is typically deployed independently and can be easily integrated with the runway lighting system.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

PVASI splits its architecture in two: a conventional power path and an electronic angle-sensing path that neither PAPI nor VASI needs. The light unit itself is powered from the constant current series circuit through an isolating transformer exactly like other visual approach aids, with the CCR setting overall lamp brightness for day, twilight, or night — that part is fixed-current, loop-level control, same as always. What's different is the pulsating behaviour: an internal electronic driver (or an external control unit, depending on installation) modulates the beam on and off at a rate that depends on how far the aircraft's line of sight sits from the design glide path, rather than relying on a fixed optical colour split. Steady illumination means on-path; a slow flash means a small deviation; a fast flash means a large one. Because that logic lives in the driver electronics rather than in fixed lens geometry, PVASI's angular thresholds can be tuned without physically re-aiming the unit, but it also means the driver itself is an additional active component to monitor — its failure mode (stuck steady, stuck flashing, or dark) needs separate consideration from the loop-level lamp/transformer fault that the CCR already reports.

CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Light Unit → Pulse Driver (angle → pulse rate) → Beam

Signal Encoding

Deviation Read from Pulse Rate, Not Colour

Steady — On Glide Path No pulsation
Slow Pulsation — Slightly Off Path 1–2 flashes per second
Fast Pulsation — Significantly Off Path 4–6 flashes per second, correction needed now

PAPI and VASI encode position through colour (red vs. white); PVASI encodes it through timing alone, so a single white beam works for pilots who can't rely on colour discrimination — the trade-off is that direction (above vs. below) depends on system configuration rather than being visible directly in the beam itself.

Construction

Physical Construction

The PVASI unit is a single box-section housing in die-cast aluminium alloy or louvred galvanised steel, weatherproofed against UV exposure and de-icing fluid. Unlike a split red/white indicator, the optical assembly projects a single white beam whose apparent behaviour changes with the aircraft's position on the glide path, achieved through an internal pulsing circuit and lens/reflector geometry that produces a steady white signal on-path and a pulsating signal above or below it. A built-in levelling and pitch-adjustment mechanism is used to aim the unit to the set glide-path angle during commissioning. The housing mounts on a frangible mast at a fixed, surveyed height beside the runway, engineered to shear away cleanly if struck. Cabling runs underground in conduit to an isolating transformer housed in a nearby base can, with sealed, direct-burial connectors and a drainage provision to keep the transformer and cable entries free of standing water.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Beam Alignment

PVASI relies on a precisely aligned narrow beam. Even a small angular error can shift the effective glide path significantly. Regular alignment checks using precision optical tools are essential to maintain accurate indication.

Pulsating Mechanism Failure

The electronic driver or mechanical pulsating mechanism can fail, resulting in a steady light that does not indicate off‑path conditions. Regular inspection and testing of the pulsating function is critical.

Lens Contamination

Dust, rubber deposits, and de‑icing fluids can reduce light output and affect the clarity of the pulsating indication. Routine cleaning and photometric testing are required.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

PVASI units are mounted on frangible poles. Wind loading and thermal cycling can weaken couplings. Regular inspection ensures breakaway performance on impact.

Pilot Familiarity

PVASI is less common than PAPI or VASI, and some pilots may not be familiar with the pulsating indication. Airfields using PVASI should ensure pilots are aware of the system and its interpretation.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Light Unit Dark
Likely Cause
Failed LED module / lamp, or an open isolating transformer feeding the unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp/LED module if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the connectors before backfill.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at the unit, connector, or base can has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect the unit for cracked gaskets, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected unit/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Light Stuck Steady (No Pulsation on Deviation)
Likely Cause
The internal electronic pulse driver (or external control unit) has failed in a way that leaves the beam continuously illuminated regardless of glide-path deviation, which is a misleading indication since steady means on-path.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the driver's pulse output independently of the CCR-fed lamp circuit; simulate an off-path condition (per manufacturer test procedure) and confirm the beam pulsates as expected; check driver power supply and control wiring.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed pulse driver or external control unit; re-test pulsation response across the full deviation range before returning the unit to service.
Light Stuck Pulsating (Will Not Show Steady On-Path)
Likely Cause
The pulse driver has failed in a way that continuously modulates the beam even when the aircraft is on the correct glide path, which can cause pilots to misjudge their position.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the driver's pulse output; confirm whether the fault is in the driver's angle-sensing logic or in the pulse-generation circuit itself; check for a stuck relay or switching component in the driver.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed pulse driver; re-test that the unit shows steady illumination when correctly positioned on the glide path before returning to service.
Incorrect Pulse Rate for Given Deviation
Likely Cause
The pulse driver's angle-sensing calibration has drifted, causing the flash rate to no longer correspond correctly to the actual glide-path deviation.
Diagnostic Steps
Carry out a ground or flight check comparing indicated pulse rate against actual aircraft position at known deviation points; check driver calibration settings against manufacturer specification.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the driver's angle thresholds per manufacturer procedure, or replace the driver if recalibration does not restore correct pulse-rate behaviour.
Reduced or Hazy Light Output
Likely Cause
Contaminated or eroded lens, an aging LED array or halogen lamp, or the CCR running on a lower brightness step than the operational requirement.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the lens; confirm the CCR is set to the correct intensity step for prevailing conditions.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the lens, replace a degraded lamp/LED module, or correct the CCR step setting; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Pulse Driver Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Failure of the internal electronic driver (or external control unit) that modulates the beam according to angular deviation, from component wear, a firmware fault, or a power-supply transient to the driver circuit.
Effect
The unit gets stuck steady when it should be pulsing, stuck flashing when it should be steady, or goes dark — a failure mode unique to PVASI's active angle-sensing electronics and not covered by the CCR's loop-level lamp/transformer fault reporting.
Detection Method
Flight or ground inspection comparing the observed pulse pattern against the known aircraft position, and driver self-diagnostic or status indication where fitted.
LED Module / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Because PVASI relies on a single light unit rather than a multi-unit bar, a lamp failure removes the entire glide-slope indication for that runway, not just a fraction of it.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and visual confirmation the unit is alight.
Isolating Transformer Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop, darkening the single light unit entirely.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Angular Threshold Miscalibration
High Severity
Root Cause
Drift in the driver electronics' angle-to-pulse-rate mapping over time, or an incorrect setpoint entered during commissioning or a subsequent adjustment.
Effect
The pulse-rate transitions (steady, slow, fast) no longer correspond to the correct angular deviations, giving pilots an inaccurate glide-slope cue even though the unit appears to be operating normally.
Detection Method
Flight inspection verifying the pulse-rate transitions occur at the correct angular deviations either side of the design glide path.
Series Cable / Connector Insulation Breakdown
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage, corroded splices, or prolonged standing water in the duct run back to the regulator and pulse-control unit.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR, or an intermittent partial fault that degrades insulation resistance.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test and sectionalised testing to pinpoint the fault location.
Frangible Mount Fatigue
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic loading from mower or vehicle contact, material fatigue over the service interval, or corrosion at the mount threads.
Effect
The single unit leans, loosens, or separates from its base, which — since PVASI has no second unit to fall back on — removes the entire indication.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the mount, and periodic aim/level checks against tolerance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The unit runs on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling cable connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near an active runway requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM while the unit is dark or out of tolerance; because PVASI has no second unit to fall back on, taking it out of service removes the entire glide-slope indication for the runway.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible mount with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the mount must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

Verify Pulse-Rate Calibration After Driver Work
Operational

After replacing or adjusting the pulse driver electronics, confirm the angle-to-pulse-rate transitions occur at the correct angular deviations before returning the unit to service; a miscalibrated driver can look normal while giving an inaccurate glide-slope cue.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the base can is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the fixture, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage the pulse-control electronics.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the fixture and base can are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the unit out of aim tolerance, at reduced intensity, or with an unverified pulse-rate calibration after maintenance; confirm photometric, positional, and calibration conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Pulsation Rate Drift Is a Hard Fault, Not a Cosmetic One
Calibration

Unlike PAPI's discrete red/white boxes, PVASI's entire cue lives in the pulsation timing electronics; a slow drift in pulse rate is much harder to catch by eye than a colour change, so verify pulse timing with instrumentation rather than counting flashes visually.

No Second Unit to Cross-Check a Developing Fault
Design

With only one light unit, there's no independent second reference to compare against during a walk-by inspection; lean more heavily on scheduled photometric and timing verification than on visual comparison, which has nothing to compare against.

Irregular Pulsing Is the Early Warning, Not Silence
Maintenance

A pulsation mechanism nearing failure often produces irregular or asymmetric flash patterns well before it stops working outright; treat irregular pulsing as an early fault indicator rather than a minor cosmetic issue to defer.

Steady-State and Pulsation Thresholds Calibrate Separately
Calibration

The on-slope steady beam and the above/below pulsation thresholds are set by different parts of the control electronics; recalibrating one without checking the other can leave the unit indicating on-slope inaccurately even though pulsation still triggers correctly at the extremes.

Economical Power Supplies Have Less Surge Tolerance
Electrical

Because PVASI is chosen for low cost, some installations pair it with a lighter-duty power supply that's more vulnerable to network surges damaging the pulsation control electronics than the more robust supplies used with PAPI; size isolating transformer or surge protection to the local network quality, not just the fixture's low draw.

Smaller Airfields Need an Explicit Photometric Interval
Compliance

Because PVASI is often chosen for smaller, lighter-budget airfields, units sometimes get checked visually but not photometrically for extended periods; specify a photometric verification interval explicitly rather than assuming the operator will default to a standard PAPI cycle.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about PVASI: pulsating signals, specifications, circuits, standards, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is a PVASI?

The Pulsating Visual Approach Slope Indicator is a compact, economical glide slope guidance system that uses a single light unit with a pulsating beam instead of the red/white colour transitions of PAPI or VASI. The beam appears steady when the aircraft is on the correct path.

Q2Why is PVASI used?

It gives smaller airfields, heliports and austere locations a working glide slope reference without the cost, complexity and power demand of a multi-unit PAPI or VASI array.

Q3How does a pilot read PVASI signals?

A steady white light indicates the aircraft is on the glide path. Off-path conditions are shown by a slow pulse (about 1-2 Hz) or a fast pulse (about 4-6 Hz), so position is conveyed by pulsation rate rather than colour.

Q4Where is PVASI installed?

General aviation airports with low traffic and limited budgets, heliports needing a compact system, remote and regional airstrips with limited electrical infrastructure, and military forward operating bases.

Q5What are the typical PVASI specifications?

An LED array or 45/65 W halogen lamp, white light up to 10,000 cd, a 3 degree glide path (adjustable 2.5-4 degrees), one unit or two for redundancy, and frangible elevated mounting typically 10-20 m from the runway edge.

Q6How is a PVASI powered and controlled?

It runs on a constant current series circuit through a 45-65 W isolating transformer. A dedicated flasher or control circuit drives the pulsation independently of the CCR intensity step.

Q7What photometric performance is expected?

On-axis intensity of at least 6,000 cd up to 10,000 cd at full intensity, white chromaticity within the ICAO Annex 14 boundary, a ±25% field tolerance at each CCR step, and pulsation rates verified at the control unit.

Q8Which standards apply to PVASI?

ICAO Annex 14 (guidance for pulsating visual approach slope indicators), FAA AC 150/5340-25 (closest FAA equivalent guidance), IEC/TS 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief #67.

Q9How often should a PVASI be maintained?

Quarterly lens cleaning; semi-annual pulsating mechanism test through the steady, slow and fast rates; and annual beam alignment, photometric check, frangible coupling inspection, insulation resistance test and cable and connector inspection.

Q10What if the PVASI light unit is dark?

The usual causes are a failed LED module or lamp, or an open isolating transformer. Confirm series loop continuity through the transformer primary, megger the transformer, and check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
