Approach Light Junction Box

A robust, weatherproof enclosure designed to protect critical electrical connections and isolating transformers within approach lighting systems. Engineered to withstand extreme weather, chemical exposure, and mechanical stress, the junction box ensures the integrity and reliability of the series lighting circuit in the harshest airfield environments.

Overview

What Is It?

An Approach Light Junction Box is a fundamental yet critical component of the airfield ground lighting (AGL) infrastructure. It serves as a central connection point for primary and secondary cables, housing splices, terminations, and providing an accessible interface to isolating transformers. Built from materials such as glass-fiber reinforced polyester (GRP), polycarbonate, or cast aluminium/steel, these enclosures are designed to provide a watertight and corrosion-resistant environment for electrical connections.

The junction box acts as the backbone of the series lighting circuit, ensuring electrical continuity while providing mechanical protection and preventing moisture ingress. It facilitates maintenance and troubleshooting by providing organised access to circuit connections, making it an indispensable asset for the reliable operation of approach lighting systems (ALSF, MALSF, Calvert, etc.).

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The approach light junction box's purpose is to give the extensive network of cable splices and terminations that an approach lighting system depends on a protected, organised, and accessible home, rather than leaving them buried and unreachable or exposed to the elements. Because an approach lighting array like ALSF-2 involves hundreds of individual connections between primary cable, isolating transformers, and fixtures spread across a long approach path, the junction box's watertight, corrosion-resistant enclosure is what keeps each of those connections electrically sound over years of ground moisture and temperature cycling, while its accessible lid means a technician can open, test, and re-seal a specific connection during troubleshooting without having to dig up or replace the whole run of cable.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Approach light junction boxes are deployed across all types of approach lighting systems that require secure and protected electrical connections:

  • ALSF‑1 and ALSF‑2 systems – protecting connections for centreline, crossbar, barrette, and SFL fixtures in CAT I, II, and III precision approaches.
  • MALSF and MALSR systems – providing weatherproof enclosures for medium‑intensity approach lighting connections.
  • Calvert I and II systems – housing connections for barrette and crossbar configurations.
  • Runway and Taxiway Lighting – used extensively in edge and centreline lighting circuits.
  • Equipment Shelters – mounted on concrete pads inside shelters to protect transformer and splice connections.
  • Direct Burial Applications – buried underground to maintain airfield surface clearance while providing access points for maintenance.
Placement

Installation & Location

Approach light junction boxes are sited at intervals along the approach lighting cable run, at the base of each structure or grouped where several fixtures share a nearby splice point, breaking the primary and secondary cabling into manageable sections between the CCR and the fixtures. Buried units sit in a handhole or pit set to grade in the often unpaved or rough ground beneath the approach lighting array, sealed against water ingress and set out so they remain locatable and accessible despite being off any paved surface. Where a box is mounted inside an equipment shelter, it sits on a concrete pad close to the transformer and CCR feed it interfaces with, and box positions throughout are tied into the same survey used to set out the structures and cabling above them.

Function

How Does It Work?

The approach light junction box operates by providing a secure, organised, and protected environment for electrical connections within the series circuit:

  • Cable Termination: Primary and secondary cables enter the box through cable glands or knockouts. Inside, they are terminated to terminal blocks, isolating transformers, or directly spliced using insulated connectors.
  • Transformer Housing: In many configurations, the junction box houses the isolating transformer itself, providing a compact, protected assembly that simplifies installation and maintenance.
  • Environmental Protection: The enclosure is sealed to IP67 or IP68 standards, preventing ingress of water, dust, and de‑icing chemicals. This ensures long-term reliability of the electrical connections.
  • Mechanical Protection: The robust construction protects internal components from physical damage due to excavation, ground movement, or vehicle loading.
  • Accessibility: The box features a removable or hinged lid with secure fastenings (stainless steel captive screws), allowing authorised personnel to access the connections for testing, maintenance, and fault finding.
  • Earthing: Provides a designated earthing point to ensure the safety of the circuit and protection against lightning strikes.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Junction boxes sit at the physical joints of the approach lighting series loop and are, functionally, the sectionalising points a technician uses when the ALCMS reports a loop-level fault. Because the CCR's fault detection only identifies that loop impedance has risen, not where, fault-finding in practice works backward through the string of junction boxes: opening each box in turn and taking a loop-resistance or insulation reading isolates which segment of cable, which transformer, or which fixture is responsible. Where the box houses the isolating transformer itself, it also becomes the natural point to swap a failed transformer without disturbing the fixture or the buried primary cable. The box's own contribution to reliability is therefore indirect but structural — every termination inside it is a potential source of the very high-resistance faults the CCR is designed to detect, which is why sealing to IP67/IP68 and using corrosion-resistant, captive-screw connections matters as much to the control architecture's fault-reporting accuracy as it does to weatherproofing.

Construction

Physical Construction

The enclosure body is moulded from glass-fibre reinforced polyester (GRP), polycarbonate, or cast aluminium/steel, chosen for a combination of mechanical strength and resistance to de-icing chemicals, salt spray, and jet fuel. Cable enters through multiple pre-installed glands or knockouts (typically M20, M25, or M32) sized to the primary and secondary conductors passing through, and gasketed lids sealed with stainless-steel captive screws or a padlock hasp give the box its IP67 (temporary immersion) or IP68 (continuous immersion) rating. Inside, terminal blocks or sealed splice connectors organise the primary-to-transformer and transformer-to-fixture connections in a layout that a technician can trace without disturbing adjacent circuits, and an integrated earthing terminal ties the enclosure into the site's grounding system. Depending on the installation, the box is set for direct burial, mounted on a concrete pad, or fixed to a pole, with the same enclosure design serving all three.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Water Ingress and Seal Failure

The most frequent issue is water ingress due to failed gaskets, improperly tightened cable glands, or cracked enclosures. Moisture leads to corrosion of connections and insulation breakdown. Regular inspection of seals and proper tightening of glands are essential.

Corrosion of Internal Connections

Despite IP ratings, condensation or minor leaks can cause corrosion of terminal blocks and copper conductors. Using anti-oxidation compound and ensuring proper cable preparation can mitigate this. Regular thermal imaging can help identify hot spots.

Physical Damage from Excavation

Junction boxes buried at shallow depths are vulnerable to damage from excavation or surface works. Marking cable routes and using concrete covers are essential. Any excavation near the box should be preceded by cable location.

Cable Gland Loosening

Vibration and thermal cycling can cause cable glands to loosen over time, compromising the IP seal. Regular torque checks and re-tightening of glands are recommended as part of the maintenance schedule.

Condensation and Heat Dissipation

Internal condensation can occur due to temperature fluctuations, leading to moisture accumulation. Using breathable drain plugs or desiccant packs, and ensuring proper ventilation (where specified), can help manage internal humidity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Moisture Found Inside the Box During Inspection
Likely Cause
A failed gasket, an improperly tightened cable gland, or a cracked enclosure has allowed water ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect all seals, gaskets, and cable glands for damage or looseness; check the enclosure body for cracking.
Corrective Action
Replace failed gaskets or the enclosure, and re-tighten cable glands to the specified torque before re-sealing.
Corrosion Found on Terminal Blocks or Conductors
Likely Cause
Condensation or a minor leak has caused corrosion inside the box despite its IP rating.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect terminal blocks and conductors for corrosion; use thermal imaging under load to identify hot spots caused by degraded connections.
Corrective Action
Clean corroded terminals, re-terminate with anti-oxidation compound applied, and address the source of moisture identified during inspection.
Box Found Cracked or Displaced Near Recent Ground Works
Likely Cause
Excavation or surface works near the shallow-buried junction box has caused physical damage.
Diagnostic Steps
Check recent work records against the cable/box route map, and inspect the box for cracking or displacement.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged box, and improve route marking and concrete cover protection to prevent recurrence.
IP Seal Compromised Without Obvious Damage
Likely Cause
Vibration and thermal cycling have gradually loosened a cable gland over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Check gland torque against the specified value as part of the routine maintenance schedule.
Corrective Action
Re-tighten glands to the specified torque, and add gland torque checks to the recurring maintenance schedule if not already included.
Internal Moisture Accumulation Without an External Seal Failure
Likely Cause
Temperature fluctuations have caused internal condensation inside the sealed enclosure.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for the presence and condition of breathable drain plugs or desiccant packs, and inspect ventilation provisions where specified.
Corrective Action
Install or replace breathable drain plugs/desiccant packs, and ensure any specified ventilation path is clear.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Enclosure Seal / Gasket Failure Allowing Moisture Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Degraded lid gasket or a cracked enclosure seal from age, UV exposure, or repeated opening during troubleshooting.
Effect
Moisture reaches the splices and terminations inside, the exact failure the watertight enclosure exists to prevent, risking multiple connection faults at once.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the gasket and enclosure seal, and standing-water check inside the box during scheduled maintenance.
Splice / Termination Corrosion
High Severity
Root Cause
Years of ground moisture and temperature cycling attacking an individual splice or termination inside the box, even where the enclosure seal itself remains sound.
Effect
Intermittent operation or an open/ground fault at that specific connection, affecting the fixture or circuit segment it serves.
Detection Method
Visual inspection and resistance testing of individual splices and terminations during scheduled maintenance, without needing to disturb the rest of the run.
Lid / Access Hardware Seizure or Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion or debris seizing the lid fasteners, or physical damage to the access hardware from repeated field use.
Effect
A technician can't open, test, or re-seal the box during troubleshooting without additional effort or damage, undermining the accessible-by-design purpose of the junction box.
Detection Method
Lid hardware operability check during scheduled maintenance, with lubrication or hardware replacement as needed.
Thermal-Cycling Fatigue at Connection Points
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Repeated expansion and contraction of terminated conductors through seasonal temperature swings gradually loosening a connection over years of service.
Effect
A connection that tested sound initially degrades slowly into an intermittent fault, harder to catch than an outright failure.
Detection Method
Torque check and resistance testing of terminations on a periodic schedule rather than only when a fault is reported.
Overheating From Overcrowded or Poorly Dressed Cable Runs
Low Severity
Root Cause
Too many conductors routed through a box not sized for the load, or poor cable dressing restricting heat dissipation inside the enclosure.
Effect
Elevated internal temperature accelerating insulation ageing at the splices, shortening their service life.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of cable fill and dressing against the box's rated capacity during installation and maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The junction box houses splices carrying constant-current series loop power that can reach several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the box before opening it or handling connections inside.

Confirm the Correct Box on Multi-Circuit Arrays
Operational

Approach lighting arrays (ALSF, MALSF, Calvert) often route several circuits through adjacent boxes; positively identify the correct box before isolating work, since opening the wrong one can leave live conductors exposed while the intended circuit stays dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice or termination work, megger test the affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the box and cable shielding are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting.

Seal Integrity on Reinstatement
Electrical Safety

Renew gaskets and properly seat cable glands on reassembly; a compromised seal on a GRP, polycarbonate, or cast-iron enclosure allows moisture into the splices it is meant to protect.

FOD Control at the Enclosure
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the box is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent foreign object debris on the approach light array or movement area.

Buried or Frangible-Zone Access
Operational

Follow the aerodrome's permit-to-dig and airside access procedures where the box is buried or sited within the approach lighting frangible zone, and check for water or debris accumulation before opening it.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a junction box with a loose connection, an unsealed gland, or a mis-seated cover after maintenance; verify insulation resistance and secure closure before closing out the work order.

Quick Reference

Airport Lighting Junction Box

An airport lighting junction box is a sealed enclosure that holds the cable joints, splices and terminations of an airfield lighting circuit. On approach lighting lines it is the central connection point between the primary and secondary cables and the isolating transformers, and it gives a place to open the circuit for testing or fault finding.

It must stay watertight and accessible. Check the seals, cable entries and lid on every inspection, and record any signs of water ingress, because moisture in a junction box is a common cause of insulation faults. For the general-purpose version, see the junction box page.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Gasket Condition Matters More Than the Enclosure's Rated Material
Durability

A GRP or cast-iron junction box body can be structurally sound for decades while its lid gasket has hardened and lost its seal years earlier; moisture ingress in most junction boxes traces back to a degraded gasket rather than a failure of the enclosure material itself, so inspect gasket condition at every access, not just at overhaul.

Trend Loop Insulation Resistance at Every Box Access
Troubleshooting

Since the junction box is where splices and terminations are made and re-made over the life of the circuit, it's the natural point to take insulation resistance readings; logging these over successive visits catches a slowly failing splice long before it becomes a loop-down fault.

Verify Bonding Continuity Through the Box, Not Just Around It
Electrical

Where a junction box forms part of the earthing/bonding path for isolating transformers or metallic conduit, a poorly re-seated lid or a painted-over bonding point can silently break continuity through the box even though the enclosure looks mechanically intact; verify continuity across the box after any lid removal, not just at initial installation.

Keep Splices Organised — Future Troubleshooting Depends on It
Field Practice

A junction box with unlabelled or haphazardly arranged splices turns a routine fault-find into a much longer job for whoever opens it next; maintaining clear, consistent labelling and dressing of conductors inside the box pays off directly the next time someone has to troubleshoot that circuit under time pressure.

Pavement Loading Can Crack an Undersized or Poorly Bedded Box
Installation

A junction box installed in or near a trafficked pavement area needs a load rating and bedding suited to the actual traffic it will see, including snow-clearing and maintenance vehicles; an undersized or poorly bedded box can crack under repeated loading even if it never experiences a single overload event.

Confirm the Box Is Genuinely Closed Out Before Leaving Site
Compliance

A lid that's set back in place but not fully fastened, or a gland left loose after a cable change, is easy to miss visually and can undo the box's ingress protection entirely; confirm all fasteners are torqued and glands are sealed before closing out the work, not just that the lid appears seated.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about approach light junction boxes: construction, IP ratings, transformer housing, standards, installation, testing, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an approach light junction box?

It is a robust, weatherproof enclosure that serves as the connection point for primary and secondary cables in approach lighting, housing splices and terminations and providing accessible access to isolating transformers. It protects the electrical connections of the series lighting circuit from moisture, chemicals and mechanical stress.

Q2How does an approach light junction box work?

Primary and secondary cables enter through cable glands or knockouts and are terminated on terminal blocks, connected to isolating transformers, or spliced with insulated connectors. The enclosure is sealed to IP67 or IP68, has a hinged or removable lid with stainless-steel captive screws for authorised access, and provides a designated earthing point.

Q3What is a junction box made of and what IP rating does it have?

Typical materials are glass-fibre reinforced polyester (GRP), polycarbonate or cast aluminium/steel, chosen for strength and resistance to de-icing chemicals, salt spray and jet fuel. The typical ingress protection is IP67 (temporary immersion) or IP68 (continuous immersion), with a temperature range of -40°C to +70°C and cable entries typically M20, M25 or M32.

Q4Does the junction box contain the isolating transformer?

It can. In one configuration the cables are terminated to terminal blocks or spliced and the transformer sits outside the box. In another, the isolating transformer sits inside the box alongside the terminations as one compact, protected assembly.

Q5How are approach light junction boxes mounted?

Typical mountings are direct burial in a handhole or pit set to grade, on a concrete pad (including inside equipment shelters), or pole-mounted. Boxes are sited at intervals along the cable run, at the base of each structure or grouped where several fixtures share a nearby splice point.

Q6How do junction boxes help with fault-finding?

They sit at the physical joints of the series loop and act as sectionalising points. The CCR's fault detection only shows that loop impedance has risen, not where, so technicians use the boxes along the run to break the circuit into sections and narrow down the fault location.

Q7Which standards apply to approach light junction boxes?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-42 (airport light bases, transformer housings, junction boxes and accessories), IEC 60529 (IP code) and UL 50E (enclosures for electrical equipment). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8What are the key installation requirements for a junction box?

Set the box at the design position and depth for its mounting type, fit and seal each cable gland (M20, M25 or M32) with adequate strain relief, and lay out splices and terminals so a technician can trace them without disturbing adjacent circuits. Connect the earthing terminal and confirm continuity, and fit the gasketed lid with stainless-steel captive screws or a padlock hasp to achieve the rated IP67/IP68 protection.

Q9How should a junction box be tested at commissioning?

Check watertightness of the lid, gaskets and glands before backfill, verify continuity and polarity at every splice and termination, and megger-test cables and splices (at least 50 MΩ). Where the box houses an isolating transformer connection, confirm secondary voltage against the rated ratio, then verify earthing continuity and gland torque and record the results in the commissioning dossier.

Q10How often should a junction box be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for seal and gasket inspection, cable gland torque checks, internal connection and thermal imaging checks, and route marking and cover verification, and semi-annual for desiccant and breathable drain plug inspection. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11Why is there moisture inside my junction box?

The most frequent causes are failed gaskets, improperly tightened cable glands or a cracked enclosure. Inspect all seals, gaskets and glands and check the body for cracks, then replace failed gaskets or the enclosure and re-tighten glands to the specified torque. If there is no external seal failure, condensation from temperature swings may be the cause, and breathable drain plugs or desiccant packs help manage it.

Q12What safety precautions apply when opening an approach light junction box?

The box houses splices carrying series loop power that can reach several kV, so de-energise and lock out the CCR and verify zero energy before opening it. Positively identify the correct box on multi-circuit arrays, follow permit-to-dig and airside access procedures for buried boxes, renew gaskets and reseat glands on reassembly, control FOD, and megger-test the circuit before restoring power.

Q13What is an airport lighting junction box?

An airport lighting junction box is a sealed enclosure that holds cable joints, splices and terminations in an airfield lighting circuit. It connects primary and secondary cables and isolating transformers, keeps the connections dry and protected, and gives a point where the circuit can be opened for testing and fault finding.
