PAPI Siting & Installation Comparison

Elevated Installation VS Other Configurations

Elevated installation mounts each PAPI light unit above the surrounding ground or pavement — the conventional arrangement seen on most runways. Other configurations — raised mounts for snow areas, temporary mounts, and flush or recessed concepts that place the optic at or below the surface — are special cases: the standard PAPI equipment specification is written around the elevated, frangible-mounted unit. The optical principle never changes; what changes is how the equipment is physically installed and protected.

02 · Elevated PAPI

The Conventional Arrangement

Elevated installation is the conventional arrangement where each PAPI light unit sits on a support structure above the ground, with the four units normally installed in a horizontal row so the pilot reads them together as one continuous indicator. Each unit stands on adjustable mounting legs with frangible couplings, fixed to a concrete pad, so that it breaks away cleanly if struck (FAA AC 150/5345-28H); the same principle applies to visual aids near the runway under ICAO Annex 14 and Doc 9157 Part 4.

03 · Why It's Standard

Why Elevated Installation Is Common

The elevated arrangement provides several practical advantages that make it the default choice on most runway projects:

  • Clear optical field of view
  • Easy access for inspection
  • Easy aiming and alignment
  • Easy cleaning
  • Easy replacement of components
  • Less risk of the optical window being covered by surface contamination
  • Simple cable routing and equipment access
  • Frangible mounting, so an impact breaks the unit away cleanly
  • Can be raised where snow would otherwise obscure the beam

The units can be positioned accurately to establish the required approach-slope indications for the specific runway.

04 · Main Disadvantage

Physical Exposure of Elevated Units

The light unit and its support are physically exposed, so the installation must be located and protected in accordance with the applicable aerodrome design requirements. Potential concerns include:

  • Vehicle/equipment impact
  • Debris if the unit is struck and breaks away (the frangible design limits this)
  • Jet blast effects
  • Snow and ice loading on the unit (the FAA specification requires the beam not to shift under it)
  • Grass/vegetation around the unit
  • Exposure to weather
  • Damage to support structures
05 · Flush / Recessed Configuration

Reducing the Above-Surface Profile

A recessed or flush arrangement places the optical/light-emitting portion closer to the pavement or ground surface, with the objective of reducing the physical obstruction above the surface.

A recessed installation is not simply a conventional PAPI unit buried in the pavement. The optical geometry, housing, drainage, thermal management, structural loading and certification must all be suitable for that specific equipment and installation.

Flush/recessed is not a standard PAPI equipment class. FAA AC 150/5345-28H defines PAPI light units as units on adjustable mounting legs with frangible couplings, installed on a concrete pad. A flush or recessed PAPI therefore sits outside that specification — it would need a purpose-designed product accepted by the regulator and the airport, not just a different mounting.

06 · Physical Exposure

Elevated vs Recessed Exposure

Elevated

More exposed above the pavement — clear optics and easy access, but a higher physical profile facing vehicles, jet blast and ground equipment, mitigated by frangible mounting.

Recessed / Flush

Less exposed to impact — but at the cost of more complex housing, drainage and structural design, and greater vulnerability to snow, slush and debris covering the optic.

07 · Maintenance

Getting to the Unit

Elevated PAPI

Maintenance is relatively straightforward — the technician has direct access to the unit.

  • Inspect
  • Clean
  • Align
  • Test
  • Replace

Recessed PAPI

Access typically runs through a cover or pit before reaching the optical unit, which can make maintenance more involved.

  • Access cover / pit
  • Inspect & clean the optical unit
  • Drainage check
  • Alignment
  • Test
08 · Drainage

Why Drainage Matters More for Recessed Units

Drainage becomes particularly important when equipment is installed at or below pavement level. Poor drainage can result in:

  • Water accumulation
  • Condensation
  • Corrosion
  • Electrical problems
  • Optical contamination
  • Freezing problems in cold climates

This is one reason recessed installations require careful civil and electrical coordination that a standard elevated installation does not need to the same degree.

09 · Optical Alignment

Alignment Is Critical for Every Configuration

The physical installation must maintain the required elevation and azimuth alignment regardless of how the unit is mounted. For an elevated installation, adjustment is generally straightforward. For a recessed/special installation, the mounting structure must still maintain the required angular accuracy.

The installation configuration must never compromise the specified PAPI optical geometry — this holds true whichever mounting arrangement is used.

10 · Pavement & Runway Environment

Sitting Above vs Sitting Flush

Elevated

The unit sits outside the pavement surface, in the runway strip environment rather than on the paved area itself.

Flush / Recessed

This can be advantageous where above-ground equipment presents an obstruction or clearance concern.

11 · Jet Blast & Operating Environment

What the Installation Has to Withstand

PAPI installations are exposed to the operating environment around the runway, so the installation should consider:

  • Jet blast
  • Propeller wash
  • Vehicle movement
  • Maintenance equipment
  • FOD
  • Ground maintenance activities

Elevated units are physically more exposed to these factors, while recessed configurations can reduce the physical profile presented to them.

12 · Important Distinction

"Other Configuration" Is Not One Standard Type

Unlike the 4-unit vs 2-unit distinction, "other PAPI installation configurations" is not necessarily a single standardized category. Possible arrangements can vary according to:

  • Standard elevated installation
  • Special mounting arrangements
  • Side-of-runway constraints
  • Structural support requirements
  • Recessed/flush equipment designed for the application (non-standard)
  • Raised mounting where snow would obscure the beam (top of unit up to 3 m per FAA Order 6850.2B)
  • Temporary or portable units on temporary mounting legs, where the manufacturer offers them
  • Installation on one or both sides where applicable

The exact configuration should always be checked against the applicable ICAO/FAA requirements and the specific PAPI manufacturer's approved installation design — it is never assumed from a generic description.

13 · Common Mix-Up

Elevated PAPI vs Elevated Approach Lights

Do not confuse elevated PAPI with elevated approach lighting. PAPI is specifically an approach-slope indicator, giving the red/white vertical-slope indication. Approach lighting provides runway/approach visual alignment and identification — a different function using a different light pattern along the extended centreline.

15 · Selection Logic

Which Configuration Fits the Site

Choose Elevated When

  • Standard runway environment
  • Adequate clearance is available
  • Easy maintenance is preferred
  • No significant vehicle/ground-equipment conflict
  • Standard installation is acceptable

Consider Recessed/Special When

  • Above-ground obstruction is a concern
  • Ground equipment interaction is significant
  • Pavement/clearance constraints exist
  • A specific approved recessed PAPI product is available
  • Civil/drainage requirements can be properly implemented
16 · Engineering Verdict

Elevated vs Other Configurations

Engineering Verdict

Elevated installation is the simple, accessible, conventional way to mount a PAPI — the light units sit above ground on frangible mounts with a clear field of view and easy access for alignment and maintenance, and it is the arrangement the standard PAPI equipment specification is written around. Recessed, flush or other special configurations trade that simplicity for a reduced physical profile, at the cost of greater installation, civil, drainage and maintenance considerations.

In every configuration the priority is the same: maintain the required PAPI optical geometry, photometric performance, alignment and obstacle-clearance requirements.

ElevatedEasy access + easy alignment + more exposed.
Recessed/OtherProtected + low profile + more complex installation.

Do not design a PAPI installation from a generic diagram. The final siting, mounting and drainage design must always come from the applicable governing standard and the specific manufacturer's approved installation.

17 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between elevated and recessed PAPI installation?

Elevated installation mounts the light unit above the ground on a support structure. Recessed or flush installation places the optic at or near pavement level. The underlying PAPI optical principle is identical in both cases — only the physical mounting and protection differ.

Is a recessed PAPI just a normal unit buried in the pavement?

No. A recessed installation requires purpose-built optical geometry, housing, drainage, thermal management, structural loading and certification suited to that specific equipment — it is not a standard elevated unit simply placed below grade.

Why is elevated installation the standard choice?

It gives a clear optical field of view, easy aiming and alignment, easy cleaning and component replacement, and simple cable routing — all of which keep installation cost and maintenance effort lower than special or recessed designs. It also matches the equipment specification itself: FAA AC 150/5345-28H defines PAPI light units on adjustable, frangible-coupled mounting legs.

Why does drainage matter more for recessed PAPI units?

Because the equipment sits at or below pavement level, poor drainage can cause water accumulation, condensation, corrosion, electrical faults, optical contamination and freezing — issues an elevated unit is far less exposed to.

Is "other configuration" a single standardized PAPI installation type?

No. Unlike the 4-unit vs 2-unit distinction, "other configurations" covers a range of possible arrangements — special mounts, side-of-runway constraints, or recessed/flush designs — and must be checked against the applicable ICAO/FAA requirements and the specific manufacturer's approved design.

Is there a standard flush or recessed PAPI?

Not in the FAA equipment specification, which defines PAPI light units as elevated units on frangible legs mounted on a concrete pad. Any flush or recessed concept would be a special, purpose-designed product needing specific acceptance by the regulator and the airport.

Can PAPI units be raised where snow is a problem?

Yes. FAA Order 6850.2B allows the top of the light unit to be up to 6 ft (3 m) above ground where snow is likely to obscure the beams. Raising the units may require siting them farther from the runway edge to keep adequate clearance for the most critical aircraft.

Is elevated PAPI the same thing as elevated approach lighting?

No. PAPI is an approach-slope indicator giving a red/white vertical-slope indication. Approach lighting is a separate system providing runway/approach visual alignment and identification along the extended centreline.

18 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I · Doc 9157 Part 4

Aerodrome Design and Operations — PAPI configuration, siting and photometric requirements; Doc 9157 Part 4 covers visual-aid design guidance, including frangibility of visual aids.

FAA AC 150/5345-28H — PAPI Systems

Equipment specification: light units on at least three adjustable mounting legs with frangible couplings, flanged for a concrete pad, no taller than 1 m at minimum mounting height, with wind and snow-load rigidity and a frangibility test.

IS 7785 · IS 12290 (BIS)

IS 7785 covers elevated-type aerodrome lighting fittings and IS 12290 covers isolating transformers. They are older standards (1975–1987) and are not a PAPI light-unit specification; Indian AGL tenders commonly call up FAA/ICAO requirements for the fixtures themselves.

FAA AC 150/5340-30 · FAA Order JO 6850.2B

Siting and installation details for airport visual aids (current edition 5340-30J), and Visual Guidance Lighting Systems, which permits raised PAPI mounting where snow is likely to obscure the beam.

DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — the Indian regulatory requirement adopting the ICAO Annex 14 provisions; also verify AAI and project-specific specifications.

Final Engineering Note

Do not design a PAPI installation from a generic web diagram. The final siting, mounting and alignment must always be developed from the current governing standard, approved airport design and project-specific requirements.
