Wind Sensor

An electronic sensor that accurately measures wind speed and direction, providing critical data for ATC, weather reporting, automated systems, and aircraft operations. Typically uses ultrasonic, cup-and-vane, or hot-wire technology for high-precision wind measurement.

Overview

What Is It?

A Wind Sensor is an electronic instrument that measures wind speed and direction with high accuracy, providing real-time data for air traffic control, weather reporting, and automated airfield systems. Unlike traditional mechanical wind cones or anemometers, electronic wind sensors offer precise numerical data that can be integrated into ATC displays, approach systems, and weather networks. Common technologies include: ultrasonic (using sound waves to measure wind speed and direction), cup-and-vane (a classic mechanical design with electronic readout), and hot-wire (using thermal dissipation to measure airflow). Wind sensors are typically mounted on masts or towers at a height of 6–10 metres, away from obstructions, to ensure accurate, undisturbed measurements. The data from wind sensors is critical for runway selection, approach procedures, and aircraft performance calculations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Electronic wind sensors exist to give ATC, pilots, and automated airfield systems precise numerical wind data rather than the approximate visual read a windsock provides, which matters when a crosswind or gust is close to an aircraft's operating limits and a rough visual estimate isn't precise enough to base a safety decision on. Because the data can be fed directly into ATC displays and automated systems, it also supports faster, more consistent runway-selection and go/no-go decisions than would be possible from visual observation alone.

This precision becomes especially valuable during marginal conditions — gusting or rapidly shifting wind — where the difference between an acceptable and an unacceptable crosswind component may be only a few knots, and where a pilot or controller needs a trustworthy number rather than an impression to decide whether an approach or departure should proceed.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at all major airports, military airbases, and many smaller airfields where precise wind data is required. Typically placed near the runway threshold, at the centre of the airfield, or at multiple locations for comprehensive wind coverage. Used for:

  • Air Traffic Control – providing real-time wind data for takeoff and landing clearance
  • Weather Reporting – feeding data into METAR, ATIS, and AWOS systems
  • Approach Systems – providing wind data for ILS and other precision approach systems
  • Runway Selection – helping ATC determine the active runway based on wind conditions
  • Safety Systems – providing data for runway incursion prevention and emergency response

Wind sensors are also used at heliports, offshore platforms, and temporary airfields where portable weather stations are deployed.

Placement

Installation & Location

Mounted on a mast or cross-arm sited in open, unobstructed air, following the same siting principles as the anemometer it is often paired with: clear of buildings, trees, and other masts by a wide margin, and set at the standard meteorological exposure height of around 10 m above ground level, or a lower touchdown-zone height where local practice dictates, to keep readings representative of the wind actually experienced on the runway. Multiple units are typically installed at different points along the airfield to cover local variation, each on its own mast with a concrete foundation and lightning protection and earthing appropriate to its height. Power and data cabling run underground in duct back to the AWOS/ATC display system, and the sensor head is aligned to true or magnetic north during commissioning so direction readings are accurate.

Function

How Does It Work?

The wind sensor operates by measuring the movement of air and converting it into electrical signals. Common technologies include:

  • Ultrasonic Sensors: Use pairs of ultrasonic transducers to measure the time-of-flight of sound pulses in different directions. The difference in travel time between pulses sent with and against the wind is used to calculate wind speed and direction. No moving parts, high accuracy, and low maintenance.
  • Cup-and-Vane Sensors: A classic mechanical design where a rotating cup assembly measures wind speed (via a magnetic or optical encoder) and a wind vane measures direction (via a potentiometer or optical encoder). Simple, robust, and cost-effective.
  • Hot-Wire Sensors: Use a heated wire or thermistor that is cooled by airflow. The cooling rate is proportional to wind speed, and the direction is determined by multiple sensors or a rotating head.

The sensor's electronics process the raw signals and output data via standard communication protocols (RS-485, SDI-12, NMEA, or digital outputs). The data is then fed into ATC displays, weather systems, and automated airfield systems. The sensor is typically housed in a weatherproof enclosure with heating to prevent ice buildup.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Unlike the windsock, the wind sensor is a fully active, addressable instrument with its own control loop rather than a purely mechanical indicator. The sensing element feeds raw signals to onboard signal-conditioning electronics, which digitise the measurement and output it over a standard interface (RS-485, SDI-12, NMEA, or 4-20mA). This is transmitted to a data acquisition unit (DAU) or data concentrator, which polls multiple meteorological sensors across the airfield and aggregates them into a single feed for the AWOS/ATIS processing system. From there, the data drives ATC wind displays, automated METAR/ATIS generation, and runway or approach selection logic. A separate, self-contained control loop operates the sensor's internal heater: a thermostat or RTD switches the heating element on once temperature falls below a set threshold, preventing ice from forming on the transducers or cups, entirely independent of the main measurement path. A heater fault is reported as a maintenance alarm rather than a wind-reading fault — at least until ice actually accumulates and begins to affect the reading.

Sensor Head → Signal Conditioning → DAU → AWOS/ATIS → ATC Display (with independent anti-icing loop)

Construction

Physical Construction

An electronic wind sensor is built as a sealed instrument head containing the sensing elements — ultrasonic transducers, a cup-and-vane rotor assembly, or hot-wire elements, depending on technology — housed in a weatherproof enclosure of anodised aluminium or corrosion-resistant composite rated to IP66/IP67. Ultrasonic designs have no moving parts, with the transducer arms fixed rigidly to the sensor body so the acoustic path length between them remains precisely known; cup-and-vane designs instead rely on sealed, low-friction bearings supporting the rotating cups and directional vane, which are more exposed to mechanical wear and icing than a solid-state ultrasonic head. Many sensor heads incorporate internal heating elements around the transducer faces, bearings, or vane pivot to prevent ice accretion from stalling the mechanism or blocking the acoustic path in freezing conditions. The sensor mounts on a rigid pole or boom at the standard reference height, positioned clear of nearby structures or turbulence sources, with shielded, weather-rated cable carrying power and signal down to the base-mounted interface or data logger, and all external fasteners and housings built to withstand continuous, unsheltered outdoor exposure.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Calibration Verification
Annual

Check wind speed and direction output against a certified reference to confirm accuracy stays within the ±0.1–0.5 m/s and ±1–3° specifications.

Sensor Element Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean ultrasonic transducers or cup-and-vane elements of dust, insect residue, and salt film so readings stay accurate and unobstructed.

Heater Function Check
Annual

Test the heating element ahead of the icing season, since frost or ice buildup on cups, vanes, or ultrasonic transducers can stall or bias readings.

Bearing & Moving Parts Lubrication
Semi-annual

For cup-and-vane sensors, lubricate bearings and confirm free rotation, since friction here directly biases wind speed readings low.

Output Signal & Communication Link Test
Semi-annual

Confirm the sensor's RS-485/SDI-12/NMEA 0183/analog output reaches the AWOS or ALCMS correctly and without signal noise.

Seal & Weatherproofing Inspection
Annual

Check housing seals and gaskets for cracking or degradation and confirm the IP66/IP67 rating is still intact.

Mounting Height & Alignment Verification
Annual

Confirm the sensor remains at the specified 6–10m height and correctly oriented to true north, since misalignment biases every direction reading.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Examine signal and power wiring for corrosion or loose terminations, a common source of erratic or dropped-out readings.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Icing and Snow Accumulation

Ice buildup on the sensor (especially cup-and-vane or ultrasonic transducers) can cause inaccurate readings or complete failure. Heated sensors and regular de-icing are essential in cold climates.

Obstruction-Induced Turbulence

Placing the sensor too close to buildings, trees, or other structures can cause turbulence and inaccurate readings. Proper siting with clear fetch (at least 10 times the obstacle height) is critical.

Signal Interference and Data Corruption

Electromagnetic interference from nearby radar, transmitters, or power lines can corrupt sensor data. Using shielded cables, proper grounding, and optical isolation is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Erroneous or No Wind Speed/Direction Reading
Likely Cause
Icing on cups, vane, or ultrasonic transducers, mechanical bearing seizure, or a faulty transducer/module.
Diagnostic Steps
Check heater operating status, inspect for physical ice or debris on the sensing elements, and run a self-test or calibration check against the output signal.
Corrective Action
Activate or repair the heater element, clean the sensor, and replace the transducer or sensor module if it fails self-test.
Signal Dropout or Corrupted Data at the Display/Recorder
Likely Cause
Electromagnetic interference, a faulty cable or connector, or a grounding fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect cable shielding and grounding continuity, check connectors for corrosion or moisture, and monitor signal quality against known interference sources.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace shielded cable, improve grounding, and reroute cabling away from interference sources.
Readings Consistently Inaccurate Compared to a Reference Instrument
Likely Cause
Calibration drift, incorrect siting causing turbulence, or a mounting height error.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a calibration verification against a reference anemometer, review siting for obstructions within the required fetch, and confirm mounting height against specification.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the sensor, and re-site or adjust mounting height to meet fetch and height requirements.
Heater Not Functioning in Cold Weather
Likely Cause
Failed heater element, a blown fuse or tripped circuit, or a wiring fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Check heater circuit voltage at the connector, test heater element resistance, and inspect the fuse or breaker.
Corrective Action
Replace the heater element, repair the wiring, and reset or replace the protection device.
Mechanical Sensor Not Rotating Freely (Cup/Vane Type)
Likely Cause
Bearing wear or seizure, or debris and bird nesting fouling the moving parts.
Diagnostic Steps
Manually check rotational freedom of the cups/vane and inspect bearings and moving parts for debris or wear.
Corrective Action
Clean and lubricate the bearings, remove debris or nesting material, and replace the bearing assembly if seized.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Icing of Sensing Elements
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ice buildup on cups, vane, or ultrasonic transducers in cold conditions, especially where heating is absent or has failed.
Effect
Inaccurate readings or complete loss of wind speed/direction output.
Detection Method
Heater operating-status check, and physical inspection for ice or debris on the sensing elements.
Bearing Wear or Seizure (Mechanical Types)
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Bearing wear from continuous rotation, or debris and bird nesting fouling the moving parts of a cup/vane sensor.
Effect
Restricted or stopped rotation that produces erroneous or stuck wind speed/direction readings.
Detection Method
Manual check of rotational freedom on the cups/vane, and inspection of bearings and moving parts for debris or wear.
Transducer / Sensor Module Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Component aging or an internal fault in the ultrasonic transducer or electronic sensing module.
Effect
Erroneous or absent wind speed/direction output that fails the unit's self-test.
Detection Method
Self-test or calibration check run against the output signal, comparing results with expected behaviour.
Heater Element Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Element burnout, a blown fuse, tripped circuit, or wiring fault in the heater circuit.
Effect
The sensor is unprotected against icing in cold weather, increasing the risk of frozen or fouled sensing elements.
Detection Method
Heater circuit voltage check at the connector, heater element resistance test, and inspection of the fuse or breaker.
Cable / Grounding Fault (EMI Susceptibility)
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Damaged cable shielding, a faulty connector, or a grounding fault allowing electromagnetic interference from nearby radar, transmitters, or power lines to couple in.
Effect
Signal dropout or corrupted data at the display/recorder, without any fault at the sensing element itself.
Detection Method
Cable shielding and grounding continuity inspection, connector check for corrosion or moisture, and signal-quality monitoring against known interference sources.
Calibration Drift / Siting-Induced Turbulence
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual sensor drift over time, or degraded siting where nearby buildings, trees, or structures have encroached on the required clear fetch.
Effect
Readings are consistently inaccurate compared to a reference instrument, even though the sensor itself passes self-test.
Detection Method
Calibration verification against a reference anemometer, combined with a siting review for obstructions within the required fetch and mounting height.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise the sensor supply and, separately, the anti-icing heater circuit before opening the housing or disconnecting cable, since the heater is thermostat/RTD-switched on an independent supply from the signal path.

Moving Parts / Pinch Hazard
Mechanical

Cup, vane, or propeller assemblies can rotate in ambient wind even with the sensor powered down; secure or restrain the moving assembly before hands-on work and keep fingers clear.

Hot Heating Element
Personnel Safety

Allow the anti-icing heater to cool before handling after active operation; heater draw can reach 50-100W, well above the sensor's unheated power consumption, and the housing can retain heat after shutdown.

Storm and Lightning Exposure
Electrical Safety

Avoid mast-top work during an electrical storm or high wind, and confirm mast grounding and surge protection are intact before returning the sensor to service after lightning activity.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Work on masts within the aerodrome's obstacle-limitation surfaces or movement area requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where wind data will be unavailable, with vehicle access following the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

PPE and Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools when working on live-adjacent connections, and confirm the sensor and mast bonding/grounding are intact before reconnecting supply.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the sensor is reporting correctly on both the signal path and heater status before closing out maintenance, and cancel the associated NOTAM once wind data is confirmed reliable.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Verify the Heater Ahead of Winter, Not Just in Summer Testing
Maintenance

An ultrasonic transducer with a failed or absent heater can ice over silently, losing accurate wind data exactly when icing conditions make it most critical; verify heater function specifically ahead of winter rather than relying on a functional test performed in mild weather.

Trend Low-Wind Readings to Catch Bearing Wear Early
Diagnostics

Cup-and-vane bearing wear typically shows up first as underreporting at low wind speeds, from an increased starting threshold, before it affects high-speed accuracy; trend low-speed readings specifically rather than judging bearing condition from strong-wind performance alone.

Verify True North Alignment as Its Own Check Item
Alignment

A sensor that's mechanically sound but rotated a few degrees off true north introduces a systematic direction bias that's easy to miss without a dedicated alignment check; verify north reference specifically after any remount or maintenance, not just electrical function.

Clean Hot-Wire Elements on a Tighter Cycle in Coastal or Dusty Sites
Calibration

Hot-wire sensor elements are sensitive to contamination from dust or salt, which changes their thermal response and skews readings gradually; clean and re-verify calibration more frequently at coastal or dusty airfields than the sensor's generic maintenance interval assumes.

Investigate Small Offsets Between Redundant Sensors Early
Verification

Where a backup wind sensor is fitted, comparing it against the primary is the fastest way to catch a slow calibration drift in either unit; investigate a small but persistent offset between paired sensors rather than waiting for a large discrepancy to prompt action.

Verify the Full Data Chain to the Display, Not Just the Sensor
Configuration

A unit mismatch, such as knots versus metres per second, introduced anywhere between the sensor and the ATC display can silently misrepresent wind data even when the sensor itself is perfectly calibrated; verify the complete chain through to the displayed value, not just the sensor's own output.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about airfield wind sensors: technologies, accuracy, siting, outputs, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an airfield wind sensor?

A wind sensor is an electronic instrument that measures wind speed and direction and supplies real-time numerical data for air traffic control, weather reporting and automated airfield systems. Unlike a wind cone it gives precise figures that can feed ATC displays, approach systems and weather networks.

Q2What types of wind sensor are used?

Ultrasonic sensors, which time sound pulses between transducer pairs and have no moving parts, cup-and-vane sensors, a classic mechanical design with electronic readout, and hot-wire sensors that use thermal dissipation to measure airflow.

Q3How accurate is an airfield wind sensor?

Ultrasonic units are typically accurate to ±0.1 m/s in speed and ±1° in direction. Cup-and-vane units are typically ±0.5 m/s and ±3°. Speed range is up to 75 m/s (270 km/h) depending on model, and direction covers 0–360°.

Q4Why use an electronic wind sensor instead of a windsock?

A windsock gives only an approximate visual read. A sensor gives precise numerical wind data, which matters when a crosswind or gust is near an aircraft's operating limits, and it feeds ATC, runway selection, METAR, ATIS and AWOS systems directly.

Q5Where should a wind sensor be installed?

On a mast or cross-arm in open, unobstructed air, well clear of buildings, trees and other masts. Height is typically about 10 m above ground level (6–10 m range), or a lower touchdown-zone height where local practice dictates, so the readings represent the wind at the runway. Some airfields use several sensors for coverage.

Q6What outputs does a wind sensor provide?

Digital RS-485, SDI-12 or NMEA 0183, or analog 4–20 mA or 0–10 V. RS-485 runs can reach up to 1,200 m using shielded twisted-pair cable, while unboosted analog loops need shorter runs.

Q7How are wind sensors powered and protected from ice?

They run on 230 V AC or 12–24 V DC, drawing 2–5 W unheated and 50–100 W or more with the anti-icing heater active. The heater has a separate thermostat or RTD-switched supply. Line and signal surge arrestors sit at the sensor base and the data acquisition unit entry point.

Q8Which standards apply to wind sensors?

The main references are ICAO Annex 3 (meteorological service for international air navigation), WMO No. 8 (guide to meteorological instruments and methods of observation) and FAA AC 150/5220-16 (airport weather observation systems). Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q9How often should a wind sensor be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for sensor element cleaning, semi-annual for bearing lubrication on cup-and-vane units and output signal and communication link tests, and annual for calibration verification against a certified reference and heater function checks before icing season. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is my wind sensor giving wrong or no readings?

Common causes are ice on the cups, vane or ultrasonic transducers, bearing seizure or a faulty transducer or module. Check heater status, inspect for ice or debris and run a self-test. If the signal drops out or is corrupted at the display, suspect electromagnetic interference, a faulty cable or connector, or a grounding fault.
