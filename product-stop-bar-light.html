Product Overview

Stop Bar Light

What It Is

Stop bar lights provide a highly conspicuous red visual indication at runway holding positions and other controlled aircraft movement locations.

They form part of the airport’s runway-incursion prevention and surface-movement control system by showing where aircraft or vehicles must stop before proceeding toward a protected area. A stop bar is normally installed across a taxiway at the holding position as a row of in-pavement lights oriented to approaching traffic.

Selected According To

  • Runway holding-position configuration
  • Taxiway geometry
  • Low-visibility operating requirements
  • Stop-bar length and fixture spacing
  • Light direction, red output and beam characteristics
  • Selective switching and centreline interlocking
  • Series-circuit configuration and AGL control system
  • Inset housing arrangement and pavement conditions
  • Project technical specification

The final stop-bar system should always be selected against the approved airport lighting/control design and applicable aerodrome standards.

ICAO material describes stop bars as a series of unidirectional red lights embedded in the pavement, installed at right angles to the taxiway centreline at the runway holding position.

Annex 14 requires a stop bar at every runway-holding position serving a runway intended for use in runway visual range below 350 m, subject to the exceptions in the standard, such as suitable aids and procedures that prevent inadvertent runway incursions. At an intermediate holding position, a stop bar is provided where markings are to be supplemented by lights for visual traffic control. Annex 14 also recommends a stop bar where RVR is between 350 m and 550 m, subject to similar exceptions. Confirm against the applicable edition.

Function

A Clear Visual Command: STOP

How It Works

  • Illuminated: movement beyond the holding position is not authorized
  • Extinguished on clearance, per the airport’s approved operational and control logic
  • Pilot or vehicle operator receives an unambiguous stop indication

A stop bar is both a lighting product and a controlled safety function.

Red Light Configuration

Unidirectional Red Toward the Approach

Red Directional Light

Stop bar lights emit red directional light toward the intended approach direction. For runway-holding applications the light is unidirectional in the approach direction.

Under the FAA fixture specification, L-852S is the in-pavement stop-bar fixture classification. FAA AC 150/5345-46F specifies red output for L-852S with minimum intensity and beam-coverage values set in the fixture specification.

Typical Installation Comprises

  • Red optical assembly, LED or approved light source, lens
  • Mounting hardware and gasket/sealing system
  • Secondary cable and isolating transformer
  • Primary circuit connector

Fixture Spacing

Spacing for a Continuous Visual Indication

ICAO

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I (Chapter 5, stop bars) specifies stop-bar lights spaced uniformly at no more than 3 m across the taxiway. ICAO also provides for a pair of elevated stop-bar lights added at each end of the bar where in-pavement lights could be obscured. Confirm the clauses against the edition applicable to the project.

FAA

Stop-bar light spacing and layout are covered by AC 150/5340-30J. Use the spacing shown in the current edition and the approved airport drawing.

Project standards differ — the approved airport drawing and governing standard control the final spacing.

Selective Switching

Selectively Switchable Operation

Controlled ON / OFF

  • Red stop indication ON → aircraft must hold
  • Stop bar OFF → aircraft authorized to proceed, subject to clearance and operating procedures

Reference

ICAO provisions have addressed selectively switchable stop bars in conjunction with taxiway centreline lighting extending beyond the stop bar.

Interlocking

Stop Bar & Taxiway Centreline Interlocking

The Conflict to Prevent

Stop bar = RED / STOP while centreline beyond = GREEN / PROCEED at the same time is a potentially confusing condition and must be avoided.

Guidance

FAA guidance states the stop bar should be interlocked with the taxiway lights. ICAO material describes arrangements in which centreline lights beyond an illuminated stop bar are extinguished for at least 90 m.

Exact logic is established through the airport’s approved AGL/ALCMS control philosophy.

FAQ

Stop Bar Light — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a stop bar light?

Stop bar lights provide a highly conspicuous red visual indication at runway holding positions and other controlled aircraft movement locations. They form part of the airport's runway-incursion prevention and surface-movement control system by showing where aircraft or vehicles must stop. A stop bar is normally installed across a taxiway at the holding position as a row of in-pavement lights oriented to approaching traffic.

When does ICAO require a stop bar?

Annex 14 requires a stop bar at every runway-holding position serving a runway intended for use in runway visual range below 350 m, subject to the exceptions in the standard, such as suitable aids and procedures that prevent inadvertent runway incursions. At an intermediate holding position, a stop bar is provided where markings are to be supplemented by lights for visual traffic control. Annex 14 also recommends a stop bar where RVR is between 350 m and 550 m, subject to similar exceptions. Confirm against the applicable edition.

How does a stop bar work and how is it switched?

When the stop bar is illuminated, movement beyond the holding position is not authorized. It is extinguished on clearance, per the airport's approved operational and control logic, so the aircraft is authorized to proceed subject to clearance and operating procedures. A stop bar is both a lighting product and a controlled safety function, and it is selectively switchable where required.

What are the red light characteristics of a stop bar?

Stop bar lights emit red directional light toward the intended approach direction, and for runway-holding applications the light is unidirectional in the approach direction. Under the FAA fixture specification, L-852S is the in-pavement stop-bar fixture classification, and FAA AC 150/5345-46F specifies red output for L-852S with minimum intensity and beam-coverage values set in the fixture specification. Verify the actual product against the current applicable FAA specification and project requirements.

How are stop bar lights spaced and oriented?

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I (Chapter 5, stop bars) specifies stop-bar lights spaced uniformly at no more than 3 m across the taxiway, and provides for a pair of elevated stop-bar lights added at each end of the bar where in-pavement lights could be obscured. For FAA installations, the spacing and layout are covered by AC 150/5340-30J, and the beam axis is oriented away from the runway, perpendicular to the holding-position marking, within the tolerance in the cited FAA guidance and the approved project drawing. The approved airport drawing and governing standard control the final spacing.

Why must a stop bar be interlocked with taxiway centreline lights?

A stop bar showing red while the centreline beyond shows green is a potentially confusing condition and must be avoided. FAA guidance states the stop bar should be interlocked with the taxiway lights, and ICAO material describes arrangements in which centreline lights beyond an illuminated stop bar are extinguished for at least 90 m. The exact logic is established through the airport's approved AGL/ALCMS control philosophy, and the control system may interface with the AGL control panel and CCR, ALCMS, individual light controllers, a stop-bar control module, air traffic control and surface movement guidance and control systems.

What electrical checks apply before replacing or adding a stop bar fixture?

A typical arrangement runs from the CCR through the primary series circuit and isolating transformer to the secondary cable and the in-pavement stop bar light, with a control and monitoring interface. Verify the CCR output current and circuit topology, the number of stop-bar fixtures, transformer rating, primary and secondary cable, connector type, intensity setting and switching method, and the monitoring system and ALCMS interface. Incorrect compatibility may result in incorrect intensity, circuit instability, transformer stress, switching problems, false failure indications or premature fixture failure.

What should be verified for the pavement and installation?

Verify the holding-position reference, stop-bar location and marking alignment, taxiway centreline relationship, optical direction and perpendicularity to the marking, uniform fixture spacing, complete taxiway coverage and the relationship to the taxiway edge. For the pavement, check the housing type and dimensions, installation depth, pavement cut-out, drainage, fixture seating, fastener torque, cable routing and sealing. FAA guidance identifies L-868 load-bearing bases for in-pavement stop-bar installations.

What is tested at commissioning?

Commissioning tests both lighting and control. That includes fixture, lens, red output, alignment and spacing, circuit continuity and insulation resistance, transformer and secondary connection, series-circuit current, CCR operation and intensity steps, local and remote ON/OFF and selective switching, status and fault indication, interlock logic and centreline interaction, and monitoring and fail-safe behaviour where specified. Photometric verification covers red colour, intensity, beam distribution and consistency across the bar.

What are common stop bar failure modes and how are they diagnosed?

Typical symptoms are a single light off, the entire stop bar off, a stop bar that does not switch, an incorrect centreline interlock, low red intensity and an uneven stop-bar appearance. For a complete failure, test the control path first, because the lights may be electrically healthy but intentionally extinguished by the control or interlocking system. The diagnostic sequence runs through the control system, interlock logic, CCR and power, primary circuit, isolating transformer, secondary cable, connector, fixture, light source, optical alignment and photometry.

How is a stop bar different from a runway guard light?

A stop bar gives a stop indication and movement control, is red, is typically inset, and is selectively switchable where required, with a strong link to centreline and surface movement control. A runway guard light is an enhanced warning at a runway/taxiway intersection, is yellow, may be elevated or inset, and may operate continuously or flashing per design. FAA examples are L-852S for the stop bar and L-804 (elevated) or L-852G (inset) for runway guard lights, and the project design determines whether one or both systems are required.

Which standards apply to stop bar lights?

ICAO Annex 14 contains requirements for stop bars at runway-holding positions and other applicable locations, describing them as red, unidirectional in-pavement lights with selective switching and integration with taxiway centreline lighting. Amendment 18 to Annex 14 Vol. I (applicable from 27 November 2025) included visual-aid provisions, so confirm the stop-bar clauses against the amended edition. FAA references are AC 150/5340-30J and AC 150/5345-46F, and in India DGCA requirements, airport-specific AGL specifications, the approved holding-position layout and the AGL/ALCMS control philosophy should also be verified.

What should be confirmed before ordering a stop bar?

Confirm the airport and project, runway holding position, taxiway identification and width, holding-position marking, stop-bar length, number of fixtures and spacing, light colour, optical direction, beam distribution and intensity, series-circuit current, isolating transformer, connector type, housing type, installation depth, pavement cut-out and drainage. Also confirm selective switching, centreline interlock, the ALCMS interface, monitoring requirements, environmental rating, applicable standard, and the manufacturer datasheet, photometric and compliance documentation.
