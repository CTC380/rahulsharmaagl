RCA-546 Repeated Transformer Failure RCA

Symptom — The same isolation transformer position, transformer batch, or circuit repeatedly returns to the fault log with winding failure or short/open findings within a short interval of the last replacement, rather than the fixture population showing a normal random failure spread.

Equipment / System

L-830 series-circuit isolation transformers across CCR-fed loops; maintenance/CMMS failure records, insulation resistance test history, and transformer batch/procurement records.

Possible Causes
HighSystemic wattage/tap mismatch against the fitted lamp/LED load, so every replacement unit is stressed the same way as the one it replaced
HighA bad batch of transformers from a specific manufacturing lot with a common insulation or potting defect
MediumChronic water ingress into a specific base can (poor drainage, cracked can, failed gasket) that re-floods after every repair
MediumSustained overvoltage or harmonic distortion from the CCR feeding that loop, stressing every transformer on the circuit equally
LowIncorrect torque or crimp practice at that base can during transformer changeouts, repeatedly damaging the replacement unit during installation
LowConfirmation bias in the maintenance log — multiple distinct failure modes being coded under one generic "transformer fault" entry, masking the real pattern
Required Tools
CMMS/FRACAS failure history exportPareto chart of failure counts by location and batchInsulation resistance trend logCCR output voltage/THD loggerTransformer batch/serial traceability recordsBase-can moisture inspection log
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before removing any transformer for forensic inspection.
  • Treat a suspect transformer as potentially still energised by induced voltage from an adjacent live loop until confirmed isolated.
  • Bag and tag failed units for lot traceability rather than discarding on site.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionPull the full failure history for the position/circuit/batch in question spanning at least the last 12–24 months from the CMMS or maintenance log.
  2. Trend AnalysisPlot failures by base-can location, transformer batch/lot number, and calendar date to distinguish a location-specific pattern from a batch-specific one.
  3. HypothesisDraft 5-Whys or fishbone (Ishikawa) branches for the leading candidate causes — wattage mismatch, batch defect, water ingress, and CCR-side stress — before inspecting the current failed unit.
  4. InvestigationPhysically inspect the most recently failed transformer for wattage/tap rating, potting condition, and evidence of moisture versus thermal damage, and cross-check against the design load.
  5. InvestigationVerify CCR output voltage and current at that circuit against the design spec, and check for harmonic distortion if the CCR is inverter-based.
  6. Root CauseCorrelate the physical evidence with the trend data to select the single leading cause, ruling out the alternatives with specific evidence rather than by elimination alone.
Data Points Reviewed
Failure count by base-can location, last 24 months
Failure count by transformer batch/lot number
Wattage/tap rating vs. design spec at the affected position
CCR output voltage and THD on the affected circuit
Base-can drainage condition at the affected position
Torque/crimp record from the last changeout, if logged
Root Cause Verification

Install a correctly specified transformer from a known-good batch, correct any identified base-can drainage or CCR-side issue, and monitor insulation resistance and failure recurrence at that specific position for at least two full PM cycles before closing the investigation.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the failed transformer with a unit of verified correct wattage/tap rating from a confirmed-good batch.
  • Quarantine and return any remaining units from an implicated bad batch, and notify the supplier.
  • Remediate the base can (drainage, gasket, can integrity) if water ingress is the confirmed cause.
  • Correct CCR output voltage/harmonic issues at the source if the circuit itself is implicated.
Preventive Action
  • Add batch/lot number capture to the transformer replacement record in the CMMS to enable early batch-defect detection.
  • Set a CMMS trigger for repeat failure at the same position within a defined interval (e.g. two failures in 12 months) to auto-flag for RCA.
  • Include transformer wattage/tap verification as a mandatory step on every changeout work order.
  • Trend circuit-level insulation resistance rather than relying on single spot readings.

RCA-547 Repeated CCR Trip RCA

Symptom — A specific Constant Current Regulator repeatedly trips on the same protection (overcurrent, earth fault, over-temperature) at a similar time interval after reset, rather than the trip being a single isolated event, and the trip pattern survives a straightforward like-for-like component swap.

Equipment / System

CCR (transformer-type or TSCR/inverter-type), its protection relays/settings, the series circuit and loop it feeds, and the CCR failure/trip log or SCADA/ALCMS event history.

Possible Causes
HighA genuine and still-present fault on the loop (developing insulation breakdown, water ingress) that the CCR is correctly detecting, and the trip is being reset without the underlying fault being located and fixed
HighProtection relay settings not matched to the actual loop length/impedance, causing nuisance trips under normal, non-faulty load
MediumCCR component degradation (SCR/IGBT, output choke, capacitor bank) causing an internal fault masquerading as a loop fault
MediumThermal trip from inadequate CCR house/pit ventilation, especially correlating with time of day or season
LowUpstream supply quality issue (voltage sag, phase imbalance) tripping the CCR input protection rather than the output stage
LowRecurring human-factor cause — an operator resetting the trip without logging or investigating, so the same undiagnosed fault repeats indefinitely
Required Tools
CCR event/trip log export (ALCMS or local panel)Insulation resistance test history for the fed loopClamp-on ammeter and power quality analyzerCCR house/pit temperature logProtection relay settings printout vs. design calculation5-Whys / fishbone template
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the CCR before internal inspection; do not rely on the trip alone as proof of de-energisation.
  • Allow discharge time for output capacitors per the manufacturer procedure before touching internal components.
  • Coordinate any extended CCR outage with ATC given the runway/taxiway lighting it serves.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionExport the full trip history for the specific CCR, including trip type, timestamp, and any load/current data logged at the moment of trip.
  2. Trend AnalysisPlot trip frequency against time of day, ambient temperature, weather (rain events for earth fault trips), and any recent maintenance activity on the loop.
  3. HypothesisSeparate the candidate causes into loop-side (real fault) versus CCR-side (protection or component issue) branches for a fishbone diagram before further testing.
  4. InvestigationWith the CCR isolated, megger the full series loop to rule in or out a genuine, still-present insulation fault as the trigger.
  5. InvestigationVerify protection relay settings (overcurrent, earth fault threshold, trip delay) against the design calculation for the actual loop impedance and length.
  6. Root CauseCross-reference the trip timestamps against the insulation and thermal data to isolate whether the pattern tracks a real loop fault, a settings mismatch, or CCR internal degradation.
Data Points Reviewed
Trip event log — type, timestamp, load at trip
Loop insulation resistance, full end-to-end
Protection relay settings vs. design calculation
CCR house/pit ambient temperature trend
Upstream supply voltage/phase balance
Output stage component condition (SCR/IGBT, capacitor bank)
Root Cause Verification

After the identified cause is corrected — loop repair, relay re-setting, or component replacement — monitor the CCR trip log for at least 30 days or one full PM cycle spanning varied load and weather conditions with zero recurrence before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Repair the loop fault if a genuine, still-present insulation or cable fault is confirmed as the trigger.
  • Re-calculate and re-set protection relay thresholds to match the actual loop impedance and length.
  • Replace degraded CCR internal components identified during inspection.
  • Improve CCR house/pit ventilation if thermal trips correlate with ambient conditions.
Preventive Action
  • Require a documented fault-location step before any CCR trip reset is logged as resolved, rather than allowing reset-and-move-on.
  • Add automatic trip-event logging with timestamp and load snapshot to the CCR/ALCMS configuration if not already present.
  • Include protection relay setting verification in the periodic CCR maintenance checklist, not only at commissioning.
  • Trend CCR house temperature as a standard PM data point.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-CCR-04 (CCR LOTO Procedure), SOP-CCR-07 (Protection Relay Verification)

Standards: ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5345-10 (CCRs), IEC 61439 (protection coordination)

RCA-548 Repeated Cable Failure RCA

Symptom — Primary or secondary series cable faults recur along the same route, duct run, or joint location within a short interval of the previous repair, rather than cable faults being randomly distributed across the field.

Equipment / System

L-824 primary series cable, secondary cable to fixtures, duct banks, pull boxes, and field splices/joints; cable fault location and repair history.

Possible Causes
HighA localised, chronically wet or poorly drained duct/pull-box section that continues to degrade insulation at the same joint after every repair
HighRepeated mechanical damage from ongoing civil works, mowing, or vehicle loading over a shallow or poorly marked cable route
MediumA substandard splice/joint technique or kit used at that location, causing the repair itself to be the recurring weak point
MediumRodent or wildlife damage concentrated at a specific duct entry or pull box
LowCable of incorrect voltage/insulation rating installed at that section during a past repair, understressed for the actual series-circuit voltage
LowThermal cycling and mechanical stress at a duct-to-can transition point causing progressive insulation fatigue
Required Tools
Cable fault and repair history log with GPS/station referencingTDR / series-circuit fault locator1000V/2500V insulation resistance testerAs-built duct and cable route drawingSplice kit specification sheetExcavation and utility locate records
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the CCR before any cable work; series circuits present a shock hazard beyond the immediate fault point.
  • Obtain excavation clearance and utility locates before opening pavement or verge at the repeat-fault location.
  • Coordinate airside access and any extended occupancy through ATC/airfield operations.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionCompile every fault and repair record for the route/joint in question with station chainage or GPS coordinates and dates, going back as far as records allow.
  2. Trend AnalysisPlot the fault locations on the as-built route drawing to confirm whether failures cluster at one physical point or recur along a broader section.
  3. HypothesisBuild a fishbone diagram separating environmental (water, thermal), mechanical (impact, civil works), and workmanship (splice quality, wrong cable spec) branches for the clustered location.
  4. InvestigationExcavate and expose the clustered joint/section, inspect for standing water, splice kit condition, and cable insulation appearance versus a known-good reference sample.
  5. InvestigationVerify the installed cable and splice kit specification against the design requirement for that circuit voltage and route.
  6. Root CauseMatch the physical evidence at the excavation to the leading fishbone branch, confirming with insulation resistance testing before and after the section is isolated.
Data Points Reviewed
Fault location history plotted on route drawing
Duct/pull-box water presence at the clustered location
Splice kit type and condition vs. specification
Cable insulation rating vs. actual circuit voltage
Depth of cover and route marking at the repeat-fault point
Civil works or vehicle loading history over the route
Root Cause Verification

After repair with a correctly specified splice kit and, if required, duct/drainage remediation, megger the full loop end-to-end, re-energise, and re-test at the next two scheduled PM visits with no recurrence before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Re-splice using the correct kit and technique for the circuit voltage class.
  • Remediate duct/pull-box drainage or re-route the cable away from a chronically wet section if repair alone will not resolve recurrence.
  • Replace cable found to be of incorrect insulation rating for the circuit.
  • Improve route marking and depth of cover where mechanical damage is implicated.
Preventive Action
  • Log fault location by station/GPS on every repair to make clustering visible over time rather than only after several repeats.
  • Standardise splice kit selection and require photo documentation of every buried splice before backfill.
  • Flag routes under active civil works for temporary cable protection or re-scheduling of works.
  • Include periodic full-loop insulation resistance trending in the PM programme.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-RCL-02 (Series Circuit Fault Location Procedure)

Standards: IEC 60364 (insulation testing), ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5340-30

RCA-549 Repeated Connector Failure RCA

Symptom — L-823 or similar plug/receptacle connectors at a specific fixture, base-can type, or region of the field repeatedly show corrosion, moisture ingress, or contact-resistance faults shortly after being reseated or replaced.

Equipment / System

L-823 primary connectors, secondary lamp-to-transformer connectors, base cans, and the connector maintenance/replacement history for the affected population.

Possible Causes
HighDielectric grease not applied, or an incompatible/incorrect grease used, at every reconnection, leaving the contact exposed to moisture ingress
HighA specific base-can design or drainage condition that keeps connectors submerged or damp between maintenance visits
MediumConnector pins not fully seated or properly torqued during reassembly, a workmanship issue that recurs with routine turnover of maintenance staff
MediumA batch of connectors with a manufacturing defect in the seal or pin plating
LowRepeated mechanical stress on the connector from a poorly supported or too-short secondary cable, straining the joint every time the fixture is serviced
LowIncompatible connector types mixed at the same joint (e.g., differing manufacturer standards) causing a poor mechanical/electrical fit
Required Tools
Connector failure history log by base-can/fixture IDContact resistance meterDielectric grease specification sheetBase-can moisture/drainage inspection logTorque specification for connector assemblySample connectors for teardown inspection
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before disconnecting or handling any connector suspected of internal arcing or moisture contamination.
  • Wear appropriate PPE when handling connectors with evidence of corrosion or chemical residue.
  • Treat a wet base can as a potential shock hazard even after isolation, until confirmed dry and safe.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionPull the connector replacement/repair history for the affected population, noting base-can ID, connector type, and grease/torque records where logged.
  2. Trend AnalysisDetermine whether failures cluster by base-can design, geographic/drainage zone, connector batch, or maintenance crew/shift.
  3. HypothesisDraft fishbone branches for workmanship (grease, torque, seating), design (base-can drainage, connector compatibility), and material (batch defect) causes.
  4. InvestigationPerform teardown inspection of several recently failed connectors, checking pin plating, seal condition, and evidence of grease application.
  5. InvestigationInspect a sample of the implicated base cans for standing water or drainage defects independent of the connector condition.
  6. Root CauseCorrelate teardown findings with the clustering pattern to confirm whether the dominant driver is workmanship, base-can design, or a connector batch defect.
Data Points Reviewed
Failure count by base-can ID and connector type
Contact resistance on recently serviced connectors
Grease type and coverage on teardown samples
Base-can water/drainage condition at failure sites
Connector batch/date-code on failed units
Torque values on recently reassembled joints, if logged
Root Cause Verification

Reassemble with the specified grease and torque, or replace the base can/connector type where design is implicated, and re-check contact resistance at the next two PM visits with no recurrence before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Re-terminate with correctly specified dielectric grease and connector torque.
  • Replace connectors from an implicated defective batch and quarantine remaining stock.
  • Remediate base-can drainage where a chronic moisture condition is confirmed.
  • Retrain the maintenance crew where a workmanship pattern is confirmed as the driver.
Preventive Action
  • Standardise and mandate the approved grease/torque procedure with sign-off on every connector work order.
  • Add connector batch/date-code capture to the CMMS for early batch-defect detection.
  • Include base-can drainage checks as a standard PM item independent of connector condition.
  • Provide refresher training on connector assembly technique at defined intervals.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-RCL-03 (Fixture Replacement Procedure)

Standards: FAA AC 150/5345-26, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (L-852), ICAO Annex 14 Vol I

RCA-550 Repeated Fixture Failure RCA

Symptom — A specific fixture position, fixture model, or fixture batch returns to the fault log significantly more often than the general population, with the failure mode varying (lamp, LED, optics, housing) rather than pointing to one obvious component.

Equipment / System

Complete inset or elevated AGL fixture assemblies (housing, optics, lamp/LED module, base), and the fixture-level failure history across the affected population.

Possible Causes
HighLocation-specific physical stress — a fixture positioned in a high jet-blast, snow-plough, or vehicle-traffic zone experiencing repeated impact damage regardless of the component replaced
HighA specific fixture model or batch with a systemic design or manufacturing weakness (housing seal, gasket, optic mounting)
MediumEnvironmental exposure specific to that location — standing water, de-icing chemical exposure, or extreme thermal cycling not representative of the wider field
MediumIncorrect fixture type installed for the location (e.g., a lower-duty-cycle fixture in a high-traffic zone)
LowPower quality issues specific to the circuit feeding that fixture, stressing the internal components disproportionately
LowInconsistent maintenance practice at that specific location — inspections skipped or superficial, allowing minor issues to progress to failure
Required Tools
Fixture-level failure history by position and batch/serialPareto chart of failure mode by locationFixture inspection and teardown kitDe-icing chemical exposure log, if applicableJet-blast/traffic zone mapping for the runway or taxiwayFixture specification and duty-cycle rating sheet
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before fixture removal.
  • Coordinate airside access and any extended occupancy through ATC/airfield operations.
  • Use FOD-safe tools and account for every item before leaving the movement area.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionCompile the full failure history for the fixture position/model/batch across at least 12–24 months, noting failure mode for each event.
  2. Trend AnalysisMap failure locations against known high-stress zones (jet blast, snow-plough routes, vehicle crossings) and against fixture batch/serial data.
  3. HypothesisSeparate location-driven (environmental/mechanical) from fixture-driven (design/batch) candidate causes on a fishbone diagram.
  4. InvestigationPhysically inspect the current failed fixture and, where available, a retained failed unit from the same position for a consistent damage signature.
  5. InvestigationCompare the fixture model/duty-cycle rating installed at that location against the design requirement for that zone.
  6. Root CauseConfirm whether the dominant driver is the location (supporting fixture-type upgrade or physical protection) or the fixture itself (supporting a batch/model-level corrective action).
Data Points Reviewed
Failure count and mode by fixture position, last 24 months
Fixture model/batch/serial at each failure event
Physical damage signature on retained failed units
Fixture duty-cycle rating vs. zone requirement
De-icing chemical or standing-water exposure at the location
PM inspection completion record for the position
Root Cause Verification

Install the corrected fixture type or remediate the location-specific stressor, then monitor that position specifically for at least two PM cycles or one full winter/summer season, whichever is more relevant to the confirmed cause, before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Upgrade to a fixture model rated for the actual duty cycle/exposure at that location if a mismatch is confirmed.
  • Add physical protection (frangible mounting, reinforced housing) where jet-blast or vehicle impact is confirmed as the driver.
  • Replace remaining units from a confirmed defective batch and notify the supplier.
  • Correct any confirmed power-quality issue on the feeding circuit.
Preventive Action
  • Flag high-stress zones in the CMMS so repeat failures there are recognised as a pattern rather than treated as isolated events.
  • Capture fixture batch/serial on every installation and replacement for future traceability.
  • Review fixture specification against zone duty-cycle requirements at design/procurement stage for future projects.
  • Reinforce PM inspection thoroughness at flagged high-stress locations.

RCA-551 Repeated LED Failure RCA

Symptom — LED modules at a specific fixture position, driver batch, or fixture model fail (dark, dim, wrong colour, or reduced photometric output) well before their rated life, and the pattern recurs after replacement with a nominally identical module.

Equipment / System

LED light-emitting modules and their drivers within AGL fixtures, plus LED failure and photometric test history.

Possible Causes
HighDriver-LED mismatch — a driver supplying current outside the LED module's rated range, causing accelerated thermal degradation
HighInadequate thermal management at that fixture design/location (poor heat sinking, restricted airflow, or elevated ambient temperature) shortening LED life
MediumA specific LED module batch with a manufacturing defect in the phosphor coating or die bonding
MediumVoltage transients or harmonic content from the CCR/driver combination stressing the LED junction over time
LowMoisture ingress at the optic-to-housing seal degrading the LED module from the back side rather than a pure electrical failure
LowIncorrect LED module colour/type installed at that position, drifting out of the ICAO/FAA chromaticity box faster than the correct type would
Required Tools
LED/driver failure history by position and batchPhotometric test equipment (luminous intensity, chromaticity)Thermal imaging cameraDriver output current/voltage meterLED module datasheet and thermal derating curveSample failed modules for teardown
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before removing any LED module or driver.
  • Allow LED driver capacitors to discharge per manufacturer procedure before handling.
  • Use appropriate PPE when handling modules with suspected moisture or chemical contamination.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionCompile the LED/driver failure history for the position/batch/model, including hours in service where duty logging is available.
  2. Trend AnalysisCompare actual hours-to-failure against the manufacturer's rated LED life to quantify the severity of the shortfall, and check for batch or location clustering.
  3. HypothesisDraft fishbone branches for driver mismatch, thermal design, batch defect, and power-quality causes.
  4. InvestigationMeasure driver output current/voltage against the LED module's rated range under actual field conditions.
  5. InvestigationUse thermal imaging on an operating fixture at that position to check for elevated junction/heat-sink temperature versus a known-good reference fixture.
  6. Root CauseCorrelate the electrical and thermal measurements with the failure-mode evidence from teardown to isolate driver mismatch, thermal design, or batch defect as the leading cause.
Data Points Reviewed
Hours-to-failure vs. rated LED life
Driver output current/voltage vs. LED module rating
Heat-sink/junction temperature (thermal imaging)
Failure count by LED batch/date-code
Photometric output (intensity, chromaticity) on failed units
Optic seal condition on teardown
Root Cause Verification

Install a correctly matched driver/LED combination or corrected thermal design, then re-test photometric output immediately and again at the next scheduled photometric survey, with hours-in-service tracked at that position to confirm life has returned to expected range.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the driver with one correctly matched to the LED module's rated current/voltage range.
  • Improve thermal management at the fixture design/location if elevated temperature is confirmed.
  • Replace remaining modules from a confirmed defective batch and notify the supplier.
  • Correct any confirmed power-quality issue at the CCR/driver interface.
Preventive Action
  • Capture LED/driver batch and installation date in the CMMS to enable hours-to-failure trending against rated life.
  • Include a driver-to-module compatibility check as a standard step whenever either component is replaced independently.
  • Add thermal imaging of a sample of fixtures to the periodic PM programme in warmer climates or high-ambient locations.
  • Trend photometric survey results by fixture batch, not only by division.

RCA-552 Repeated Sign Failure RCA

Symptom — A specific illuminated taxiway/runway guidance sign, or a class of signs on a particular circuit, repeatedly returns to the fault log for internal lamp/LED, panel, or ballast/driver failure within a short interval of the last repair.

Equipment / System

L-858 illuminated signs (mandatory instruction and information signs), their internal light sources/drivers, panel assemblies, and sign failure/repair history.

Possible Causes
HighInternal heat build-up from restricted ventilation or a undersized/failing cooling fan, shortening lamp/LED and driver life inside that sign housing
HighWater ingress through a degraded door seal or cable gland specific to that sign's installation or design revision
MediumA sign panel or internal wiring harness batch defect causing recurring electrical faults
MediumIncorrect ballast/driver specification for the fitted light source, causing repeated premature failure
LowSign positioned in a zone with elevated vibration (adjacent to a high-traffic taxiway or near jet-blast) fatiguing internal connections
LowA recurring vandalism/impact pattern at that specific sign location rather than a component-level fault
Required Tools
Sign failure/repair history by sign ID and locationThermal imaging cameraDoor seal and cable gland inspection kitBallast/driver specification sheet vs. fitted light sourceInternal wiring harness batch recordsVibration/traffic proximity mapping
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before opening the sign housing.
  • Confirm the sign structure is stable and properly anchored before working at height or in proximity to moving traffic.
  • Coordinate airside access through ATC/airfield operations.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionCompile the failure/repair history for the specific sign ID or sign class over the available record period.
  2. Trend AnalysisDetermine whether failures cluster at one physical sign, a sign design/batch, or a circuit-wide pattern affecting all signs on that CCR.
  3. HypothesisDraft fishbone branches for thermal, moisture ingress, component mismatch, and location/vibration causes.
  4. InvestigationOpen the sign housing and inspect for heat discolouration, moisture staining, seal condition, and cooling fan operation.
  5. InvestigationVerify the fitted ballast/driver specification against the light source actually installed.
  6. Root CauseCorrelate the internal inspection findings with the failure-history pattern to confirm the leading cause before closing on a corrective action.
Data Points Reviewed
Failure count by sign ID/location and by circuit
Internal housing temperature vs. specification
Door seal and cable gland condition
Ballast/driver rating vs. fitted light source
Cooling fan operation, if fitted
Sign structural condition and proximity to traffic/vibration source
Root Cause Verification

After repair or component correction, re-inspect the sign at the next two scheduled PM visits and confirm no recurrence of the same failure mode, with an interim inspection at approximately 90 days if the sign is in a high-risk category.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the failed internal component with one of correct specification for the fitted light source.
  • Restore door seal/gland integrity where moisture ingress is confirmed.
  • Improve ventilation or cooling-fan reliability where thermal build-up is confirmed.
  • Replace panel/harness from a confirmed defective batch.
Preventive Action
  • Add sign ID-level failure tracking to the CMMS to make repeat-failure patterns visible earlier.
  • Include door seal and cooling-fan checks as standard PM items for illuminated signs.
  • Verify ballast/driver-to-light-source compatibility whenever either component is serviced.
  • Review sign siting relative to known vibration/traffic-proximity zones for future placements.

RCA-553 Repeated Earth Fault RCA

Symptom — The earth-fault protection on a specific CCR or circuit trips or the earth-loop resistance test repeatedly fails at the same or a nearby location, despite apparently isolated repairs, rather than the earthing system showing a stable, compliant baseline across visits.

Equipment / System

AGL earthing/grounding system — CCR earth bar, transformer secondary earthing, fixture body earthing, and earth-loop/earth-fault protection settings, plus the earth-fault trip and earth resistance test history.

Possible Causes
HighA corroded, loose, or missing bond at a specific earth point (fixture body, base can, or CCR earth bar) that recurs after every visual "repair" because the underlying corrosion mechanism is not addressed
HighRising soil resistivity at a specific location (seasonal moisture change, soil composition) pushing earth-loop resistance above the acceptable threshold intermittently
MediumA genuine, unresolved insulation fault feeding current to earth at a location upstream of where repairs have been focused
MediumIncompatible dissimilar-metal bonding at a specific joint causing galvanic corrosion of the earth connection over time
LowEarth-fault protection threshold set tighter than the design tolerance for that circuit's natural leakage/capacitive current

Case Study: Division 21 — Preventive Maintenance Troubleshooting

Rev 0 — Initial issue
RCA-561 Human Error Root Cause

Symptom — An investigation traces a fault, near-miss, or nonconformance to an action or omission by a person (technician, operator, or engineer) rather than to a purely equipment, material, or design cause, and the finding needs to be developed into a defensible, non-punitive root cause rather than left as a generic "human error" label.

Equipment / System

Not equipment-specific — applies across CCR, cable, fixture, and sign work; relevant records are the specific work order, procedure/SOP in force at the time, and training records for the individual(s) involved.

Possible Causes
HighA procedure/SOP that is unclear, outdated, or does not match the actual field conditions, so a well-intentioned technician followed a flawed instruction correctly
HighInadequate or outdated training on the specific task, so the individual lacked the knowledge to recognise the error at the time
MediumFatigue, time pressure, or an unreasonably short access/closure window that increased the likelihood of a slip or lapse
MediumA design or tooling factor that made the correct action difficult or the incorrect action easy (poor labelling, look-alike connectors, no error-proofing)
LowA one-off lapse or slip by an otherwise competent, well-trained individual under normal conditions, with no identifiable systemic contributing factor
LowDeliberate deviation from a known correct procedure, distinct from an unintentional error, requiring a different corrective path (supervision/accountability rather than training/design)
Required Tools
The specific SOP/procedure in force at the time of the eventIndividual training and competency recordsHuman Factors Analysis and Classification System (HFACS) or equivalent frameworkFishbone/Ishikawa diagram templateNon-punitive interview protocolFatigue/workload and shift-scheduling records, if relevant
Safety Precautions
  • Conduct the investigation and interviews in a non-punitive, just-culture manner to encourage honest input rather than defensive answers.
  • Do not use the RCA process to assign blame; focus on identifying and correcting systemic contributing factors.
  • Where an immediate safety risk is identified during investigation, address it without waiting for the full RCA to conclude.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionGather the factual timeline of the event — what was done, by whom, using which procedure and parts, and under what conditions (time, access window, fatigue).
  2. InterviewConduct a structured, non-punitive interview with the individual(s) involved, focused on understanding the conditions and decision points rather than assigning fault.
  3. 5-WhysAsk "why" repeatedly against the immediate action to trace back from the error itself to the systemic condition that made it possible or likely.
  4. ClassificationClassify the contributing factors using a human-factors framework (e.g., procedure adequacy, training adequacy, environmental/workload conditions, design/error-proofing) rather than stopping at "operator error."
  5. DistinctionExplicitly distinguish an unintentional slip/lapse/mistake from a deliberate procedural violation, since the two require different corrective paths.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the systemic root cause (procedure, training, workload, or design) that, if corrected, would prevent a similarly-conditioned individual from making the same error.
Data Points Reviewed
Procedure/SOP clarity and currency at time of event
Training/competency record for the individual on this specific task
Access window/time pressure and fatigue indicators
Design/tooling error-proofing features present or absent
Distinction between unintentional error and deliberate deviation
Prior similar events across the wider team (systemic vs. isolated)
Root Cause Verification

After the systemic contributing factor is corrected (procedure revision, training, workload adjustment, or design/error-proofing change), monitor for recurrence of the same error type across the team, not just the individual, over the following review period before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Revise the SOP/procedure if it was unclear, outdated, or mismatched to actual field conditions.
  • Provide targeted training to close the specific competency gap identified.
  • Adjust scheduling/workload where fatigue or unreasonable time pressure was a contributing factor.
  • Address the specific event outcome (equipment repair, safety correction) independently of the systemic corrective action.
Preventive Action
  • Introduce error-proofing (poka-yoke) design changes where a look-alike or ambiguous component/connector contributed to the error.
  • Build the lesson into onboarding and refresher training for the wider team, not only the individual involved.
  • Review scheduling practices to avoid systemic fatigue or excessive time pressure on safety-relevant tasks.
  • Maintain a just-culture reporting environment so similar near-misses are reported early rather than hidden.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-HR-01 (Just Culture and Non-Punitive Investigation Procedure)

Standards: ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 10.2, ICAO Doc 9859 (Safety Management Manual — human factors principles)

Case Study: Division 25 — Field Case Study Troubleshooting

Rev 0 — Initial issue
RCA-562 Installation Error Root Cause

Symptom — A fault is traced back to how a component was installed (wrong torque, wrong orientation, wrong depth, incorrect wiring) rather than to the component itself or a maintenance action, and the finding needs to be developed into a defensible installation-stage root cause, distinguishing it from a later maintenance-caused error.

Equipment / System

Applies across newly installed or recently reworked fixtures, cable, transformers, CCRs, and signs; relevant records are the installation work package, inspection/test records (ITP), and as-built documentation from the original installation.

Possible Causes
HighInstallation carried out against an out-of-date or incorrect revision of the drawing/specification, so the work was internally consistent but did not match current design intent
HighInadequate inspection and test plan (ITP) coverage at the installation stage, so the error was not caught before energisation/handover
MediumInstaller training or supervision gap on a specific technique (torque values, cable bend radius, connector seating) not covered in general electrical training
MediumDesign documentation itself ambiguous or missing a critical dimension/value, leaving the installer to interpret or assume
LowMaterial substitution made on site without proper change-control approval, altering the installation from what was specified
LowTime/schedule pressure at project handover leading to rushed final checks or incomplete punch-list closure
Required Tools
Original installation work package and as-built drawingsInspection and Test Plan (ITP) records and sign-offsDesign drawing revision historyInstaller training/certification recordsSite instruction/change-control logTorque and test value specification sheets
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before any re-inspection or rework of the suspect installation.
  • Treat any newly discovered installation defect as potentially affecting adjacent or similar installations from the same work package, pending wider inspection.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionPull the original installation work package, ITP records, and as-built drawing revision used for the specific item, and confirm which drawing revision was current at the time.
  2. 5-WhysTrace from the physical defect found back through the installation steps to identify at which stage the deviation from specification occurred.
  3. ClassificationDetermine whether the root cause is a documentation gap (wrong/ambiguous drawing), a process gap (ITP did not catch it), a training gap, or an unauthorised site change.
  4. InvestigationInspect a sample of similar installations from the same work package or installation crew/date to determine whether the error is isolated or systemic.
  5. Cross-CheckCompare the ITP sign-off record against what was physically found to identify whether the inspection step was skipped, performed incorrectly, or the criteria itself was inadequate.
  6. Root CauseConfirm whether the root cause sits with the design documentation, the ITP/inspection process, installer competency, or an unauthorised site substitution.
Data Points Reviewed
Drawing revision used vs. current approved revision
ITP sign-off record vs. physical as-found condition
Sample inspection of similar items from the same work package
Installer/crew and date associated with the original work
Site instruction/change-control log for the item
Torque/test values as-found vs. specification
Root Cause Verification

After rework to the correct specification and, where a systemic pattern is found, re-inspection of the full affected batch, confirm compliance across the sample and monitor for recurrence of the same defect type on any subsequent installation work before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Rework the specific item to the correct current specification.
  • Extend inspection to the full batch/work package if a systemic pattern is confirmed, and rework any further non-conforming items found.
  • Correct the design documentation if it was found to be ambiguous or outdated at the time of installation.
  • Formalise any site substitution that was made without proper change-control approval, or reverse it if not acceptable.
Preventive Action
  • Strengthen the ITP to explicitly cover the specific defect type found, with hold/witness points where appropriate.
  • Implement document control to ensure only the current drawing revision is available to installation crews.
  • Provide targeted training on the specific technique found deficient.
  • Require formal change-control sign-off for any on-site material or method substitution.

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-QA-02 (Inspection and Test Plan Procedure), SOP-DESIGN-02 (Design Change Control)

Case Study: Division 25 — Field Case Study Troubleshooting

Rev 0 — Initial issue
RCA-563 Design Error Root Cause

Symptom — A recurring or systemic fault across multiple otherwise-correctly installed and maintained units is traced back to the original design — an undersized component, an incorrect calculation, or a specification that does not match actual operating conditions — rather than to installation or maintenance practice.

Equipment / System

Applies at the system/design level — circuit design calculations, component specification selection, and design drawings — rather than to any single physical unit; relevant records are the original design calculation package and any design change history.

Possible Causes
HighA design calculation (circuit loading, voltage drop, thermal, or structural) based on incorrect or outdated assumptions that do not match actual field conditions
HighA component specified at or below its true operating margin for the application (undersized transformer wattage, wrong-category fixture) due to a specification error rather than a manufacturing defect
MediumDesign standard/version used was current at the time but has since been superseded, and the installed base has not been reviewed against the updated requirement
MediumA design review/checking process gap — the calculation or specification was not independently checked before issue
LowA design that was correct for original conditions but has been invalidated by subsequent changes (added fixtures, extended circuit, changed traffic pattern) without a corresponding design review
LowSoftware/tool error in the design calculation itself (spreadsheet formula error, outdated design software version) rather than an engineering judgement error
Required Tools
Original design calculation package and design basisApplicable design standard version in force at time of designDesign review/checking sign-off recordsAs-built vs. as-designed comparison across the affected populationDesign software/tool version and validation recordIndependent re-calculation using current methodology
Safety Precautions
  • Where a design error has immediate safety implications (e.g., undersized protection, structural inadequacy), flag for urgent interim mitigation without waiting for the full RCA to conclude.
  • LOTO affected circuits before any physical verification testing.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionPull the original design calculation package, design basis, and the standard/version in force at the time of design.
  2. Independent Re-CalculationIndependently re-calculate the parameter in question using current methodology and compare against the original design figure.
  3. Trend AnalysisCheck whether the defect appears consistently across all units designed to the same specification, confirming a design-level (not installation-level) cause.
  4. ClassificationDetermine whether the root cause is an assumption/methodology error, a superseded standard not yet reviewed, a missing design-check step, or an invalidating field change made without design review.
  5. Cross-CheckReview the design review/checking sign-off record to determine whether an independent check was performed and what it covered.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the specific design element and cause, and quantify the population of installed units affected by the same design.
Data Points Reviewed
Original design calculation vs. independent re-calculation
Design standard version at time of design vs. current
Design review/checking sign-off record
Consistency of the defect across all units to the same design
Field changes since original design (added load, extended circuit) vs. design review status
Design software/tool version used
Root Cause Verification

After the design is corrected and the affected population is identified and remediated (or a phased remediation plan is in place), verify a sample of remediated units against the corrected design and confirm the defect does not recur on any new installation using the corrected design before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Issue a corrected design calculation/specification and re-issue affected drawings.
  • Remediate installed units within the affected population according to a risk-prioritised plan.
  • Update the design standard reference if a superseded version was in use.
  • Add or strengthen the independent design-check step if that was found to be the gap.
Preventive Action
  • Require formal design review against any field changes (added load, circuit extension) that could invalidate the original design basis.
  • Establish a periodic review cycle to check installed designs against current standard revisions.
  • Validate design calculation tools/software against known reference cases periodically.
  • Maintain a register of standard design specifications and their applicable population for efficient future updates.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-DESIGN-01 (Design Calculation and Review Procedure)

Standards: ICAO Annex 14 Vol I, FAA AC 150/5340-30, applicable IS/IEC design standards for the component

Case Study: Division 24 — Complete Runway Lighting Failure

Rev 0 — Initial issue
RCA-564 Material Failure Root Cause

Symptom — A component fails in a way inconsistent with normal wear, thermal, or electrical stress, and physical/metallurgical or polymer evidence points to the material itself — composition, contamination, or intrinsic defect — rather than to how the component was installed, operated, or maintained.

Equipment / System

Applies to the specific material/component in question (cable insulation compound, transformer potting, connector polymer, fixture housing alloy, LED encapsulant) and the batch/supplier record for that material.

Possible Causes
HighA raw material composition or formulation defect from the supplier, present across an identifiable batch or production run
HighContamination introduced during manufacturing (foreign particulate, moisture, or incorrect curing) affecting a specific lot
MediumMaterial specified was of the wrong grade/rating for the actual environmental or electrical duty, even though correctly manufactured to its own (inadequate) specification
MediumStorage or handling degradation before installation (UV exposure, incorrect temperature storage, exceeded shelf life) affecting the material prior to it ever being installed
LowA material substitution made by the supplier or a sub-tier vendor without notification, differing from the originally qualified material
LowNormal end-of-life material degradation being mistaken for a premature material defect, when the population is in fact within expected life distribution
Required Tools
Failed sample retained for laboratory/metallurgical or polymer analysisMaterial specification and qualification test certificateSupplier batch/lot traceability recordsStorage and handling log for the material prior to installationComparable failure data from other users of the same material/batch, if availableStatistical/Weibull life-distribution analysis for the population
Safety Precautions
  • Handle failed material samples appropriately, particularly where thermal or chemical decomposition products may be present.
  • LOTO the circuit before removing any sample for analysis.
  • Package and label retained samples for chain-of-custody if supplier/warranty claims may follow.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionRetain a properly documented sample of the failed material/component and record its batch/lot number, installation date, and service conditions.
  2. Laboratory AnalysisArrange metallurgical, polymer, or chemical analysis of the failed sample as appropriate, comparing against the original material specification and a known-good reference sample.
  3. Trend AnalysisCheck failure incidence against batch/lot number and installation date to determine whether the defect is confined to an identifiable population.
  4. Statistical AnalysisWhere enough data exists, perform a Weibull or similar life-distribution analysis to determine whether failures are occurring earlier than the expected distribution for genuine end-of-life wear-out.
  5. Supplier VerificationCross-check the material specification and qualification certificate against what was actually supplied and installed, including any unauthorised substitution.
  6. Root CauseConfirm whether the material defect is a supplier/batch issue, a specification-adequacy issue, a storage/handling issue, or in fact normal expected end-of-life behaviour.
Data Points Reviewed
Laboratory/metallurgical or polymer analysis result vs. specification
Failure incidence by batch/lot number
Installation date and service condition of failed samples
Weibull/life-distribution analysis of the affected population
Material specification vs. actual supplied certificate
Storage/handling record prior to installation
Root Cause Verification

After replacement with verified-conforming material and, where a batch defect is confirmed, removal or monitoring of the remaining affected population, track failure incidence in that population over the following review period and confirm it returns to the expected baseline rate before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the failed component with material verified against a current, conforming certificate.
  • Quarantine and, where warranted, proactively replace remaining stock or installed units from a confirmed defective batch.
  • Pursue a supplier corrective action / warranty claim where the batch defect originates with the manufacturer.
  • Correct the material specification if it was found to be inadequate for the actual duty, independent of the batch issue.
Preventive Action
  • Require batch/lot traceability capture at installation for materials with known criticality, to enable faster batch-level RCA in future.
  • Add incoming material inspection/certificate verification as a standard receiving step for critical components.
  • Periodically request supplier quality data and any known field-issue notifications for qualified materials.
  • Review material specifications against actual field duty conditions periodically, not only at initial qualification.
Related References

SOP: SOP-RCA-01 (Root Cause Analysis and FRACAS Procedure), SOP-QA-03 (Incoming Material Inspection Procedure)

Standards: ISO 9001:2015 Cl. 8.4/8.7, relevant material standards (e.g. IEC/IS cable and polymer standards) for the component

Case Study: Division 05 — Runway Centreline Light Troubleshooting

Rev 0 — Initial issue
RCA-565 Manufacturing Defect Root Cause

Symptom — A component fails in a manner traced to how it was built by the manufacturer — an assembly, process control, or quality-escape issue — distinct from a raw material defect, an installation error, or normal operational wear, typically confirmed by teardown showing a consistent defect signature across a specific batch or production date range.

Equipment / System

Applies to the specific manufactured component (transformer, CCR module, connector, fixture, LED driver) and its manufacturing/quality record — production batch, factory acceptance test (FAT) data, and quality escape history.

Possible Causes
HighA process control failure at the manufacturer (incorrect assembly torque, missed soldering/potting step, inadequate curing) affecting a specific production run
HighA quality escape — the defect was measurable at Factory Acceptance Test but not caught due to inadequate FAT coverage or a sampling gap
MediumA design-for-manufacture issue where the design is correct in principle but difficult to consistently build correctly, leading to variable quality even within specification
MediumA supplier/sub-component change at the manufacturer not communicated or re-qualified, altering the build from the originally approved configuration
LowDamage occurring during the manufacturer's packaging, storage, or transit before the component ever reached site
LowA single random assembly-line error affecting an isolated unit, not indicative of a wider batch-level issue
Required Tools
Teardown and failure-mode analysis of the failed unitManufacturer batch/production date and serial traceabilityFactory Acceptance Test (FAT) records for the unit and batch, where availableManufacturer quality escape / field-return history for the product lineComparable failure reports from other operators using the same product, if availableStatistical analysis of failure rate by production batch
Safety Precautions
  • Handle and store the failed unit for teardown analysis with appropriate LOTO and PPE for any residual stored energy (capacitors, potted assemblies).
  • Package and label the unit for chain-of-custody if a manufacturer warranty/quality claim will follow.
Investigation Procedure
  1. Data CollectionRecord the manufacturer, model, batch/production date, and serial number of the failed unit, and pull FAT records if available.
  2. Teardown AnalysisPerform a controlled teardown of the failed unit to identify the specific defect signature — assembly, soldering, potting, or component placement issue.
  3. Trend AnalysisCheck failure incidence against production batch/date range to determine whether the defect is confined to an identifiable manufacturing run.
  4. BenchmarkWhere possible, obtain and inspect a unit from a different, unaffected batch of the same model to confirm the defect is batch-specific rather than inherent to the design.
  5. Manufacturer LiaisonShare the teardown findings with the manufacturer and request their internal quality/process records for the implicated batch.
  6. Root CauseConfirm whether the defect originates from a process control failure, a FAT coverage gap, a design-for-manufacture weakness, or an isolated single-unit assembly error.
Data Points Reviewed
Teardown defect signature vs. known manufacturing process steps
Failure incidence by production batch/date range
FAT record for the failed unit and batch, where available
Comparison unit from an unaffected batch
Manufacturer quality escape/field-return history for the model
Packaging/transit damage indicators
Root Cause Verification

After replacement with a unit confirmed from an unaffected batch or corrected production, and any manufacturer corrective action (process fix, expanded FAT coverage) is confirmed in place, track failure incidence in the remaining installed population for the following review period before closing the RCA.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the failed unit and any remaining units confirmed from the same defective batch.
  • Formally notify the manufacturer and pursue warranty replacement/quality corrective action for the implicated batch.
  • Increase incoming inspection or FAT witnessing for future deliveries of the same product until manufacturer corrective action is verified.
  • Escalate to design-for-manufacture review with the manufacturer if the issue is confirmed to stem from a marginal design rather than a one-off process lapse.
Preventive Action
  • Capture manufacturer batch/production date at installation for critical components to enable faster future batch-level RCA.
  • Request and review manufacturer quality escape data periodically for key product lines.
  • Where volume justifies it, negotiate FAT witnessing or expanded sampling for future orders of a product line with a known quality history.
  • Maintain a supplier/manufacturer quality scorecard informed by field failure RCA outcomes.
