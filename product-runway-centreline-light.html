Product Overview

Runway Centreline Light

What It Does

Runway centreline lights provide longitudinal visual guidance along the runway centreline and form an important component of the visual aids associated with precision approach and low-visibility runway operations.

Unlike runway edge lights, which define the lateral limits of the runway, centreline lights provide visual guidance along the runway's longitudinal axis.

The final fixture configuration should always be based on the approved runway lighting layout and applicable aerodrome requirements.

ICAO Annex 14 requires runway centre line lights on CAT II and CAT III precision approach runways, and on runways intended for take-off with operating minima below an RVR of the order of 400 m. It recommends them on CAT I precision approach runways, particularly where the runway is wider than 50 m. Confirm the clause and edition for the project.

Function of Centreline Lighting

The Primary Longitudinal Reference Through the Runway Environment

What the Lights Do

The runway centreline provides the primary longitudinal reference through the runway environment. Centreline lighting supports visual alignment by creating a controlled sequence of light sources along the runway centreline.

Centreline lighting should therefore be evaluated as an integrated photometric and electrical system.

Inset Configuration

In-Pavement Installation on the Aircraft Movement Path

Inset / In-pavement Installation

Runway centreline lights are generally associated with in-pavement / inset installation because the runway centreline is an aircraft movement path.

The fixture must be installed correctly within the pavement system to maintain optical alignment, mechanical integrity and serviceability.

The complete inset installation should be considered as an assembly, not simply as a light fitting.

Colour Sequence

Do Not Select by the Generic Term “Runway Centreline Light”

Colour Sequence

The colour sequence of runway centreline lighting is a critical technical characteristic. The applicable sequence depends on the runway configuration, runway length and governing aerodrome requirements.

Under ICAO Annex 14, Volume I (Chapter 5, runway centre line lights), the colour changes toward the runway end to give pilots distance-remaining information: white along the main length of the runway, alternating red and white from 900 m to 300 m before the runway end, and red for the last 300 m. On shorter runways the alternating section begins at the runway midpoint. FAA practice quotes the same zones in feet: white until 3,000 ft remain, alternating red and white from 3,000 ft to 1,000 ft, and red for the last 1,000 ft. Confirm the exact clause and edition applicable to the project.

Longitudinal spacing is also a selection input. ICAO Annex 14 spaces centreline lights at approximately 15 m, and allows approximately 30 m only where the serviceability objective can be demonstrated and the runway is used in RVR of 350 m or more. FAA practice is 50 ft (15 m). Confirm the clause and edition for the project.

The optical and colour configuration must match the approved runway lighting design.

Directional Optical Configuration

Correct Orientation Is Critical

Directional Optical Outputs

Centreline lights may be designed with directional optical outputs appropriate to the intended viewing direction. Important optical characteristics include:

  • Unidirectional output
  • Bidirectional output
  • Directional beam
  • Beam spread
  • Horizontal coverage
  • Vertical coverage
  • Intensity distribution
  • Colour-specific optical output

Why Orientation Matters

An incorrectly oriented centreline fixture can produce inadequate longitudinal guidance even when the fixture is electrically operational.

Optical direction, colour and beam distribution must be confirmed for each fixture position against the approved runway lighting layout.

On a bidirectional fixture, each side must show the sequence colour for its own direction of view, including the red and alternating red/white sections near the runway ends.

Why the Whole Chain Matters

Fixtures installed within an AGL series circuit must be electrically compatible with the complete circuit. The centreline fixture should be selected together with its associated isolating transformer and circuit configuration. Incorrect electrical selection can result in:

  • Incorrect light intensity
  • Improper intensity control
  • Electrical stress
  • Circuit instability
  • Transformer overheating
  • Premature fixture failure
  • Monitoring abnormalities

The complete circuit should be evaluated during design and commissioning. On precision approach runways, centreline circuits are normally arranged so that a single circuit failure does not leave an ineffective pattern (for example by interleaving fixtures across circuits); confirm against ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 8 and Doc 9157 Part 5.

Safety

Electrical Safety — Isolation and Lock-Out

WARNING — never open a live series circuit. An open series circuit driven by a constant-current regulator can produce dangerously high voltage. Opening, cutting or disconnecting any part of an energised circuit can cause serious injury or death and damage equipment.

  • De-energise the circuit at the regulator and apply the site lock-out / tag-out (LOTO) procedure before any work
  • Where the circuit must be isolated or shorted, use the series circuit cut-out switch or approved isolation device — not an improvised bypass
  • Verify the circuit is de-energised and the output is isolated before touching conductors, connectors, splices, transformers or fixtures
  • Do not disconnect or reconnect a fixture's secondary connector while the series circuit is energised: an open secondary on a series isolating transformer can develop hazardous voltage and damage the transformer
  • Work on the movement area only under the airport's permit-to-work system, with the runway or taxiway closed as required, using qualified personnel and the manufacturer's instructions
  • Follow the applicable national electrical safety regulations and site procedures

FAQ

Runway Centreline Light — Frequently Asked Questions

What does a runway centreline light do?

Runway centreline lights provide longitudinal visual guidance along the runway centreline and are an important part of the visual aids for precision approach and low-visibility runway operations. Unlike runway edge lights, which define the lateral limits of the runway, they give a visual reference along the runway's longitudinal axis.

When does ICAO require runway centre line lights?

ICAO Annex 14 requires runway centre line lights on CAT II and CAT III precision approach runways, and on runways intended for take-off with operating minima below an RVR of the order of 400 m. They are recommended on CAT I precision approach runways, particularly where the runway is wider than 50 m. Confirm the clause and edition for your project.

What is the colour sequence of runway centreline lights?

Under ICAO Annex 14, Volume I, the lights are white along the main length of the runway, alternating red and white from 900 m to 300 m before the runway end, and red for the last 300 m; on shorter runways the alternating section begins at the runway midpoint. FAA practice quotes the same zones in feet: white until 3,000 ft remain, alternating red and white from 3,000 ft to 1,000 ft, and red for the last 1,000 ft. Confirm the exact clause and edition applicable to the project.

What spacing is used for runway centreline lights?

ICAO Annex 14 spaces centreline lights at approximately 15 m, and allows approximately 30 m only where the serviceability objective can be demonstrated and the runway is used in RVR of 350 m or more. FAA practice is 50 ft (15 m). Confirm the clause and edition for the project.

Why are runway centreline lights installed in the pavement?

The runway centreline is an aircraft movement path, so centreline lights are generally inset (in-pavement) fixtures. The complete installation should be treated as an assembly: fixture, housing, optical assembly, sealing and drainage arrangement, secondary connection, isolating transformer and primary circuit connection. Correct installation in the pavement maintains optical alignment, mechanical integrity and serviceability.

What is the difference between unidirectional and bidirectional centreline lights?

Centreline lights may have unidirectional or bidirectional optical output, appropriate to the intended viewing direction. On a bidirectional fixture, each side must show the sequence colour for its own direction of view, including the red and alternating red/white sections near the runway ends. An incorrectly oriented fixture can give inadequate longitudinal guidance even when it is electrically operational.

How are runway centreline lights powered?

They are fed from the AGL series circuit: constant current regulator, primary AGL cable, isolating transformer, secondary cable, then the inset centreline light. The fixture should be selected together with its isolating transformer and circuit configuration. On precision approach runways, centreline circuits are normally arranged so that a single circuit failure does not leave an ineffective pattern, for example by interleaving fixtures across circuits; confirm against ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 8 and Doc 9157 Part 5.

Is a centreline light that is switched on automatically compliant?

No. A centreline light that is electrically operational is not automatically photometrically compliant. Colour, intensity, beam distribution, optical direction, centreline alignment and colour sequence should be verified through the applicable inspection or measurement process rather than by visual observation alone.

What are the common faults in runway centreline lights?

Typical symptoms are a light that is off (open series circuit, failed light source, isolating transformer, connector or secondary cable fault), low light output, intermittent operation (loose connection or moisture ingress), incorrect colour and incorrect beam direction. Troubleshoot in sequence: CCR output, primary series circuit, isolating transformer, secondary cable, connector, inset fixture, light source and optical system, then confirm output photometrically. See the electrical safety warning before working on any circuit.

Which standards apply to runway centreline lights?

Relevant references include ICAO Annex 14, Volume I (Chapter 5 for runway centre line lights, Appendix 1 for colours, Appendix 2 for light characteristics, Chapter 8 for electrical systems and Chapter 10 for maintenance), ICAO Doc 9157 Parts 4 and 5, FAA AC 150/5340-30J for layout, spacing and colour, and FAA AC 150/5345-46F for the L-850A runway centreline fixture. Indian projects should also check DGCA requirements and the airport-specific specification. Verify the applicable edition before procurement.

What should be confirmed and requested before ordering centreline lights?

Confirm the runway length and classification, approach category, colour sequence, fixture spacing, optical direction, beam distribution, intensity steps, series-circuit current, isolating transformer, connector type, housing type, installation depth and pavement cut-out. Request the product datasheet, photometric data, colour sequence information, housing drawing, installation depth details, installation and maintenance manuals, compliance certificates and type-test reports.
