Insulation Resistance Tester

A specialized high-voltage test instrument used to measure the insulation resistance of AGL cables, transformers, and electrical equipment. Also known as a "megohmmeter" or "megger," this tool is essential for detecting insulation degradation, moisture ingress, and potential breakdown risks before they cause system failures.

Overview

What Is It?

An Insulation Resistance Tester (commonly referred to as a megger or megohmmeter) is a test instrument that applies a high DC voltage to an electrical cable or equipment and measures the resulting leakage current to determine the insulation resistance. In AGL systems, it is used to assess the health of:

  • Primary and secondary AGL cables – detecting insulation breakdown, moisture ingress, and mechanical damage
  • Isolating transformers – verifying primary-to-secondary and winding-to-ground insulation
  • Light fixtures – checking internal insulation integrity
  • Junction and distribution boxes – ensuring insulation between conductors and earth
  • CCRs and power equipment – verifying insulation of windings and bus bars

The tester typically offers multiple test voltages (250V, 500V, 1000V, 2500V, 5000V) to suit different cable ratings and applications. Results are displayed in megohms (MΩ) or gigohms (GΩ), with higher values indicating better insulation quality.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The insulation resistance tester exists to catch the kind of insulation breakdown — from moisture ingress, mechanical damage, or age-related degradation — that won't necessarily show up as an obvious fault yet, but represents a developing safety and reliability risk. A cable with insulation resistance quietly dropping over months of service isn't causing problems yet, but it's heading toward a leakage path or short that eventually will, and this test is what catches that trend early.

Applying a range of test voltages lets the instrument match the stress level to what a given cable or component is actually rated for, giving a meaningful health reading whether it's testing a low-voltage fixture circuit or a transformer's high-voltage winding insulation — making it one of the most routine tools for verifying that buried and hard-to-access AGL cabling remains safe and functional between visual inspections.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Insulation resistance testing is a critical part of AGL system commissioning, maintenance, and fault diagnosis:

  • Installation Commissioning: Testing newly installed cables and equipment to verify insulation integrity before energizing.
  • Preventive Maintenance: Periodic testing to detect gradual insulation degradation due to aging, moisture, or thermal stress.
  • Fault Diagnosis: Identifying cables with low insulation resistance that may cause nuisance tripping or earth faults.
  • Post-Repair Verification: Confirming that cable splices, terminations, and repairs have restored insulation integrity.
  • Transformer Testing: Verifying the insulation resistance of isolating transformer windings to detect internal faults.
  • Safety Verification: Ensuring that equipment is safe to operate and does not present a shock hazard.

Testing is performed on de-energized circuits, with proper safety precautions including lockout/tagout and discharge procedures.

Care

Storage & Handling

After each test, the cable or equipment under test is fully discharged before the leads are disconnected, since insulation resistance testing can leave a stored charge on the tested conductor that presents a shock hazard if handled immediately. Test leads are coiled loosely and stored in the case's dedicated slots, kept separate from other test leads to avoid confusing high-voltage-rated leads with those from lower-voltage instruments. The instrument is kept dry and within its rated storage conditions, with batteries removed for long periods of disuse, and is protected from the moisture and dust typical of buried cable testing environments during transport between test locations. Calibration is checked against the manufacturer's schedule, particularly given that this instrument's readings are often used as a pass/fail commissioning record where measurement accuracy needs to be demonstrably current.

Function

How Does It Work?

The insulation resistance tester operates by applying a known DC voltage between the conductor under test and ground (or between two conductors). The tester measures the tiny leakage current that flows through the insulation. Using Ohm's Law (R = V/I), it calculates and displays the resistance. Key principles include:

  • Test Voltage Selection: The appropriate voltage is selected based on the cable's rated voltage (typically 500V for 600V cables, 1000V for higher ratings).
  • Leakage Current Measurement: The tester measures currents in the microampere range to calculate resistance in megohms.
  • Timed Tests: Many testers support timed tests (e.g., 1-minute or 10-minute) to assess absorption characteristics and polarization index.
  • Automatic Discharge: After testing, the instrument automatically discharges the cable to ensure safety before disconnecting.
  • Data Logging: Advanced testers store results with timestamps for trend analysis and reporting.

The polarization index (PI) – the ratio of 10-minute to 1-minute insulation resistance – is a valuable diagnostic parameter that indicates the condition of insulation independent of temperature and humidity variations.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Because the currents being measured are tiny — nanoamps on good insulation — the instrument's architecture is built around keeping that signal clean from generation through to display. A regulated DC-DC converter steps the battery voltage up to the selected test voltage (250 V to 5 kV depending on model) and holds it stable under the near-open-circuit load that healthy insulation presents. The return current flows through a precision, low-noise current-to-voltage converter rather than a simple shunt, since a shunt's own leakage would swamp a nanoamp-scale signal, and this stage is typically guarded — surrounded by a driven shield at the same potential as the sense input — to stop surface leakage across the test leads or terminals from being counted as insulation leakage. The MCU digitises this signal, converts it to resistance via Ohm's law, and for timed tests runs a background clock that samples resistance at 1 minute and 10 minutes to compute the polarization index without operator intervention. When the test ends, the MCU switches the output relay from the HV source to an internal discharge resistor and holds the "test in progress" interlock active until the residual voltage decays to a safe level, only then releasing the leads for handling.

The guarded input stage — not the high voltage — is what makes a nanoamp-scale insulation measurement possible in the presence of surface contamination and lead leakage.
Operating Principle

Timed Test & Polarization Index

The Polarization Index compares the 10-minute and 1-minute insulation-resistance readings from the same test, giving a condition assessment that holds up across temperature and humidity swings that would otherwise skew a single-point reading.
Construction

Physical Construction

An insulation resistance tester is built as a rugged, hand-held instrument housed in an impact-resistant polymer case, typically rated for drop protection and sealed against dust and light moisture ingress for field use around airfield equipment. Internally, a high-voltage DC generator circuit — either battery-powered with a step-up converter or hand-cranked in older designs — produces the selectable test voltage, isolated from the low-voltage measurement and display electronics by internal insulation barriers rated well above the instrument's maximum test voltage. The test leads are heavy-duty, high-voltage-rated cable with insulated probes or crocodile clips, designed to be handled safely at the instrument's full output voltage. The front panel houses a backlit LCD display, rotary or push-button voltage selection, and connection terminals recessed or shrouded to prevent accidental contact during a test. A rechargeable battery pack or replaceable cells power the unit, and the case is typically fitted with a carrying strap or holster along with a soft case or hard shell for transport and storage in a maintenance vehicle.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Moisture Effects on Readings

High humidity or condensation on cable surfaces can significantly lower insulation resistance readings, leading to false indications of insulation failure. Testing should be performed under dry conditions, or readings should be corrected for humidity using standard correction factors.

Temperature Dependence

Insulation resistance decreases with increasing temperature. For consistent comparison, test results should be corrected to a standard reference temperature (typically 20°C) using temperature correction factors provided by cable manufacturers or standards.

Insufficient Discharge Time

Failing to fully discharge the cable after testing can result in electric shock or damage to the tester. Always allow the tester's automatic discharge function to complete, and verify that the cable is discharged before disconnecting test leads.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Insulation Resistance Reading Unexpectedly Low
Likely Cause
High humidity or surface condensation on the cable was present at the time of the test, artificially depressing the reading.
Diagnostic Steps
Check ambient humidity and cable surface condition at the time of test; repeat the test under dry conditions or apply the standard humidity correction factor.
Corrective Action
Retest once conditions are dry, or apply the appropriate correction factor before comparing the result against acceptance criteria.
Reading Does Not Match a Previous Test on the Same Cable
Likely Cause
The two tests were performed at different cable temperatures, and the result was not corrected to the standard reference temperature.
Diagnostic Steps
Record the cable temperature at time of test and apply the manufacturer's or standard's temperature correction factor to normalise both readings to 20°C.
Corrective Action
Recalculate both readings at the reference temperature before drawing any conclusion about a genuine change in insulation condition.
Technician Reports a Shock or the Tester Behaves Erratically After a Test
Likely Cause
The cable was disconnected before the tester's automatic discharge function had fully completed, leaving residual charge on the cable.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the test procedure used; check whether the discharge indicator or timer was allowed to run to completion before lead removal.
Corrective Action
Retrain on always waiting for the automatic discharge to finish and verifying full discharge before disconnecting test leads; inspect the tester for damage after any incident.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Calibration Drift
High Severity
Root Cause
Gradual ageing of the instrument's internal voltage source or measurement circuitry shifting readings away from a traceable calibration standard over repeated use.
Effect
A resistance reading no longer reflects the true insulation condition of the cable or component under test, risking a missed developing insulation breakdown or a false alarm on sound equipment.
Detection Method
Periodic calibration against a certified reference resistance per the manufacturer's schedule, with calibration certificates checked before commissioning or diagnostic work.
Incorrect Test Voltage Selection
High Severity
Root Cause
A test voltage selected that doesn't match what the cable or component under test is actually rated for, whether by operator error or a fault in the voltage-selection circuitry.
Effect
Too low a voltage gives a meaningless health reading that fails to stress the insulation realistically; too high a voltage risks damaging equipment that was never rated for it, such as a low-voltage fixture circuit tested at a transformer-winding voltage.
Detection Method
Confirmation of the equipment's voltage rating against the tester's selected test voltage before every test, and a functional check of the voltage-selection switch during scheduled instrument maintenance.
Test Lead or Probe Contact Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wear, corrosion, or damage to the test leads or probe tips that apply the high DC test voltage and measure the resulting leakage current.
Effect
Added contact resistance at the tester's own leads is folded into the reading, understating the true insulation resistance of the cable or component actually being tested.
Detection Method
Test lead continuity and resistance check before each test session, as a standard verification step.
Residual Charge Discharge Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A fault in the instrument's discharge circuit that's meant to safely bleed off the capacitive charge built up on a cable during a high-voltage insulation test.
Effect
A tested cable or component remains at a hazardous residual voltage after the test appears complete, creating a shock risk for the technician handling it next.
Detection Method
Discharge function verification during scheduled instrument maintenance, and a discharge time check as part of standard test procedure.
Battery / Internal Power Source Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Ageing battery or degraded internal power source reducing the instrument's ability to sustain its rated test voltage under load.
Effect
Reduced or unstable test voltage output, undermining the meaningful health reading the instrument is meant to provide at higher test voltages.
Detection Method
Battery condition check and test voltage verification against a known load before critical commissioning or trending measurements.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

High-Voltage Test Precautions
Electrical Safety

Treat the tester's output as a genuine high-voltage source even though its current is limited; keep test leads and probes clear of personnel and confirm the correct test voltage before applying it to a circuit.

Circuit De-Energisation Before Testing
Electrical Safety

Confirm the circuit under test is fully de-energised and isolated from its supply before connecting the tester; insulation testing an energised circuit can damage the instrument and endanger the operator.

Discharge Residual Charge After Test
Electrical Safety

Allow the tester's automatic discharge function to complete, or manually discharge the circuit, before disconnecting leads; cable capacitance can retain a hazardous charge briefly after the test voltage is removed.

Calibration Currency Verification
Operational

Check the tester's calibration due date before use and do not rely on results from an instrument that is out of calibration for pass/fail compliance decisions.

PPE / Insulated Tools
Personnel Safety

Use insulated gloves and tools rated for the test voltage in use, and follow the site's electrical safety procedures for working near circuits that will be energised for testing.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Always Normalise to Reference Temperature Before Comparing Trends
Methodology

Comparing raw megohm readings taken on a cold winter morning against a reading from a hot summer afternoon can look like dramatic insulation degradation when it's actually just temperature effect. Apply the manufacturer's temperature correction before logging any value meant for trend comparison.

Surface Moisture Can Mimic a Genuine Insulation Fault
Methodology

A cable that's electrically sound but has surface condensation or dew on its jacket can produce a misleadingly low reading that looks like real insulation breakdown. Where practical, test in dry conditions or note ambient humidity so a low reading can be correctly attributed before condemning a cable.

Never Skip the Discharge Step, Even Under Time Pressure
Safety

Cables and transformer windings retain a stored charge after a megger test that can deliver a genuine shock if the tester's discharge cycle is interrupted or bypassed to save time. Always confirm full discharge before disconnecting leads, regardless of schedule pressure.

Match Test Voltage to the Component, Not a Default Setting
Test Voltage

Applying a higher test voltage than a component is rated for — a lamp fixture rated for 500V tested at 2500V, for example — can itself cause insulation breakdown that wasn't present before the test. Confirm rated test voltage for each component type before testing, don't default to the highest available setting.

A Single Low Reading Is Less Useful Than a Declining Trend
Trending

One low insulation resistance reading can be a measurement artifact from moisture or temperature, but a steadily declining trend across successive tests on the same cable is a much more reliable indicator of genuine insulation degradation. Log and compare against prior results rather than judging each test in isolation.

Record Test Voltage and Duration Alongside Every Result
Documentation

A megohm reading without its test voltage and dwell time recorded is difficult to compare meaningfully against a future test taken under different settings; standardise and log the full test parameters, not just the final number.
