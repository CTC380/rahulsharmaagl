Power Quality Monitor

A sophisticated instrumentation device that continuously measures and analyses the quality of electrical power supplied to AGL equipment. It monitors voltage, current, frequency, harmonics, power factor, and other critical parameters to ensure stable and reliable operation of Constant Current Regulators and lighting systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Power Quality Monitor (PQM) is an advanced electronic instrument designed to continuously assess the quality of electrical power in AGL distribution systems. It measures a wide range of parameters including voltage sags/swells, harmonics (THD), flicker, unbalance, frequency deviations, and transient events. By providing real-time data and historical trend analysis, the PQM enables early detection of power quality issues that could affect the performance and lifespan of Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), UPS systems, and sensitive electronic controls.

In the context of AGL, power quality is paramount — even minor disturbances can cause flickering lights, erratic CCR behaviour, or premature equipment failure. The PQM acts as a diagnostic tool for maintenance teams, helping them identify root causes of power-related problems and enabling proactive corrective actions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The PQM exists because CCRs and other AGL electronics can degrade or fail from power-quality problems that never trip a simple overcurrent or overvoltage protection device — harmonics, flicker, and unbalance can quietly stress equipment for months before a failure finally appears, by which point the root cause is far harder to trace back to its source. By continuously logging these parameters rather than just reacting to gross faults, the monitor turns an otherwise invisible, slow-developing degradation into visible trend data that maintenance teams can act on before a CCR or UPS actually fails. Its purpose is therefore preventive rather than protective: it doesn't stop a bad event from reaching equipment, but it gives the airfield's maintenance program the diagnostic evidence needed to fix the underlying power-supply problem before repeated stress turns into an unplanned outage.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Power Quality Monitors are typically installed at key points in the AGL power distribution network, including:

  • Main Incoming Supply: At the utility metering point to monitor the quality of power entering the airfield.
  • Power Distribution Panels: At the input of each distribution panel to monitor power quality for all connected CCRs.
  • CCR Inputs: At individual CCR input feeders to detect supply anomalies that could affect regulator performance.
  • UPS Input and Output: To verify that the UPS is providing clean power and to detect any degradation in UPS performance.
  • Generator Interface: At generator output to ensure stable power quality when operating on backup.
  • Integration with ALCMS: Providing continuous data to the central ALCMS for remote monitoring, alarming, and historical analysis.

PQM units are also used for compliance verification against IEEE 519, EN 50160, and other power quality standards, and to support warranty claims on sensitive AGL equipment.

Placement

Installation & Location

PQM units are DIN-rail or panel-mounted at the specific point in the network they are monitoring, rather than in one central location — units are fitted at the utility metering point, at the input of each power distribution panel, at individual CCR input feeders, and at the UPS and generator interface, with voltage connections made directly or through PTs and current sensed via CT loops around the incoming conductors. Each unit's communication port (RS-485, Ethernet, or fibre for IEC 61850) is wired back to the ALCMS network so readings from every monitoring point can be trended and alarmed centrally. Because CT and PT wiring must not be opened while energised, units are installed with test terminal blocks or shorting links to allow safe isolation for maintenance.

Function

How Does It Work?

A Power Quality Monitor operates by sampling voltage and current waveforms at high speed (typically up to 512 samples per cycle) and performing digital signal processing to extract a comprehensive set of power quality parameters. Key functions include:

  • Voltage and Current Measurement: True RMS values for each phase, including neutral and ground currents.
  • Harmonic Analysis: Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) and individual harmonic orders up to the 50th or 63rd, identifying harmonic sources and their impact.
  • Power Factor and Energy: Displacement and true power factor, active, reactive, and apparent power, and energy consumption.
  • Event Detection: Detection and recording of sags, swells, transients, interruptions, and frequency deviations with timestamping and waveform capture.
  • Flicker Measurement: Short-term and long-term flicker (Pst and Plt) as per IEC 61000-4-15.
  • Unbalance Monitoring: Negative sequence and zero sequence voltage and current unbalance.
  • Data Logging and Trend Analysis: Continuous recording of parameters with configurable intervals, enabling trend analysis and predictive maintenance.
  • Alarming and Communication: Configurable alarms for threshold violations, with notifications via Modbus, SNMP, or digital outputs to the ALCMS.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A Power Quality Monitor is an observation point, not a control point — it has no direct authority over the current loop, the CCR, or any fixture. Its role in the wider control architecture is to sample, analyse, and report: the diagram below shows how one sampled waveform is processed in parallel into every power quality parameter, then merged into a single logging and alarming stream that is pushed upstream via Modbus, SNMP, or a digital output. What happens next is decided elsewhere. Alarm data typically reaches the ALCMS, where it can be correlated with occupancy, weather, or maintenance schedules and turned into an action — dispatching a technician, logging a trend for predictive maintenance, or, where the PQM is monitoring a CCR's output directly, informing a decision to derate or trip that circuit. On sites using a Smart CCR, the two devices often sit side by side on the same communications network: the Smart CCR already self-monitors its own output quality internally, so an independent PQM adds an unbiased second measurement point plus broader visibility across sags, swells, and harmonics that a single regulator's own diagnostics wouldn't otherwise capture.

Construction

Physical Construction

A Power Quality Monitor is typically a compact DIN-rail or panel-mounted instrument, housed in a moulded plastic or steel enclosure with a front-facing LCD or LED display for local readout of key parameters. Internally, high-accuracy voltage and current measurement circuitry — fed from voltage taps and current transformers on the monitored circuit — interfaces with a digital signal processor that computes harmonics, flicker, and other power quality indices. Communication ports (Ethernet, RS-485/Modbus, or USB) are provided on the rear or base for integration with the ALCMS or a dedicated power quality management system, along with terminal blocks for the voltage and CT current inputs and the unit's own low-voltage supply. Where installed within a CCR cabinet or distribution panel rather than as a standalone instrument, the PQM is typically DIN-rail mounted alongside the other control electronics, sharing the parent enclosure's cooling and cable entry provisions.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect CT/PT Ratios

Misconfiguration of current transformer (CT) and potential transformer (PT) ratios is a frequent error, leading to inaccurate readings. Always verify CT/PT settings during commissioning and after any replacement.

Communication Loss with ALCMS

PQMs often use Modbus TCP or RTU. Network interruptions, incorrect IP addressing, or baud rate mismatches can result in loss of data to the central system. Implement redundant communication paths and regular status checks.

Data Overload and Storage Management

Continuous logging of high-resolution data can quickly fill internal storage. Configure appropriate logging intervals and implement automatic data upload to a central historian to prevent loss of critical event data.

Calibration Drift

Over time, measurement accuracy can drift. Regular calibration (typically annually) using a portable calibrator or factory calibration service is essential to maintain the integrity of power quality data.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Inaccurate or Implausible Readings
Likely Cause
Current transformer (CT) or potential transformer (PT) ratios have been misconfigured, a frequent commissioning or replacement error.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the configured CT/PT ratios in the monitor against the actual installed transformer ratings; cross-check a known load against the displayed reading.
Corrective Action
Correct the CT/PT ratio configuration in the monitor and re-verify readings against a calibrated reference.
Loss of Communication with the ALCMS
Likely Cause
Damaged or poorly terminated RS-485/Ethernet cabling, connector corrosion, or a network configuration mismatch (baud rate, parity, IP addressing) between the two devices.
Diagnostic Steps
Check cable continuity and shielding/grounding along the run; verify termination resistors are correctly placed; confirm both ends are configured with matching communication settings.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable section, correct the termination or configuration mismatch, and confirm stable communication over a sustained test period before returning to service.
Missing Event Data or Full Storage
Likely Cause
Continuous high-resolution logging has filled the monitor's internal storage, overwriting or preventing capture of new event data.
Diagnostic Steps
Check current storage utilisation and logging interval configuration; review whether automatic upload to a central historian is active.
Corrective Action
Adjust logging intervals to an appropriate resolution and configure automatic data upload to a central historian to prevent future storage loss.
Measurement Accuracy Drifting Over Time
Likely Cause
Normal component aging has caused the monitor's measurement accuracy to drift outside acceptable tolerance since its last calibration.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare current readings against a portable calibrator or reference instrument at the same measurement point.
Corrective Action
Perform calibration via a portable calibrator or the manufacturer's factory calibration service, and log the result against the annual schedule.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Sensor / CT Calibration Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing of current transformer or voltage sensing components, or thermal cycling gradually shifting the calibration reference over years of service.
Effect
Inaccurate or implausible readings that erode confidence in the monitor's data without an obvious fault indication.
Detection Method
Periodic calibration check against a reference meter, and trend review of readings for a gradual drift pattern.
Loss of Communication with the ALCMS
High Severity
Root Cause
Damaged or corroded communication cabling, a failed communication port, or electrical noise on a shared conduit run.
Effect
Power quality data stops reaching the ALCMS, leaving a gap in the centralised monitoring and trend record.
Detection Method
Communication diagnostic/heartbeat check at the ALCMS, and continuity testing of the comms cable.
Data Storage Full / Logging Failure
Low Severity
Root Cause
Storage media reaching end of write-cycle life, a full log buffer with no rotation configured, or file-system corruption after an unclean shutdown.
Effect
Missing event data or an inability to log new measurements, leaving gaps in the historical record.
Detection Method
Storage capacity and health check during maintenance, and verification that historical logs are complete after any monitor reboot.
Measurement Accuracy Drifting Over Time
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Component ageing in the analogue front-end, or accumulated thermal stress shifting reference voltages away from factory calibration.
Effect
Readings slowly diverge from actual circuit conditions, potentially masking a genuine developing power quality issue.
Detection Method
Scheduled calibration verification against a certified reference instrument, at the interval specified by the manufacturer.
Surge Damage to Input Circuits
High Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced transient or switching surge on the monitored power lines that exceeds the input protection rating.
Effect
Damaged sensing inputs, erratic readings, or complete loss of monitoring function on the affected channel.
Detection Method
Post-storm functional check of each monitored channel, and inspection of onboard surge protection devices for visible damage.
Firmware / Software Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A firmware update interrupted mid-write, an electrical transient during operation, or a latent software fault triggered by a specific data condition.
Effect
Unit becomes unresponsive, reboots unexpectedly, or produces implausible readings until power-cycled or reflashed.
Detection Method
Event log review after an anomaly, and firmware version verification against the latest validated release.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Never Open-Circuit a Live CT Secondary
Electrical Safety

If the monitor is connected via current transformers, never open the CT secondary circuit while the primary is energised; an open CT secondary can develop a dangerous high-voltage spike. Always short the secondary before disconnecting the monitor's current inputs.

Live Voltage Measurement Points
Electrical Safety

Voltage inputs are typically wired directly to distribution busbars or CCR supply terminals; treat these connections as live at all times the monitored circuit is energised, and use rated test leads and PPE for any measurement or wiring work.

PPE for Work Near Live Instrumentation Wiring
Personnel Safety

Wear appropriate PPE when working inside a panel where the monitor's CT and PT wiring is present, since these connections often can't be isolated without also isolating the circuit being measured.

ESD-Sensitive Electronics
Operational

Use anti-static precautions when handling the monitor's internal processing board or communication interface cards.

Coordinate Before Isolating a Monitored Circuit
Operational

If diagnosing a power-quality event requires isolating the circuit the monitor is connected to, coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM as for any other work on that circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm all CT/PT connections are correctly re-terminated and the monitor is reading plausible values before closing out the work, since a swapped or open CT connection can silently produce incorrect readings.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
An Incorrect CT/PT Ratio Poisons Every Reading Downstream
Commissioning

If a current or voltage transformer's ratio is entered wrong in the monitor's configuration, every parameter it derives — harmonics, sags, unbalance — will be internally consistent-looking but systematically wrong, and the error can go unnoticed for a long time since nothing looks obviously broken. Verify CT/PT ratio configuration against the actual installed transformers at commissioning, not just trust the default or as-specified value.

Calibration Drift Is Slow and Easy to Miss Without a Reference Check
Calibration

A power quality monitor's own internal sensing can drift gradually from a true reference over years without producing any alarm, since the unit has no independent way to know its own readings have become inaccurate. Periodically verify calibration against a traceable reference meter rather than relying solely on the monitor's self-reported health status.

Alarm Thresholds Should Reflect the Site's Actual Supply, Not Generic Defaults
Configuration

A monitor left on factory-default alarm thresholds can either flood operators with nuisance alarms on a site with naturally noisier supply characteristics, or miss genuinely significant events on an unusually clean supply where the defaults are too loose. Tune alarm thresholds against the site's own baseline power quality data rather than leaving generic defaults in place.

Data Logging Storage Fills Up Silently
Maintenance

A monitor logging detailed waveform captures for every transient event can fill its onboard storage and begin overwriting older data without any obvious operator notification, right around the time a historical trend would actually be needed for an investigation. Review storage utilisation and logging retention settings on a schedule rather than assuming capacity is unlimited.

Losing Comms With the ALCMS Doesn't Mean the Monitor Has Stopped Working
Diagnostics

A power quality monitor can continue measuring and logging correctly at the device itself even while its link to the ALCMS is down, and treating "no data at the ALCMS" as "the monitor has failed" leads to unnecessary equipment replacement. Check the communication link and the device's local logs separately before concluding the monitor itself needs attention.

Correlate PQM Events With CCR Fault Timestamps
Troubleshooting

A sag, swell, or transient event logged by the power quality monitor at the same timestamp as a CCR fault trip is often the real root cause of that trip, but the two systems' records are rarely cross-referenced by default. Make a habit of checking PQM event history against any CCR fault timestamp during root-cause investigation rather than treating the CCR fault in isolation.
