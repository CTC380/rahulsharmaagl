RSK-01 Asset Criticality Methodology
Purpose

Establishes a consistent method for determining the importance of each AGL asset. The methodology should avoid assigning criticality simply because an asset is expensive or technically complex.

Should Consider
  • Safety consequence
  • Operational consequence
  • Regulatory consequence
  • Failure consequence
  • Probability of failure
  • Availability of redundancy
  • Restoration difficulty
  • Availability of spares
  • Replacement lead time
  • Effect on airport operations
Basic Concept
Criticality = Consequence of Failure × Failure Likelihood

The exact scoring methodology should be formally approved by the airport/asset-management organization.

RSK-03 Safety-Critical Assets
Purpose

Safety-critical assets are assets where failure can have a significant effect on aviation safety — identified as a separate criticality category.

Potential Examples
  • Critical runway lighting assets
  • PAPI
  • Approach lighting
  • Critical runway signage
  • Critical CCR/circuit equipment
  • Other assets whose failure has a direct safety consequence
Assessment Should Consider
  • Aircraft operating environment
  • Consequence of loss of visual guidance
  • Availability of alternative systems
  • Failure duration
  • Required restoration time
  • Operational restrictions
  • Applicable requirements
Scope Boundary

Classification should be based on the airport's approved risk assessment — not on automatically treating every item of a particular equipment type as equally critical.

RSK-04 Operationally Critical Assets
Purpose

Operational criticality measures the effect of asset failure on airport operational continuity.

Key Questions
  • Will the runway become unavailable?
  • Will taxiway operations be affected?
  • Will aircraft movements be restricted?
  • Is another circuit/system available?
  • Can the failure be isolated?
  • How long will restoration take?
  • Is a replacement available?
  • Is a spare available?
Example

A CCR supplying an entire critical runway circuit may have significantly greater operational criticality than a single non-critical auxiliary component.

RSK-05 Runway Asset Criticality
Purpose

Runway assets require dedicated criticality assessment because their failure may affect runway operations and aircraft guidance.

Assets May Include
  • Runway edge lights
  • Runway threshold lights
  • Runway end lights
  • Runway centerline lights
  • Touchdown-zone lighting, where applicable
  • Runway-related signs
  • Associated isolation transformers
  • Primary circuit cables
  • Secondary connections
  • CCRs
  • Associated control/monitoring equipment
Assessment Factors
  • Number of affected lights
  • Location of failed asset
  • Circuit arrangement
  • Redundancy
  • Effect on required visual guidance
  • Failure pattern
  • Time required for restoration
  • Operational consequences
Scope Boundary

Criticality should consider the function and system context, not merely the equipment type.

RSK-06 Taxiway Asset Criticality
Purpose

Evaluates the consequence of failure of taxiway lighting and associated equipment.

Potential Assets
  • Taxiway edge lights
  • Taxiway centerline lights
  • Stop bars
  • Runway guard lights
  • Taxiway signs
  • Isolation transformers
  • Cables
  • CCRs
  • Control/monitoring systems
Assessment Questions
  • Does the failure affect a critical taxi route?
  • Is an alternate taxi route available?
  • Does the failure affect runway entry/exit?
  • Does it affect low-visibility operations?
  • How quickly can the system be restored?
RSK-07 PAPI Criticality
Purpose

PAPI criticality assesses the importance of the PAPI system to aircraft approach guidance — identified as an individual assessment category.

Consider
  • Runway served
  • Operational role
  • Availability of alternative visual guidance
  • PAPI unit failure
  • Control/power-system failure
  • Photometric condition
  • Alignment condition
  • Reliability history
  • Restoration requirements
Asset-Level Assessment

The criticality assessment can distinguish between:

  • PAPI system
  • Individual PAPI units
  • Power supply
  • Control equipment
  • Associated cabling
RSK-08 CCR Criticality
Purpose

CCR criticality should consider the AGL circuit or circuits supplied by the regulator. A CCR may be highly critical because one equipment failure can affect multiple field assets simultaneously.

Assessment Factors
  • Number of circuits supplied
  • Runway/taxiway served
  • Circuit importance
  • Available redundancy
  • CCR capacity
  • Failure history
  • Spare availability
  • Repair complexity
  • Replacement lead time
  • Operational consequence
Example

A CCR supplying a critical runway lighting circuit may receive a higher criticality rating than a CCR serving a less operationally significant area.

RSK-09 Transformer Criticality
Purpose

Refers particularly to isolating transformers within the AGL series circuit.

Should Consider
  • Asset location
  • Lighting function served
  • Number of assets affected
  • Circuit importance
  • Failure frequency
  • Accessibility
  • Replacement difficulty
  • Spare availability
  • Environmental exposure
Important Principle

The criticality of a transformer should be evaluated based on the function it supports, not only its purchase value.

RSK-10 Cable Criticality
Purpose

Evaluates the consequence of primary/secondary cable failure.

Consider
  • Circuit served
  • Cable length
  • Number of downstream assets
  • Circuit topology
  • Fault isolation capability
  • Cable accessibility
  • Fault location
  • Repair complexity
  • Spare cable availability
  • Restoration time
Example

A primary series-circuit cable supplying a major runway circuit can have much higher criticality than a short secondary connection serving an individual fixture.

RSK-11 Sign Criticality
Purpose

Airfield-sign criticality should consider the operational function of the sign.

Potential Factors
  • Sign location
  • Sign type
  • Runway/taxiway served
  • Navigation importance
  • Low-visibility operational relevance
  • Alternative visual information
  • Failure consequence
  • Restoration time
Examples

Potentially different criticality categories could apply to: runway designation signs, mandatory instruction signs, location signs, direction signs, and taxiway guidance signs. The assessment should be based on actual airport operational context.

RSK-12 ALCMS Criticality
Purpose

ALCMS criticality should consider the system's role in controlling and monitoring AGL — identified as its own category within Division 03.

Consider
  • Number of AGL systems controlled
  • Monitoring functions
  • Control dependency
  • Communication/network dependency
  • Redundancy
  • Manual fallback capability
  • Failure consequence
  • Recovery time
  • Software/hardware obsolescence
  • Vendor support

RSK-13 Failure Consequence Assessment
Purpose

Failure consequence assessment asks: what happens if this asset fails? This is one of the most important steps in the risk process.

Consequence Categories
  • Safety — aircraft safety impact, loss of visual guidance, reduced operational safety margin
  • Operational — runway restriction, taxiway restriction, reduced airport capacity, operational delay
  • Regulatory — non-compliance, required operational restrictions, inspection/testing consequences
  • Financial — repair cost, replacement cost, downtime cost, operational disruption
  • Reputation/service — operational disruption, passenger impact, airline impact
RSK-14 Probability of Failure
Purpose

Probability of failure estimates how likely an asset is to fail within a defined period.

Evidence Can Include
  • Historical failures
  • Failure frequency
  • Age
  • Condition
  • Inspection findings
  • Testing results
  • Maintenance history
  • Manufacturer information
  • Environmental exposure
  • Operating conditions
  • Repeat failures
  • Obsolescence
Example Scale

The actual definitions should be established by the approved risk methodology.

RSK-18 Risk-Based Maintenance Priority
Purpose

Risk-based maintenance means maintenance resources are prioritized according to risk rather than simply equipment age or a fixed schedule.

High-Risk Asset May Require
  • More frequent inspection
  • More frequent testing
  • Condition monitoring
  • Critical spare
  • Faster corrective response
  • Detailed failure analysis
  • Earlier replacement consideration
Lower-Risk Asset May Receive
  • Normal inspection
  • Standard preventive maintenance
  • Standard testing
  • Normal replacement planning
Cross-Reference

This connects Division 03 directly with Division 09 — Maintenance Strategy & Optimization.

RSK-19 Risk Mitigation Planning
Purpose

Risk mitigation defines what should be done to reduce unacceptable asset risk.

Mitigation Options
  • Reduce probability — preventive maintenance, condition monitoring, inspection, testing, component replacement, reliability improvement
  • Reduce consequence — redundancy, alternative circuits, spare equipment, emergency response, faster restoration, operational contingency
  • Eliminate risk — replacement, technology upgrade, system redesign, asset removal

RSK-20 Criticality Review
Purpose

Criticality should not remain permanently fixed. An asset's criticality may change because of airport operational changes, runway/taxiway configuration changes, new equipment, system modification, redundancy changes, asset degradation, failure trends, obsolescence, or a change in regulatory requirements.

Review Triggers

Criticality should be reconsidered following:

  • Major failure
  • Repeated failure
  • Major modification
  • System expansion
  • Change in runway/taxiway operation
  • Change in redundancy
  • Significant condition deterioration
  • Technology change
  • Regulatory change
Review Record Should Capture
  • Previous criticality
  • New criticality
  • Reason for change
  • Supporting evidence
  • Reviewer
  • Approval
  • Review date

Most Important Design Principle

Division 03 should remain the risk-decision layer. It determines which assets matter most, what can happen if they fail, how likely failure is, and where management and maintenance resources should be prioritized — it does not define the overall strategy (Division 01), and it does not hold the detailed asset data structure (Division 02).

Criticality = Consequence of Failure × Failure Likelihood. An old asset is not automatically a critical asset, and a new asset is not automatically low risk — criticality should always be based on consequence and likelihood evidence, not equipment age or purchase value alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Asset Criticality & Risk Assessment in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 03 — Asset Criticality & Risk Assessment in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 03 is the risk-decision layer. It covers 20 topics on criticality methodology, safety/operational criticality, risk matrices, critical asset registers and risk-based maintenance priority.

Q2What is an asset criticality methodology for AGL assets?

It is a consistent method for determining the importance of each AGL asset. It considers safety, operational and regulatory consequence, failure consequence and probability of failure, and should not assign criticality simply because an asset is expensive.

Q3How does criticality ranking affect asset management?

Criticality ranking converts the assessment into an understandable priority level. That level then drives maintenance priority, inspection and testing frequency, spare strategy and condition monitoring. The actual rating thresholds should come from the approved airport risk methodology.

Q4Which AGL assets get their own criticality assessment?

Division 03 covers runway assets, taxiway assets, PAPI, CCRs, isolating transformers, cables, airfield signs and ALCMS, each with its own criticality topic. It also identifies safety-critical and operationally critical assets as separate categories.

Q5Why is CCR criticality assessed by the circuits it supplies?

A CCR may be highly critical because one equipment failure can affect multiple field assets. The assessment therefore considers the number of circuits supplied, the runway or taxiway served, circuit importance, available redundancy and CCR capacity.

Q6How do probability, consequence and the risk matrix work together?

Failure consequence asks what happens if the asset fails, and probability of failure estimates how likely failure is within a defined period, using factors such as historical failures, age, condition, inspection findings and testing results. The risk matrix combines the two, and asset risk ranking then sets the relative priority of assets.

Q7What is a critical asset register?

The critical asset register is a controlled subset of the master asset register. It holds the assets that need enhanced management because of their risk or criticality, with fields such as asset ID, type, location, criticality, risk score and safety consequence.

Q8What is risk-based maintenance priority, and should criticality be reviewed?

Risk-based maintenance prioritizes resources according to risk rather than simply equipment age or a fixed schedule, for example with more frequent inspection or testing, condition monitoring, critical spares and faster corrective response. Criticality is not permanent: a major or repeated failure, a major modification, system expansion or a change in runway or taxiway operation should trigger a review.
