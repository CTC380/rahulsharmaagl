Touchscreen HMI

The primary local operator interface for Airfield Ground Lighting systems. A rugged, industrial-grade touchscreen panel that provides intuitive visualisation, control, and monitoring of CCRs, lighting circuits, and system status, enabling rapid operator response and streamlined maintenance workflows.

Overview

What Is It?

A Touchscreen HMI (Human-Machine Interface) is an industrial-grade operator panel that provides a graphical, touch‑based interface for local control and monitoring of the Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS). Typically installed in CCR control rooms, substations, or maintenance workshops, the HMI displays real‑time system status, circuit health, alarm conditions, and performance data in an intuitive visual format. Operators can change brightness steps, switch circuits on/off, acknowledge alarms, and perform diagnostics directly through the touchscreen. The HMI is designed for harsh industrial environments—featuring rugged enclosures, wide temperature tolerance, sunlight‑readable displays, and glove‑compatible touch technology. It communicates with the AGL control system (PLCs, Remote I/O, ALCMS server) via industrial Ethernet or serial protocols, providing a reliable and responsive local control point that complements the central ALCMS workstation.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The touchscreen HMI exists to give an operator direct, local control over AGL circuits at the point where that control is most immediately needed — a CCR room or substation — without requiring a trip to the central ALCMS workstation or a phone call to relay a command. In situations where a fast local response matters, such as isolating a faulted circuit or adjusting brightness during changing visibility, having a rugged, responsive interface right at the equipment shortens the time between noticing a problem and acting on it.

Its ruggedised, sunlight-readable, glove-compatible design exists because this interface has to work reliably in the same harsh conditions the equipment it controls operates in — a touchscreen that fails in cold weather or can't be read in direct sun would undermine the very purpose of giving operators a dependable local control point.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Touchscreen HMIs are deployed in various locations across the airfield where local, real‑time AGL control and monitoring are required:

  • CCR Control Rooms: Primary HMIs for operators to manage all lighting circuits, view system status, and respond to alarms.
  • Remote Substations: HMIs at satellite CCR locations for local control and troubleshooting during maintenance or emergency situations.
  • Runway Edge Cabinets: Compact HMIs for local circuit status and override control during installation or testing.
  • Maintenance Workshops: HMIs used for testing and diagnostics of lighting circuits and components.
  • ATC Towers (auxiliary): Secondary HMIs providing tower controllers with quick visibility of lighting status (e.g., brightness step, circuit availability).
  • Approach Lighting Control Shelters: HMIs for local control of sequencers, strobe power supplies, and flasher units.
  • RWSL (Runway Status Light) System: HMIs for local monitoring of stop bar and runway guard light status.
  • Training and Simulation: HMIs used for operator training and system familiarization.
Placement

Installation & Location

HMIs are panel-mounted into the CCR control room console, substation cabinet door, or a portable maintenance stand, positioned at a height and angle suited to standing or seated operator use at that specific location. Units in outdoor or unconditioned cabinets (runway edge cabinets, remote substations) use a sealed, temperature-rated panel-mount enclosure, while control room units can use a standard desk or console mount; network cabling back to the local PLC or ALCMS runs on structured Ethernet, with power fed from the same cabinet supply as the PLC it displays, so the HMI's local control and display capability survives a network interruption to the central server.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Touchscreen HMI functions as a local operator interface, providing a rich set of features for controlling and monitoring the AGL system:

  • System Visualization: Displays a graphical overview of the airfield layout, lighting circuits, CCRs, and field devices, with colour‑coded status indicators (green = OK, red = fault, amber = warning).
  • Circuit Control: Allows operators to turn individual circuits on/off, adjust brightness steps (typically 5‑step control), and set operational modes (e.g., manual, automatic, standby).
  • Alarm Management: Presents active alarms with priority, timestamp, and description. Operators can acknowledge, silence, or investigate alarms, and view historical alarm logs.
  • Data Monitoring: Displays real‑time electrical parameters (current, voltage, power, load) for each CCR and circuit, as well as lamp failure status and circuit continuity.
  • Event Logging: Records operator actions, system events, and alarm history for audit trails and post‑incident analysis.
  • Diagnostic Tools: Provides built‑in tools for loop testing, lamp test, and circuit continuity checks, facilitating rapid fault finding.
  • Security & Access Control: Enforces user authentication (password or RFID) and role‑based permissions to prevent unauthorized control actions.
  • Communication: Interfaces with the PLC, ALCMS server, or Remote I/O via Ethernet (Modbus TCP, Profinet, Ethernet/IP) or serial (Modbus RTU, Profibus) to exchange data and commands.

The HMI runs a dedicated runtime application that is configured using engineering software (e.g., Siemens WinCC, Schneider Vijeo Designer, Beckhoff TwinCAT HMI) to match the specific AGL system architecture and operator workflows.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Touchscreen HMI is typically a local operator interface mounted at or near the equipment it controls — a CCR cabinet or remote substation panel — rather than the central ALCMS workstation in the tower or ops room. It connects directly to the local PLC or CCR, most often over the same Ethernet or serial link the PLC uses to talk to the wider ALCMS, and gives maintenance or duty staff at that location the ability to view circuit status and issue commands without needing access to the central workstation. Depending on the installation, it either mirrors a subset of what the central ALCMS shows or operates semi-independently for local diagnostics, but commands it issues still pass through the same PLC validation and interlock logic as commands from the central workstation — it is an additional access point into the control chain, not a parallel one.

Construction

Physical Construction

The unit is built around a TFT display and touch sensor (projected capacitive or resistive) bonded to a front bezel that meets IP65/NEMA 4X sealing at the front face, gasketed against the surrounding panel cutout to keep the operator-facing surface protected from washdown, dust, and de-icing fluid even though the rear enclosure need only meet the lower IP20 rating typical of an equipment-room panel mount. Behind the display, the processor board, memory, and storage are mounted on standoffs inside a metal or high-impact plastic rear housing, with communication ports (Ethernet, serial, USB) brought out through the rear or bottom face away from the sealed front. The assembly mounts into a panel cutout using integrated mounting clips or a VESA bracket, and where deployed in a fully field-exposed location rather than a control-room panel, the entire enclosure is upgraded to a sealed IP65 rear housing with cable glands for each conductor entry. Sunlight-readable backlight options and glove-compatible touch sensitivity are built into the display stack itself rather than added as a separate module, keeping the front assembly a single sealed unit.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Display Burn-in / Image Retention

Static screens (e.g., constant display of a circuit overview) can cause burn‑in or image retention on some display technologies. Use screen savers, dimming, or periodic screen rotation to mitigate.

Touch Calibration Drift

Over time, the touchscreen can lose calibration, leading to inaccurate touch registration. Re‑calibrate periodically using the built‑in utility and ensure the touch technology is suitable for glove use (PCAP recommended).

Communication Loss with PLC/Server

Network interruptions or cable faults can cause the HMI to lose communication with the control system, rendering it useless. Implement redundant network connections and diagnostic alarms for communication loss.

Sunlight Readability Issues

In locations with direct sunlight (e.g., ATC towers), standard HMIs may be difficult to read. Use high‑brightness (≥1000 cd/m²) displays or anti‑glare filters.

Firmware/Application Version Mismatch

Updating the PLC firmware or ALCMS software without updating the HMI runtime can cause compatibility issues. Maintain version control and test updates in a staging environment.

Security Vulnerabilities

HMIs with default passwords or exposed network services can be exploited. Change default credentials, disable unused services, and place HMIs in a secured network segment.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Ghost Image Persists on Static Screens
Likely Cause
Burn-in or image retention from prolonged static display content.
Diagnostic Steps
Check how long the static screen has been displayed without a screensaver or rotation, and inspect the display technology type.
Corrective Action
Enable a screen saver, dimming, or periodic screen rotation; replace the panel if retention is permanent.
Touch Input Registers in the Wrong Location
Likely Cause
Touch calibration drift over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Run the built-in touch calibration utility and test touch accuracy across the full screen area.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the touchscreen, and confirm the touch technology (PCAP recommended) suits the operating environment (e.g., glove use).
HMI Unresponsive / Cannot Control the System
Likely Cause
Communication loss with the PLC/server from a network interruption or cable fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the HMI's communication status indicator, test network cabling and switch ports, and review the diagnostic alarm history.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the faulted network link and verify the redundant network path is active if fitted.
Screen Unreadable in Bright Conditions
Likely Cause
Insufficient display brightness or a lack of anti-glare treatment for direct sunlight exposure.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure ambient light levels at the installation and compare display brightness against specification (≥1000 cd/m² recommended).
Corrective Action
Upgrade to a high-brightness display or fit an anti-glare filter, and reposition or shade the HMI if practical.
HMI Fails to Load Screens After a Software Update
Likely Cause
Firmware/application version mismatch following a PLC or ALCMS software update.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the HMI runtime version against the updated PLC/ALCMS compatibility matrix and review error logs at startup.
Corrective Action
Update the HMI runtime/application to a verified-compatible version and restore from a staged, tested configuration.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Touch Calibration Drift
Low Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical ageing of the touch layer or a software calibration reset lost after a firmware update.
Effect
Touch input registers in the wrong location, causing operators to select the wrong control or screen element.
Detection Method
Scheduled touch calibration check, repeated after any firmware or application update.
Display Burn-In / Image Retention
Low Severity
Root Cause
Static screen elements left displayed for extended periods without a screensaver or periodic screen change.
Effect
A ghost image persists on static screens, reducing readability and looking unprofessional even where function is unaffected.
Detection Method
Display condition inspection looking specifically for retained imagery on a blank or differently-coloured test screen.
Communication Loss with PLC / Server
High Severity
Root Cause
Network cable damage, switch failure, or a fault in the local PLC/CCR or ALCMS server the HMI depends on.
Effect
The HMI becomes unresponsive and cannot control the system, removing the operator's local interface during the outage.
Detection Method
Communication link test between the HMI and its PLC/server, and review of the connection status log.
Firmware / Application Version Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
An HMI application or firmware update applied without corresponding changes on the PLC/server side, or vice versa.
Effect
The HMI fails to load screens after a software update, or displays data that no longer matches the underlying system state.
Detection Method
Firmware and application version audit comparing the HMI against the PLC/server it interfaces with.
Sunlight Readability / Backlight Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Backlight ageing reducing peak brightness, or a display panel not suited to the installed location's ambient light levels.
Effect
The screen becomes unreadable in bright conditions, forcing operators to shade the display or rely on a secondary station.
Detection Method
Display condition inspection under the worst-case ambient lighting the installed location actually experiences.
Security Vulnerability from Unpatched Software
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Missed security patches or default credentials left unchanged on an HMI that sits on the operational network.
Effect
An avoidable exposure to unauthorised access or malware, given the HMI's ability to issue commands to connected AGL systems.
Detection Method
Security patch review and a role-based authentication test as part of scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the HMI's power supply and verify zero energy before opening the panel enclosure or servicing internal wiring; the front touch face can remain a live hazard indicator even when the airfield circuits it controls are de-energised.

Verify Commanded State Before Acting
Operational

Confirm the actual circuit or brightness step change on the field side (or via a secondary status source) after issuing a command from the touchscreen, since a stuck or unresponsive touch input can leave the operator believing a change was made when it was not.

Coordinate Before Taking Offline
Operational

Notify other control points (central ALCMS workstation, other HMIs) before disconnecting or rebooting a unit, since it is one of potentially several local control points feeding the same system.

PPE & ESD Precautions
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for electrical work and anti-static precautions when handling internal circuit boards.

Access Control
Compliance

Restrict panel access to authorised, trained operators, since the HMI can directly switch runway and taxiway circuits on or off.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify the HMI reflects true field status and communicates correctly with the ALCMS before closing out the work and returning it to operator use.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Stuck Touch Input Can Look Like a Successful Command
Verification

A touchscreen that registers a tap and updates its own on-screen state doesn't guarantee the underlying circuit actually changed; always confirm the field-side result through an independent status source (central ALCMS, a secondary indicator) before treating a command as executed, especially for a safety-relevant action like a runway circuit change.

Touch Calibration Drift Is Gradual and Position-Specific
Diagnostics

A capacitive or resistive touch panel can drift so that taps near one edge of the screen register slightly offset from where the operator actually touched, without affecting the rest of the screen noticeably; a complaint that a specific button "sometimes doesn't respond" is worth checking as a localised calibration issue before assuming the whole unit needs replacement.

Multiple Control Points Can Silently Fall Out of Sync
Reliability

Where several HMIs or a central workstation share control of the same circuits, a communication fault on one unit can leave it displaying stale status while still accepting operator input; periodically cross-check that all control points agree on current field state, rather than assuming synchronisation is self-evidently maintained.

Firmware and Software Versions Need the Same Change Control as Hardware
Maintenance

An HMI's software is as much a part of its safety function as its wiring, but version updates are often applied more casually than a hardware change would be; treat firmware and software updates with the same documented sign-off and rollback plan used for other safety-relevant AGL work.

Access Control Failures Are Often Procedural, Not Technical
Compliance

An HMI with correctly configured access control can still be effectively open if operators share login credentials or leave a session logged in unattended; audit actual usage behaviour periodically, not just the configured permission settings, since the technical control and the operational reality can diverge over time.

Panel Heat Buildup Shortens Touchscreen Life in Enclosed Cabinets
Reliability

An HMI mounted in a poorly ventilated control cabinet alongside other heat-generating equipment can run consistently hotter than its rated operating range without any single fault triggering an alarm; where touchscreen failures at a site seem to recur faster than the manufacturer's expected life, check cabinet thermal conditions before assuming a batch of defective units.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the touchscreen hmi: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a touchscreen HMI in an AGL system?

A touchscreen HMI (human-machine interface) is an industrial-grade operator panel that gives a graphical, touch-based interface for local control and monitoring of the Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS).

Q2What can operators do with an AGL touchscreen HMI?

They can view a graphical overview of the airfield lighting with colour-coded status, switch circuits on and off, adjust brightness steps (typically 5-step control), set operating modes such as manual, automatic and standby, acknowledge and silence alarms, and review alarm history and diagnostics.

Q3Where are touchscreen HMIs installed?

In CCR control rooms, remote substations at satellite CCR locations, runway edge cabinets (compact units) and maintenance workshops, wherever local, real-time control and monitoring are needed.

Q4What display and touch specifications are typical?

Display size ranges from 7 inch to 21.5 inch TFT with LED backlight, resolution from 800 x 480 to 1920 x 1080, projected capacitive or resistive touch that is glove-compatible, and brightness of 400 to 1,000 cd/m² with sunlight-readable options.

Q5What communication protocols does an HMI support?

Typical support includes Modbus TCP and RTU, Ethernet/IP, Profinet, OPC UA, MQTT and SNMP, over 2 to 4 Ethernet ports, 2 to 4 serial ports (RS-232 or RS-485), USB and optional fieldbus, communicating with PLCs, remote I/O and the ALCMS server.

Q6How is a touchscreen HMI powered?

By 24 V DC (18-32 V) or 120/230 V AC panel mount, drawing about 10 W to 40 W depending on size and backlight. It is a standalone control-panel device powered separately from the airfield lighting circuit, and interfaces by data or relay only.

Q7Which standards apply to touchscreen HMIs?

The main references are IEC 61131-2 (programmable controller equipment), ISO 9241-110 (ergonomics of interaction), FAA AC 150/5345-56 (L-890 ALCMS, covering the HMI as the operator interface) and IEC 61000-6-2 (EMC immunity). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a touchscreen HMI be checked?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for touch calibration and communication link tests, and annual for display condition, firmware and application version audit and security patch review. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with touchscreen HMIs?

Common problems are display burn-in or image retention, touch calibration drift, communication loss with the PLC or server, sunlight readability issues, firmware and application version mismatch, and security vulnerabilities from unpatched software. An unresponsive HMI that cannot control the system often points to loss of communication.
