Runway

The fundamental paved surface on an airport used for aircraft take‑off and landing. Designed with specific dimensions, pavement strength, markings, and AGL lighting systems to support safe operations in all visibility conditions. The runway is the core element of the airfield infrastructure, integrating with approach lighting, navigation aids, and surface movement guidance systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Runway is a defined rectangular paved area on an aerodrome prepared for the landing and take‑off of aircraft. It is the most critical infrastructure element of any airport, designed to support the dynamic loads of aircraft operations while providing the necessary surface friction, drainage, and visual guidance for pilots. Runways are typically constructed of asphalt or concrete and are marked with standardised visual aids including centreline markings, threshold markings, and aiming point markings.

Key characteristics of a runway include:

  • Dimensions: Length and width vary based on the aircraft type and airport category, from short grass strips for general aviation to 4,000+ metre paved runways for large commercial aircraft.
  • Orientation: Runways are numbered according to their magnetic heading (e.g., Runway 09/27 for an east‑west runway).
  • Pavement Type: Flexible (asphalt) or rigid (concrete) pavement, each with specific load‑bearing characteristics.
  • Surface Texture: Grooved or textured to provide adequate friction for braking and directional control, especially in wet conditions.
  • Lighting Systems: Integrated AGL systems including edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, and touchdown zone lights to enable night and low‑visibility operations.
  • Visual Markings: Painted markings (threshold bars, centreline, aiming points, touchdown zone marks) that provide visual cues to pilots.
Purpose

Purpose & Function

The runway's purpose is to give an aircraft the one thing it cannot generate for itself while stationary or moving slowly: enough uninterrupted, adequately firm, and correctly aligned surface to accelerate to flying speed or decelerate safely from it. Its length, width, orientation, and pavement strength are all set by what a given aircraft type actually needs to do this within a margin of safety, not by convenience — which is why the same airport may need a longer runway for wide-body jets than for regional aircraft, and why runway orientation is chosen to align with prevailing winds rather than site layout. The surface texture and grooving exist for the same underlying reason in wet-weather form: maintaining enough friction for braking and directional control when the pavement is compromised by rain. Everything else associated with the runway — its markings, its embedded AGL lighting — exists to extend that same core purpose into conditions, night operations and low visibility, where the pilot cannot rely on the pavement's visual cues alone.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Runways are the primary operating surfaces at all types of airports:

  • Commercial Airports: Serving large passenger and cargo aircraft with long, heavily‑constructed runways capable of handling high traffic volumes and heavy aircraft loads.
  • General Aviation Airports: Smaller runways for private aircraft, typically shorter and with lighter pavement structures.
  • Military Airfields: Runways designed for tactical and transport aircraft, often with additional blast pads and strengthened pavements.
  • Heliports: Specialised landing areas for helicopters, often with smaller dimensions but still requiring proper markings and lighting.
  • Remote and Offshore Airports: Runways in challenging environments (e.g., mountainous regions, oil rigs) with unique design considerations.

The runway is used for:

  • Take‑off: Aircraft accelerate from a standing start to lift‑off speed, requiring a smooth, high‑friction surface.
  • Landing: Aircraft touch down and decelerate, requiring adequate braking friction and visibility of markings and lights.
  • Taxi and Ground Movement: Although taxiways are used for most ground movement, aircraft may use the runway for back‑tracking or when taxiing to the apron.
  • Emergency Operations: Runways may be used for emergency landings or as staging areas during incidents.
Placement

Installation & Location

Runway location, orientation, and length are set by the aeronautical site survey rather than by a fixed rule — orientation is chosen to align with the prevailing wind for maximum usable crosswind coverage, while length, width, and pavement strength are set from the design aircraft's performance requirements, elevation, and temperature. The surrounding runway strip, obstacle-limitation surfaces, and clearways/stopways are established as part of the same survey so that AGL fixtures, signs, and NAVAIDs sited relative to the runway all reference the same as-built centreline and threshold coordinates. Because every other AGL component's setout (edge lights, centreline lights, signs, ILS-related equipment) is ultimately measured from the runway's own surveyed geometry, any later resurfacing, realignment, or threshold displacement triggers a re-survey and corresponding re-setout of the dependent lighting and signage.

Function

How Does It Work?

The runway serves as the interface between the aircraft and the ground during the most critical phases of flight. Its functionality relies on several integrated systems:

  • Structural Design: The pavement is designed to distribute the load of landing aircraft over the subgrade, preventing failure. Pavement thickness and materials are chosen based on the expected aircraft weight and traffic volume (using the Aircraft Classification Number – Pavement Classification Number, ACN‑PCN system).
  • Surface Friction: Runway surfaces are textured (through grooving or tining) to provide consistent friction in wet conditions, reducing the risk of hydroplaning. Regular friction testing is performed to monitor surface condition.
  • Drainage: Runways are cambered transversely (ideally 1.5% for code letter C to F, 2% for A or B) and held to tight longitudinal slope limits so water runs off quickly rather than standing, since standing water degrades braking and raises the risk of aquaplaning.
  • Markings and Signage: Standardised runway markings (threshold markings, designators, centreline, aiming points, touchdown zones) provide visual cues for pilots during approach, landing, and roll‑out. Runway edge markings define the usable width.
  • Lighting Systems: AGL components (edge, centreline, threshold, touchdown zone, and approach lights) illuminate the runway for night and low‑visibility operations. These are controlled and monitored by the ALCMS.
  • Instrument Landing System (ILS) Integration: Runways with precision approaches are equipped with ILS localizer and glide slope antennas that provide electronic guidance to aircraft, complementing visual references.
  • Friction Measurement and Maintenance: Regular friction tests (using a friction tester vehicle) are conducted to ensure the runway meets minimum friction requirements. Surface treatments (e.g., grooving, micro‑texturing) are applied when friction drops below acceptable levels.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The runway itself hosts several independent lighting subsystems — approach, threshold, edge, centreline, and touchdown zone — and each is its own control domain within the shared ALCMS hierarchy rather than one combined circuit. A dedicated CCR feeds each subsystem's series circuit loop, so the ALCMS can switch or dim the centreline lights, for instance, without any effect on the edge lights sharing the same stretch of pavement, and a fault on one loop is detected and reported against that loop specifically. This separation is deliberate: a precision approach depends on several of these systems working together (CAT II/III operations typically require centreline and touchdown zone lighting in addition to edge and approach lighting), so keeping them electrically independent means a single circuit fault degrades one part of the visual picture rather than the whole of it.

Independent loops mean a single circuit fault degrades one part of the visual picture, never all of it at once.

System Integration

Circuit & System Overview

Each lighting subsystem is driven by its own dedicated CCR‑fed series circuit, monitored independently by the ALCMS. Together they build a continuous, layered visual picture — from initial approach guidance through to touchdown and rollout — regardless of ambient visibility.

Construction

Physical Construction

The runway pavement is built up as a layered structure engineered to spread aircraft wheel loads down to the natural subgrade: a prepared and compacted subgrade is topped with one or more granular sub-base and base course layers, followed by the wearing surface itself, either flexible asphalt concrete or rigid Portland cement concrete, with total pavement thickness sized to the design aircraft loading and traffic volume. Rigid pavement sections are cast in slabs separated by contraction, expansion, and construction joints sealed against water ingress, while flexible pavements are laid and compacted in successive lifts to achieve the required density and surface tolerance. The surface is finished with a grooved or tined texture to channel water away and maintain friction in wet conditions, and cross-falls are built into the transverse profile to drain surface water toward the shoulders. Embedded within and alongside the pavement structure are the base cans, ducts, and conduits for the edge, centreline, and other AGL lighting systems, along with the underlying drainage network, all installed before or integrated with the paving process so the finished surface remains flush and structurally continuous.

Prepare and compact the subgrade to design density before placing sub-base and base course layers, since inadequate compaction is the root cause of later settlement and pavement distress.

Pavement Layer Thickness & Density
Civil

Place and compact each sub-base, base, and wearing course layer to the design thickness and density, verified by field testing before the next layer is laid.

Longitudinal & Transverse Grade Control
Civil

Survey and confirm the finished surface holds the design longitudinal slope and transverse cross-fall within tolerance, so surface water drains correctly to the shoulders.

Base Can & Duct Pre-Installation

Joint Sealing (Rigid Pavement)

On rigid pavement sections, seal contraction, expansion, and construction joints against water ingress before the runway is opened to traffic.

Surface Texturing

Finish the surface with grooving or tining per design to channel water away and maintain the required wet-friction coefficient.

Pre-Opening Sign-off
Documentation

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Friction Testing
Periodic

Run continuous friction measurement equipment over the surface to confirm wet-friction coefficient stays above the required threshold, especially in the touchdown zone.

Pavement Condition Survey
Annual

Inspect for cracking, spalling, rutting, and joint deterioration, and calculate the Pavement Condition Index to prioritise resurfacing or repair work.

Rubber Deposit Removal
Periodic

Remove built-up tyre rubber from the touchdown zone by high-pressure water blasting or chemical treatment to restore surface texture and friction.

Marking Retro-Reflectivity Check
Annual

Measure marking retro-reflectivity and repaint threshold bars, centreline, and touchdown zone markings once they fall below the visibility standard.

Drainage & Grading Inspection
Annual

Check transverse slope, grooving, and shoulder drainage for standing water risk, particularly after heavy rain events or resurfacing work.

Foreign Object Debris (FOD) Sweep
Daily

Sweep the full runway length and shoulders for debris that could be ingested by engines or damage tyres, logging findings for trend review.

RESA & Strip Inspection
Annual

Verify the runway end safety area and strip remain graded, obstacle-free, and to width, since these are critical overrun protection zones.

Joint & Sealant Inspection
Annual

Inspect expansion joints and sealant for cracking or loss of adhesion that could allow water infiltration into the pavement structure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Pavement Cracking and Spalling

Temperature variations, heavy aircraft loads, and ageing cause asphalt or concrete pavement to develop cracks and spalling. This can lead to FOD (Foreign Object Debris) and reduced structural integrity. Regular pavement condition surveys (PCI) and timely crack sealing, patching, or overlays are essential to maintain runway serviceability.

Rubber Buildup on Surface

Aircraft tyres deposit rubber on the runway surface, particularly in the touchdown zone. This reduces friction, especially in wet conditions. Regular rubber removal (using high‑pressure water or chemical methods) is necessary to restore friction levels. Friction testing should be performed after rubber removal to confirm effectiveness.

Poor Drainage and Standing Water

Inadequate transverse or longitudinal slopes can cause water to pool on the runway, increasing the risk of hydroplaning. Regular cleaning of drainage channels, maintenance of pavement slopes, and possible regrooving are required to ensure rapid water runoff.

Lighting and Marking Degradation

AGL fixtures and painted markings can degrade over time due to weather, UV exposure, and aircraft traffic. Dimming lights or faded markings reduce pilot visibility. Regular inspections and maintenance (lamp replacement, lens cleaning, repainting) are necessary to maintain visual guidance quality.

Pavement Settlement and Shoulder Erosion

Subgrade settlement can cause uneven pavement surfaces, affecting drainage and potentially causing water ponding. Erosion of unpaved shoulders can lead to edge failure. Regular surveying and repairs to shoulders and edges are needed to maintain runway integrity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Cracking, Spalling, or FOD Risk on the Pavement Surface
Likely Cause
Temperature cycling, heavy aircraft loading, and general ageing of the asphalt or concrete pavement.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct a pavement condition survey (PCI) of the affected area; inspect crack width, depth, and spalling extent; check for loose debris posing an FOD risk.
Corrective Action
Seal cracks, patch spalled areas, or schedule an overlay based on survey severity, and remove any loose debris immediately as an FOD control measure.
Reduced Friction in Wet Conditions
Likely Cause
Rubber deposit buildup from aircraft tyres, particularly concentrated in the touchdown zone.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a friction test in the affected zone; visually assess rubber deposit coverage against the surrounding pavement.
Corrective Action
Carry out rubber removal using high-pressure water or approved chemical methods, then re-run friction testing to confirm restored levels.
Standing Water or Hydroplaning Risk After Rain
Likely Cause
Inadequate transverse/longitudinal slope, blocked drainage channels, or worn grooving no longer channelling water effectively.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect drainage channels for blockage; survey pavement slope for low spots; assess groove condition and depth against design specification.
Corrective Action
Clear blocked drainage, correct low spots where practical, and regroove the pavement if worn grooving is confirmed as the cause.
Reduced Pilot Visibility of Lighting or Markings
Likely Cause
Weathering, UV exposure, and traffic wear degrading AGL fixtures and painted markings over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Take photometric readings on suspect fixtures and visually assess marking fade; compare against the last inspection/repaint record.
Corrective Action
Replace dimmed lamps/lenses and repaint faded markings with high-visibility, reflective paint on a schedule matched to actual wear rate.
Uneven Pavement Surface or Shoulder Damage
Likely Cause
Subgrade settlement causing pavement unevenness, or erosion of unpaved shoulders affecting the pavement edge.
Diagnostic Steps
Survey the affected area for elevation changes and edge condition; inspect shoulder material for erosion, especially after heavy rainfall.
Corrective Action
Repair settled pavement sections and restore eroded shoulders promptly to prevent edge failure and drainage complications.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Structural Pavement Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Subgrade weakness or drainage failure beneath the pavement, cumulative fatigue from repeated heavy aircraft loading, or under-design of the pavement structure for the traffic it actually carries.
Effect
Deep cracking, rutting, or localised depression that compromises load-bearing capacity and generates FOD risk to aircraft.
Detection Method
Periodic Pavement Condition Index (PCI) surveys, non-destructive deflection testing, and visual inspection for progressive crack patterns.
Loss of Surface Friction
High Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposit accumulation in the touchdown zone, worn or closed grooving, or polishing of the aggregate surface from prolonged traffic.
Effect
Reduced braking action and increased hydroplaning risk in wet conditions, potentially forcing a runway friction restriction or closure.
Detection Method
Scheduled continuous friction testing (CFME), and visual assessment of rubber coverage against the surrounding pavement.
Drainage System Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Blocked or collapsed drainage channels, an as-built pavement slope out of tolerance, or sediment build-up reducing runoff capacity.
Effect
Standing water on the pavement that increases hydroplaning risk and accelerates subgrade saturation beneath the affected area.
Detection Method
Post-rainfall visual survey for ponding locations, and periodic inspection/clearing of drainage channels and inlets.
AGL Fixture / Marking Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure, weathering, and abrasion from aircraft and vehicle traffic acting on embedded lighting fixtures and painted markings over their service life.
Effect
Reduced pilot visual guidance at night or in low visibility, from dimmed lights or faded/obscured markings.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity testing of AGL fixtures, and visual marking-condition assessment against the last repaint record.
Shoulder / Edge Erosion
Low Severity
Root Cause
Water runoff concentrating along the pavement edge, insufficient shoulder stabilisation, or vegetation loss on the unpaved shoulder.
Effect
Undermining of the pavement edge that can progress to edge failure if left unaddressed, and disruption of adjacent drainage paths.
Detection Method
Visual survey of shoulder condition and edge line after heavy rainfall, and periodic elevation/edge-condition surveying.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Runway Closure & ATC Coordination
Operational

Any work on or crossing the runway surface, strip, or graded area requires a formal runway closure or protected work window coordinated with ATC, with a NOTAM issued before work begins and cancelled only once the surface is confirmed clear and compliant.

Foreign Object Debris (FOD) Control
Operational

Sweep and inspect the full work area for tools, fasteners, pavement fragments, and other debris before reopening the runway; FOD on an active runway is a direct hazard to aircraft tyres, engines, and airframes.

Vehicle & Personnel Movement
Personnel Safety

Restrict vehicle and personnel movement on or near the runway strip to what is authorised under the closure, follow the aerodrome's vehicle-on-runway procedures, and maintain positive radio contact with ATC throughout.

Surface Friction & Drainage
Compliance

Verify surface friction, rubber deposit levels, and drainage performance meet the applicable standard before returning the runway to service after resurfacing, grooving, or cleaning work.

Wildlife Hazard Management
Operational

Follow the aerodrome's wildlife hazard management plan during and after runway work, since disturbed ground, standing water, or open trenches can attract birds or animals onto the movement area.

De-Icing & Chemical Exposure
Environmental

Handle de-icing and anti-icing chemicals per the manufacturer's safety data sheet, and be aware of their corrosive effect on pavement markings, joint sealant, and any embedded AGL components during winter operations.

Restore Markings & Lighting Before Reopening
Compliance

Confirm all associated markings, edge and centreline lighting, and signage are compliant and undamaged before cancelling the NOTAM and returning the runway to full operational status.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Rubber Buildup Degrades Friction Before It's Visible
Maintenance

Rubber deposits accumulate fastest in the touchdown zone and reduce surface friction well before the buildup is obvious to the eye; schedule friction testing more frequently there than on the rest of the runway rather than applying one uniform interval.

Seal Cracks Promptly Rather Than Batching Repairs
Maintenance

A small crack sealed promptly keeps water from infiltrating and causing much larger structural damage through a freeze-thaw cycle; timeliness of crack sealing matters more here than waiting to accumulate several repairs into one visit.

Re-Verify Drainage Grade After Any Resurfacing
Verification

A small elevation error introduced during an overlay or resurfacing project can create ponding that wasn't present in the original design; re-check surface drainage patterns after resurfacing work, not just before it.

Ageing Joints and Paint Edges Are a Growing FOD Risk
Safety

Pavement joints and marking paint edges generate more foreign object debris risk as they age and begin to lift, even while the surrounding pavement looks sound; inspect joint and paint-edge condition specifically for FOD potential, not just for general cosmetic wear.

Verify Marking Retroreflectivity With a Meter, Not by Eye
Verification

A runway marking can look visually acceptable in daylight while its retroreflective performance for night operations has dropped well below standard; measure retroreflectivity directly, especially ahead of the season when low-light operations increase.

Track Recurring Soft Spots as a Subsurface Drainage Signal
Diagnostics

A failing subsurface drain or pipe often shows up first as a slow-developing soft or ponding area rather than a sudden failure; track any recurring soft spot across seasons as a possible drainage issue rather than treating each occurrence as an isolated pavement defect.
