Fiber Converter

A media conversion device that bridges electrical communication signals (copper Ethernet, serial, or fieldbus) to fiber optic transmission, enabling long-distance, noise-immune data links across the airfield. Fiber converters are the backbone of reliable AGL communication infrastructure, providing galvanic isolation, lightning protection, and high bandwidth for ALCMS data.

Overview

What Is It?

A Fiber Converter (also known as a media converter or fiber optic transceiver) is a device that converts electrical signals from copper-based communication interfaces (such as Ethernet, RS‑232, RS‑485, or fieldbus protocols) into optical signals for transmission over fiber optic cables, and vice versa. In AGL applications, fiber converters are deployed to extend communication distances far beyond the limits of copper cabling (typically 100 m for Ethernet) while providing complete galvanic isolation and immunity to electromagnetic interference (EMI) from high-voltage AGL power circuits, lightning, and nearby RF transmitters. They are essential for connecting remote CCR substations, field junction boxes, and sensor nodes back to the central ALCMS control room, enabling robust, high-speed, and secure data communication across the entire airfield.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The fiber converter exists to solve two problems copper cabling can't handle well on an airfield: distance and electrical noise. Copper Ethernet is limited to roughly 100 metres before signal quality degrades, far short of what's needed to reach CCR substations and field cabinets scattered across a large airfield, and copper runs near high-voltage AGL circuits or exposed to lightning strikes are vulnerable to induced surges that can damage sensitive control electronics.

By converting to optical signals for the long-haul run, the converter both extends communication reach well beyond copper's practical limit and provides complete galvanic isolation between the remote field equipment and the central control room, protecting both ends of the link from electrical faults or transients that might otherwise propagate through a continuous copper connection.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Fiber converters are deployed throughout the airfield wherever long-distance, noise-immune communication is required. Key applications include:

  • ALCMS Network Backbone: Connecting the central ALCMS server to remote substations and field controllers over fiber optic rings or point-to-point links.
  • CCR Substation Connectivity: Providing Ethernet or fieldbus communication between the main PLC cabinet and remote CCR units located at runway ends or substations.
  • Remote I/O Networks: Extending fieldbus or Ethernet communication to distributed Remote I/O modules located in runway edge cabinets, junction boxes, and approach light shelters.
  • RWSL (Runway Status Light) Systems: Linking RWSL sensors, stop bar controllers, and processing units to the central control system.
  • Approach Lighting Control: Communicating with remote approach light control cabinets and sequencer modules.
  • Airfield Sensor Networks: Connecting weather sensors, wind measurement systems, visibility sensors, and RVR systems to the ALCMS.
  • Surveillance & Radar Systems: Transmitting radar and surveillance data from remote sensor locations to ATC and control centers.
  • Inter-building Links: Connecting terminal buildings, ATC towers, maintenance facilities, and fire stations to the airfield communication network.
Placement

Installation & Location

Converters are DIN-rail mounted inside the field cabinet or shelter at each end of the fibre run, paired with a matching unit at the ALCMS server room or the remote substation it connects to. Fibre entry to the cabinet is via a dedicated gland or patch panel kept separate from the copper power and signal glands, with enough slack coiled inside the cabinet to allow re-termination without pulling more cable from the duct run; outdoor cabinet installations use a sealed, temperature-rated converter rather than an indoor-only unit. The copper (Ethernet or fieldbus) side connects to the local PLC, Remote I/O, or switch on a short patch cable, keeping the electrically-isolated fibre run as the only long-distance link between the two ends.

Function

How Does It Work?

The fiber converter operates by translating electrical signals into optical pulses and vice versa. Key functional aspects include:

  • Signal Conversion: On the electrical side, the converter interfaces with copper media (e.g., RJ‑45 Ethernet, RS‑485 terminals) and extracts the digital data stream. On the optical side, it drives a laser or LED (light-emitting diode) to transmit corresponding light pulses over single-mode or multi-mode fiber.
  • Wavelength Management: Fiber converters operate at specific wavelengths—typically 850 nm (multi-mode), 1310 nm, or 1550 nm (single-mode)—depending on the fiber type and distance. Some models support wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) to send and receive data on a single fiber.
  • Media Independence: Converters are protocol-agnostic at the physical layer, supporting a wide range of protocols including Ethernet (10/100/1000BASE-T), Profibus, Modbus, RS‑232, RS‑485, and others, making them versatile for various AGL applications.
  • Galvanic Isolation: The optical link provides complete electrical isolation between the two ends, protecting sensitive control equipment from ground loops, voltage surges, and lightning-induced transients that are common on airfield copper cabling.
  • Distance Extension: Fiber converters enable communication over distances up to 2 km (multi-mode) or 80+ km (single-mode), far exceeding the 100 m limit of copper Ethernet.
  • Diagnostics & Monitoring: Advanced converters provide link status LEDs, optical power monitoring, and link-loss alarm contacts, enabling proactive maintenance and rapid fault isolation.

Fiber converters are typically powered by 24 VDC (airfield standard) or 12 VDC, and are housed in rugged industrial enclosures suitable for outdoor or substation environments.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The fiber converter sits at the transition point between a short copper Ethernet run at a piece of equipment (a CCR cabinet, remote substation, or field enclosure) and the fibre backbone that carries the signal the longer distance across the airfield. It is protocol-transparent — whatever frames arrive on the copper side are re-encoded as optical pulses and sent unchanged, so from the ALCMS server's perspective the converter is invisible and the PLC or switch on the far end appears to be on the same local segment. A matching converter at the far end restores the signal to copper before it reaches the destination switch or device. Because the airfield's fibre backbone is what actually links physically separated equipment rooms, substations, and the central ALCMS, a converter or fibre fault at any one point breaks connectivity for everything downstream of it on that run, independent of whether the copper-side equipment itself is healthy.

Construction

Physical Construction

The converter is built into a compact extruded-aluminium or plastic DIN-rail housing sized to fit alongside other control-cabinet devices, with the electrical interface (RJ-45, terminal block, or fieldbus connector) on one face and the fibre-optic connectors (SC, ST, or LC) on the opposite face to keep copper and optical cabling physically separated. The optical transceiver is a sealed SFP or fixed-pigtail module mounted directly to the internal circuit board, isolating the copper-side electronics from the fibre link and providing the galvanic isolation the device is chosen for. Internal electronics are conformally coated against dust and humidity, and where the unit must be field-mounted outside a cabinet, it is housed in a sealed IP65 enclosure with cable glands for the copper conductor entry and a sealed fibre feedthrough. Power terminates at a screw-clamp terminal block rated for the 24 VDC (or 12 VDC) nominal supply, with a chassis earth stud provided for cabinet grounding.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Fiber Optic Cable Damage

Fiber cables are susceptible to damage from digging, construction, or rodent bites. The converter will show a loss of link (LOS) indication. Regular cable inspection and use of armored or duct-protected fiber is recommended.

Optical Connector Contamination

Dust, dirt, or oil on SC/ST/LC connectors can cause significant signal attenuation (loss of optical power). Clean connectors with a proper fiber cleaning kit (e.g., pen cleaner or wipes) before reconnecting.

Incompatible Fiber Types

Using single-mode fiber with a multi-mode transceiver (or vice versa) will result in very high attenuation and link failure. Always match the converter's optical interface to the installed fiber type.

Power Supply Instability

Fiber converters are sensitive to voltage fluctuations. Use a dedicated 24 VDC power supply with sufficient capacity and backup (UPS) to prevent converter resets or communication dropouts.

Auto-Negotiation Mismatch

Ethernet converters with mismatched speed/duplex settings (auto-negotiation disabled) can cause excessive collisions or link instability. Ensure both ends are configured consistently.

Temperature Extremes

Outdoor enclosures without proper ventilation can exceed the converter's operating temperature range, causing intermittent faults or permanent damage. Use industrial-rated converters and ensure proper enclosure cooling.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Loss of Link (LOS) Indication
Likely Cause
A break in the fiber optic cable itself, typically from digging, construction activity, or rodent damage along the buried or duct-run path.
Diagnostic Steps
Use an OTDR or visual fault locator to trace the break location along the fiber run; check recent excavation or construction activity along the route as a likely cause.
Corrective Action
Splice or replace the damaged fiber section; consider upgrading to armored or duct-protected fiber along vulnerable sections to prevent recurrence.
Weak or Degraded Signal Without Full Link Loss
Likely Cause
Dust, dirt, or oil contamination on the SC/ST/LC optical connectors causing signal attenuation without a full break.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure received optical power against the expected budget for the link; inspect connector end-faces under a fiber microscope if available.
Corrective Action
Clean connectors with a proper fiber cleaning kit (pen cleaner or wipes) before reconnecting; re-measure optical power to confirm the link is back within budget.
Link Failure Immediately After a Converter or Transceiver Swap
Likely Cause
The replacement transceiver or converter doesn't match the installed fiber type — single-mode fiber paired with a multi-mode transceiver (or vice versa) causes very high attenuation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the fiber type marking on the cable jacket against the transceiver/converter's rated optical interface.
Corrective Action
Replace with the correctly matched transceiver/converter for the installed fiber type; re-verify link establishment and optical power afterward.
Converter Resets or Communication Drops Repeatedly
Likely Cause
Power supply instability, since fiber converters are sensitive to voltage fluctuations and may not be running from a dedicated, adequately sized supply with UPS backup.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the power supply type and rating feeding the converter; correlate reset events with known power disturbances or UPS switchover events.
Corrective Action
Install or repair a dedicated 24 VDC supply with sufficient capacity and UPS backup if not already correctly fitted.
Excessive Collisions or Unstable Ethernet Link
Likely Cause
Mismatched speed/duplex settings between the two ends of the Ethernet link, typically from auto-negotiation being disabled on one side only.
Diagnostic Steps
Check speed/duplex configuration on both ends of the link; confirm whether auto-negotiation is enabled and configured consistently on each end.
Corrective Action
Set both ends to matching speed/duplex settings, either both auto-negotiating or both fixed to the same values; re-verify link stability after the change.
Intermittent Faults Correlating with Outdoor Temperature Extremes
Likely Cause
The converter's operating temperature range is being exceeded due to an outdoor enclosure without adequate ventilation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check enclosure temperature during peak heat or cold against the converter's rated range; inspect enclosure ventilation and cooling provisions.
Corrective Action
Improve enclosure ventilation/cooling, or replace with an industrial-rated converter suited to the site's temperature extremes.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Fiber Port Laser Safety
Personnel Safety

Never look directly into an active fiber port or the end of a connected fiber patch lead; although typically low-power (Class 1) and normally eye-safe, use an optical power meter rather than direct viewing to check for a live signal, and cap unused ports to keep connector ends clean.

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise the converter's 24VDC (or 12VDC) supply and verify zero energy before opening the housing or disconnecting power wiring.

ESD-Sensitive Electronics
Operational

Use anti-static precautions when handling the converter's internal circuit board or SFP transceiver module.

Coordinate Before Taking Down a Long-Haul Link
Operational

The converter often extends a comms link beyond copper Ethernet's distance limit to a remote field cabinet; confirm what monitoring or control capability is lost at the far end before isolating it, and coordinate with control-room operators accordingly.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm link status and signal quality are correct at both ends of the fiber run before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Inspect the Connector Every Time It's Disconnected
Maintenance

A single fingerprint or dust particle on an SC/LC ferrule can introduce enough attenuation to push a marginal link over the loss budget, and this damage is invisible without a fibre inspection scope. Make connector inspection and cleaning a mandatory step every time a fibre connector is mated or re-mated, not an occasional troubleshooting measure.

Loss Budget Margin Shrinks Over the Life of the Link
Design

A fibre run that passes commissioning with a small positive margin can drift into failure years later as connectors degrade slightly with each re-mate and splices age. Design in meaningful loss budget margin above the minimum requirement at commissioning, since a marginal link has no room left for the gradual degradation every fibre path experiences over its service life.

Single-Mode and Multi-Mode Are Not Interchangeable, Even Briefly
Compatibility

Connecting a single-mode transceiver to multi-mode fibre (or vice versa) even temporarily, for example during a quick field test, can produce a link that appears to work at short distances but fails or degrades at the actual installed run length. Always verify fibre type against the transceiver's rated interface before connecting, especially when borrowing test equipment or spares from a different part of the system.

The Isolation Benefit Only Holds if Nothing Bridges It
Electrical Safety

A fibre converter's galvanic isolation between two ends of a run can be silently defeated if a shared earth, a grounded cable shield, or a secondary metallic path (like a common conduit bonded at both ends) connects the two sides anyway. Verify the actual installation doesn't reintroduce a conductive path the fibre link was specifically chosen to eliminate.

Auto-Negotiation Mismatches Produce Confusing, Asymmetric Symptoms
Troubleshooting

When one end of an Ethernet-over-fibre link has auto-negotiation enabled and the other is fixed to a specific speed/duplex, the resulting collisions or errors often show up as one-directional packet loss, which technicians can misread as a cabling fault. Check and match negotiation settings on both ends as an early troubleshooting step whenever traffic behaves asymmetrically.

Wire the Link-Loss Alarm Contact — Don't Leave It Unused
Monitoring

Many fiber converters include a dry-contact alarm output for link loss, but it's frequently left unconnected because it wasn't part of the original scope. Wiring this contact back to the monitoring system gives an immediate, independent indication of fibre failure rather than waiting for the affected field device to time out and raise its own alarm.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about fibre converters in AGL communication networks: distance, isolation, specifications, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a fibre converter?

A fibre converter, also called a media converter or fibre optic transceiver, converts electrical signals from copper interfaces (Ethernet, RS-232, RS-485 or fieldbus) into optical signals for transmission over fibre, and back again.

Q2Why are fibre converters used on airfields?

They solve two problems copper handles poorly: distance and electrical noise. Copper Ethernet is limited to roughly 100 m, far short of what is needed to reach CCR substations and remote equipment, and fibre is immune to electrical interference.

Q3Where are fibre converters used?

Wherever long-distance, noise-immune communication is needed, such as the ALCMS network backbone connecting the central server to remote substations and field controllers.

Q4How does a fibre converter work?

It translates electrical signals into optical pulses and back. The copper side connects through RJ-45 or RS-485 terminals, and the optical side uses a fibre transceiver.

Q5What distances and fibre types are supported?

Up to about 2 km on multi-mode fibre and 20-80 km on single-mode depending on the transceiver. Typical wavelengths are 850 nm (multi-mode) and 1310/1550 nm (single-mode), or WDM over a single fibre, with SC, ST or LC connectors.

Q6Does a fibre converter provide electrical isolation?

Yes. The fibre side is inherently electrically isolated from the copper side, which helps protect equipment from ground potential differences and surges.

Q7What power does a fibre converter need?

Typically 24 VDC (18-32 VDC) or 12 VDC, drawing 2-10 W, with the enclosure bonded to local earth and alarm contacts for power loss or link failure.

Q8Which standards apply?

IEEE 802.3 (Ethernet), ITU-T G.652/G.655 (single-mode fibre), IEC 60825 (laser product safety for transceiver modules) and IEC 61000-6-2 / -6-4 (industrial EMC immunity and emissions).

Q9How often should fibre converters be maintained?

Semi-annually for optical connector cleaning and thermal performance checks, and annually for fibre cable and connector inspection, link configuration (fibre type, wavelength, auto-negotiation) and power supply checks.

Q10What causes a loss-of-link indication?

Most often a break in the fibre itself from digging, construction or rodent damage along the buried or duct path. Use an OTDR or visual fault locator to trace the break.
