Introduction

AGL Cable Identification Tags

What It Is

Clear cable identification improves maintenance, troubleshooting and installation control within complex airfield lighting systems. Tags suit new installations and existing systems during maintenance, modification and rehabilitation.

Identification formats can be coordinated with cable schedules, circuit drawings, equipment references and airport maintenance requirements.

Key Features

  • Clear identification of AGL cables and circuits
  • Suitable for primary and secondary cable systems
  • Identifies individual cables and connection points
  • Coordinated with project cable schedules
  • Suitable for field installation and maintenance
  • Durable for outdoor airfield environments
  • Attachment methods selected for cable and installation conditions
  • Useful for new installations and existing infrastructure
  • Improves cable tracing and troubleshooting

Primary Cable Identification

Circuits Over Significant Distances

Role

Primary circuits form an important part of the series circuit and may run long distances between equipment. Install tags at suitable points, particularly at cable ends, connection points, junctions and equipment interfaces, so personnel identify the correct cable before testing, disconnection or repair.

Multiple Circuits

Where several primary circuits share an area, consistent identification is especially important to prevent incorrect circuit selection during maintenance.

Safety: tags support identification only and do not replace isolation procedures. De-energise the circuit at its CCR and prove it safe before any cable is cut, opened or disconnected. Never open an energised series circuit.

Secondary Lead Identification

Transformers to Fixtures and Equipment

Role

Tags can identify the corresponding circuit, equipment position or cable reference, coordinated with fixture numbering and airfield drawings, linking the physical installation to maintenance documentation.

Principle

Identification should stay legible after installation and not interfere with termination, connector installation or servicing.

Junction & Connection Points

Where Several Cables or Circuits Meet

Tags May Be Applied At

  • Junction boxes, junction pits, handholes
  • Cable joints, primary and secondary connections
  • Equipment bases, panel interfaces

Benefit

Helps personnel determine cable origin, destination, circuit reference and connection function without relying solely on drawings.

Cable Marking Format

Consistent Throughout the System

Tags May Include

  • Cable identification number, circuit number or designation
  • Equipment reference, runway or taxiway reference, location reference
  • Cable origin and destination
  • Circuit or constant current regulator (CCR) reference where applicable
  • Project-specific maintenance reference

Principle

Establish the format in project documentation. Give enough information for practical field identification without making the tag large or hard to read.

Material & Environmental Performance

Outdoor Electrical Infrastructure

Exposure May Include

  • Moisture, rain and standing water, UV radiation
  • Temperature variation, dust and dirt
  • Routine cleaning, mechanical handling, maintenance activities

Principle

The marking method should remain legible for the expected service period under the installation location's conditions.

Attachment System

Secure, Without Damaging the Cable

Methods

Cable ties, fixing wire, clips or other specified fastening systems, depending on the application. In wet pits and handholes, use UV-stabilised ties or stainless-steel fixings, and avoid dissimilar metals that corrode together.

Principle

Avoid excessive pressure on the cable sheath or interference with movement, termination or inspection. At equipment and connection points, keep tags accessible without obstructing covers, connectors or serviceable components.

Cable Joints & Connections

Particular Attention Needed

Role

Joints and connection points may contain multiple cables with different circuit references. Tags establish a clear reference between the physical connection and the cable schedule or drawing.

Rehabilitation

Tags help personnel identify existing circuits before testing, isolation or replacement.

Standards & Project Requirements

Follow the Airport's Identification System

Coordinate Format, Numbering, Material, Marking and Locations With

  • Cable schedules, circuit diagrams, as-built drawings
  • Equipment identification systems
  • Maintenance documentation, project specifications

Precedence

Where an airport or authority has a defined cable identification convention, that convention takes precedence over a generic marking format.

Reference documents may include ICAO Doc 9157 Part 5 (Electrical Systems), FAA AC 150/5370-10 (Items L-108 and L-110), FAA AC 150/5340-26C (Maintenance of Airport Visual Aid Facilities) and FAA AC 150/5340-30J (Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids). Confirm the applicable clause and edition with your project specification.

FAQ

AGL Cable Identification Tags — Frequently Asked Questions

What are AGL cable identification tags?

They are tags that give clear identification of AGL cables and circuits, which improves maintenance, troubleshooting and installation control within complex airfield lighting systems. They suit primary and secondary cable systems, individual cables and connection points, and can be used in new installations and in existing systems during maintenance, modification and rehabilitation.

Where are cable identification tags applied?

Tags are applied to primary airfield lighting cables, secondary leads, cable ends and connection points, junctions and cable joints, junction boxes, pits and handholes, field equipment connections, electrical panels, cable routes needing local identification, and existing circuits during maintenance and rehabilitation.

What information can a tag carry?

A tag may include the cable identification number, circuit number or designation, equipment reference, runway or taxiway reference, location reference, cable origin and destination, the circuit or constant current regulator (CCR) reference where applicable, and a project-specific maintenance reference. The format should be established in project documentation and give enough information for practical field identification without making the tag large or hard to read.

What materials and marking methods are used?

The tag material is a durable identification material selected for the installation environment, for example UV-stabilised polymer, stainless steel or anodised aluminium. Marking may be printed, engraved, embossed or another specified method. The tag must suit outdoor airfield conditions such as moisture, rain, standing water, UV, temperature variation, dust, cleaning and handling, and remain legible for the expected service period.

How are tags attached to cables?

Attachment may use cable ties, fixing wire, clips or other specified fastening systems. In wet pits and handholes, use UV-stabilised ties or stainless-steel fixings, and avoid dissimilar metals that corrode together. Avoid excessive pressure on the cable sheath or interference with movement, termination or inspection, and keep tags accessible without obstructing covers, connectors or serviceable components.

Do identification tags replace isolation procedures?

No. Tags support identification only and do not replace isolation procedures. De-energise the circuit at its CCR and prove it safe before any cable is cut, opened or disconnected, and never open an energised series circuit.

Why is identifying primary cables important?

Primary circuits form an important part of the series circuit and may run long distances between equipment. Tags should be installed at suitable points, particularly cable ends, connection points, junctions and equipment interfaces, so personnel identify the correct cable before testing, disconnection or repair. Where several primary circuits share an area, consistent identification is especially important to prevent incorrect circuit selection during maintenance.

How are secondary leads and joints identified?

Tags on secondary leads can identify the corresponding circuit, equipment position or cable reference, coordinated with fixture numbering and airfield drawings, and should stay legible without interfering with termination, connector installation or servicing. Joints and connection points may contain several cables with different circuit references, and tags establish a clear reference between the physical connection and the cable schedule or drawing.

How do tags work with marker posts and other AGL cable products?

Marker posts identify underground routes and infrastructure locations, while tags provide more detailed identification at the cable and connection level. Tags are used alongside primary cable, secondary lead, primary and secondary connector kits, joint and splice kits, cable duct, junction pits and handholes.

How should tags be maintained and updated?

During routine maintenance check legibility, the correct cable reference, secure attachment, physical damage, fading or deterioration, and consistency with current drawings and cable schedules. Replace damaged, missing or illegible tags. After cable replacement, circuit modification, joint replacement, equipment relocation, junction pit modification, cable route changes or circuit reconfiguration, review the identification so field markings match the updated circuit documentation.

What standards and conventions apply to cable identification?

Coordinate the format, numbering, material, marking and locations with cable schedules, circuit diagrams, as-built drawings, equipment identification systems, maintenance documentation and project specifications. Where an airport or authority has a defined cable identification convention, it takes precedence over a generic marking format. References may include ICAO Doc 9157 Part 5, FAA AC 150/5370-10 (Items L-108 and L-110), FAA AC 150/5340-26C and FAA AC 150/5340-30J, with the clause and edition confirmed against the project specification.
