Reference

How the FAA Values Are Defined

Main beam and 10 percent beam

The average intensity inside the main beam must meet the tabulated value, and no point inside it may fall below half of that value. A larger concentric beam, where one is given, must reach at least 10 percent of the value at every point on its edge. For in-pavement fixtures, any part of the 10 percent beam that lies below grade is disregarded.

Angles are taken from the photometric axes of a correctly installed fixture. The horizontal axis is parallel to the runway centreline, the vertical axis is perpendicular to the ground, and horizontal angles toward the runway centreline are positive. The test allows the beam to be shifted by up to 1° in each direction (in-pavement: 0.5° vertical and 1° horizontal) to fit the curve.

Upper limit

The measured average may not exceed three times the specified average. For L-850E, where a minimum rather than an average applies, the measured minimum may not exceed three times the specified minimum. Bidirectional split-colour fixtures with a single light source are exempt from this limit. When averaging along a test axis, the largest reading used may not exceed three times the smallest.

ICAO Annex 14 cross-reference

LightAnnex 14, Vol I, Appendix 2
Runway edge lightsA2-9 (45 m wide runway), A2-10 (60 m wide runway)
Runway centre line lights, whiteA2-6 (30 m spacing), A2-7 (15 m spacing)

ICAO gives intensities as isocandela diagrams for each light type, category and spacing. Those values are not reproduced on this page, so take them from your copy of Annex 14. The FAA and ICAO methods differ (ellipse averaging against minimum intensities at defined points), so the two sets of numbers are not interchangeable and a fixture must be checked against the standard its project specifies.

What this page does not cover

It does not include taxiway or heliport fixtures, approach lights, or PAPI. It shows new-fixture design values, not in-service maintenance limits, and it does not give chromaticity limits (SAE AS25050, and the ITE traffic-signal standard for L-850T, L-852G, L-852S, L-862S and L-804). Light output at lower CCR brightness steps is lower and depends on the lamp or LED driver; see FAA Engineering Brief 67 for LED fixtures.

FAQ

Runway Light Photometry Reference: Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers on how the FAA photometric values are defined and how to check a measured intensity.

What does the Runway Light Photometry Reference cover?

It is a quick reference for the FAA photometric values of in-pavement, directional elevated and omnidirectional elevated runway light fixtures. You can filter the fixture tables by use, mounting and colour, and check a measured intensity against the specified value. It is based on FAA AC 150/5345-46F (30 Sep 2024). It is a reference summary, not a substitute for the standard.

How do I check a measured intensity against the specified value?

Pick the fixture type and colour from the same list as the Fixture tables tab and enter the measured average intensity in candela, averaged over the main beam in the colour being checked. The page shows the reading as a percentage of the specified value. The specified values are new-fixture design values at full rated output.

What are the main beam and the 10 percent beam?

The average intensity inside the main beam must meet the tabulated value, and no point inside it may fall below half of that value. A larger concentric beam, where one is given, must reach at least 10 percent of the value at every point on its edge. For in-pavement fixtures, any part of the 10 percent beam that lies below grade is disregarded.

How are the photometric angles defined?

Angles are taken from the photometric axes of a correctly installed fixture. The horizontal axis is parallel to the runway centreline, the vertical axis is perpendicular to the ground, and horizontal angles toward the runway centreline are positive. The test allows the beam to be shifted by up to 1° in each direction to fit the curve, or 0.5° vertical and 1° horizontal for in-pavement fixtures.

Is there an upper limit on measured intensity?

Yes. The measured average may not exceed three times the specified average. For L-850E, where a minimum rather than an average applies, the measured minimum may not exceed three times the specified minimum. Bidirectional split-colour fixtures with a single light source are exempt from this limit. When averaging along a test axis, the largest reading used may not exceed three times the smallest.

Are the FAA values the same as the ICAO Annex 14 values?

No. ICAO gives intensities as isocandela diagrams for each light type, category and spacing, and those values are not reproduced on this page. The page cross-references Annex 14, Volume I, Appendix 2, for example A2-9 and A2-10 for runway edge lights and A2-6 and A2-7 for white centreline lights. The FAA and ICAO methods differ (ellipse averaging against minimum intensities at defined points), so the numbers are not interchangeable and a fixture must be checked against the standard its project specifies.

Why do L-861 red and green fixtures cover 180° horizontally?

The omnidirectional elevated fixtures table gives vertical angles. L-861 red and green cover 180° horizontally because they are used only in bidirectional fixtures.

What does this page not cover?

It does not include taxiway or heliport fixtures, approach lights or PAPI. It shows new-fixture design values, not in-service maintenance limits, and it does not give chromaticity limits (SAE AS25050, and the ITE traffic-signal standard for L-850T, L-852G, L-852S, L-862S and L-804). Light output at lower CCR brightness steps is lower and depends on the lamp or LED driver, and FAA Engineering Brief 67 covers LED fixtures.
