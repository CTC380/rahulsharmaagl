Series Circuit Earth‑Fault Detector

A critical protection device that continuously monitors the AGL series circuit for insulation degradation and earth‑fault conditions. Detects current leakage to earth, providing early warning of cable damage, moisture ingress, or fixture faults before they escalate into hazardous failures.

Overview

What Is It?

A Series Circuit Earth‑Fault Detector (also known as a ground‑fault detector or leakage monitor) is a specialised protection device that monitors the insulation integrity of an AGL series lighting circuit. It continuously measures the current flowing from the circuit conductors to earth (ground) and provides an alarm or trips the system when a fault current exceeds a preset threshold.

In AGL series circuits, the isolating transformers are designed with primary and secondary windings that are electrically isolated from each other and from earth. However, cable insulation can degrade over time due to ageing, mechanical damage, moisture ingress, or chemical attack. When insulation fails, a leakage path to earth is created, which can cause nuisance tripping, create a shock hazard, or lead to a complete circuit failure. The earth‑fault detector provides an early warning of such degradation, allowing maintenance to be scheduled before a full failure occurs.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The earth-fault detector exists to catch a specific and dangerous failure mode that the isolating transformers themselves are designed to prevent from spreading, but can't prevent from starting: insulation on a cable or fixture slowly degrading from age, moisture, or mechanical damage until current begins leaking to ground. Left undetected, that leakage path can create a shock hazard for anyone working near the circuit and can eventually progress to a full insulation failure that takes the fixture, or worse, part of the loop, out of service.

By continuously monitoring for this leakage and alarming well before it becomes a complete failure, the detector turns an otherwise invisible, slow-developing hazard into an actionable maintenance item — giving technicians the chance to locate and repair degrading insulation on their own schedule, rather than discovering the problem only when it causes an unplanned outage or, worse, an actual shock incident.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Series Circuit Earth‑Fault Detectors are installed in airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters, and AGL power centres. They are used in all types of AGL series lighting installations where insulation monitoring is required, including:

  • Runway Lighting Systems: Monitoring edge, centreline, threshold, and touchdown zone circuits for insulation degradation.
  • Taxiway Lighting Systems: Protecting taxiway edge, centreline, and stop bar circuits from earth faults.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Ensuring the integrity of approach lighting circuits (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSR, etc.).
  • Precision Approach Runways (CAT I, II, III): Where high reliability is mandated, and early fault detection is essential to prevent circuit failures during low‑visibility operations.
  • Integration with ALCMS: Providing real‑time leakage current data and alarms to the central control system for remote monitoring and maintenance planning.
  • Preventive Maintenance Programs: Enabling condition‑based maintenance by tracking insulation resistance trends over time.
Placement

Installation & Location

The detector is installed in the equipment shelter alongside the CCR and series circuit it protects, wired to sense leakage current across the full loop rather than at any single point, since an earth fault can develop anywhere along the buried cable run or at any fixture connected to it. Sensing connections are typically made at the CCR output or via a dedicated current transformer around the full set of circuit conductors, positioned so both the outgoing and return paths are measured together and any imbalance between them is detected as leakage to earth. Alarm outputs are wired back to the ALCMS alongside other AGL monitoring cabling, clearly labelled to the specific circuit being protected in equipment rooms serving multiple regulators. Because the detector's usefulness depends on catching insulation degradation early, it's commissioned with a baseline leakage reading for the healthy circuit, giving a reference against which a developing fault can be measured as it progresses.

Function

How Does It Work?

The earth‑fault detector operates by injecting a small, safe test signal (typically a low‑frequency AC voltage or DC pulse) between the series circuit and earth, or by using a current transformer to monitor the imbalance between the circuit conductors. The most common methods include:

  • Residual Current Monitoring: A differential current transformer (CT) is placed around both conductors of the series circuit. Under normal conditions, the algebraic sum of currents in the two conductors is zero. When a fault to earth occurs, a portion of the current returns via earth, causing an imbalance that is detected by the CT.
  • Insulation Monitoring (Active Method): A test signal is injected between the circuit and earth, and the resulting leakage current is measured. This method can detect insulation degradation even when the circuit is de‑energised, and it provides a quantitative measurement of insulation resistance (typically in megohms).
  • Voltage Monitoring: Measures the voltage between the circuit conductors and earth. A significant shift indicates a change in the balance of the circuit and can be used to infer the presence of an earth fault.

When a fault is detected, the detector can either raise an alarm (via dry contacts, LEDs, or communication) or, in some systems, trip the CCR to de‑energise the circuit. The threshold for alarm or trip is adjustable, typically ranging from a few milliamperes to several hundred milliamperes, depending on the sensitivity required.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The detector sits inline with the CCR's own fault monitoring rather than as a separate control layer — it adds a fault class (earth leakage) that the CCR's current/voltage regulation loop cannot see on its own, since a balanced leakage path doesn't necessarily register as an open or short circuit. Where wired to trip the CCR directly, the detector's alarm threshold effectively becomes another input into the loop's overall protection logic, sitting alongside the CCR's regulation function rather than overriding it. Where wired only to raise an alarm via dry contact or comms to ALCMS, the circuit stays energised and the decision to de-energise is left to an operator or a higher-level automated response, rather than being made locally by the detector.

Diagram

Detecting What Shouldn't Be There

The detector's differential CT reads both conductors of the series circuit at once and simply adds their currents: in a healthy loop, everything that goes out comes back and the sum is zero. The moment degraded insulation gives current a path to earth — even a fraction of an amp — the two readings stop cancelling out, and that residual imbalance, not the fault current's absolute size, is what trips the alarm. It's why the detector can catch failures developing gradually, well before enough current leaks to blow a fuse or trip on overcurrent alone.

Construction

Physical Construction

The detector is built around a sensitive current transformer, typically a zero-sequence or core-balance CT, clamped around all the series-circuit conductors together so it responds only to the small imbalance current that flows to earth through an insulation fault rather than to normal load current. This sensor feeds a signal-conditioning and threshold-comparison circuit housed in a panel- or rack-mount case, isolated from the series circuit's operating voltage by the CT itself and by internal isolation barriers on the sensing input. An adjustable threshold and time-delay setting, set via front-panel controls or dip switches, is provided so nuisance trips from normal leakage (such as damp cable insulation within acceptable limits) can be distinguished from a genuine developing fault. Output is via an alarm relay and, on networked units, a communications port, with rear terminals bringing in the CT leads, auxiliary power, and alarm/communications wiring into clearly separated terminal groups.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Nuisance Tripping

Moisture ingress into cable joints or transformers can cause intermittent earth faults, leading to nuisance alarms or tripping. Proper installation of waterproof joints and regular insulation testing can help identify and address these issues before they become problematic.

CT Saturation

In differential CT‑based detectors, the CT can saturate under high fault currents or during lightning transients, causing the detector to miss a genuine fault. Use of properly sized CTs with high saturation margins and transient protection is recommended.

Insulation Resistance Drift with Temperature

The insulation resistance of cables and transformers decreases with temperature. Seasonal temperature variations can cause the leakage current to fluctuate, potentially triggering alarms. Temperature‑compensated detectors or careful setting of thresholds can mitigate this issue.

Communication Loss with ALCMS

The detector's serial or Ethernet communication can fail, preventing the ALCMS from receiving leakage current data and alarms. Regular testing and redundant communication paths are recommended.

Ageing of Detector Electronics

Like any electronic device, earth‑fault detectors can drift in sensitivity over time. Periodic calibration or replacement (typically every 5–10 years) is recommended to maintain accuracy and reliability.

Troubleshooting Guide

Nuisance Alarms or Intermittent Trips
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress into a cable joint or transformer is causing an intermittent earth fault below the level of a hard failure.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform insulation resistance testing across circuit segments to localise the affected joint or transformer; check joint waterproofing.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the affected joint or transformer seal, and re-test insulation resistance before clearing the alarm.
Detector Misses a Genuine Fault
Likely Cause
The differential CT has saturated during a high fault current or lightning transient, preventing correct fault detection.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CT's saturation margin and transient protection against the site's expected fault and lightning transient levels.
Corrective Action
Replace with a properly sized CT with adequate saturation margin and transient protection.
Alarms Correlating with Seasonal Temperature Changes
Likely Cause
Insulation resistance naturally decreases with temperature, and seasonal swings are pushing leakage current close to the alarm threshold.
Diagnostic Steps
Review alarm history against seasonal temperature trends; check whether thresholds account for expected seasonal variation.
Corrective Action
Adjust alarm thresholds appropriately or fit a temperature-compensated detector if nuisance alarms persist seasonally.
Loss of Remote Alarm Reporting at the ALCMS
Likely Cause
A communication fault has broken the detector's serial or Ethernet link to the ALCMS.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect communication cabling and connectors; test the link status between the detector and the ALCMS.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the damaged cable/connector, and consider a redundant communication path if the link is critical.
Detector Sensitivity No Longer Matches Commissioning Values
Likely Cause
The detector's electronics have drifted in sensitivity due to normal component ageing.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare current sensitivity/threshold behaviour against commissioning records or a test fault injection if available.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the detector, or replace it if it has reached the manufacturer's recommended 5-10 year service interval.

High Severity
Root Cause
The detector's trip threshold shifting away from its commissioned value due to component ageing or an uncorrected configuration change.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Never Open-Circuit a Live Differential CT
Electrical Safety

Do not disconnect the detector's differential current transformer wiring while the primary series circuit is energised; an open CT secondary under load can develop a dangerously high voltage across the terminals.

Isolate Before Internal Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the series circuit before working inside the detector's enclosure, and verify zero energy before touching internal terminals.

Investigate Before Silencing an Alarm
Operational

Treat every earth-fault alarm as a real fault until checked; do not disable or ignore the alarm to keep a circuit in service, since the detector exists specifically to catch insulation breakdown before it becomes a shock or fire hazard.

Distinguish Nuisance Trips from Real Faults
Verification

Cross-check a suspected nuisance trip against seasonal weather or known inrush/fault-current events before concluding it isn't real, since CT saturation and seasonal insulation-resistance drift are both known causes of alarms that look like — but aren't — an actual earth fault.

Verify Differential CT Sizing
Verification

Confirm the differential CT is correctly sized for the circuit's actual inrush and fault current levels, since an undersized CT is a leading cause of nuisance tripping that erodes operator trust in genuine alarms.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any wiring work on the circuit or detector, megger test before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or leave a real fault condition undetected.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working near live series-circuit or CT wiring, and confirm the detector enclosure is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before closing it up.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the detector's alarm threshold and reporting to the ALCMS are correct, and that any fault identified during troubleshooting has been resolved, before closing out the work.

Quick Reference

Airport Series Circuit Earth Fault: Cause, Detection & Response

Airport Series Circuit Earth Fault

An airport series circuit earth fault is a leakage path from the circuit conductors to earth. It usually starts as insulation degradation from ageing, mechanical damage, moisture ingress or chemical attack. Isolating transformers keep the primary and secondary windings isolated from each other and from earth, so a healthy series circuit has no deliberate path to earth.

The earth-fault detector monitors for this leakage and raises an alarm or trips when it exceeds the set level, giving early warning before the insulation breakdown becomes a shock hazard or a complete circuit failure.

An alarm shows that a circuit has a problem but not where. Where the installation allows sections of the loop to be isolated, sectionalising against the leakage reading narrows the location faster than inspecting the whole loop. Treat every earth-fault alarm as a real fault until checked, and do not disable or ignore it to keep a circuit in service. Check the primary cable and the isolating transformers as the usual places for the leakage.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Trend Leakage Current, Don't Just Watch for the Trip Threshold
Diagnostics

Insulation breakdown is almost always a gradual process, and a leakage current that's climbing steadily but still well under the trip threshold is exactly the early warning this device is meant to provide; logging the trend, not just the pass/fail alarm state, is what actually lets maintenance get ahead of a failure.

Moisture-Related Leakage Can Be Weather-Correlated, Not Constant
Troubleshooting

A leakage path caused by moisture ingress at a connector or splice often shows elevated readings during or after rain and recovers somewhat as things dry out, which can make an intermittent fault look inconsistent if checked only on dry days. Cross-reference leakage trend data against recent weather when chasing an intermittent alarm.

Set Trip Thresholds Against Measured Baseline Leakage, Not a Generic Default
Configuration

Every series circuit has some small amount of baseline capacitive leakage that varies with cable length and age, and a detector set to a generic factory threshold either nuisance-trips a long, healthy circuit or fails to catch a real fault on a short one with low baseline leakage. Characterise each circuit's healthy baseline at commissioning.

Sectionalise the Loop to Locate the Fault Faster
Troubleshooting

An earth-fault alarm tells you a circuit has a problem but not where; where the installation allows isolating sections of the loop, sequential sectionalising against the leakage reading narrows the fault location considerably faster than inspecting the entire loop end to end.

A Nuisance-Trip History Doesn't Justify Raising the Threshold Without Investigation
Safety

Repeated nuisance trips are frustrating, but simply raising the detection threshold to stop them removes the early-warning margin this device exists to provide and can mask a genuine developing fault; investigate and fix the source of the nuisance trips rather than desensitising the protection.

Test the Detector's Own Trip Function Periodically, Not Just the Circuit
Testing

A protection device that has simply never had cause to trip in years of service hasn't necessarily been confirmed to still work; a periodic functional test using a simulated fault current verifies the detector itself, since a healthy circuit alone doesn't prove the protection is still functional.
