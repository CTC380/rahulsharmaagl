Product Overview

High Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B)

What It Is

The High Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B) provides a high level of visual conspicuity for structures that require enhanced aviation obstacle identification.

For tall structures, a complete installation may consist of multiple lights at defined elevations, making the structure identifiable from relevant aircraft viewing directions while maintaining the required optical characteristics.

Depending on configuration, Type A or Type B may be selected, with final intensity, colour, flash characteristics and operating arrangement determined by the project requirements.

Depending on Configuration, the System Can Incorporate

  • High-intensity optical output
  • Defined aviation colour
  • Flashing operation
  • Multi-level installation
  • Flash synchronization
  • Automatic control
  • Status monitoring
  • Remote alarm interfaces

Precision AGL Solutions supplies these lights for new installations, major infrastructure projects, replacement systems and obstacle-marking applications.

Confirm from the Approved Design

ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 6 uses Type A where an object stands more than 150 m above the surrounding ground and an aeronautical study shows high-intensity lights are essential for recognising it by day. It uses Type B for towers supporting overhead wires or cables where a study shows they are essential for recognising the wires. The classification is still application-specific.

The final classification should be confirmed from the approved aviation obstruction-lighting design rather than selected solely from the product category.

Where Specified

High-intensity obstruction lighting commonly uses flashing operation where specified by the applicable aviation configuration.

The exact flash characteristic must be confirmed from the applicable project and aviation requirements.

Where several lights are installed on the same structure, synchronization may be required to maintain a coordinated visual signal; Type A lights on one structure flash simultaneously. Type B lights on a tower supporting overhead wires or cables flash in sequence (middle light first, then top, then bottom), so the controller must support the correct mode.

Approved Design

Very tall structures may require multiple obstruction-lighting levels. The quantity and position of lights depend on the approved obstruction-lighting design.

ICAO calls for intermediate levels to be spaced as equally as practicable, normally not more than about 105 m apart (confirm in the current edition). The final arrangement should be established through the approved aviation lighting assessment.

FAQ

High Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B) — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a high intensity obstruction light (Type A/B)?

It is an aviation obstruction light that provides a high level of visual conspicuity for structures that need enhanced obstacle identification. For tall structures a complete installation may consist of multiple lights at defined elevations. Type A or Type B may be selected depending on configuration, with final intensity, colour, flash characteristics and operating arrangement determined by the project requirements.

When is Type A used and when is Type B used?

ICAO Annex 14 Chapter 6 uses Type A where an object stands more than 150 m above the surrounding ground and an aeronautical study shows high-intensity lights are essential for recognising it by day. It uses Type B for towers supporting overhead wires or cables where a study shows they are essential for recognising the wires. The classification is still application-specific and should be confirmed from the approved obstruction-lighting design rather than selected from the product category.

What intensity and flash rate do these lights have?

The light is white and flashing. Typical ICAO effective intensity is 200,000 cd by day, 20,000 cd at twilight and 2,000 cd at night for Type A, and 100,000 / 20,000 / 2,000 cd for Type B. ICAO flash rate is 40 to 60 flashes per minute (FAA L-856: 40 per minute). Confirm all values against the applicable edition.

How do lights on the same structure flash?

Type A lights on one structure flash together, while Type B lights on a tower supporting overhead wires or cables flash in sequence (middle light first, then top, then bottom), so the controller must support the correct mode. Where several lights are installed, synchronization can be provided by local synchronization, central timing control, controller-based synchronization, or GPS or other timing references where specified.

How many lighting levels does a tall structure need?

Very tall structures may require multiple obstruction-lighting levels, and the quantity and position of lights depend on the approved obstruction-lighting design. ICAO calls for intermediate levels to be spaced as equally as practicable, normally not more than about 105 m apart (confirm in the current edition). The final arrangement should be established through the approved aviation lighting assessment.

What monitoring and automatic control are available?

Monitoring may include individual light status, controller status, power status, flash operation, communication status, system fault indication and remote alarm output, and it can be integrated with a site SCADA, building-management or airport infrastructure system. Control functions can include automatic day, twilight and night intensity switching by photocell, flash control, synchronization, system enable and disable, fault reporting and remote status indication.

How are the lights mounted and powered?

Typical mounting arrangements are tower platforms, mast brackets, structural steelwork, equipment platforms, rooftop structures and dedicated obstruction-light mounting assemblies. Mounting must provide secure support and keep the required optical orientation under wind and vibration, with suitable access for maintenance. Electrical considerations include supply voltage, AC or DC configuration, circuit, surge and lightning protection, cable routing, earthing, and control and monitoring interfaces, coordinated with the structure's power infrastructure.

What is checked when commissioning obstruction lights?

Commissioning covers mounting and structural fixing, light orientation, electrical connections, supply voltage and protection, light operation, colour and optical output, flash characteristics, synchronization between lights, automatic control functions, monitoring and alarm outputs, and cable routing and weather sealing. Record the results. Where required, photometric verification should be performed using suitable calibrated measurement equipment and the applicable procedure.

How should obstruction lights be maintained safely?

Maintenance may include visual inspection, cleaning optical surfaces, checking mounting hardware, cable entries and electrical connections, testing flash operation and synchronization, verifying monitoring signals, and inspecting surge protection and enclosure integrity. Isolate and lock off the supply before servicing, as some units hold stored energy after switch-off, and work at height only under the site permit-to-work and fall-protection procedures. Set intervals from the installation environment, manufacturer recommendations and site procedures.

What are common faults, and what should be done if a light fails?

Typical conditions are a light not operating (check supply, protection and controller), reduced visibility (inspect and clean the optical window), incorrect flashing (verify flash configuration), lights out of synchronization (check the synchronization system), monitoring alarms, intermittent operation, moisture inside the enclosure and light movement from a loose mounting. A failed obstruction light is an aviation safety matter and should be reported promptly to the aviation authority or NOTAM originator under the airport and authority procedure.

Can high intensity obstruction lights be used to replace or retrofit an existing system?

Yes, where legacy equipment needs replacement, improved reliability or updated control and monitoring. The evaluation should consider the existing classification, light positions, structure geometry, required intensity, flash characteristics, supply voltage, control equipment, synchronization method, monitoring interfaces, cable arrangement and current aviation requirements. A replacement should be selected on the complete optical, electrical and operational requirements rather than physical dimensions alone.

Which standards apply to high intensity obstruction lighting?

References include ICAO Annex 14 Vol. I Chapter 6 with Doc 9157 Part 4, FAA AC 70/7460-1N and AC 150/5345-43J (obstruction lighting equipment, including L-856 high-intensity flashing white), and DGCA India CAR Section 4 with the airport NOC conditions. Near an aerodrome, an aeronautical study should decide whether night intensity needs reducing to avoid dazzling pilots. Verify the current edition of each reference.

What information is needed for an obstruction light assessment?

Provide the structure type, overall height and location, the applicable aviation authority, the Type A or Type B requirement, light colour, intensity and flashing requirements, the number of lighting levels and quantity of lights, available power supply, existing control system, synchronization and monitoring requirements, mounting arrangement, environmental conditions, and whether it is a new installation or a replacement. This lets the configuration be evaluated as part of the complete structure rather than as an individual light.
