Glide Slope

The ground-based radio transmitter that provides precise vertical guidance to aircraft during the final approach phase of an Instrument Landing System (ILS) approach. The glide slope generates a signal that defines the optimal descent path to the runway, typically at a 3° angle, enabling pilots to maintain the correct vertical profile for a safe landing.

Overview

What Is It?

The Glide Slope (also known as the Glide Path) is a ground-based radio navigation system that forms the vertical guidance component of the Instrument Landing System (ILS). It consists of an antenna array and transmitter located to the side of the runway, typically 230 to 380 metres (750–1,250 ft) from the threshold, offset from the runway centreline by roughly 120 to 200 metres (390–660 ft). The glide slope transmits a UHF signal (between 328.6 MHz and 335.4 MHz) that creates two overlapping lobes: one modulated at 90 Hz (above the glide path) and one at 150 Hz (below the glide path). The aircraft's receiver compares the modulation depths to determine whether it is above or below the ideal descent angle (typically 3°), providing the pilot with a deviation indication on the Instrument Landing System indicator (CDI).

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The glide slope's purpose is to keep an aircraft descending along one specific, obstacle-clearing angle from the point it intercepts the beam all the way to the touchdown zone, rather than leaving vertical profile to the pilot's judgement of sink rate and distance. Because the two overlapping lobes only produce a true on-path indication when the aircraft is exactly on the designed 3° angle, any deviation above or below immediately shows up as an imbalance in modulation depth that the pilot can correct before it develops into a significant vertical error. This precision matters most in the final stages of an approach in reduced visibility, where the pilot has little or no outside visual reference to judge descent rate independently, making the glide slope's guidance the primary means of staying on a safe vertical path to the runway.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The Glide Slope is installed on precision approach runways that support ILS operations. It is a critical component for:

  • CAT I Precision Approaches: Providing vertical guidance down to a decision height of 200 feet (60 metres) with RVR of 550 metres or greater.
  • CAT II Precision Approaches: Providing vertical guidance down to a decision height of 100 feet (30 metres) with RVR of 350 metres or greater.
  • CAT III Precision Approaches: Providing vertical guidance down to a decision height as low as 15 feet (5 metres) with RVR as low as 200 metres (CAT IIIA), 50 metres (CAT IIIB), or even zero (CAT IIIC).
  • Military Airfields: Supporting military aircraft ILS operations.
  • Civil Airports: Supporting commercial and general aviation ILS approaches.
Placement

Installation & Location

The glide slope antenna array is sited beside the runway, offset laterally roughly 120-180 m from the centreline, positioned abeam the desired touchdown point so the projected glide path crosses the threshold at the specified height (typically 50 ft / 15 m). The antenna mast and equipment shelter sit within the runway's obstacle-free zone requirements, and the surrounding ground is graded flat and kept clear of vehicles, snow banks, or structures that could reflect the signal and distort the glide path. The transmitter and monitoring electronics are housed in the shelter at the base of the mast, fed from dual power supplies with automatic changeover, and connected by cable to a remote status and control panel in the ATC tower or navaid maintenance facility.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Glide Slope operates on the principle of amplitude modulation and spatial signal distribution:

  • Signal Generation: The glide slope transmitter generates an RF carrier at the assigned UHF frequency. Two modulating signals (90 Hz and 150 Hz) are applied to the carrier.
  • Antenna Array: A directional antenna array (typically an end‑fire or broadside array) creates a pattern with two overlapping lobes. The 90 Hz signal is predominant above the glide path, while the 150 Hz signal is predominant below the glide path.
  • Glide Path Angle: The glide path angle is typically set to 3° above the horizontal, but can be adjusted (e.g., 2.5° to 4°) based on terrain and obstacle clearance requirements.
  • Aircraft Reception: The aircraft's ILS receiver measures the difference in modulation depth between the two signals. The difference is displayed as a vertical deviation indication on the Course Deviation Indicator (CDI), showing the pilot whether the aircraft is above or below the glide path.
  • Coverage: The glide slope provides vertical guidance typically from the threshold out to a range of 10 to 18 nautical miles (18 to 33 km), depending on the configuration.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Like the localizer it works alongside, the glide slope facility is not remotely commanded in real time for its RF output — the control logic is a self-monitoring and protection loop. Integral near-field monitors sample the radiated signal directly at the antenna and check course angle, displacement sensitivity and RF power against tolerance, with some installations adding a far-field monitor for independent confirmation. Dual-equipment sites run online and standby transmitters, and the monitor logic executes an automatic changeover to standby on an out-of-tolerance reading; if neither channel can meet tolerance, the facility shuts itself down and radiates no signal rather than risk erroneous vertical guidance. Status, alarms and changeover events are reported to the airport's remote control and monitoring system for ATC and maintenance visibility.

Construction

Physical Construction

The glide slope antenna array, typically three to five end-fire or broadside radiating elements, is mounted on a mast alongside the runway at a carefully surveyed offset and height to establish the correct glide path angle, with each element's phasing and power distribution fed from a weatherproof equipment hut at the mast base through low-loss coaxial cable. The equipment hut houses the dual redundant UHF transmitters, modulation electronics generating the 90 Hz/150 Hz course signals, and continuous monitoring equipment, all mounted in a temperature-controlled, weatherproof cabinet similar to other ILS ground equipment. Mast structures are engineered to resist wind loading and are fitted with lightning protection and obstruction lighting per airfield safety requirements, and buried or ducted feeder runs between the hut and antenna array are sealed against moisture ingress. Automatic monitoring continuously checks course alignment, modulation depth, and power output, triggering standby transmitter switchover and remote alarms if a parameter drifts out of tolerance.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Antenna Array Misalignment

Physical displacement or misalignment of the glide slope antenna array can cause the glide path angle to shift, resulting in a false vertical guidance indication. Regular alignment checks using specialized survey equipment and calibration aircraft are essential.

Terrain & Obstacle Interference

Reflections from nearby terrain, buildings, or vehicles can cause multipath interference, distorting the glide slope signal and creating false indications. Proper site selection and obstruction clearance are critical.

Modulation Balance Drift

Over time, the modulation balance between the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals can drift, causing the glide path to shift. Regular monitoring and calibration using flight inspection is required to maintain accuracy.

Power Amplifier Failure

The transmitter power amplifiers are subject to wear and thermal stress. Regular maintenance and redundancy (hot standby) are essential to prevent loss of the glide slope service.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Flight Inspection Reports Glide Path Angle Shift
Likely Cause
Physical displacement or misalignment of the antenna array, often from ground settlement, storm damage, or unauthorised work near the site.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the antenna array's physical alignment against the last certified survey; inspect the mounting structure and mast for movement, corrosion, or damage; review recent flight inspection trend data for a gradual versus sudden shift.
Corrective Action
Re-align the antenna array to the certified position using survey equipment, and confirm correction with a follow-up calibration flight before returning the facility to unrestricted service.
Course Roughness or Bends Reported by Aircraft
Likely Cause
Multipath interference from terrain, buildings, vehicles, or vegetation growth near the site distorting the reflected signal.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the obstruction clearance survey for recent changes near the site; check for parked vehicles, new structures, or vegetation growth within the critical/sensitive area; correlate reported roughness with aircraft type or approach direction if possible.
Corrective Action
Remove or relocate the offending obstruction, enforce critical-area vehicle/equipment restrictions, and confirm resolution with a flight check.
Glide Path Indication Drifting Off Nominal
Likely Cause
Modulation balance between the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals has drifted due to component ageing in the transmitter.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the current 90 Hz/150 Hz modulation depth and balance against the monitor readout; review monitor alarm history for a gradual trend versus a sudden step change.
Corrective Action
Adjust modulation balance per the maintenance manual and verify with flight inspection; if drift is rapid or recurring, investigate the modulator/transmitter stage for a developing fault rather than just re-trimming.
Transmitter Trips to Standby or Off-Air
Likely Cause
Power amplifier failure under thermal stress or age-related component wear in the primary transmitter.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the monitor alarm log to identify which parameter (power, modulation, angle) triggered the changeover; check power amplifier output level and temperature; confirm standby unit is radiating correctly within tolerance.
Corrective Action
Service or replace the faulted power amplifier stage before returning the primary unit to service, and re-verify with a calibration/monitor check; do not leave the facility running on standby longer than necessary.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

RF Exposure Around the Antenna Array
Personnel Safety

Follow the facility's RF safety procedure and any posted exclusion distance when working near the radiating antenna array, and confirm the transmitter is placed in maintenance/standby mode before working at or near the mast.

Transmitter Shelter Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the transmitter before working inside the equipment shelter, and verify zero energy before disturbing internal connections; RF power amplifier stages can carry hazardous voltages that remain present briefly after shutdown.

Mast Work at Height
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate fall protection and follow mast climbing procedures when accessing antenna elements, and confirm the feeder is disconnected from the transmitter before disturbing antenna connections.

Keep the Critical Area Clear
Operational

Keep vehicles, personnel, and equipment out of the glide slope critical area during low-visibility operations or whenever the facility is radiating for an aircraft on approach, since reflections from an obstruction there can distort the signal in flight.

NOTAM for Service Interruption
Operational

Issue a NOTAM before taking the glide slope out of service or below tolerance for maintenance, since aircraft on an ILS approach rely on it for vertical guidance.

Verify Monitor Function Before Return to Service
Compliance

Confirm the near-field (and far-field, where fitted) monitor correctly detects an out-of-tolerance angle, sensitivity, or power reading and executes changeover or shutdown before returning the facility to service after maintenance.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate PPE and confirm the mast, shelter, and feeder are bonded to the facility earth system and lightning protection before working on internal connections.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the facility radiating out-of-tolerance course angle, modulation, or power after maintenance; verify monitored parameters are within tolerance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Ground Reflection Plane Changes Shift the Angle
Siting

Vegetation growth, new drainage work, or even standing water near a null-reference or image-type glide slope antenna can alter the effective ground reflection plane enough to shift the broadcast angle within a tolerance band without any equipment fault. Check the ground condition before chasing an electronics cause.

Flight Inspection and Ground Checks Can Legitimately Differ
Verification

A ground monitor reading within tolerance does not guarantee the in-space signal is equally clean, since near-field ground checks don't fully capture far-field multipath effects an aircraft receiver sees on approach. Treat a flight inspection discrepancy as informative, not as a sign the ground test was wrong.

Vehicle and Aircraft Movement Near the Antenna Matters
Operational

Large vehicles or aircraft parked or taxiing near the glide slope antenna during a critical area incursion can perturb the radiated signal enough to trip a monitor alarm that has nothing to do with the transmitter itself. Correlate alarm timestamps against ground movement logs before troubleshooting hardware.

Sideband Antenna Balance Degrades Gradually
Maintenance

On sideband-reference systems, a slow imbalance between clearance and course antenna elements shows up first as a subtle modulation trend rather than a hard alarm. Trending modulation percentage over successive checks catches this before it drifts into an out-of-tolerance condition.

Lightning Events Warrant a Full Parameter Check
Troubleshooting

After a nearby lightning strike, a facility that comes back up and shows nominal power can still have subtle course or clearance degradation from surge damage to a single module. A full parameter verification after any lightning event catches this rather than relying on the automatic monitor alone.

Correlate Monitor Trends With Weather
Process

Seasonal ground moisture changes can produce a repeatable annual pattern in monitored course angle at some sites. Logging monitor readings alongside weather conditions over a full year helps distinguish a genuine equipment drift from a recurring, benign seasonal effect.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the ILS glide slope: how it works, frequencies, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is the glide slope in an ILS?

The glide slope, also called the glide path, is the ground-based radio system that gives the vertical guidance component of the Instrument Landing System (ILS). It uses a transmitter and antenna array beside the runway.

Q2How does the glide slope work?

The transmitter modulates a UHF carrier with 90 Hz and 150 Hz tones. The antenna array creates two overlapping lobes, with 90 Hz predominant above the glide path and 150 Hz below. The aircraft receiver compares the two to show whether it is high or low.

Q3What is the standard glide path angle?

Typically 3° above horizontal, adjustable from about 2.5° to 4° depending on terrain and obstacle clearance requirements.

Q4What frequencies does the glide slope use?

UHF from 328.6 MHz to 335.4 MHz, paired with the localiser frequency.

Q5Which approach categories use the glide slope?

It is installed on ILS precision approach runways and gives vertical guidance for CAT I, II and III approaches. Wide-aperture arrays are used where higher-category performance is needed.

Q6Which standards apply to the glide slope?

The main references are ICAO Annex 10, FAA Order 6750.16 (ILS siting criteria), EUROCAE ED-82 and RTCA DO-192 for airborne receivers.

Q7How often should a glide slope be maintained?

Typically annual antenna array alignment checks tied to the flight check schedule, semi-annual modulation balance checks, and site and terrain surveys periodically or after site changes.

Q8What are the common problems with glide slope systems?

Antenna array misalignment, reflections from terrain, buildings or vehicles causing multipath interference, modulation balance drift between the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals, and transmitter power amplifier failure.
