Runway Lighting

The visual reference system that defines the runway itself — edges, centreline, threshold, end, and touchdown zone — giving pilots continuous positional awareness through landing, roll-out and takeoff, day or night.

Overview

What Is It?

Runway lighting is the set of fixed and, in some cases, sequenced light systems that mark the physical boundaries and key reference points of a runway. Unlike approach lighting, which guides an aircraft toward the runway, runway lighting defines the runway itself once the aircraft is over it or rolling along it — where the edges are, where the usable surface begins and ends, and where the touchdown zone lies.

Each runway lighting subsystem communicates something different through colour, position and, in some systems, flash pattern — together they let a pilot judge alignment, remaining distance and runway limits without relying solely on instruments.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Runway lighting's purpose is to let a pilot judge exactly where they are relative to the runway itself — its edges, its usable start and end points, the touchdown zone — at the specific phases of flight (final approach, landing, rollout) where that judgment can't be deferred to instruments alone. Each subsystem's colour and position exists to answer a different question at a glance: white edge lighting answers 'where are the boundaries', red end lighting answers 'how much runway is left', and touchdown zone lighting answers 'am I landing in the right place' — together giving the pilot the same spatial awareness in fog or at night that would otherwise only be available in clear daylight.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Runway lighting subsystems are installed along and around the full length of the runway:

  • Runway Edge Lights: Run the full length of the runway on both sides, typically white, marking the lateral limits of the usable surface.
  • Runway Centreline Lights: Embedded along the runway centreline on precision-approach runways, aiding alignment in low visibility.
  • Threshold Lights: Mark the beginning of the runway available for landing.
  • Runway End Lights: Mark the far end of the runway, typically red when viewed from the runway.
  • Touchdown Zone Lights: Installed in the initial portion of the runway to help pilots judge the touchdown point in low visibility.
  • Runway Guard Lights: Positioned at runway-holding positions on the taxiway side, warning of the approach to an active runway.

The specific systems required at a given runway depend on its classification (e.g. non-instrument, non-precision approach, precision approach CAT I/II/III) under the applicable ICAO or FAA standard.

Placement

Installation & Location

Each subsystem is set out along the runway according to its own specified spacing and offset — edge lights at uniform intervals down both sides, centreline lights embedded along the surveyed centreline, threshold and end lights in a row at each end, and touchdown zone lights in the paired barrettes covering the initial landing zone — with the complete layout tied back to a single runway survey so every subsystem's geometry remains consistent relative to the others. Mounting method (elevated or inset) is chosen per subsystem and location, with inset fixtures used within the runway strip and touchdown zone where a protruding fixture would be a hazard, and elevated fixtures reserved for locations such as unpaved shoulders where flush mounting isn't practical. Runway guard lights, though positioned on the taxiway side of a holding point rather than on the runway itself, are set out and surveyed against the same runway reference so their warning function lines up correctly with the runway threshold they protect. Cabling for each subsystem generally runs on its own series circuit back to a dedicated regulator, so a fault in one subsystem — edge lighting, for example — doesn't take down an unrelated subsystem such as centreline or touchdown zone lighting.

Function

How Does It Work?

Runway lighting fixtures are wired into AGL series circuits fed by one or more CCRs, with each fixture isolated from the shared primary loop by its own isolating transformer. Intensity across the whole circuit is controlled together by the CCR's selectable current steps, allowing the same runway edge circuit to run at low intensity on a clear night and high intensity in fog, without rewiring anything.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Colour Coding: White for edge and most centreline lighting, red for the runway end, with colour changes near the far end of some centreline systems to warn of the remaining distance.
  • Spacing and Configuration: Longitudinal and lateral spacing is defined by the applicable standard and runway classification — consistency of spacing is itself a visual cue to the pilot.
  • Intensity Steps: Selectable brightness levels let the same fixtures serve clear-night and low-visibility operations.
  • Elevated vs. Inset Fixtures: Elevated fixtures are used where FOD and jet blast risk is lower; inset (flush) fixtures are required on and near the runway surface itself.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Runway lighting is controlled at the circuit level, the same way an individual runway edge light loop is, but a runway typically runs several such circuits side by side — separate loops for edge lighting, centreline lighting, threshold lighting, and touchdown zone lighting, each fed by its own CCR. The ALCMS or tower selects an intensity step appropriate to prevailing RVR or ATC instruction, and that command is applied per circuit, so the whole runway lighting picture is really several loop-level control systems operating in parallel rather than one.

Keeping these subsystems on separate circuits means a fault on the touchdown zone lighting, for instance, is reported and can be worked on independently of the edge or centreline circuits — and it also means intensity can, on more advanced installations, be stepped differently between subsystems (touchdown zone and centreline lighting are sometimes run brighter than edge lighting) rather than being locked to a single runway-wide value.

ALCMS → CCR (per subsystem) → Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Fixture (loop-level feedback)

System Integration

Circuit & System Overview

Every runway lighting subsystem taps the same series loop through its own isolating transformer, but each carries a different colour, position and fixture type — and the CCR's current step raises or lowers all of them together, so visibility conditions are handled by dialling the circuit rather than swapping fixtures.

Construction

Physical Construction

Despite their differing colours and positions, the subsystems share a common build. Elevated fixtures (edge, threshold, end) use a die-cast aluminium housing, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, with the optical prism gasket-sealed under an O-ring or silicone seal and bolted to a frangible coupling that shears cleanly on impact before seating on an L-867 base can set flush with the surrounding grade. Inset fixtures (centreline, touchdown zone) dispense with the frangible coupling, instead seating directly in an L-868 base can recessed into the pavement with a load-rated top surface designed to take aircraft wheel loading. In both cases, internal wiring terminates at L-823-style connectors that mate to the secondary leads of an isolating transformer housed in its own nearby base can, with stainless-steel fasteners and a drainage provision keeping the enclosure watertight through years of runway service.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Base Can Excavation by Subsystem
Civil

Excavate and set base cans to each subsystem's mount type — L-867 elevated for edge, threshold, and end lights; L-868 inset for centreline and touchdown zone lights — holding each flush with the surrounding grade or pavement.

Single-Survey Set-out
Civil

Set out every subsystem (edge, centreline, threshold, end, touchdown zone, runway guard lights) from one runway survey, at each subsystem's own specified spacing and offset, so the complete layout stays geometrically consistent.

Drainage Provision
Civil

Provide a weep hole or drainage path at each base can to prevent water pooling around the transformer and cable entry, particularly for inset fixtures.

Per-Subsystem Circuit Routing
Electrical

Route each subsystem's series circuit duct back to its own dedicated regulator, keeping cabling separate so a fault in one subsystem cannot take down an unrelated one.

L-823 Connector Termination
Electrical

Terminate and seal the primary connector at every fixture per manufacturer procedure, keeping each splice submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm each isolating transformer's wattage matches its fixture and its subsystem circuit's rated current step before connection.

Mount-Type Fixing by Subsystem
Mechanical

For elevated units, torque the frangible coupling to spec; for inset units, seat the housing flush and confirm it meets wheel-load tolerance.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record base can position, cable route, connector seal, and subsystem/circuit assignment for every fixture as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test each series loop across the edge, threshold, and centreline circuits before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity and record total loop resistance for each runway lighting circuit before first energisation.

Colour & Zone Transition Verification
Energisation

Confirm colour transitions between zones (white edge to red-and-white to red, or white to red at the threshold) occur at the correct distance from the runway end.

CCR Brightness Step Response
Energisation

Cycle each CCR through every intensity step and confirm every fixture on its loop responds correctly, ruling out marginal transformers or loose connections.

Fixture Alignment & Aim Verification
Final Acceptance

Check elevation and azimuth of representative fixtures across each circuit against the design aiming diagram, correcting any deviation before sign-off.

Photometric Commissioning Survey
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity, colour, and beam spread with a calibrated photometer at sample points across the runway against ICAO/FAA commissioning tolerances.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, continuity, colour-transition, and photometric readings alongside as-built layout in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Fixture Damage from Jet Blast / FOD

Inset and elevated fixtures alike are exposed to jet blast, snow-clearing equipment and general FOD; lens and housing damage is one of the most common inspection findings.

Colour or Intensity Drift Over Time

LED and lamp degradation can shift perceived colour or reduce intensity below the required threshold, which photometric field testing is designed to catch before it affects operations.

Uneven Circuit Intensity

A mix of ageing and new fixtures on the same series circuit can create visibly uneven brightness along the runway, even though each fixture is individually within tolerance.

Water Ingress in Inset Fixtures

Flush-mounted centreline and touchdown zone fixtures sit in a wet, load-bearing environment; seal failure leads to moisture ingress, corrosion, and eventual fixture failure.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fixture Cracked, Dislodged, or Missing After Winter Operations
Likely Cause
Jet blast, snow-clearing equipment, or general FOD has physically struck an inset or elevated fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a post-event walk-through or drive-through inspection of the affected section, noting cracked lenses, dislodged housings, or missing fixtures.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged fixture and inspect the base can/mounting for secondary damage before reinstating; log the location for closer monitoring during future winter operations.
Photometric Test Flags Colour or Intensity Below Threshold
Likely Cause
LED or lamp degradation has gradually shifted the perceived colour or reduced output below the required standard.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a scheduled photometric survey and compare the fixture's colour and intensity readings against the applicable ICAO/FAA threshold.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module or lamp on any fixture that fails the threshold, and re-test to confirm compliance before closing the finding.
Visibly Uneven Brightness Along a Series Circuit
Likely Cause
A mix of ageing and newly replaced fixtures on the same circuit is producing inconsistent brightness even though each unit individually passes its test.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct a night visual inspection along the full circuit length, noting any fixtures that stand out as brighter or dimmer than their neighbours.
Corrective Action
Plan a phased replacement of the oldest fixtures on the circuit rather than one-off swaps, to bring the whole run back to a consistent output level.
Inset Fixture Fails or Shows Signs of Moisture Ingress
Likely Cause
A seal failure on a flush-mounted centreline or touchdown zone fixture has allowed water into a load-bearing, wet environment.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the fixture's top seal, gasket, and pressure vent (if fitted); check for internal condensation or corrosion when the unit is removed.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed seal/gasket and the fixture if internal corrosion has already occurred, then re-torque and re-test the seal integrity before reinstating.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Series Circuit / CCR Failure Affecting an Entire Subsystem
High Severity
Root Cause
A fault at the constant current regulator or a break in the series circuit loop serving a runway lighting subsystem such as edge, centreline, or touchdown zone lighting.
Effect
Every fixture on that subsystem's circuit goes dark or drops to an incorrect intensity together, removing an entire category of runway reference at once rather than a single fixture.
Detection Method
CCR output and loop-resistance testing at the regulator, and loop-level fault review before dispatching a fixture-by-fixture field check.
ALCMS Intensity-Step Command Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Fault in the ALCMS or its communication path to a subsystem's CCR, or a misconfigured RVR/ATC/PCL intensity mapping.
Effect
A subsystem runs at the wrong brightness step for current conditions, which can be as operationally significant as an outright outage in poor visibility.
Detection Method
Cross-check of commanded versus actual intensity step at the CCR against the RVR or ATC instruction that should be driving it.
Subsystem Colour-Coding Confusion From a Wrong-Colour Fixture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
An incorrect fixture type or a chromaticity-shifted lens installed on a subsystem, so it no longer reads as the colour convention (white edge, red end, etc.) it's meant to represent.
Effect
A pilot misreads what a subsystem is telling them — boundary, remaining distance, or touchdown zone — because the colour cue itself is wrong or degraded.
Detection Method
Chromaticity spot-check against the subsystem's specified colour during scheduled photometric testing.
Isolating Transformer / Connector Degradation at Individual Fixtures
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress or age-related insulation breakdown at an individual fixture's transformer or connector, common to any subsystem's fixtures.
Effect
A single fixture within a subsystem goes dark or intermittent while the rest of the subsystem continues operating normally.
Detection Method
Megger testing of the individual fixture's transformer and connector during scheduled maintenance.
Loop-Level Feedback Failing to Localise a Fault to a Specific Subsystem
Low Severity
Root Cause
Aggregated loop-level monitoring flagging an anomaly without distinguishing which runway lighting subsystem or fixture is responsible.
Effect
Diagnosis takes longer than necessary, delaying restoration of a subsystem a pilot may be depending on for a specific spatial judgment.
Detection Method
Cross-referencing loop-level alarms against subsystem-specific test points to narrow the fault before a field visit.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Edge, threshold, centreline, and touchdown zone circuits each run on their own constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the correct CCR for the subsystem being worked on, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening any enclosure.

Active Runway Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on runway lighting requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where a fixture will be dark or out of tolerance; work directly on the runway surface itself needs an approved closure or a protected work window, not just a NOTAM.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible coupling or mount with a rigid fitting, and do not over-torque connections during installation or repair; runway lighting fixtures must still break away or shear cleanly on aircraft impact.

Confirm Which Subsystem You're Isolating
Electrical Safety

Because edge, centreline, threshold, and TDZ lighting are typically on separate circuits and CCRs, positively confirm the correct feeder and circuit diagram before lockout; isolating the wrong loop can leave the fixture you're working on live while other lights go dark unexpectedly.

FOD Control on the Runway
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets at every fixture worked on, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating, since debris on the runway can be ingested by an engine or strike an aircraft.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work on a runway lighting circuit, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm fixtures and base cans are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting any loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave any subsystem with a fixture out of aim tolerance, at reduced intensity, or otherwise non-compliant; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Loop Segmentation Strategy Affects Failure Blast Radius
Design

How a runway lighting system is divided into series loops determines how much of the runway goes dark if a single loop faults. Segment loops so a worst-case single-loop failure still leaves enough usable lighting for safe operations, rather than segmenting purely for cable-run convenience.

Backup Power Transfer Time Is as Important as Backup Capacity
Reliability

A standby generator or UPS with ample runtime doesn't help if the transfer switch takes too long to bring lighting back online during an active operation. Verify transfer time against the applicable standard's maximum outage duration, not just total backup capacity.

Size CCR Capacity With Real Growth Margin, Not Just Present Load
Electrical

Sizing a constant current regulator exactly to today's connected load leaves no room for future fixture additions or LED-to-legacy mixed loops during transition. Build in a deliberate capacity margin at design stage rather than forcing a CCR replacement the next time the loop is extended.

Standardise Connector and Cable Types Before Mixed Stock Becomes the Norm
Maintenance

Runway lighting systems installed and extended over many years often accumulate mixed connector and cable types loop to loop. Standardising on a single specification for new work — even if it means an interim mismatch — keeps spares and troubleshooting simpler than letting variation compound indefinitely.

Category Match Should Be Re-Verified, Not Assumed to Carry Forward
Compliance

A runway's approach and lighting category can change with new instrument procedures or operational upgrades without a corresponding lighting system upgrade being triggered automatically. Periodically re-verify the installed lighting configuration still matches the runway's current published category.

System-Level Uniformity Checks Catch What Fixture-Level Testing Misses
Verification

Every fixture on a runway can individually pass photometric spec while the system as a whole shows visible brightness or colour banding loop to loop. Include a system-level uniformity walk or flight check in commissioning and periodic verification, not only individual fixture testing.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about runway lighting: the subsystems, colour coding, power, intensity control, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is runway lighting?

Runway lighting is the set of fixed and, in some cases, sequenced light systems that mark a runway's boundaries and key reference points. Unlike approach lighting, which guides an aircraft toward the runway, it defines the runway itself once the aircraft is over it or rolling along it.

Q2What are the main parts of a runway lighting system?

Runway edge lights, centreline lights (on precision-approach runways), threshold lights, runway end lights and touchdown zone lights. Each answers a different question for the pilot: boundaries, alignment, where the usable surface begins and ends, and where the touchdown zone is.

Q3What do the different runway light colours mean?

White marks the edges and most of the centreline, green marks the threshold and red marks the runway end. Edge lighting can change colour near the runway end and centreline lighting changes near the end on some systems, so pilots can judge remaining distance.

Q4Which runway lights are inset and which are elevated?

Edge and threshold lights are typically elevated, while centreline and touchdown zone lights are typically inset (flush-mounted).

Q5How bright are runway lights?

Typical minimums are 10,000 cd for white edge lights, 8,000 cd for green threshold and red end lights and 6,500 cd for white centreline lights. Field intensity tolerance is ±25% of rated value at each CCR step.

Q6How is a runway lighting system powered?

Fixtures run on constant-current series circuits fed by CCRs at a standard 6.6 A loop current, each fixture isolated from the loop by its own isolating transformer. Edge, threshold, centreline and touchdown zone lighting are typically on separate circuits so intensity can be controlled independently and faults isolated.

Q7How is runway light intensity controlled?

The CCR's selectable current steps (2.8 A, 3.4 A, 4.1 A, 5.2 A and 6.6 A) change the intensity of the whole circuit together. The same edge circuit can run low on a clear night and high in fog without rewiring.

Q8How is a runway lighting layout set out?

Each subsystem follows its own spacing and offset: edge lights at uniform intervals on both sides, centreline lights along the surveyed centreline, threshold and end lights in rows at each end and touchdown zone lights in paired barrettes. The whole layout is tied to a single runway survey so geometry stays consistent.

Q9Which standards apply to runway lighting?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5340-24 and -30, IS 7785 (Indian Standard for airfield lighting equipment and installation practice) and DGCA CAR Section 4 for Indian aerodrome lighting requirements. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q10How often should runway lighting be inspected?

Typically annually for fixture damage inspection, photometric output checks, circuit intensity balance checks and inset fixture seal and drainage checks. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11What damages runway lights most often?

Jet blast, snow-clearing equipment and foreign object debris are common causes of cracked lenses, dislodged housings and missing fixtures. LED or lamp degradation can also drift colour or intensity below the required threshold, which scheduled photometric surveys are meant to catch.
