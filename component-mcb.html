Miniature Circuit Breaker (MCB)

A compact, automatic electrical switch designed to protect low‑current circuits (typically up to 125A) from damage caused by overload and short circuits. MCBs are the most common overcurrent protection devices in AGL control panels, distribution boards, and secondary circuits, offering reliable, reusable protection with standardised trip characteristics.

Overview

What Is It?

A Miniature Circuit Breaker (MCB) is a small, automatic electrical switch that provides overcurrent protection for low‑voltage circuits up to 125A. MCBs are designed for use in final sub‑circuits and are widely used in Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) systems for protecting:

  • Control circuits – powering PLCs, relays, and communication modules in ALCMS racks.
  • Lighting control panels – protecting individual lighting circuit branches.
  • Distribution boards – providing overcurrent protection for feeder cables to smaller loads.
  • CCR auxiliary circuits – protecting fans, heating elements, and internal control power supplies.

Unlike fuses, MCBs can be reset after a fault is cleared, making them more convenient and cost‑effective. They are available with different trip characteristics (B, C, D curves) to match the specific nature of the load (resistive, inductive, or motor loads).

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The MCB's purpose is to automatically remove power from a faulted circuit before an overload or short circuit can damage the cable, connected equipment, or start a fire, while being simple and quick enough to reset that it doesn't turn a minor, transient fault into an extended outage. In an AGL context, that means a fault on one control circuit, auxiliary load, or lighting branch trips only its own MCB rather than requiring the whole panel to be de-energised, which keeps the impact of any single fault as localised as possible. Its trip-curve selection (B, C, or D) exists so the breaker's sensitivity can be matched to how the specific load actually behaves — tolerating a motor's normal inrush current, for instance, without also becoming so insensitive that it fails to protect the circuit from a genuine fault.

Application

Where Is It Used?

MCBs are used extensively throughout the AGL electrical infrastructure:

  • In CCR Rooms: Protecting the internal control power supplies, cooling fans, and auxiliary circuits.
  • In ALCMS Racks: Providing overcurrent protection for PLC power supplies, network switches, and I/O modules.
  • In Lighting Control Panels: Protecting individual stop bar circuits, taxiway lighting sections, and apron lighting feeds.
  • In Distribution Boards: Protecting sub‑circuits feeding smaller loads like sign boards, wind cones, and obstruction lights.
  • In Remote Equipment Huts: Protecting power supplies and control circuits in field‑located equipment.
Placement

Installation & Location

MCBs are DIN-rail mounted inside CCR rooms, ALCMS racks, lighting control panels, distribution boards, and remote equipment huts, positioned close to the circuits they protect so the run of unprotected cable ahead of the breaker is as short as possible. Panel layout groups MCBs by function — control power, auxiliary circuits, individual lighting sections — with clear labelling so a tripped breaker can be identified and reset quickly. Spacing between adjacent breakers follows the manufacturer's thermal derating guidance, since MCBs mounted tightly together in a sealed cabinet can trip prematurely under normal load if ventilation is inadequate, and the panel location itself is chosen for easy access during fault-finding without exposing the technician to other live sections of the board.

Function

How Does It Work?

An MCB operates using a combination of thermal and magnetic trip mechanisms:

  • Thermal Overload Protection: A bimetallic strip heats up and bends when current exceeds the rated value for a sustained period. The bending triggers the trip mechanism after a time delay that is inversely proportional to the square of the current (I²t). This provides protection against moderate, prolonged overcurrents.
  • Magnetic Short‑Circuit Protection: An electromagnetic coil generates a force proportional to the current. Under high fault currents (short circuits), the magnetic force is sufficient to instantly open the contacts, providing near‑instantaneous interruption (typically < 10 ms).
  • Trip Curves: MCBs are classified by their trip characteristic:
    • B‑curve: Trips at 3‑5 times rated current – suitable for resistive loads (lighting, control circuits).
    • C‑curve: Trips at 5‑10 times rated current – suitable for inductive loads (motors, transformers).
    • D‑curve: Trips at 10‑20 times rated current – suitable for high‑inrush loads (welding, X‑ray equipment).
  • Arc Extinction: When the contacts open, an arc is formed. MCBs use arc chutes with de‑ionizing plates to extinguish the arc quickly, ensuring safe interruption.
  • Reset: After a fault is cleared, the MCB can be reset manually by pushing the handle to the ON position.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

MCBs occupy the lowest, most granular tier of the AGL protection hierarchy, typically protecting a single final circuit or piece of control equipment downstream of a larger MCCB or distribution breaker. Their trip curve (B, C, or D) is chosen specifically so that, under a fault, the MCB opens before the upstream device does — a discrimination scheme that confines an outage to the smallest branch possible rather than tripping an entire distribution board for a fault on one final circuit. An MCB has no communication or remote-control capability by design: its trip is purely thermal or magnetic, and its status is only visible at the handle itself, which is why panels with many MCBs are usually laid out with clear circuit labelling so a tripped breaker can be identified quickly on inspection rather than through any reporting system.

Construction

Physical Construction

The MCB's compact insulated housing is moulded from flame-retardant thermoset or thermoplastic material, enclosing a set of silver-alloy contacts operated by a spring-loaded toggle mechanism that snaps open or closed with a positive, tactile action. A bimetallic strip provides thermal overload tripping while an electromagnetic solenoid delivers instantaneous magnetic tripping on short-circuit currents, both acting on a shared trip bar so a fault on either path opens the contacts. A small arc chamber with steel splitter plates extinguishes the arc drawn when contacts open under load, containing it safely within the moulded case. The body clips directly onto a standard 35mm DIN rail via a spring-loaded rear catch, and shrouded, box-type terminals at top and bottom accept incoming and outgoing conductors while shielding live parts from accidental contact once installed in a distribution board.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Incorrect Trip Curve Selection

Using a B‑curve MCB on a circuit with high inrush current (e.g., transformers, motor loads) can cause nuisance tripping. Select the appropriate trip curve (C or D) based on the load type to avoid unnecessary interruptions.

Loose Terminal Connections

MCB terminals must be tightened to the correct torque. Loose connections cause heating, arcing, and eventual failure. Always use a torque screwdriver and follow the manufacturer's specifications.

Inadequate Breaking Capacity

If the available fault current exceeds the MCB's breaking capacity (Icu), the breaker may fail to interrupt safely. Always verify the fault current level and select an MCB with adequate Icu.

Daisy‑Chaining and Grouping Derating

Multiple MCBs mounted adjacent to each other on a DIN rail may require derating due to heat build‑up. Consult the manufacturer's derating tables when grouping many breakers in a panel.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Nuisance Tripping on Normal Load Switching
Likely Cause
The installed trip curve (e.g., B-curve) is too sensitive to the inrush current of the connected load, such as a transformer or motor.
Diagnostic Steps
Identify the load type and its typical inrush current profile; check the MCB's trip curve rating against the manufacturer's selection guidance for that load type.
Corrective Action
Replace with an MCB rated for the correct trip curve (typically C or D for high-inrush loads), and re-verify normal operation without nuisance tripping.
Warm or Discoloured Breaker Body or Terminals
Likely Cause
Loose terminal connections causing resistive heating, arcing, or the early stages of a connection failure.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise and inspect terminal torque against the manufacturer's specification using a calibrated torque screwdriver; check for discolouration, pitting, or arc marks at the terminals.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate to the correct torque, and replace the breaker if terminal damage (melting, pitting) is found; schedule a follow-up thermal check after re-energising.
Breaker Fails to Clear a Fault Cleanly
Likely Cause
The available fault current at the installation point exceeds the MCB's rated breaking capacity (Icu).
Diagnostic Steps
Calculate or verify the prospective fault current at the installation point; compare against the installed MCB's Icu rating on its nameplate.
Corrective Action
Replace with an MCB (or upstream device) rated for the actual fault current level, and re-verify discrimination/coordination with adjacent protective devices.
Breaker Runs Hotter Than Expected in a Panel
Likely Cause
Multiple MCBs mounted closely together on a DIN rail are generating cumulative heat that the manufacturer's derating tables account for but the installation didn't apply.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the panel layout and breaker density against the manufacturer's grouping/derating tables; measure ambient temperature inside the enclosure under normal load.
Corrective Action
Re-rate or space out breakers per the derating tables, improve enclosure ventilation, or reduce load on affected circuits to bring operating temperature back within limits.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Upstream Isolation Before Removal
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the upstream supply before removing or replacing an MCB, and verify zero energy at its terminals before disconnecting wiring, even though the breaker's own contacts are open.

Correct Rating on Replacement
Compliance

Fit a replacement MCB with the same rated current and breaking capacity as the original, verified against the protected circuit's design load and prospective fault current; an undersized breaking capacity can fail to safely interrupt a fault.

Operate, Don't Force, a Tripped Breaker
Personnel Safety

Investigate the cause of a trip before resetting an MCB; repeatedly forcing a breaker closed against a persistent fault can damage the breaker and leave the circuit inadequately protected.

Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any work downstream of an MCB, verify insulation resistance and correct load connection before closing the breaker, since a residual fault at re-energisation can cause a nuisance trip or equipment damage.

PPE for Panel Work
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working inside a distribution board or control panel, and be aware of adjacent live circuits on other breakers in the same enclosure.

FOD Control at the Panel
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and breaker blanks accounted for at all times when a panel is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, particularly in panels serving critical control or lighting circuits.

Check for Thermal Damage on Replacement
Personnel Safety

Inspect the busbar and adjacent breakers for discolouration or heat damage when replacing an MCB that tripped repeatedly or showed signs of overheating, since a hot connection can damage neighbouring equipment.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a panel with an incorrectly rated breaker, an unlabelled circuit, or a loose termination after maintenance; verify correct labelling and torque before closing out the work order.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Match the Trip Curve to the Load, Not Just the Current Rating
Selection

Two MCBs with the same amperage rating but different trip curves (B, C, D) behave very differently on inrush-heavy loads like PLC power supplies or transformer primaries; nuisance tripping on control circuits is often a curve mismatch, not an undersized breaker.

Verify Discrimination With the Upstream MCCB, Not Just Datasheet Ratings
Coordination

A downstream MCB and an upstream MCCB can both be correctly rated individually and still fail to discriminate under fault conditions, tripping the upstream device and taking out more of the distribution board than necessary; check the manufacturer's discrimination tables for the specific pairing in use.

Repeated Tripping Without an Obvious Fault Warrants a Load Study
Troubleshooting

An MCB that trips intermittently with no fault found on inspection is often responding to a marginal, cumulative load condition — several control loads switching simultaneously, for example — rather than a fault on the breaker or wiring; a brief load logging exercise usually finds it faster than repeated fault-finding.

Panel Ambient Temperature Shifts the Effective Trip Rating
Design

MCB trip characteristics are rated at a reference ambient temperature; a densely packed panel running hot in service can cause thermal-element tripping below the nameplate current. Where panels run warm, derate selection accordingly rather than assuming the nameplate figure always applies.

Exercise Breakers That Rarely Operate
Maintenance

MCBs protecting standby or rarely-used circuits can develop stiff or sluggish mechanisms from years without operation; periodically operating them manually during planned maintenance windows catches a mechanism that won't trip cleanly before it's needed in a real fault.

Record Replacement Curve and Rating, Not Just "Like for Like"
Documentation

When replacing a failed MCB, confirm and record the exact curve type and breaking capacity fitted, not just the current rating; a superficially "equivalent" spare with a different curve or lower breaking capacity can silently undermine the panel's fault coordination.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about miniature circuit breakers in AGL distribution: trip curves, ratings, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is an MCB?

A Miniature Circuit Breaker is a small automatic switch giving overcurrent protection for low-voltage circuits up to 125 A, used in AGL final sub-circuits.

Q2What does an MCB protect in an AGL system?

It automatically removes power from a faulted circuit before overload or a short circuit can damage cable or equipment or start a fire, and it resets quickly so a transient fault is not a long outage.

Q3Where are MCBs used?

In CCR rooms protecting control power supplies, cooling fans and auxiliary circuits, and in ALCMS racks protecting PLC power supplies and network switches.

Q4How does an MCB trip?

With a bimetallic thermal element for sustained overload and a magnetic element for instant short-circuit tripping.

Q5What is the difference between B, C and D curves?

B-curve trips at 3-5 times rated current (In), C-curve at 5-10 In and D-curve at 10-20 In. Choose the curve to suit the load's inrush, for example D for transformers and motors.

Q6What ratings are typical?

1-125 A rated current, 230/400 V AC, breaking capacity (Icu) of 6, 10 or 15 kA, 1- to 4-pole, on 35 mm DIN rail. Icu must exceed the prospective fault current at the point of installation.

Q7Which standards apply?

IEC 60898 (MCBs), IEC 60947-2 (some industrial-duty products are dual-rated), UL 1077 and IS/IEC 60898-1.

Q8How often should MCBs be checked?

Annually: re-torque terminals, test trip function where facilities allow, review load and breaking capacity, and inspect the body for discolouration or heat damage.

Q9Why does an MCB trip on normal switching?

The trip curve, for example B, may be too sensitive to the inrush of a transformer or motor load. Identify the load's inrush and consider a C or D curve.
