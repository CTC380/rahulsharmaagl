Transmission Tower Light

A specialized aviation obstruction light for high-voltage transmission towers and power line structures, featuring medium-intensity red lights at multiple levels to delineate tower height. Engineered to withstand extreme weather, corona discharge, and electromagnetic interference while providing reliable 24/7 obstruction marking for power grid infrastructure.

Overview

What Is It?

A Transmission Tower Light is an aviation warning light system installed on high-voltage electrical transmission towers to alert aircraft of their presence. These lattice steel structures often exceed 50–100 metres in height and are located in vast corridors that can cross flight paths. The lighting system typically uses medium‑intensity red steady‑burning or flashing lights installed at the tower's top and at intermediate levels (every 45 metres) to clearly indicate the structure's height and shape. The lights are designed to operate reliably in the presence of strong electromagnetic fields, corona discharge, and severe weather, with robust surge protection and non‑corrosive materials.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Transmission tower lighting exists because these structures are often strung across long corridors far from any airport, in locations where a pilot has no other reason to expect a tall obstacle and no surrounding urban lighting context to draw attention to it, unlike a building obstruction light situated in a lit city skyline. Marking both the tower top and intermediate levels serves the same purpose as on any tall structure — conveying the true height and shape rather than just the summit — but here it's applied to a lattice steel structure whose thin profile can otherwise be genuinely difficult to see against open sky or terrain even in daylight. The lighting's engineering emphasis on withstanding electromagnetic interference, corona discharge, and surge events exists because this is the one obstruction-light category installed directly on energized high-voltage infrastructure, where a lighting failure caused by the tower's own electrical environment would defeat the very purpose the light is there to serve.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on high‑voltage transmission towers (typically 110kV to 765kV and above) that exceed 45 metres (150 feet) AGL or are located near aerodromes or aviation corridors. Used extensively across power grids worldwide. Multiple lights are installed at the top and at intermediate levels (every 45 metres) on very tall towers to provide a clear outline. The lights must be visible from all directions, requiring omnidirectional or 360° coverage with specific beam patterns. Special attention is given to mounting hardware that does not compromise the tower's structural integrity or insulation properties.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the tower top and at intermediate levels at the specified vertical spacing, mounted on brackets that hold the light well clear of energized conductors, insulators, and hardware, with clearances set to the tower's electrical design rather than to lighting convenience alone. Mounting hardware and cabling are specified for the electromagnetic field strength and corona discharge present on energized towers, with surge protection, shielding, and bonding integrated so the lighting circuit is isolated from the tower's high-voltage system. Access for installation and maintenance follows the utility's live-line or de-energized work procedures, since these fixtures sit on active electrical infrastructure rather than a passive structure. Power is typically taken from a low-voltage supply run alongside the tower rather than tapped from the transmission conductors themselves, with cabling routed to avoid any point where lighting and high-voltage circuits could interact.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each transmission tower light is powered either from the tower's own power supply (via a dedicated distribution transformer) or from an independent solar‑powered system with battery backup. The light source is typically an LED array with a red filter, providing medium intensity (~2,000 cd) for nighttime and low‑visibility conditions. Some installations use flashing lights (20–40 fpm) for enhanced visibility. The lights are controlled by an integral photocell or a remote monitoring system. The housing is constructed from non‑corrosive materials (marine‑grade aluminium or stainless steel) with IP66/IP67 protection, and features heavy‑duty surge protection to withstand lightning strikes and switching surges. Special EMI shielding prevents interference with the tower's control systems.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Each tower light operates as a self-contained switching unit rather than a fixture on a shared circuit: the photocell integral to the fixture (or a remote monitoring interface) reads ambient light level directly at the tower and switches the LED array on at dusk and off at dawn without any external scheduling command. Where flashing operation is specified, an internal flash controller sets the 20–40 fpm rate locally at each light — there is no synchronising signal between lights on the same tower or between towers, since obstruction lighting on a static structure does not require the fleet-wide flash coordination used on wind farms or airfield circuits. Power reaches the fixture either from the tower's own distribution transformer on grid-tied installations, or from a dedicated solar panel and battery bank where an independent supply is used, with surge protection and EMI shielding isolating the light's switching circuit from the high-voltage environment of the structure it is mounted on. Fault reporting runs through the remote monitoring interface: each light, or a light group, reports operating status — on/off state, lamp current, and battery state of charge for solar units — back to a central monitoring system or the utility's SCADA network, so a failed unit at height is flagged without requiring a visual inspection climb.

Power Source → Photocell → Flash Controller (per fixture) → Protected LED Array → Remote Monitoring / SCADA

Construction

Physical Construction

The lantern head is built around a die-cast aluminium housing, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance and shielded internally to protect the driver electronics from the strong electromagnetic fields and corona discharge present near high-voltage conductors. The red LED array sits behind a moulded polycarbonate dome lens with internal prismatic ribbing to spread light evenly across the full 360° horizontal arc, gasket-sealed to the housing to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating against wind-driven rain and dust. The housing mounts to the lattice steel tower via a galvanised steel bracket or crossarm fitting, bolted directly to the tower structure and bonded to its earthing and lightning-protection system; units at intermediate levels use standardised clamp fittings so they can be positioned at the required 45-metre intervals along the tower's height. Internal driver electronics are potted or conformal-coated and isolated in a shielded compartment away from the optical chamber to further reduce susceptibility to induced currents, with cabling routed through EMI-filtered, surge-protected glands. All external hardware, brackets, and fasteners are stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised, and where the tower lacks mains supply the fixture may instead draw from a dedicated solar panel and battery assembly mounted on the structure.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Intermediate-Level Positioning
Civil

Confirm top-of-tower and intermediate-level lights are positioned at the design 45-metre intervals using standardised clamp fittings, so the tower's full height is traced for approaching aircraft.

Bracket/Crossarm Fixing
Mechanical

Bolt the galvanised steel bracket or crossarm fitting directly to the lattice steel tower structure per the fixing schedule.

Earthing & Lightning Bonding
Electrical

Bond the fixture housing to the tower's earthing and lightning-protection system, since proximity to high-voltage conductors makes this connection critical for equipment survival.

EMI-Filtered Gland Termination
Electrical

Route and terminate cabling through the EMI-filtered, surge-protected glands as designed, keeping the driver electronics compartment shielded from induced currents near the conductors.

Supply Source Confirmation
Electrical

Confirm whether the fixture draws from the tower's mains supply or a dedicated solar panel and battery assembly, and terminate that source before energisation.

Dome Seal Check
Mechanical

Confirm the gasket seal between the lens dome and housing seats correctly to achieve the rated IP66/IP67 protection before commissioning.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record fixture positions, bonding, and cable termination as-built before the tower is signed off and climbing access is closed out.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the supply cable and distribution transformer (or solar-battery circuit) before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum given the high-voltage environment.

EMI Shielding & Surge Protection Verification
Pre-Energisation

Confirm the fixture's EMI shielding and surge protection are correctly fitted and bonded before energisation, given the strong electromagnetic fields and corona discharge near energised conductors.

Photocell Threshold Calibration
Energisation

Calibrate the integral photocell's day/night switching threshold against a reference light meter so the fixture switches on at dusk and off at dawn.

Photometric Intensity Test
Final Acceptance

Measure light output at the tower top and each intermediate level with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA medium-intensity thresholds (~2,000 cd).

Intermediate-Level Coverage Verification
Final Acceptance

Confirm every intermediate-level light at the specified ~45m spacing is present and correctly outlines the tower's full lattice height.

Grid-Tied / Solar Power Path Verification
Final Acceptance

Confirm the fixture draws correctly from its distribution transformer tap-off or, where independent, from the solar panel and battery backup, with clearances maintained from energised conductors and hardware.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, shielding, threshold calibration, and photometric readings alongside as-built level positions in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corona Discharge Interference

High‑voltage fields can cause corona discharge, which may interfere with the light's electronics and create radio frequency noise. Using shielded cables and EMI‑hardened components is essential. Regular inspection of insulators and grounding is required.

Lightning and Switching Surges

Transmission towers are frequent lightning targets. Surge protection devices and proper grounding are critical. Repeated surges can damage the light's power supply and control electronics. Use of heavy‑duty surge arresters is mandatory.

Structural Vibration and Fatigue

Towers experience wind‑induced vibration and conductor galloping, which can cause mechanical fatigue in mounting brackets. Use of vibration‑damping mounts and regular bolt torque checks is recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Light Completely Dark — Grid-Tied Unit
Likely Cause
Failed feed from the tower's distribution transformer, a blown fuse or tripped breaker at the light's supply, or a failed LED array or driver.
Diagnostic Steps
Check output at the distribution transformer; test driver output and LED array continuity; inspect the fuse or breaker protecting the light's feed.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the transformer feed, replace the driver or LED array, or restore the protection device before re-energising.
Light Completely Dark — Solar-Powered Unit
Likely Cause
Depleted or failed battery bank, a faulty solar panel or charge controller, or a failed LED array.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure battery voltage and state of charge; test solar panel output and charge controller function; check LED array continuity.
Corrective Action
Replace the battery bank, repair or replace the panel or charge controller, or replace the LED array.
Light Fails to Switch On at Dusk or Off at Dawn
Likely Cause
Photocell failure, a stuck flash-controller override, or a remote-monitoring fault not being reflected at the fixture itself.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the photocell's response to shading; check flash controller override settings; verify the fixture's status against the remote monitoring interface.
Corrective Action
Replace the photocell, correct or replace the flash controller, or resolve the monitoring interface fault.
Erratic Flash Rate
Likely Cause
Internal flash controller fault, or firmware/timing drift within the fixture's own local controller.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure the actual flash rate against the specified 20–40 fpm; check the controller for a fault indication; inspect for moisture or corrosion at the controller board.
Corrective Action
Replace the flash controller and reseal the housing to prevent further moisture ingress.
Radio Frequency Noise or Electronics Malfunction Near Energised Conductors
Likely Cause
Corona discharge coupling into unshielded wiring, degraded EMI shielding, or a compromised grounding/bonding connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect shielded cable runs and EMI gaskets for damage; check insulator condition; measure grounding/bonding resistance.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace damaged shielding and cable runs, restore grounding/bonding, and replace affected electronics if corona-induced damage is confirmed.
Repeated Surge-Related Failures
Likely Cause
Degraded surge arrester or inadequate grounding allowing lightning or switching surges to reach the light's electronics.
Diagnostic Steps
Test surge arrester function; measure grounding system resistance; review fault history via SCADA or remote monitoring for surge-correlated events.
Corrective Action
Replace the surge arrester, improve grounding, and confirm the surge protection rating matches the tower's exposure.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Distribution Transformer Feed Failure (Grid-Tied)
High Severity
Root Cause
A failed feed from the tower's distribution transformer, a blown fuse or tripped breaker, or a failed LED array or driver.
Effect
Light goes completely dark on grid-tied units, removing the obstruction warning near energised conductors.
Detection Method
Output checks at the distribution transformer, driver-output and LED-array continuity testing, and fuse/breaker inspection.
Battery Bank / Charge Controller Failure (Solar)
High Severity
Root Cause
A depleted or failed battery bank, a faulty solar panel or charge controller, or a failed LED array on solar-powered units.
Effect
Light goes completely dark on solar-powered units, particularly after extended low-sun periods.
Detection Method
Battery voltage and state-of-charge measurement, solar panel and charge-controller output testing, and LED array continuity checks.
Corona-Induced Electronics Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Corona discharge coupling into unshielded wiring, degraded EMI shielding, or a compromised grounding/bonding connection near energised conductors.
Effect
Radio-frequency noise or electronics malfunction, potentially accelerating degradation of nearby components.
Detection Method
Inspection of shielded cable runs and EMI gaskets, insulator condition checks, and grounding/bonding resistance measurement.
Surge Arrester Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
A degraded surge arrester or inadequate grounding allowing lightning or switching surges to reach the light's electronics.
Effect
Repeated surge-related failures, often correlated with storm activity or switching events on the transmission line.
Detection Method
Surge arrester function testing, grounding-system resistance measurement, and SCADA fault-history review for surge-correlated events.
Flash Controller Timing Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Internal flash controller fault, or firmware/timing drift within the fixture's own local controller.
Effect
Erratic flash rate that falls outside the specified 20-40 fpm range.
Detection Method
Flash-rate measurement against the specified range, controller fault-indication checks, and inspection for moisture or corrosion at the controller board.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

High-Voltage Line Permit
Electrical Safety

Never climb or work on a transmission tower without the utility's own permit-to-work and confirmation that the adjacent conductors are de-energised or that safe clearance distances are being maintained.

Induced Voltage / EMF
Electrical Safety

Be aware that even with the light circuit isolated, nearby energised conductors can induce voltage into tower steelwork and metallic fixtures; use appropriate grounding and induced-voltage precautions per the utility's procedure.

Lattice Tower Climbing
Fall Protection

Only climb with current tower-climbing certification and fall-arrest equipment rated for lattice steel structures.

Fixture Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the obstruction-light supply at its dedicated source and verify zero energy before opening the housing, independent of the high-voltage isolation above.

Corona Discharge & Weather
Operational

Avoid tower work in wet or humid conditions that increase corona discharge activity, and never climb during an active electrical storm.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated, non-conductive tools and full fall-protection and grounding PPE as specified by the utility's electrical safety procedure.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify intensity and colour at each mounted level before closing out the work and confirming with the utility that the tower is returned to normal operation.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Corona Discharge Is a Real Interference Source Unique to This Fixture
Electrical

A light mounted on a live high-voltage transmission tower sits in a corona and EMI environment that no other obstruction light on the airfield inventory experiences, and unshielded electronics can suffer nuisance faults or accelerated degradation that would never show up on a chimney or building light. Verify EMI shielding integrity specifically for this fixture rather than assuming standard obstruction-light hardening is sufficient.

Access Requires the Utility's Isolation Procedure, Not Just the Lighting Lockout
Safety

De-energising the light's own supply does nothing to make the tower itself safe to work on or near; access planning has to go through the transmission owner's live-line and isolation procedures first, treating the lighting maintenance as secondary to the electrical safety of the structure it's mounted on.

Confirm the Actual Supply Source Before Troubleshooting
Diagnostics

Transmission tower lights are sometimes fed from a tap on the line itself rather than a separate low-voltage feed, and misidentifying which supply arrangement is actually in place leads to the wrong troubleshooting path entirely. Confirm the supply source configuration for the specific tower before assuming a standard mains-fed fault sequence applies.

Earthing Bond Condition Has Two Different Threats to Guard Against
Reliability

The earthing and lightning bond on a transmission tower fixture has to cope with both direct lightning strikes to the structure and induced transients from the power line itself, a dual stress that a standalone mast or building fixture's bonding never sees. Inspect and test bonding continuity on a schedule that reflects this heavier duty rather than a generic obstruction-light interval.

Conductor Galloping Puts Unusual Loads on Nearby Fixtures
Mechanical

Ice- or wind-induced conductor galloping on the line near the tower can transmit sympathetic vibration into crossarm-mounted lights that a fixture on a purely static structure wouldn't experience. Check vibration-damping mount condition with this specific loading mechanism in mind, particularly at sites with a history of galloping events.

Grid-Tied and Solar-Backed Circuits Need Different Verification Steps
Testing

Where the light draws from the grid it marks rather than an independent solar or battery source, backup power verification has to account for the fact that a fault on the very grid the tower carries could take out both the primary and the "backup" simultaneously. Confirm the backup path is genuinely independent of the tower's own circuit before signing off power redundancy at commissioning.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about transmission tower lights: when they are required, colour, spacing, EMI and lightning protection, power options, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a transmission tower light?

A transmission tower light is an aviation warning light installed on high-voltage transmission towers so aircraft can see them. It is typically a medium-intensity red LED light, steady or flashing, designed to work near strong electromagnetic fields, corona discharge and severe weather.

Q2Which transmission towers need obstruction lights?

Towers, typically 110 kV to 765 kV and above, that exceed about 45 m (150 ft) above ground level, or that are near aerodromes or aviation corridors. Towers often cross long corridors far from any airport, where pilots have no other cue to expect a tall obstacle.

Q3What colour and intensity are transmission tower lights?

Aviation red at about 2,000 cd for night, with the standards range being 2,000–20,000 cd depending on day or night mode. The light may be steady or flash at 20–40 flashes per minute for extra visibility.

Q4How many lights does a transmission tower need?

Lights go at the tower top and, on very tall towers, at intermediate levels typically every 45 m so the structure's height and shape are clear. Each light gives 360° coverage and is mounted well clear of energised conductors, insulators and hardware.

Q5How are transmission tower lights protected from corona and EMI?

Electronics are shielded and cable is EMI and corona-shielded, routed and bonded to avoid interaction with the high-voltage conductors. Housings are non-corrosive, and the fixture is bonded to the tower's earthing and lightning protection system, coordinated to avoid a ground loop with the HV structure.

Q6How are transmission tower lights powered?

Either from the tower's own supply through a dedicated distribution transformer, typically 230 V AC, or from an independent solar panel with battery backup where mains is unavailable. A photocell or timer switches the light dusk to dawn.

Q7Which standards apply to transmission tower lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 70/7460-1, FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment) and IEC 62305 for lightning protection and bonding. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q8How often should transmission tower lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for lens and housing cleaning, and annual for grounding and surge arrester checks, insulator and shielding inspection, mounting bolt torque, vibration damping mount checks and photometric intensity. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9Why does wind and conductor movement matter for tower lights?

Wind-induced vibration and conductor galloping can fatigue mounting hardware over time. Annual bolt re-torque checks and inspection of vibration-damping mounts keep the fixture secure.

Q10Why is a transmission tower light completely dark?

On a grid-tied unit, likely causes are a failed feed from the tower's distribution transformer, a blown fuse or tripped breaker, or a failed LED array or driver. On a solar unit, look for a depleted or failed battery bank, a faulty solar panel or charge controller, or a failed LED array.
