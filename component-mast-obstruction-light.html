Mast Obstruction Light

A dedicated aviation warning light for communication masts, utility poles, and tall towers, typically medium or high intensity with red or white output. Engineered to withstand extreme wind loads, ice, and lightning strikes while providing reliable 24/7 obstruction marking for structures often exceeding 150 metres.

Overview

What Is It?

A Mast Obstruction Light is an aviation warning light installed on communication masts (radio, TV, microwave), utility towers, and similar tall lattice structures. These lights are critical for collision avoidance, especially for structures exceeding 150 metres (500 feet) AGL. They typically feature medium‑intensity red (steady or flashing) lights for nighttime and low‑visibility conditions, and high‑intensity white flashing lights for daytime use on taller masts. The lights are mounted at the top and, on taller structures, at intermediate levels set per height-based spacing tables (often cited as roughly every 45 metres, though the exact interval depends on total structure height) to clearly delineate the structure's height and shape. They are designed to withstand extreme weather, ice loading, and lightning strikes, with robust mounting and electrical protection.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Mast lighting exists to protect against collision with structures that are frequently among the tallest fixed obstacles in a region — communication and broadcast masts often exceed 150 metres — while presenting an extremely thin, hard-to-see lattice profile against open sky. The dual-mode approach of medium-intensity red at night and high-intensity white flashing by day serves distinct purposes for each condition: red at night provides sufficient warning without excessive glare or light pollution, while high-intensity white is needed during daytime because a red light of ordinary intensity is easily lost against a bright sky background. Marking multiple levels along the mast's height, not just the top, exists because tall masts are frequently guyed structures with a slender profile for their full height, so a pilot needs the intermediate lights to correctly judge where the structure — and any supporting guy wires — actually extends, not just where it ends.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on communication masts (broadcast, cellular, microwave), utility transmission towers, meteorological masts, and any tall lattice structure that requires obstruction lighting under the applicable national aviation authority's rules — in the US, generally any structure exceeding 61 m (200 ft) AGL, or lower where FAA determination requires it near flight paths. 45 m (150 ft) AGL is a separate, lower threshold: above it, the FAA specifies medium‑intensity flashing red (L‑864) rather than low‑intensity steady‑burning (L‑810) lighting. Used extensively in telecommunications, broadcasting, and energy sectors. Multiple lights are installed at the top and, on taller structures, at intermediate levels set per the applicable height-based spacing table (commonly cited as roughly every 45 m, though exact spacing depends on total structure height) to provide a clear outline. The lights must be visible from all directions, requiring omnidirectional or 360° coverage with specific beam patterns.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed at the mast top and at intermediate levels at the specified vertical spacing, mounted on brackets that clear the mast's own lattice members, guy wire attachment points, and any antennas so the light is visible from all directions without being shadowed by the structure it's marking. Mounting hardware is specified for the ice loading, wind-induced vibration, and lightning exposure typical of tall guyed structures, with bonding and grounding integrated into the mast's own lightning protection system. Access for installation and maintenance is typically via the mast's climbing facility or a maintenance hoist, since intermediate lights on a very tall mast are not otherwise reachable. Power and control cabling is run down the mast in a protected conduit or cable tray to a base-mounted controller, with each level often individually monitored given the difficulty of visually inspecting lights partway up a tall structure.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each mast obstruction light is powered from a dedicated supply—either direct AC (230V) or via a low‑voltage transformer. The light source is typically a high‑intensity LED array or a xenon flashtube, with red or white output. For masts over 150m, the system often combines high‑intensity white flashing (day) and medium‑intensity red flashing or steady (night) using a photocell to switch modes. The lights are controlled by an integral controller or a remote monitoring system. The housing is constructed from lightweight yet strong materials (aluminium alloy, stainless steel) with IP66/IP67 protection, anti‑icing features, and surge protection to withstand lightning strikes.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

A mast's lighting is coordinated by a single integral controller (or remote monitoring system) that reads one photocell and applies the resulting day/night mode across every light level on the structure at once — so the top-of-mast high-intensity fixture and each ~45m intermediate red light switch mode together, even though they present at different intensities. Each level is typically wired back to the same controller and power distribution point rather than looping current from one fixture to the next, so a failure at one intermediate level doesn't take out the lights above or below it on the structure. The same controller also manages the surge protection and anti-icing power drawn by the housings, since a lightning strike or ice buildup is a structure-wide risk rather than a single-fixture one. Where a remote monitoring link is present, faults at any level are reported upstream for maintenance visibility, but — consistent with the other independently-powered obstruction lights on this site — the local day/night switching logic keeps running even if that reporting connection drops.

Structure Outline

Lighting the Full Height of the Mast

Top of Mast

High-intensity white flash by day, red steady/flash by night — the primary warning point, switched by photocell.

Intermediate Level (~45m below)

Medium-intensity red light outlines the mast's mid-height to approaching aircraft.

Intermediate Level (~90m below)

Repeats every ~45m up the structure, so very tall masts carry several intermediate lights.

Unlike a single-point warning light, the mast's job is to trace the whole shape of a tall lattice structure for an approaching aircraft — each light gives 360° omnidirectional coverage, and the housings add ice-resistant and lightning-protected construction the shorter fixtures on this site don't need.

Construction

Physical Construction

The lantern head is built around a die-cast or extruded aluminium housing, anodised or powder-coated to resist UV degradation, salt spray, and industrial pollutants encountered on mast and tower sites. The LED array or xenon flashtube sits behind a moulded polycarbonate or acrylic dome lens, engineered with internal prismatic ribbing to distribute light evenly across the full 360° horizontal arc; the lens-to-housing joint is gasket-sealed to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating, and many units include an internal heater element or hydrophobic coating to shed ice and prevent lens fogging. The housing mounts to the mast structure via a galvanised steel bracket or side-arm, bolted directly to the lattice or pole face and rated to withstand the wind loading and vibration typical of tall, slender structures; units at intermediate levels use standardised clamp fittings so they can be positioned at the required 45-metre intervals without site-specific fabrication. Internal driver and control electronics are potted or conformal-coated and housed in a separate compartment isolated from the optical chamber, with surge arrestors and lightning protection bonded to the structure's earthing system, and all external hardware is stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised to prevent corrosion over the structure's service life.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Intermediate-Level Positioning
Civil

Confirm top-of-mast and intermediate-level lights are positioned at the design 45-metre intervals using standardised clamp fittings, so the full lattice structure is traced for approaching aircraft.

Bracket/Side-Arm Fixing
Mechanical

Bolt the galvanised steel bracket or side-arm directly to the lattice or pole face per the fixing schedule, rated for the wind loading and vibration typical of tall, slender structures.

Lightning Protection Bonding
Electrical

Bond surge arrestors and the lantern housing to the structure's earthing system, since mast-top locations are highly exposed to lightning strikes.

Supply Termination
Electrical

Terminate the 230V AC or 24V DC supply at each lantern, confirming the converter (where fitted) is correctly rated before energisation.

Lens-to-Housing Seal Check
Mechanical

Confirm the gasket at the lens-to-housing joint seats correctly to achieve the rated IP66/IP67 protection, and check the heater element or hydrophobic coating is functional for ice resistance.

Day/Night Mode Configuration
Configuration

Configure the photocell or control logic so the fixture switches correctly between the high-intensity white day flash and the reduced-intensity red night mode.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record fixture positions, bracket fixing, bonding, and cable termination as-built before the mast is signed off and climbing access is closed out.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the supply cable and transformer (where fitted) at each mounting level before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Grounding & Surge Arrester Continuity Check
Pre-Energisation

Verify the fixture's grounding connection and any surge arrester are correctly bonded to the mast's lightning protection system before energisation.

Photocell Threshold Calibration
Energisation

Calibrate the day/night switching threshold against a reference light meter so the top-of-mast and every intermediate-level light switch mode together.

High-Intensity / Medium-Intensity Mode Switching Verification

For masts over 150m, confirm the top-of-mast fixture correctly switches between high-intensity white flashing (day) and red flashing or steady-burning (night).

Multi-Level Flash Synchronisation Test
Energisation

With every level energised, confirm the top and each intermediate-level light switch mode and flash together, tracing the mast's full height as one coherent signal.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lightning Damage

Masts are prime lightning targets. Surge protection and proper grounding are critical. Repeated lightning strikes can damage the light's electronics and power supply. Use of heavy‑duty surge arresters and regular inspection of grounding systems is mandatory.

Ice and Wind Loading

Ice accumulation on the light housing and mounting brackets can cause mechanical failure. Anti‑icing coatings and robust mounting hardware are essential. Regular visual inspection and removal of ice buildup (if safe) is recommended.

Vibration Fatigue

Masts experience constant wind‑induced vibration, which can cause solder joint fatigue and connection loosening. Use of vibration‑resistant mounting and lock‑wired connections can extend service life.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Level Dark, Rest of Mast Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module or flashtube at that level, a blown fuse on the feed from the controller's power distribution point, or a severed conductor between the distribution point and that level.
Diagnostic Steps
Check supply voltage at the affected fixture; test controller output to that level in isolation; inspect the cable run between the distribution point and the level for damage.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED module or flashtube, repair the damaged cable run, or restore the blown fuse before re-energising that level.
Entire Mast Lighting System Dark
Likely Cause
Failed integral controller, an upstream supply outage, or a blown main fuse or tripped breaker feeding the controller.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming mains or transformer supply at the controller; test controller output to all levels; inspect the main protection device for a blown fuse or tripped breaker.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the controller, restore the incoming supply, or reset/replace the main fuse or breaker.
Lights Stuck in Wrong Day/Night Mode
Likely Cause
Photocell failure, ice or debris obscuring the sensor, or a mode-switching fault in the integral controller.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the photocell's response by shading and exposing it; inspect and clean the sensor window; check the controller's mode-override or threshold settings.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the photocell, correct the controller's threshold settings, or replace the controller if the fault persists.
Repeated Lightning-Related Failures
Likely Cause
Degraded surge arrester, a poor grounding/earth bond, or lapsed lightning-protection maintenance allowing surge energy to reach the electronics.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the surge arrester's continuity and function; measure earth resistance at the mast's grounding system; inspect all bonding connections for corrosion or looseness.
Corrective Action
Replace the surge arrester, repair or improve the grounding system, and retorque or renew bonding connections.
Ice Buildup Blocking Output or Anti-Icing Failure
Likely Cause
Ice accumulation on the housing or dome obstructing light output, or a failed anti-icing heater element.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect for ice buildup on the housing and dome; test the anti-icing heater element for continuity and power draw; check photometric output under icing conditions if safe to do so.
Corrective Action
Safely clear ice from the housing, repair or replace the anti-icing heater element, and confirm the heater is energising correctly on the next cold cycle.
Intermittent Flicker or Connection Loss
Likely Cause
Vibration-induced solder joint fatigue or a loosened terminal connection at the fixture or its mounting bracket.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect solder joints and terminal connections for cracking or looseness; check for corrosion at connectors; monitor the connection under simulated vibration if practical.
Corrective Action
Re-solder or re-terminate affected connections, fit vibration-resistant mounting hardware, and lock-wire exposed connectors.

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Single-Level Feed Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Failed LED module or flashtube at that level, a blown fuse on the feed from the controller's distribution point, or a severed conductor to that level.
Effect
One level of the mast lighting goes dark while the rest of the mast continues operating normally.
Detection Method
Supply-voltage checks at the affected fixture, isolated controller-output testing to that level, and cable-run inspection.
Integral Controller Failure
High Severity

Failed integral controller, an upstream supply outage, or a blown main fuse or tripped breaker feeding the controller.

Detection Method
Incoming supply checks at the controller, output testing to all levels, and main protection-device inspection.
Surge Arrester / Grounding Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Degraded surge arrester, a poor grounding/earth bond, or lapsed lightning-protection maintenance.
Effect
Repeated lightning-related failures across the mast's electronics after storm activity.
Detection Method
Surge arrester continuity/function testing, earth-resistance measurement, and bonding-connection inspection.
Ice Accumulation / Anti-Icing Heater Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ice accumulation on the housing or dome obstructing light output, or a failed anti-icing heater element.

Blocked or attenuated light output during icing conditions, reducing visibility of the obstruction warning.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for ice buildup, and anti-icing heater continuity and power-draw testing.

Low Severity

Vibration-induced solder-joint fatigue or a loosened terminal connection at the fixture or mounting bracket.
Effect

Detection Method
Solder-joint and terminal-connection inspection, and connector corrosion checks.

Fall Protection

Only climb with current tower-climbing certification and fall-arrest equipment rated for lattice-mast structures; follow the mast owner's permit-to-work before ascending.

Lightning & Grounding
Electrical Safety

Communication and utility masts are frequent lightning targets; confirm grounding/bonding is intact before work and never climb during an active storm.

Co-Located RF / EMF Exposure
Personnel Safety

Check for co-located radio, TV, or microwave antennas before climbing; RF exposure near active transmitters may require power-down coordination with the antenna owner or use of RF-monitoring PPE.

Electrical Safety

Lock out the fixture supply at the base station and verify zero energy before opening the housing at height.

Ice-Fall Hazard
Operational

Barricade the ground area below during winter maintenance; accumulated ice shedding from the mast or fixture housing is a falling-object hazard to personnel and equipment at the base.

PPE

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Ice Loading Changes the Mounting Hardware Failure Mode
Mechanical

On lattice masts in icing climates, accumulated ice adds both static load and impact loading as it sheds unevenly, which can loosen or fatigue mounting hardware faster than the vibration alone would predict. Weight the mounting hardware inspection interval toward the site's actual icing history rather than a generic vibration-based schedule.

Grounding Continuity Degrades Silently Between Strikes
Electrical

A communications or utility mast is a natural lightning attachment point, and a grounding or surge-arrester connection can lose continuity from corrosion or a loosened bond without any visible sign until the next strike passes fault current through equipment it should have bypassed. Test grounding and surge-arrester continuity with a meter on schedule, not just visually.

Multi-Level Synchronisation Faults Are Easy to Miss From the Base
Diagnostics

With lights at the top and multiple intermediate levels needing synchronised flashing, a single level drifting out of sync can be very hard to spot by eye from ground level, especially in daylight. Verify multi-level flash synchronisation with test equipment rather than relying on a visual check, particularly on masts too tall to observe all levels clearly at once.

Day/Night Mode Switching Failure Has Two Very Different Consequences
Photocell

A mast light stuck in high-intensity white daytime mode after dark is disorienting to nearby air traffic and to anyone in the vicinity of the mast at night, while one stuck in medium-intensity night mode during the day under-marks the structure; treat the two failure directions as equally urgent rather than assuming one is more tolerable.

Co-Located RF Equipment Complicates Access Planning
Coordination

Communication and broadcast masts carry live RF equipment that requires its own isolation or exclusion-zone procedure before lighting personnel climb the structure, distinct from the electrical lockout of the lighting circuit itself. Coordinate with the mast's RF operator on isolation before any climb, not just with the lighting system's own safety procedure.

Side-Arm Fixings See More Load Reversal Than Face-Mounted Fixtures
Design

A light mounted on a side-arm bracket standing off from the lattice face experiences a different, often larger, moment load under wind reversal than a directly face-mounted fixture, which is worth accounting for when specifying bracket fixing hardware rather than treating all mounting positions on a lattice mast as equivalent.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about mast obstruction lights: when they are required, day and night modes, spacing, lightning protection, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a mast obstruction light?

A mast obstruction light is an aviation warning light fitted to communication masts (radio, TV, microwave), utility towers and similar tall lattice structures. It typically uses medium-intensity red at night and, on taller masts, high-intensity white flashing by day.

Q2Which masts need obstruction lights?

Requirements are set by each national aviation authority. In the US, generally any structure over 61 m (200 ft) above ground level needs lighting, or lower where an FAA determination requires it near flight paths. Above 45 m, the FAA specifies medium-intensity flashing red rather than low-intensity steady-burning red. Always check your local authority's rules.

Q3What colour and intensity are mast obstruction lights?

Night lighting is red at about 2,000 cd, with the range spanning 32–2,000 cd depending on mast height and level. Day lighting on taller masts is white flashing, up to 200,000 cd for high-intensity units, or 2,000–20,000 cd where medium-intensity day marking is used.

Q4How many lights does a mast need and how are they spaced?

Lights are installed at the top and, on taller masts, at intermediate levels set by height-based spacing tables, often cited as roughly every 45 m though the exact interval depends on total structure height. Each light must be mounted on brackets that clear lattice members, guy wire attachments and antennas so it is visible from all directions.

Q5Do mast obstruction lights flash in sync?

Where flashing units are fitted, the flash rate is 20–40 flashes per minute, synchronised between mast levels through synchronised control wiring. A loss-of-sync alarm flags units that fall out of step.

Q6How are mast obstruction lights protected from lightning?

Masts are prime lightning targets, so each mounting level has the fixture bonded to the mast's earth and lightning protection system, with surge protective devices at every level and lightning-protected cable. Grounding continuity and surge arresters should be tested regularly.

Q7How does ice and wind affect mast obstruction lights?

Ice can load the housing and brackets and obscure the beam, and constant sway causes vibration fatigue and cable chafing. Lights are specified as ice-resistant with anti-icing coatings and robust mounting hardware, and are inspected seasonally for ice and annually for vibration damage.

Q8Which standards apply to mast obstruction lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 70/7460-1, FAA AC 150/5345-43 (obstruction lighting equipment) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED equipment. Always verify the current edition and your national authority's requirements.

Q9How often should mast obstruction lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are seasonal for ice and coating inspection, semi-annual for lens and housing cleaning, and annual for grounding and surge arrester checks, mounting hardware and vibration checks, photometric intensity checks and power supply and cable inspection. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is one level of my mast dark while the rest works?

Typical causes are a failed LED module or flashtube at that level, a blown fuse on its feed, or a severed conductor between the distribution point and that level. Check supply voltage at the fixture, test the controller output to that level and inspect the cable run for damage.
