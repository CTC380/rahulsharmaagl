CCR Main Contactor

A heavy-duty switching device that connects and disconnects the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) from the AC mains supply. Provides remote start/stop, protection coordination with the upstream circuit breaker, and a safe means of isolating the CCR for maintenance. The CCR's series-circuit output is isolated from the mains by its internal isolation transformer and by ramping output current to zero before shutdown — not by a second load-breaking contactor on the high-voltage series side.

Overview

What Is It?

A CCR Main Contactor is an electrically operated switching device that connects the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) to the AC mains supply. It is the single largest power-switching component in the CCR's input compartment, and is the component referred to as the "Main Contactor" in manufacturer documentation for units such as the ATG Micro 100+/200 and Siemens/ADB L-828 series regulators.

The contactor is typically controlled by the ALCMS, a PLC, or a local control panel, enabling remote start/stop of the CCR and automatic isolation during fault conditions. It provides overcurrent and short‑circuit protection coordination together with an upstream circuit breaker. Because the CCR feeds the series lighting circuit through an internal isolation transformer, opening the Main Contactor removes excitation from that transformer and de‑energises the output — a separate contactor switching the high‑voltage series circuit itself is not standard practice, since interrupting a live constant‑current loop under load can generate dangerous transient voltages. CCRs instead ramp output current down to zero before the Main Contactor opens in a normal shutdown. The contactor is designed for frequent operation and must withstand the high inrush currents and transient voltages characteristic of AGL power systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The Main Contactor exists to let a CCR be started, stopped, and isolated remotely and automatically, rather than requiring a technician to physically operate a switch at the equipment shelter every time the regulator needs to be energised or taken offline. It sits between the mains supply and the CCR's input terminals, coordinating with the upstream circuit breaker for overcurrent protection while giving the ALCMS a single, reliable point of remote control over the whole regulator. Because ALCMS-driven remote control means this contactor may cycle far more often than a manually operated switch would, its rating for frequent operation and high inrush tolerance is central to the component's purpose, not incidental to it.

Switching or isolating an energised series lighting circuit is a separate function from anything the Main Contactor does, and is handled by purpose-built circuit selector switchgear that only operates once the CCR's output has already been ramped to zero — never by a general-purpose load-breaking contactor. See the related Circuit Selector Cabinet component for that function.

Application

Where Is It Used?

CCR Main Contactors are installed in airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters, and AGL power centres. They are used in every AGL installation that requires remote or automated control of CCRs, including:

  • Runway Lighting Systems: Controlling CCRs that power runway edge, centreline, threshold, and touchdown zone lighting circuits.
  • Taxiway Lighting Systems: Managing CCRs for taxiway edge, centreline, and stop bar circuits.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Controlling CCRs for approach lighting configurations (ALSF-1, ALSF-2, MALSR, etc.).
  • Integration with ALCMS: Allowing remote start/stop and automatic fault isolation from the central control system.
  • Redundant CCR Schemes: Allowing the ALCMS to start the standby CCR's Main Contactor and bring it to full ramp before the primary CCR is ramped down and its own Main Contactor opened, supporting a controlled changeover rather than a live circuit break.
  • Maintenance Isolation: Providing a visible break and a safe means of de-energising the CCR for servicing, without interrupting the entire lighting system.
Placement

Installation & Location

The Main Contactor is installed within the CCR's input compartment or immediately adjacent to the CCR cabinet in the equipment shelter, wired between the mains supply (and its upstream circuit breaker) and the CCR's input terminals. Mounting within the same enclosure as the CCR keeps the high-current wiring run short, reducing both voltage drop and the length of exposed conductor carrying the CCR's input current. Control wiring from the ALCMS, PLC, or local control panel is run to the contactor coil separately from the power wiring, kept clearly labelled given that a CCR bank in a larger equipment room may have several Main Contactors operating independently. Because this contactor is expected to cycle far more frequently under remote control than a manually switched device would, its mounting position allows the clearance and ventilation the manufacturer specifies for contactors rated for frequent operation, rather than the tighter spacing that might suit an infrequently switched device.

Function

How Does It Work?

The CCR Main Contactor is a single electromagnetic contactor with a coil that, when energised, closes the main contacts to connect the CCR to the mains supply. De‑energising the coil opens the contacts, fully isolating the CCR.

Key functional aspects include:

  • Closing: Connected between the mains supply (typically 400V AC, 3‑phase) and the CCR's input terminals, and commanded by the ALCMS, a PLC, or a local start/stop signal. When closed, mains power reaches the CCR, which then runs its own soft-start sequence to bring the series circuit up to the commanded brightness step.
  • Normal opening: Before the contactor is commanded open in a routine shutdown, the CCR ramps its output current down to zero. Once at zero, opening the Main Contactor removes mains power with no arc or transient on the high-voltage side, since the isolation transformer's secondary is already unexcited.
  • Fault opening: On detection of an internal fault (overcurrent, earth fault), the CCR's protection circuitry can command the contactor open immediately, independent of the ramp sequence — this is the one situation where the contactor may interrupt real current, and it is coordinated with the upstream breaker's trip characteristics.
  • Auxiliary Contacts: The contactor provides auxiliary contacts (normally open and normally closed) for status feedback to the ALCMS or control system, indicating whether it is closed or open.
  • Arc Suppression: The contactor is fitted with arc chutes to safely quench the arc that can form when interrupting mains current during a fault trip.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The Main Contactor is the physical enable point beneath every layer of control logic described elsewhere in the AGL system — nothing the ALCMS or the CCR's own regulation can do matters if this contactor isn't closed. Command authority for closing and routine opening sits with the ALCMS (or a local start/stop station), coordinated with the CCR's soft-start and ramp-down sequencing so the contactor is never asked to make or break current under load in normal operation. Opening on fault, however, is not purely a command response: it is driven directly by the CCR's own protection circuitry, so an overcurrent or earth fault trips the contactor immediately regardless of what the ALCMS has commanded, faster than a control-room decision loop could react. This is a deliberate division of authority — the ALCMS decides when the circuit should normally be live, while the CCR's own protection decides, independent of any external command, when it must not be. Auxiliary contact feedback closes the loop by reporting actual contactor position back to the ALCMS, so the control system's picture of "circuit energised" is always confirmed by hardware state rather than assumed from the last command sent.

Construction

Physical Construction

The contactor is built with silver-alloy or silver-tungsten main contacts sized for the three-phase mains current feeding the CCR, and is engineered for the mechanical and electrical life needed to withstand frequent remote-controlled cycling rather than the occasional manual switching a simpler installation might see. It is typically mounted within the CCR's own input compartment on a DIN rail or subpanel, positioned to keep the conductors between contactor and CCR as short as practical, and spaced according to the manufacturer's clearance and ventilation requirements so heat from frequent switching doesn't shorten contact life. Indoor cabinet installations use an IP20 enclosure relying on the CCR shelter's environmental control, while contactors in unconditioned or outdoor enclosures step up to an IP54-rated weatherproof housing. Control coil wiring is terminated separately from the power connections at a dedicated low-voltage terminal block, and auxiliary contacts used for remote status feedback are wired through their own cable entry to keep control signals isolated from the switched power conductors.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Contact Pitting and Welding

Repeated fault-trip interruptions under load cause contact erosion and pitting over time. In severe cases, contacts may weld closed, preventing the CCR from disconnecting from the mains. Regular inspection and replacement of contact tips are essential.

Coil Burnout

Contactor coils can burn out due to voltage spikes, prolonged energisation, or manufacturing defects. Symptoms include failure to close or drop out. Coil replacement is typically required. Surge suppression (e.g., varistors) across coils is recommended.

Auxiliary Contact Failure

Auxiliary contacts used for status feedback can fail due to mechanical wear or oxidation, leading to incorrect indication in the ALCMS. Regular testing and cleaning of auxiliary contacts are recommended.

Ramp/Interlock Sequencing Fault

A fault in the coordination between the CCR's ramp-down logic and the contactor control circuit could in principle allow the contactor to be commanded open before output current has reached zero. Periodic functional testing of the sequencing is important for contact longevity and safety.

Arc Chute Damage

The arc chutes can become degraded over time due to repeated fault-trip arcing, reducing their ability to quench arcs. Damaged arc chutes can lead to contact welding or a fire hazard. Visual inspection and replacement of arc chutes when worn are essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Contactor Will Not Open / Series Circuit Stays Energised
Likely Cause
Contact welding from repeated switching under load, or arc chute degradation preventing clean interruption.
Diagnostic Steps
Attempt to de-energise and visually/mechanically check whether the contacts have physically separated; inspect contact tips and arc chutes for pitting, welding, or erosion.
Corrective Action
Replace welded contact tips and degraded arc chutes; do not attempt to force a welded contactor open under load — isolate upstream first.
Contactor Fails to Energise or Randomly Drops Out
Likely Cause
Coil burnout from voltage spikes, prolonged energisation, or a manufacturing defect.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure coil resistance/continuity against the manufacturer's specification; check for surge suppression (varistor) presence across the coil; review recent voltage spike history if logged.
Corrective Action
Replace the burnt-out coil and fit or verify surge suppression across the coil to reduce recurrence.
ALCMS Shows Incorrect Contactor Status
Likely Cause
Auxiliary contact failure from mechanical wear or oxidation, giving false status feedback.
Diagnostic Steps
Test auxiliary contacts directly against the actual physical position of the main contacts; inspect for oxidation or wear on the auxiliary contact surfaces.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the auxiliary contacts and re-verify that status feedback matches actual contactor position.
Contactor Opens Before Output Current Reaches Zero
Likely Cause
Faulty coordination between the CCR's ramp-down logic and the contactor control circuit, or a control-panel command that bypasses the normal shutdown sequence.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a functional test of the shutdown sequence under controlled conditions; review the ALCMS/PLC logic and wiring for how the stop command is issued to the CCR and contactor.
Corrective Action
Correct the control sequencing so the contactor only opens after output current is confirmed at zero, and re-test before returning the unit to service; treat any sequencing fault as a safety-critical repair.
Visible Arcing, Charring, or Contact Overheating
Likely Cause
Degraded arc chutes no longer quenching the arc effectively during interruption.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect arc chutes for erosion, charring, or cracking; check contact overheating with thermal imaging under load.
Corrective Action
Replace worn arc chutes promptly — this is both a reliability and fire-safety issue — and re-inspect after a set number of operating cycles going forward.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Before Internal Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the AC mains supply before working on the contactor, and verify zero energy at the terminals. Remember the CCR's own output terminals can still carry high voltage from adjacent circuits or induced EMF even with the Main Contactor open — treat the HT compartment as live until independently verified.

Verify Mechanical Separation
Electrical Safety

Physically confirm the contactor's contacts have separated after de-energising rather than trusting status feedback alone, since welded or pitted contacts can leave the CCR connected to mains while appearing open.

Never Break the Series Circuit Under Load
Electrical Safety

Never attempt to isolate a live series lighting circuit by any means other than letting the CCR ramp its output current to zero first; opening any switch on an energised constant-current loop can produce a sustained, high-voltage arc.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any contactor or wiring work, megger test the series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the circuit.

Confirm Surge Suppression Present
Verification

Check that coil surge suppression (varistor or diode) is fitted and intact when replacing a contactor, since voltage spikes without suppression are a recurring cause of coil burnout.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the enclosure is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

FOD & Enclosure Integrity
Operational

Account for all tools and fasteners before closing the enclosure, and confirm auxiliary contact wiring for ALCMS status feedback is correctly reinstated.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm both contactors switch cleanly through a full cycle and report correct status at the ALCMS before closing out the work and returning the CCR to normal service.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Fault Trips Do Almost All the Contact Wear — Not Routine Cycling
Reliability

A Main Contactor closing and opening at zero output current, in step with the CCR's own ramp sequence, barely stresses the contacts at all; nearly every bit of meaningful erosion comes from the comparatively rare fault-trip openings, where the contactor is genuinely breaking current. Base contact inspection intervals on logged fault-trip counts, not just total operation counts, since a contactor with a thousand clean cycles and five fault trips has seen more real wear than one with ten thousand clean cycles and none.

Verify Coordination With the Upstream Circuit Breaker, Not Just the Contactor's Own Rating
Coordination

The Main Contactor's rated breaking capacity assumes it's backed up correctly by the upstream overcurrent device; a contactor that's technically rated for the circuit but poorly coordinated with the breaker's trip characteristics can be damaged by a fault the breaker was too slow to clear, even though neither device is individually undersized.

A Contactor That Closes Slowly Is Already Failing
Troubleshooting

A contactor with worn contacts or a weakening coil often continues to function while closing measurably slower or with visible contact bounce; where switching sequence timing matters — particularly for coordinated CCR start/stop sequences — a slow-closing contactor is an early warning sign worth acting on before it fails to close at all.

Contact Heating Shows Up Before a Contactor Fully Fails
Diagnostics

Pitted or loosening contactor terminals typically generate localised heat well before the contactor itself misbehaves electrically; periodic thermal imaging of the CCR cabinet catches this failure mode earlier than waiting for a nuisance trip or a contactor that won't reliably close.

Frequent Remote Start/Stop Cycling Accelerates Wear Beyond Rated Life
Design

Where the ALCMS or a control strategy cycles the CCR on and off more frequently than the original design intent — for example, aggressive energy-saving scheduling — contactor mechanical life can be consumed faster than the rated operations count anticipated; factor actual observed cycling frequency into replacement planning rather than relying solely on elapsed time.

Record Coil Voltage and Auxiliary Contact Wiring Before Replacement
Documentation

Main Contactors are sometimes replaced with a superficially similar unit that has a different coil voltage or auxiliary contact arrangement than the original; document the exact coil rating and auxiliary wiring before removal so the replacement is a genuine like-for-like swap rather than requiring rework discovered mid-installation.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the CCR main contactor: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a CCR main contactor?

It is an electrically operated switching device that connects the constant current regulator (CCR) to the AC mains supply. It is the single largest power-switching component in the CCR's input compartment and is the component called the Main Contactor in manufacturer documentation for regulators such as the ATG Micro 100+/200 and Siemens/ADB L-828 series.

Q2Does the main contactor switch the series lighting circuit?

No. It switches the mains input only. Because the CCR feeds the series circuit through an internal isolation transformer, opening the main contactor removes excitation from that transformer and de-energises the output, but the series current and high-voltage side are not switched by this contactor.

Q3How does a CCR main contactor work?

It is a single electromagnetic contactor. When its coil is energised the main contacts close and connect the CCR to the mains, after which the CCR runs its own soft-start to bring the series circuit up to the commanded brightness step. In a routine shutdown the CCR ramps its output current down before the contactor is commanded open.

Q4How is the main contactor controlled?

By the ALCMS, a PLC or a local start/stop signal, allowing remote start and stop and automatic isolation during fault conditions. Auxiliary dry contacts return status feedback to the ALCMS.

Q5Where are CCR main contactors installed?

In airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters and AGL power centres, in every AGL installation with remote or automated CCR control: runway, taxiway and approach lighting.

Q6What are the typical ratings?

Rated current up to about 100 A depending on CCR size (for example up to about 60 A for a 42 kVA regulator at 400 V 3-phase), at 400/415 V AC, 3-phase, 50/60 Hz. Coil voltage is 230 V AC or 24 V DC, with silver alloy or silver-tungsten contacts and 2 to 4 auxiliary contacts.

Q7How long does a main contactor last?

Typical mechanical life is over 1 million operations and electrical life at rated load over 100,000 operations.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are IEC 60947, UL 508, BS EN 60947-4-1 and IEC 61820-3-2 (which supersedes the withdrawn IEC 61822). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should a main contactor be inspected?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for auxiliary contact verification and switching function tests, and annual for contact wear, coil voltage, terminal and busbar tightness, enclosure and seal inspection and mechanical life tracking. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with CCR main contactors?

Common problems are contact pitting and welding, coil burnout, auxiliary contact failure, ramp and shutdown sequencing faults and arc chute damage. A contactor that opens before output current reaches zero points to a sequencing fault, and one that will not open leaves the circuit energised.
