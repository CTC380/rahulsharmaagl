AGL-CS-034 — CCR Undercurrent Alarm Investigation

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A low-current investigation where a persistent CCR undercurrent alarm was traced to a partial open circuit from a deteriorated transformer secondary connection and a high-resistance cable joint — from impedance measurement through sectional testing and thermal inspection to field-circuit correction.

1Case Study Overview

During routine runway lighting operation, the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) generated a persistent undercurrent alarm while the regulator remained in service.

The CCR was set to 6.6 A, but the output current remained at 5.1 A, and several runway edge lights were noticeably dim.

The regulator continuously increased output voltage in an attempt to restore the selected current, but the alarm remained active.

A systematic investigation identified a partially open circuit caused by a failed isolating transformer secondary connection and a deteriorated cable joint, which significantly increased circuit impedance and reduced the achievable current.

This case demonstrates that a CCR undercurrent alarm is often an indication of partial circuit degradation rather than CCR failure.

3System Background

A CCR monitors:

  • Selected current.
  • Actual output current.
  • Output voltage.
  • Regulation error.
  • Alarm thresholds.

Normal operation:

Selected current achieved → No alarm

Undercurrent condition:

Actual current below selected value → CCR increases output voltage → Current still below threshold → Undercurrent alarm activated

Unlike an overcurrent trip, an undercurrent alarm often allows continued operation, making early diagnosis extremely important.

4Problem Statement

The reported condition was:

  • Selected current: 6.6 A.
  • Actual current: 5.1 A.
  • Output voltage elevated.
  • Undercurrent alarm active.
  • Several lights dim.
  • CCR remained energized.
Engineering Question

Is the CCR unable to regulate current, or is the field circuit preventing normal current flow?

7Undercurrent Principle

The CCR attempts to maintain:

Selected current = Output voltage / Circuit impedance

If circuit impedance increases beyond the CCR regulation capability:

  • Output voltage rises.
  • Current remains low.
  • Undercurrent alarm occurs.

The alarm is a symptom of insufficient current flow.

The CCR was tested with a calibrated test load.

Results:

Step 2: Measure Circuit Impedance

Measured impedance:

34 Ω

A substantial increase was identified.

Step 3: Sectional Circuit Testing

The circuit was divided into sections.

Results:

CCR–S1 Normal
S1–S2 Elevated impedance
S2–S3 Very high impedance
S3–S4 Normal

The S2–S3 section contained the dominant fault.

Step 4: Transformer Investigation

The associated isolating transformer showed:

Primary continuity Normal
Secondary continuity Intermittent
Secondary resistance Abnormally high

The transformer secondary connection was deteriorated.

Step 5: Cable Joint Inspection

The nearby cable joint showed:

  • Corrosion.
  • Moisture ingress.
  • Oxidized conductor.
  • Increased joint resistance.

The joint contributed additional circuit impedance.

Step 6: Thermal Inspection

Thermal imaging identified:

  • Transformer secondary terminal heating.
  • Cable joint heating.
  • Connector temperature significantly above adjacent components.

The heating confirmed high-resistance current flow.

10Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The CCR undercurrent alarm was caused by a partial open circuit created by a deteriorated transformer secondary connection combined with a high-resistance cable joint.

These defects increased total circuit impedance, preventing the CCR from achieving the selected 6.6 A current despite increased output voltage.

The CCR was functioning correctly.

11Corrective Action

The maintenance team:

  1. Isolated the circuit.
  2. Replaced the defective transformer secondary connection.
  3. Replaced the damaged cable joint.
  4. Installed new approved connectors.
  5. Restored environmental sealing.
  6. Performed insulation resistance testing.
  7. Measured circuit impedance.
  8. Verified CCR current regulation.
  9. Cleared the undercurrent alarm.
  10. Updated maintenance records.

14Why This Case Was Important

A CCR undercurrent alarm should not automatically be treated as a CCR fault.

It often indicates:

  • Partial open circuits.
  • High-resistance connections.
  • Transformer secondary defects.
  • Cable joint deterioration.
  • Progressive circuit degradation.

Early investigation can prevent complete circuit failure.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: Selected current: 6.6 A. Actual current: 5.1 A. Elevated output voltage. Undercurrent alarm active. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace the CCR.
B. Increase the current setting.
C. Measure circuit impedance and inspect transformer secondary connections and cable joints.
D. Replace all field transformers.
Correct answer: C. The elevated voltage and low current point to excessive impedance in the field circuit. Impedance measurement and targeted inspection of secondary connections and cable joints will identify the cause without premature CCR replacement.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about CCR undercurrent alarms, based on this case study.

Q1What is AGL-CS-034?

AGL-CS-034 is an engineering training case study on a persistent CCR undercurrent alarm with reduced runway light brightness. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2What were the symptoms?

The CCR was set to 6.6 A but output current was only 5.1 A, output voltage was elevated, several lights were dim and the undercurrent alarm stayed active. The CCR kept increasing voltage but could not restore the current.

Q3What does low current with elevated voltage indicate?

Excessive circuit impedance in the field circuit. The CCR was functioning correctly and could not reach the selected current because the circuit was preventing normal flow.

Q4What was the root cause?

A partial open circuit created by a deteriorated transformer secondary connection combined with a high-resistance cable joint. Together they increased total impedance so the CCR could not achieve 6.6 A despite higher voltage.

Q5What should you investigate first?

Measure circuit impedance and inspect transformer secondary connections and cable joints. Replacing the CCR, increasing the current setting or replacing all field transformers is not the right first step.

Q6How was the circuit repaired?

The team isolated the circuit, replaced the defective transformer secondary connection and the damaged cable joint, and installed new approved connectors. They restored sealing, tested insulation resistance, measured impedance, verified CCR regulation and cleared the alarm.

Q7How was the repair verified?

Actual current returned to 6.6 A with normal output voltage and circuit impedance of 23 Ω. Light intensity was normal and the undercurrent alarm cleared.

Q8Which test equipment was used?

The team used a true-RMS multimeter, clamp meter, CCR monitoring system, 5 kV insulation resistance tester, circuit impedance measurement equipment and thermal imaging camera, with circuit drawings.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

A CCR undercurrent alarm is usually a protective indication of reduced circuit current caused by excessive impedance from partial open circuits, high-resistance connections, transformer secondary defects, or cable joint deterioration rather than CCR malfunction.

Systematic CCR verification, circuit impedance measurement, sectional fault localization, transformer secondary testing, cable joint inspection, and thermal analysis can accurately identify the source of the undercurrent condition and restore reliable current regulation.

In AGL engineering, an undercurrent alarm is often the first warning that a circuit is gradually losing its ability to carry the required constant current.
