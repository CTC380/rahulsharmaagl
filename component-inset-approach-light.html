Inset Approach Light

A flush‑mounted high‑intensity light fixture set into the pavement surface, providing visual guidance to pilots during final approach without protruding above the runway or taxiway surface. Inset approach lights are used in approach lighting systems where snow removal, aircraft ground clearance, or operational requirements demand a smooth, obstruction‑free surface.

Overview

What Is It?

An Inset Approach Light is a high‑intensity light fixture mounted flush with the pavement surface, typically set into a base can or housing embedded in the runway or approach area pavement. Unlike elevated approach lights, inset lights do not protrude above the surface, making them suitable for runways where snowplows, aircraft ground clearance, or maintenance vehicles require a smooth, level surface. They are used as part of approach lighting systems (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSF, etc.) to provide centreline, crossbar, or barrette guidance, with the light beam directed towards approaching aircraft through a prism or lens assembly.

Inset approach lights are a standard component of many approach lighting configurations, particularly at airports with regular snow clearing or where elevated fixtures would interfere with aircraft operations. They offer the same photometric performance as elevated lights but are less conspicuous on the ground and require more careful installation to ensure proper drainage and load‑bearing capacity.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The inset approach light's purpose is to deliver the same photometric guidance as any other approach light fixture while presenting no obstruction at all to snowplows, ground vehicles, or aircraft undercarriage — a consideration that matters specifically on runways where routine snow clearing or FOD risk make any protruding fixture a liability. By keeping the entire optical assembly flush with the pavement, it lets an airport achieve full approach lighting coverage on surfaces where an elevated fixture would either be damaged during winter operations or pose an unacceptable ground-clearance risk, without compromising the beam intensity or pattern a pilot depends on during approach.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Inset approach lights are deployed on precision and non‑precision approach runways and are used in:

  • CAT I precision approach runways (ALSF‑1) – providing centreline and crossbar guidance for approaches with 200 ft decision height.
  • CAT II and III precision approach runways (ALSF‑2) – critical for low‑visibility operations with decision heights as low as 100 ft or less.
  • Medium‑intensity approach lighting systems (MALSF, MALSR) – providing enhanced visual cues for non‑precision runways.
  • Airports with regular snow removal – where elevated fixtures would be damaged by snowplows.
  • Runways with limited vertical clearance – where elevated lights could interfere with aircraft undercarriage or ground equipment.
  • Military airfields – requiring flush mounting to minimise foreign object debris (FOD) risk.
  • Runways with high‑speed taxiway exits – where a smooth surface is essential for safe aircraft manoeuvring.
Placement

Installation & Location

Inset approach lights are set flush into paved or hard-surfaced ground beneath the approach lighting array, seated in a base can recessed to grade at the fixture's designated station along the extended runway centreline. Where the approach lighting corridor crosses a paved area — an overrun, a road, or a hardstand — flush mounting avoids the strike hazard an elevated fixture would present, and the base can is sealed against surface water in the same way as inset runway and taxiway fixtures. Cabling is run underground from the nearest junction box directly to the fixture, and its position is set out from the same approach lighting survey used for the elevated fixtures elsewhere in the array, so the visual line of guidance stays continuous whichever mounting type is used at a given station.

Function

How Does It Work?

Inset approach lights operate on the same principles as other approach lighting components, with the added benefit of a flush‑mounted design:

  • Configuration: Each inset approach light is mounted in a base can or housing embedded in the pavement, with the fixture top flush with the surrounding surface. The fixture contains a prism or lens assembly that directs the light beam towards approaching aircraft.
  • Lighting: The lights are high‑intensity white, steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by the CCR (5‑step). The flush design ensures no obstruction to snowplows or aircraft, while the prism provides the required beam pattern.
  • Guidance: The lights provide lateral and distance guidance to pilots, helping maintain centreline alignment and glide‑slope tracking. The flush mounting does not affect the photometric performance, which is comparable to elevated lights.
  • Drainage and Load Bearing: Inset fixtures are designed to withstand aircraft loads (up to 150,000 kg or more) and include drainage channels to prevent water accumulation that could affect light output.
  • Maintenance: Inset lights are more challenging to access for maintenance compared to elevated lights, often requiring pavement cutting or specialised extraction tools. However, they are less prone to physical damage from snowplows and ground vehicles.
  • Integration: Inset approach lights are integrated with other approach lighting components, using the same electrical and control interfaces as elevated fixtures.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Inset approach lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or (at smaller aerodromes) pilot-controlled lighting input. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A). Every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, draws from the same current and burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no way to dim or brighten a single fixture independently of the rest of the circuit. Fault detection is likewise loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp, a failed isolating transformer, or water ingress into a flush base can, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing. Because inset fixtures are harder to inspect visually than elevated ones, field crews typically rely more heavily on this loop-level fault report, then use insulation-resistance and sectionalised loop testing to localise the failed base can before excavating pavement.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is a die-cast aluminium housing, machined and anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, set flush into an L-850-series base can recessed into the approach area pavement or a reinforced concrete pad on unpaved sections. The optical assembly — a high-intensity lamp or LED array behind a borosilicate glass or polycarbonate prism — sits under a top cap gasket-sealed with an O-ring to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating against water and dust ingress, with the top surface load-rated where vehicle or aircraft traffic may cross it. Internal wiring terminates at sealed L-823 primary connectors that mate to the secondary leads of a nearby isolating transformer, itself housed in its own base can. Stainless-steel fasteners resist corrosion, and the base can assembly includes a drainage provision to prevent water pooling around the transformer and cable entry points, along with careful backfill and compaction during installation to maintain a level, load-bearing surface around the fixture.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination and Abrasion

Inset lights are exposed to rubber deposits, de‑icing chemicals, and abrasive debris from aircraft tyres. The prism surface can become scratched or coated, reducing light output. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and gentle abrasives is essential, and prism replacement may be required over time.

Water Ingress and Drainage Failure

Inset fixtures rely on effective drainage to prevent water accumulation. Blocked drainage channels or failed seals can cause water ingress, leading to electrical faults or reduced light output. Regular inspection and cleaning of drainage paths are critical.

Pavement Settlement and Misalignment

Over time, pavement settlement can cause inset fixtures to sink or tilt, altering the beam direction. Regular alignment checks using a theodolite or laser system are essential to ensure the beam remains correctly oriented.

Base Can Corrosion and Structural Failure

Base cans are subject to corrosion from de‑icing chemicals and moisture. Corrosion can compromise the structural integrity of the mounting, leading to fixture movement or failure. Regular inspection and protective coating maintenance are recommended.

Damage from Snowplows and Ground Vehicles

Although flush‑mounted, inset lights can still be damaged by snowplow blades or ground vehicles if the blade digs into the pavement. Regular inspection after snow clearing is recommended to check for cracks or displacement.

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced Light Output from Prism Scratching
Likely Cause
Abrasive debris from aircraft tyres and de-icing chemicals has scratched or coated the prism surface.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the prism for scratching, coating, or fogging; take a photometric reading and compare to rated output.
Corrective Action
Clean with approved solvents and gentle abrasives per the manufacturer's procedure; replace the prism if scratching has permanently reduced output.
Water Ingress or Electrical Fault
Likely Cause
Blocked drainage channels or a failed seal has allowed water to accumulate around the fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect drainage paths for blockage; check seals for damage; look for standing water in the recess.
Corrective Action
Clear blocked drainage, replace failed seals, and dry out the fixture before re-energising and re-testing insulation resistance.
Beam Direction Off from Design Path
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement has caused the fixture to sink or tilt, altering the beam angle.
Diagnostic Steps
Check beam alignment with a theodolite or laser system against the design approach path angle.
Corrective Action
Re-set or shim the fixture to restore correct alignment, and re-survey after any pavement work in the area.
Fixture Movement or Structural Failure
Likely Cause
Corrosion of the base can from de-icing chemicals and moisture has compromised the mounting's structural integrity.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the base can for corrosion, pitting, or visible movement of the fixture within its mount.
Corrective Action
Replace the corroded base can, apply protective coating, and re-secure the fixture before returning to service.
Cracked or Displaced Fixture After Snow Clearing
Likely Cause
A snowplough blade or ground vehicle has struck the flush-mounted fixture, cracking the top or displacing it.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect fixtures for cracks or displacement after each snow-clearing operation.
Corrective Action
Replace the cracked or displaced fixture and confirm it is flush and properly sealed before returning to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Inset approach lights run on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening the base can or handling cable connectors.

Approach Path Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on the approach lighting requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM, since this equipment sits directly beneath low-altitude aircraft on final approach; keep vehicles and personnel clear of the surface while aircraft are landing or departing.

Recess Confined Space Awareness
Personnel Safety

Treat work inside the recessed base can as a confined-space task where site procedures require it, checking for standing water, corrosion products, or trapped gas before reaching in, particularly on units with a history of drainage failure.

Snow-Clearing Coordination
Operational

Coordinate blade height and clearing routes with snowplough operators so flush-mounted fixtures aren't struck, and inspect fixtures for cracking or displacement after every snow-clearing operation before certifying the surface fit for use.

Drainage Verification
Operational

Confirm drainage channels are clear and the fixture seal is intact before closing out any maintenance, since blocked drainage or a failed seal allows water to accumulate around the fixture and drive electrical faults.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or seal work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, given the corrosive effect de-icing chemicals and moisture have on base cans and connectors over time.

PPE & Chemical Exposure
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE, including chemical-resistant gloves, when working in a recess exposed to accumulated de-icing fluid residue, and confirm the fixture and base can are bonded/grounded before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture sitting proud, tilted, or out of tolerance after maintenance; verify the beam alignment and photometric output before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Drainage Failure Precedes Most Electrical Faults Here
Failure Mode

Standing water in the recessed base can is usually the root cause behind a run of connector corrosion and insulation faults on inset units, not a coincidence alongside them; when troubleshooting a recurring electrical fault at an inset position, check the drainage channel condition first rather than jumping straight to component replacement.

Snow-Clearing Damage Accumulates as Hairline Cracks
Mechanical

A plough blade running slightly low doesn't always shatter an inset fixture outright; it can instead produce hairline cracking in the lens or housing that only fails later under thermal or wheel-load stress, so inspect fixtures for fine cracking after every clearing operation, not just for obvious impact damage.

De-Icing Chemical Exposure Compounds With Moisture
Durability

Glycol-based de-icing fluid combined with standing water accelerates corrosion in the base can beyond what either factor alone would cause; specify seals and connectors rated for combined chemical and moisture exposure specifically for inset approach positions, rather than applying the same spec used on elevated fixtures elsewhere on the airfield.

Pavement Settlement Shifts Beam Alignment Slowly
Verification

Gradual settlement of the surrounding pavement can tilt an inset fixture's beam alignment out of tolerance without any single visible event marking the change; re-check beam alignment on a fixed schedule at these positions, since a purely reactive approach only catches the drift after it's already affected the approach light pattern.

Confined-Space Hazards Build Up Between Inspections
Personnel Safety

A recessed base can that was safe to enter at the last inspection can accumulate trapped gas or corrosion by-products in the interim, especially where drainage has been marginal; treat every entry as requiring a fresh atmosphere check rather than relying on the last inspection's result.

Wheel-Load Rating Must Survive Every Reseal
Mechanical

After reworking a seal or housing, confirm the fixture's restored assembly still meets its rated wheel-load capacity before reopening the surface, since a seal repair that prioritises watertightness over correct seating can leave the unit structurally weaker than before the work, even though it may test fine electrically.
