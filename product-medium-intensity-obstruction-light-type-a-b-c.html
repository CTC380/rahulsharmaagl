Product Overview

Medium Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B/C)

What It Is

The light provides a higher level of visual identification than low-intensity obstruction lighting for structures requiring enhanced visibility.

Depending on classification, it may operate with steady or flashing output and may form part of a multi-level obstruction-lighting arrangement.

A complete installation may include multiple lights at defined elevations so the obstacle can be identified from relevant viewing directions.

How We Supply It

Precision AGL Solutions supplies medium-intensity obstruction lights for towers, masts, communication structures, industrial installations and other tall structures that may present an aviation obstacle.

Type A, Type B or Type C configurations may be selected, with final optical and operating characteristics determined by the project specification. We support selection by optical output, operating mode, mounting, electrical supply and monitoring requirements.

ICAO Classification

What Type A, Type B and Type C Mean

ICAO TypeColourModeTypical peak intensityTypical use
Type AWhiteFlashing (20–60 per minute)About 20,000 cd by day and at twilight, reduced to about 2,000 cd at nightDay and night marking where strong daytime conspicuity is needed
Type BRedFlashing (20–60 per minute)About 2,000 cdNight marking, with day marking by paint or other means
Type CRedSteady (fixed)About 2,000 cdNight marking where a steady red light is accepted

Where They Are Used

ICAO uses medium-intensity lights for structures roughly in the 45 m to 150 m range, with low-intensity lights below that and high-intensity lights above it or where more conspicuity is needed. On tall structures, lights are placed at levels between the top and ground at about equal intervals, normally not more than about 52 m apart. Flashing lights on one structure flash together.

FAA Equivalents

FAA equipment is specified in AC 150/5345-43J as L-865 (white flashing, medium intensity) and L-864 (red flashing). FAA lighting systems are laid out under AC 70/7460-1N. FAA and ICAO systems are not interchangeable, so follow the authority for the project.

Values are typical ICAO figures from Annex 14, Vol. I, Chapter 6. Confirm intensities, flash rates and spacing against the edition and national rules in force, including DGCA India requirements and the NOC conditions for the structure.

FAQ

Medium Intensity Obstruction Light (Type A/B/C) — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a medium-intensity obstruction light?

A medium-intensity obstruction light gives a higher level of visual identification than low-intensity lighting for structures that need enhanced visibility. Depending on classification it may operate steady or flashing and may form part of a multi-level arrangement. Typical applications are communication towers, telecom masts, tall utility and industrial structures, tall buildings and cranes.

What do ICAO Type A, B and C mean?

Type A is white and flashing at 20 to 60 flashes per minute, with a peak intensity of about 20,000 cd by day and at twilight, reduced to about 2,000 cd at night. Type B is red and flashing at 20 to 60 per minute at about 2,000 cd, for night marking with day marking by paint or other means. Type C is red and steady at about 2,000 cd, for night marking where a steady red light is accepted.

What structure heights use medium-intensity lighting?

ICAO uses medium-intensity lights for structures roughly in the 45 m to 150 m range, with low-intensity lights below that and high-intensity lights above it or where more conspicuity is needed. These are typical figures from Annex 14 Volume I Chapter 6, so confirm them against the edition and national rules in force, including DGCA India requirements and the NOC conditions for the structure.

How do FAA and ICAO systems differ?

FAA equipment is specified in AC 150/5345-43J as L-865 (white flashing, medium intensity) and L-864 (red flashing), and FAA lighting systems are laid out under AC 70/7460-1N. FAA and ICAO systems are not interchangeable, so follow the authority for the project. The classification should not be assumed from the product name and must be confirmed against the approved project specification.

How are lights arranged on a tall structure?

On tall structures, lights are placed at levels between the top and ground at about equal intervals, normally not more than about 52 m apart, and flashing lights on one structure flash together. The number and position of levels come from the approved obstruction-lighting design, and positions should avoid structural elements blocking the intended optical coverage.

When is flash synchronization needed?

Where multiple flashing obstruction lights are installed on the same structure, coordinated operation may be required, depending on the selected classification and the project control philosophy. A system can include a common timing reference, local control or a central controller, status monitoring and fault indication, and the required flash pattern, timing and synchronization should be confirmed during technical selection.

What monitoring options are available?

Monitoring may cover light operating status, power status, controller status, flashing status, individual light fault indication, communication status and remote alarm output. The architecture should be coordinated with the site electrical, SCADA, building-management or aviation infrastructure where applicable.

What electrical and control arrangements are used?

The configuration is selected to suit the site supply and control system, covering supply voltage, AC or DC configuration, surge protection, cable entry, local isolation and earthing. Control can include automatic day/night operation, photocell input, scheduled operation, flash control and fault monitoring. Where several light types or intensities are used on the same structure, the control system should coordinate them according to the approved lighting design.

How is a medium-intensity system commissioned?

Verify mounting and orientation, electrical connections, supply voltage and protection, then test steady or flashing operation, colour and optical output, flash characteristics where applicable, and synchronization between lights where required. Test monitoring and alarm functions and photocell operation, and record the results. Where the project requires it, use calibrated equipment for photometric measurements.

What are the common faults?

A light that is not operating usually points to a power or control failure, and reduced optical output to a dirty lens or optical degradation. Incorrect flashing suggests a configuration or controller issue, lights out of step suggest a timing or control communication fault, and intermittent operation suggests a supply or connection problem. Moisture inside the enclosure indicates a seal or cable-entry problem, and movement of the light a loose or unsuitable mounting.

What should be provided when requesting medium-intensity lights?

Provide the structure type, overall height and location, the Type A, B or C requirement if already established, the applicable aviation authority, the required colour, steady or flashing operation and flash characteristics, the number of lighting levels and quantity of lights, the power supply, existing control system, monitoring requirements, mounting arrangement, environmental conditions and whether it is new or a replacement. A replacement should be selected on the complete optical and operational requirements rather than physical compatibility alone.
