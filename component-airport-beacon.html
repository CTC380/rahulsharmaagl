Airport Beacon

A rotating beacon system that identifies the airport location from the air, typically producing alternating green and white flashes. Serves as the primary visual landmark for pilots to locate the airfield during night and low‑visibility operations, with a distinctive colour sequence that distinguishes land airports (green/white) from water airports (yellow/white) and heliports (green/yellow/white).

Overview

What Is It?

An Airport Beacon is a rotating aviation light system installed at airports to provide a visual identification signal to aircraft. The beacon produces alternating flashes of green and white light (for land airports) or yellow and white (for water airports), with a specific flash pattern (typically 24–30 flashes per minute). The rotating assembly ensures the light is visible from all directions. The beacon is mounted on a tall structure (control tower, hangar, or dedicated mast) and is visible from up to 30–40 nautical miles in clear conditions. It is one of the most critical visual aids for pilots approaching an airport at night, providing unambiguous identification of the airfield location.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The airport beacon exists to answer the most basic question a pilot faces on a night approach before any other lighting system becomes relevant: where is the airport, and is this the correct one? Visible from up to 30–40 nautical miles, it functions as a long-range locating signal rather than a precision guidance aid, giving the pilot a confirmed heading to fly toward long before the runway's own lighting system is close enough to be useful. The green/white versus yellow/white colour distinction exists specifically so a pilot can distinguish a land airport from a water airport at that same long range, preventing a costly misidentification before the pilot has committed to an approach, which is why the beacon's colour coding is treated as safety-critical rather than merely decorative.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at all civil and military airports that support night operations or low‑visibility approaches. Typically mounted on the control tower, a hangar roof, or a dedicated beacon tower at an elevated position to ensure maximum visibility. Used at land airports (green/white), water airports (yellow/white), and heliports (green/yellow/white). The beacon is often supplemented by secondary beacons at satellite terminals or remote aprons. It is one of the first lights pilots look for when locating an airfield.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out at the highest practical point on the control tower, hangar roof, or a dedicated beacon tower, chosen so the rotating beam has an unobstructed 360° sweep with no nearby structures, antennas, or terrain intruding on the horizon. The mounting platform is engineered to carry the beacon's static weight plus wind and vibration loading from the rotating assembly, with a service platform or hatch built in for lamp changes and gearbox maintenance. Power and control cabling runs from the beacon controller (housed in the tower or an adjacent equipment room) up to the beacon head, with lightning protection and surge suppression fitted given the beacon's exposed, elevated position. Where a dedicated mast is used rather than an existing building, the mast is sited clear of the runway's obstacle-limitation surfaces and sized to keep the beacon above surrounding rooftops and terrain within its designed visibility radius.

Function

How Does It Work?

The airport beacon consists of a rotating optical assembly with two or more lamps (or LED arrays) that produce alternating colours. A motor rotates the assembly at a precise speed (typically 12–15 RPM) to produce the required flash rate. The light sources are high‑intensity halogen lamps or LED modules, with coloured filters (green and white) or dichroic lenses. The beacon is powered from the airport's electrical supply, with a backup generator or UPS to ensure continuous operation during power outages. Modern beacons use LED technology for longer life, lower power consumption, and instant‑on capability. The control system includes a photocell to turn the beacon on at dusk and off at dawn, or it may be manually controlled by ATC.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The beacon fixture is the visible output of a small closed system, not a standalone circuit — the actual decision-making happens one level up, in the Beacon Controller. A photocell, timer, or ATC command decides when the beacon should run (dusk-to-dawn, or a manual override for testing). That decision reaches the controller, which is the system's single control node: it sets the drive motor's rotation speed and independently sequences the lamp or LED colours and intensity. The Drive Motor turns the optical assembly at a fixed rate to produce the required flash cadence, while the lamp path handles colour and brightness — the two outputs run in parallel but are both timed off the same controller. If mains power fails, supply to the controller transfers automatically to a UPS or standby generator, so the flash cycle continues without an interruption a pilot would notice. Fault detection runs the opposite direction: motor current draw and lamp circuit health are both monitored by the controller, and any anomaly — a stalled motor, a failed lamp, loss of rotation — is reported upstream as a fault alarm to the ALCMS rather than corrected automatically at the beacon itself.

Command Source → Beacon Controller → Drive Motor + Lamp/LED Driver → Optical Assembly → Flash Output

Identification Key

What the Colour Pair Tells the Pilot

Land Airport

Green / White — this page's standard configuration, 24–30 flashes per minute.

Water Airport

Yellow / White — identifies a seaplane base rather than a land runway.

Heliport

Green / Yellow / White — signals a helipad, not a fixed-wing airfield.

Because pilots often spot a beacon before anything else on the ground at night, the colour pair alone has to disambiguate the site — a green/white flash means "land here," while yellow or all-white pairings mean this beacon marks something else entirely.

Construction

Physical Construction

The beacon housing is a rotating drum or fixed-optic weatherproof enclosure, typically die-cast aluminium or corrosion-resistant alloy, mounted atop a fixed pedestal or tower structure to achieve the elevation needed for 360° visibility. Twin (or single) parabolic reflector-and-lens assemblies are mounted on a precision-balanced turntable driven by the beacon motor through a gear reduction stage, with slip rings or rotary couplings carrying power and control signals across the rotating joint. The enclosure is gasket-sealed and vented to prevent condensation while achieving an IP66/IP67 weather-resistant rating, with a toughened, UV-stabilised dome or window protecting the optics from wind-driven debris and precipitation. The base structure includes lightning protection bonding, a lockable access hatch for lamp and motor servicing, and cable entry glands sealed against moisture ingress. Stainless-steel and corrosion-resistant fasteners are used throughout, given the beacon's typically exposed, elevated mounting position.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Tower/Pedestal Structural Check
Civil

Confirm the supporting tower or pedestal is rated for the beacon's weight, wind loading, and vibration before mounting, with the access hatch and cabling route accounted for in the design.

Turntable Levelling & Alignment
Mechanical

Set the beacon base plumb and level on its mount so the rotating turntable tracks true, avoiding bearing wear or beam wobble from an out-of-level installation.

Lightning Protection Bonding
Electrical

Bond the beacon housing and support structure to the site's lightning protection system, given the beacon's typically elevated, exposed mounting position.

Power & Standby Supply Termination
Electrical

Terminate the 230V AC supply and confirm the UPS/generator standby feed is wired and set to transfer automatically on mains failure before energisation.

Slip Ring / Rotary Coupling Wiring
Electrical

Terminate power and control wiring at the slip ring or rotary coupling per manufacturer procedure, checking free rotation is not fouled by cable slack or routing.

Cable Entry & Enclosure Sealing
Mechanical

Seal cable entry glands and confirm the enclosure gasket and dome/window seat correctly to achieve the rated IP66/IP67 protection before closing the access hatch.

Colour Pair Configuration
Configuration

Confirm the correct colour sequence (green/white, yellow/white, or green/yellow/white) is fitted for the site type before commissioning, since this identifies the facility to arriving pilots.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record mounting height, bonding, cable termination, and colour pair configuration as-built before the tower or pedestal access is closed out.

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the beacon's supply cable and motor windings before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Rotation Direction & Free-Spin Check
Pre-Energisation

Manually rotate the turntable to confirm free, unobstructed movement and correct rotational direction before the drive motor is powered.

Photocell / Timer Threshold Verification

Backup Power / UPS Transfer Test

Simulate a mains failure and confirm the beacon transfers to backup power without an extended outage or loss of rotation.

Mounting & 360° Sightline Clearance Check

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off

Record insulation, rotation speed, colour sequence, and photometric readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify effective intensity for both colours in the sequence against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer; degraded output usually points to filter fade or lamp aging.

Rotation Speed Verification
Semi-annual

Confirm the beacon rotates at the correct RPM to deliver the required 24-30 flashes per minute, since drive wear can slow rotation over time.

Motor Bearing Inspection
Annual

Check for noise, vibration, or drag in the rotating mechanism, and lubricate or replace bearings on schedule (typically every 5-7 years) before they seize.

Colour Filter Inspection
Annual

Check green and yellow filters for UV fade or discoloration and replace on schedule to keep the colour sequence within the required chromaticity boundary.

Lens Seal & Desiccant Check
Semi-annual

Inspect seals around the rotating lens for degradation and refresh desiccant packs to prevent moisture ingress and internal fogging of the optics.

Power Supply & Backup Test
Annual

Test UPS and generator backup transfer to confirm the beacon stays operational through a mains power loss, since continuous operation is safety-critical after dark.

Housing & Mounting Inspection
Annual

Check the tower or rooftop mounting structure, weatherproofing, and cable entries for corrosion or wear given the beacon's typically elevated exposure.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Motor Bearing Wear

The rotating mechanism is subject to continuous wear. Bearing failure can cause erratic rotation, reduced flash rate, or complete stoppage. Regular lubrication and replacement of bearings (every 5–7 years) is essential.

Colour Filter Degradation

Green and yellow filters fade over time due to UV exposure and heat, altering the colour sequence. Annual photometric inspection and filter replacement is required to maintain the correct colour identification.

Moisture Ingress in Lens Assembly

Seals around the rotating lens can fail, allowing moisture to enter and fog the optics. Regular seal inspection and replacement, along with desiccant maintenance, is critical for optical clarity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Beacon Not Rotating
Likely Cause
Seized or worn drive motor bearings, a failed drive motor, or an open circuit in the motor power feed from the beacon controller.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for motor supply voltage at the drive motor terminals; attempt to manually rotate the head with power off to feel for bearing binding; inspect motor windings for continuity and insulation resistance.
Corrective Action
Replace seized bearings or the drive motor as a unit, and repair any open wiring between the controller and motor; re-verify rotation speed matches the specified RPM before returning to service.
Erratic or Inconsistent Rotation Speed
Likely Cause
Worn drive motor bearings or gearing causing intermittent drag, or a beacon controller speed-regulation fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Time actual RPM over several rotations and compare to the rated flash/rotation rate; listen for grinding or uneven noise at the drive assembly; check controller output for a stable, correctly regulated motor supply.
Corrective Action
Service or replace the drive motor and bearings per the Beacon Drive Motor procedure, or repair the controller's speed-regulation circuit; re-time rotation after the fix.
One Colour Beam Weak, Missing, or Wrong Hue
Likely Cause
Faded or shifted colour filter (typically green, from UV and heat exposure), a cracked lens segment, or a lamp/LED source degrading on that side of the optic.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour and intensity check against ICAO/FAA chromaticity limits for each beam; visually inspect the filter and lens segment for fading, cracking, or moisture fogging.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded filter or lens segment per the Beacon Lens Assembly procedure, or replace the lamp/LED source; re-verify colour and intensity with a follow-up photometric reading.
Beacon Completely Dark
Likely Cause
Loss of mains power to the controller, a tripped circuit breaker or blown fuse, or a failed lamp/LED source with no fallback in place.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming power and breaker status at the controller; verify the controller's fault indicator or ALCMS status report; confirm lamp/LED continuity if power is confirmed present.
Corrective Action
Restore power or reset the breaker, replace a blown fuse, or replace the failed lamp/LED source; confirm the beacon resumes its normal flash sequence before closing out.
Fogged, Hazy, or Discoloured Optics
Likely Cause
Failed weatherseal or gasket around the rotating lens assembly allowing moisture ingress, or exhausted desiccant no longer controlling internal humidity.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect for visible condensation or fogging inside the lens housing; check seal and gasket condition around the rotating optic; check desiccant colour/saturation if a visual indicator is fitted.
Corrective Action
Dry out the housing, replace failed seals and gaskets, and renew the desiccant; re-inspect after the next weather cycle to confirm the seal is holding.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Drive Motor / Bearing Seizure
High Severity
Root Cause
Worn or seized drive motor bearings, motor winding failure, or an open circuit in the motor power feed from the beacon controller.
Effect
Beacon stops rotating entirely, or rotation speed becomes erratic and inconsistent with the rated flash rate.
Detection Method
Manual rotation check with power off, motor supply-voltage verification, and winding continuity/insulation resistance testing.
Beacon Controller Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Component-level failure inside the controller, often from a power surge, moisture ingress, or age-related wear on the timing/regulation circuit.
Effect
Beacon goes completely dark, or the flash/rotation sequence becomes unregulated even with a healthy motor and lamp.
Detection Method
Check of incoming power and breaker status, the controller's fault indicator or ALCMS status report, and confirmation of lamp/motor continuity independent of the controller.
Colour Filter / Lens Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
UV and heat exposure fading a dyed glass filter (most commonly the green segment) or causing a dichroic coating to peel or delaminate.
Effect
One colour beam appears weak, missing, or shows the wrong hue while the other beam remains normal.
Detection Method
Photometric colour and intensity checks against ICAO/FAA chromaticity limits, and visual inspection of the filter and lens segment.
Weatherseal / Gasket Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Degraded or failed seals and gaskets around the rotating lens assembly allowing moisture ingress, often accelerated by day/night thermal cycling.
Effect
Fogged, hazy, or discoloured optics that reduce beam clarity and visible range.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for condensation or fogging inside the lens housing, seal/gasket condition checks, and desiccant saturation checks where fitted.
Lamp / LED Source End-of-Life
Low Severity
Root Cause
Ordinary lumen depreciation or filament/LED wear reaching the end of rated service life.
Effect
Gradual reduction in overall beacon intensity, or the beacon going completely dark if no fallback source is fitted.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity checks and lamp/LED continuity testing during routine maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Elevated Access
Fall Protection

The beacon is mounted on a control tower, hangar roof, or dedicated mast; use fall-arrest equipment and follow the facility's rooftop or tower-access permit before approaching the unit.

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the beacon supply at the controller before opening the lens housing or drive assembly, and verify zero energy before touching motor or lamp connections.

Rotating Assembly Pinch Points
Mechanical

Keep hands and tools clear of the turntable and drive train until it is confirmed stationary and electrically isolated; never attempt to manually check rotation speed on an energised unit.

Direct Beam Viewing
Personnel Safety

Avoid looking directly into the beam at close range when the beacon is under test with the lens installed; the focused output is intense enough to cause temporary glare or discomfort.

ATC / Airfield Coordination
Operational

Notify ATC and raise a NOTAM before any planned outage, since the airport beacon is a primary night-identification aid and its loss affects arriving traffic beyond the immediate airfield.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Wear cut-resistant gloves when handling the colour filter/lens segments and insulated tools for electrical work, and use fall-protection PPE appropriate to the mounting height.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm correct flash rate, colour sequence, and photometric output before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Check the Green Beam First, Not the White
Inspection

The dyed green filter is almost always the first colour to visibly fade under UV, so a periodic chromaticity check should target the green segment before the white beam even shows a symptom. Waiting for a pilot report of a "weak green" means the filter has already drifted well past the point a photometric check would have caught it.

Time the Rotation, Don't Just Watch It
Diagnostics

A beacon can look like it's rotating normally to the eye while running measurably off the rated RPM, which throws the flash rate out of the 24–30 flashes-per-minute window without ever stopping. Clock actual rotations against a stopwatch during routine inspection rather than relying on a visual "looks fine" check.

Desiccant Condition Predicts the Next Fogging Event
Reliability

Lens fogging on a rotating beacon is rarely the seal failing outright; it's usually exhausted desiccant that stopped absorbing the small amount of moisture a "good" seal still lets through over years. Track desiccant colour/saturation as a leading indicator at every inspection, not just as a line item after fogging is already reported.

Re-Survey the Sweep After Any Nearby Construction
Siting

A beacon sited correctly at commissioning can lose part of its 360° sweep years later if a crane, new hangar, or antenna goes up nearby. Re-verify unobstructed line-of-sight around the tower whenever new structures appear on the airfield, rather than assuming the original siting survey still holds.

Rule Out the Controller Before Replacing the Motor
Troubleshooting

Erratic rotation speed is diagnosed as a motor/bearing problem by default, but a controller with a drifting speed-regulation circuit produces the identical symptom on a mechanically healthy motor. Confirm the controller's output is a stable, correctly regulated supply before committing to a motor or bearing replacement.

Surge Protection Earns Its Keep on This Fixture
Electrical

Because the beacon sits at the highest point on the tower or mast, it takes the brunt of any lightning-induced transient reaching the site. Verify surge suppression at both the controller and the beacon head at every major service, since a degraded arrester often shows no symptom until the next strike takes the controller with it.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about airport beacons: colour sequence, flash rate, visibility, power, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an airport beacon?

An airport beacon is a rotating aviation light installed at an airport to give pilots a visual identification signal. It sweeps alternating flashes through 360° so the airfield can be located from a long distance at night, well before the runway lighting is close enough to be useful.

Q2What colours does an airport beacon show?

A civil land airport beacon alternates green and white flashes. A water airport beacon alternates yellow and white, and a heliport beacon uses a green, yellow and white sequence.

Q3What is the flash rate of an airport beacon?

The beacon produces 24–30 flashes per minute, achieved by rotating the optical assembly at 12–15 RPM with two flashes per revolution. Rotation speed is typically held within ±1% so the flash rate stays consistent.

Q4How far can an airport beacon be seen?

In clear conditions an airport beacon is visible from about 30–40 nautical miles. Effective intensity can reach up to 50,000 cd, with a narrow vertical beam of roughly 2°–3° to maximise range at the horizon.

Q5Where is an airport beacon installed?

It is mounted at the highest practical point, such as the control tower, a hangar roof or a dedicated beacon tower, typically 15–50 m above ground level. The site must give an unobstructed 360° sweep, with no structures, antennas or terrain intruding on the horizon.

Q6What power supply does an airport beacon use?

It runs on a dedicated mains-fed circuit, typically 230 V AC single-phase, independent of the airfield series lighting circuit. A UPS or standby generator with automatic changeover keeps rotation and flashing going during a mains failure, and a photocell or astronomical clock with the Beacon Controller sets day and night intensity.

Q7Which standards apply to airport beacons?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (colour, intensity and flash pattern), FAA AC 150/5345-12 (specification for airport rotating beacons) and IES LM-79 / LM-80 for LED photometric testing and lumen maintenance. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8What are the photometric requirements for an airport beacon?

Peak beam intensity is typically at least 2,000 cd per colour, per ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5345-12. Chromaticity must stay within the ICAO Annex 14 boundaries for white and green (or yellow), and rotation speed tolerance is about ±1% of rated RPM.

Q9How often should an airport beacon be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are annual for photometric intensity, motor bearing and colour filter checks, and semi-annual for rotation speed verification and lens seal and desiccant checks. Bearings are typically replaced every 5–7 years. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is an airport beacon not rotating?

The usual causes are seized or worn drive motor bearings, a failed drive motor, or an open circuit in the motor supply from the beacon controller. Check for supply voltage at the motor terminals, turn the head by hand with power off to feel for binding, and test winding continuity and insulation resistance. Confirm the correct RPM before returning the beacon to service.
