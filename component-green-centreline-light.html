Green Centreline Light

A vital taxiway guidance light that provides precise centreline tracking for aircraft during ground movement. Emitting a distinctive green colour, these inset lights help pilots maintain accurate positioning on the taxiway centreline, especially in low visibility conditions, enhancing safety and operational efficiency.

Overview

What Is It?

A Green Centreline Light is a flush-mounted (inset) AGL luminaire installed along the centreline of a taxiway. It emits a steady green light, providing pilots with a clear visual reference to maintain the aircraft precisely on the taxiway centreline during ground movement. Unlike taxiway edge lights (blue) which define the lateral boundaries, centreline lights guide the pilot along the optimal path, particularly in low visibility operations (RVR < 550m). These lights are an essential component of the taxiway guidance system, especially at busy airports with complex taxiway layouts.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The green centreline light exists because knowing you are somewhere between two taxiway edges is not the same as knowing you are safely centred between them — and for a large aircraft, the difference matters. Wingspan and engine clearance from taxiway-edge obstacles, parked aircraft, or other traffic depend on the aircraft tracking the centreline closely, something a pilot cannot judge reliably from the edge lights alone, especially at night or in low RVR conditions where peripheral references are hard to use. By giving a precise, continuous line to follow rather than just a boundary to stay inside, the fixture reduces the pilot's workload during taxi and removes the guesswork from an otherwise purely visual judgement, which is why it becomes indispensable at busy airports where taxiway layouts are complex and the tolerance for lateral drift is small.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Green centreline lights are installed on taxiways at airports that support low visibility operations. They are typically used on:

  • Precision approach runways with CAT II/III capability requiring enhanced taxiway guidance
  • Major international airports with complex taxiway networks and high traffic density
  • Taxiway straight sections where centreline guidance is critical for accurate tracking
  • Taxiway curves and intersections where precise path following is essential
  • Apron taxi lanes where aircraft manoeuvring in confined areas requires accurate guidance
  • Rapid exit taxiways where aircraft exit the runway at high speed and need clear centreline guidance
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are recessed into a saw-cut or cored pocket along the taxiway centreline marking, using the standard load-rated inset base can set flush with the pavement surface, at 15–30 m spacing on straight sections tightening to 7.5–15 m through curves and intersections. Cabling is run in duct beneath the pavement from each base can back to its isolating transformer and on to the taxiway centreline series circuit, with fixture position checked against pavement slab joints during setting-out so no base can straddles a joint or crack; layout follows the taxiway centreline and marking survey along the full route the green lights guide.

Function

How Does It Work?

Green centreline lights are powered from the series circuit via an isolating transformer. Each light typically contains an LED array or a halogen lamp with a green filter, producing a steady green light. The optical system is designed to project a uni-directional beam along the taxiway centreline, visible to the pilot from the cockpit. The lights are spaced at intervals that provide continuous guidance:

  • Spacing: Typically 15m to 30m apart on straight sections, closer on curves.
  • Intensity Control: Brightness is controlled by the CCR with 5 intensity steps, allowing adjustment for visibility conditions.
  • Optical Design: Precision optics (reflector and prism) shape the beam to be visible from the cockpit angle.
  • Integration: The lights are integrated with the ALCMS and can be dimmed or switched off as required.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Green centreline lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit assigned to a given taxiway route or section. The ALCMS selects which taxiway centreline circuits are energised and at which CCR step, either as a straightforward on/off routing decision or, on airports fitted with a follow-the-greens system, as a dynamically illuminated path that switches on ahead of a taxiing aircraft and off behind it as the aircraft progresses — in that case the "loop" the ALCMS is switching may be a short, sectionalised run of fixtures rather than the whole taxiway. Whichever mode is used, every fixture within an active section, via its own isolating transformer, draws the same current and burns at the same intensity simultaneously; the ALCMS cannot dim or brighten one green light independently of the others sharing its circuit. Fault detection remains loop-based: the CCR tracks loop voltage against commanded current, and a voltage rise — typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer — is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault, isolated to a specific fixture only through sectionalised field testing.

ALCMS (route / follow-the-greens) → CCR → Centreline Series Circuit Section → Isolating Transformer → Green Fixture

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture body is a die-cast aluminium housing set into an L-868 base can flush with the taxiway pavement, load-rated for aircraft wheel loading up to 100,000kg. A green-filtered lens — glass or high-strength polycarbonate — sits over the LED array or halogen lamp, gasket-sealed with an O-ring for an IP67/IP68 rating against water and de-icing fluid ingress. Internal wiring runs to an L-823 primary connector, sealed for direct burial, which mates to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer housed in its own nearby base can; stainless-steel fasteners and a drainage channel in the base can keep water from pooling around the transformer and cable entries.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination from Rubber Deposits

Green centreline lights are exposed to tyre rubber deposits from aircraft, especially on rapid exit taxiways and heavy-traffic routes. These deposits reduce light output and alter the colour. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and non-abrasive brushes is essential to maintain optical clarity and photometric performance.

Green Filter Degradation

In halogen-based units, the green filter can degrade over time due to thermal stress and UV exposure, causing the colour to shift toward yellow or become less saturated. Regular photometric testing and filter replacement are recommended to ensure compliance with ICAO colour requirements.

Misalignment from Pavement Settlement

Pavement settlement can cause inset lights to tilt, altering the beam pattern and reducing visibility from the cockpit. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are critical to maintain proper beam orientation.

LED Driver Failure

LED-based centreline lights rely on electronic drivers that can fail due to thermal stress, power surges, or component ageing. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers is essential to maintain system reliability and prevent dark spots in the centreline guidance.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Reduced Light Output or Dull Green Colour
Likely Cause
Tyre rubber deposits or general contamination on the lens reducing transmitted light and shifting apparent colour.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for rubber deposits, especially near rapid exit taxiways; take a photometric reading before and after a test clean.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and non-abrasive brushes, and increase cleaning frequency for fixtures on heavy-traffic routes.
Colour Drifting Toward Yellow
Likely Cause
Green filter degradation from thermal stress and UV exposure in a halogen-based unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour check against ICAO chromaticity requirements; check the filter's service age against the recommended replacement interval.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded green filter and re-verify colour compliance with a follow-up photometric test.
Beam Pattern Off-Target or Reduced Visibility From the Cockpit
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement has tilted the inset fixture, altering its beam orientation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture alignment with a digital level or alignment jig against the specified tolerance; inspect surrounding pavement for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-aim the fixture, and address underlying pavement settlement if it is the root cause to prevent recurrence.
Fixture Dark or Dim With Power Present
Likely Cause
LED driver failure from thermal stress, power surges, or component ageing.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify supply voltage is present at the fixture; check driver output for correct current/voltage; inspect the driver for visible signs of thermal or surge damage.
Corrective Action
Replace a failed or degraded LED driver, and confirm restored output with a follow-up photometric check.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the centreline circuit and verify zero energy at the fixture before opening the inset base can or disturbing connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Because centreline lights are relied on in low-visibility ground movement (RVR < 550 m), coordinate with ATC/ground control and raise a NOTAM where a fixture will be dark, keeping vehicles clear of active taxi routes.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and gaskets accounted for while the flush-mounted base can is open, and secure or remove loose material before reinstating the fixture.

Live-Traffic Exposure
Personnel Safety

Inset fixtures sit flush in an active taxiway surface; keep personnel and open base cans clear of the taxi path and use spotters or a closure when working during active ground movement.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected circuit after any cable, connector, or transformer work, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken adjacent fixtures on the same loop.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the base can is bonded/grounded before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify colour, intensity, and flush seating against grade before closing out the work; do not leave a centreline light out of tolerance on a route used in low-visibility operations.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Flush Fit Matters as Much as Photometrics
Mechanical

An inset fixture sitting even slightly proud of grade becomes a snag or FOD risk for aircraft tyres and ground vehicles well before it fails photometrically; verify flush seating against the surrounding pavement grade at every reinstall, not just light output.

Base Can Seal Is the Long-Term Failure Point on Inset Fixtures
Durability

Standing water and de-icing fluid pooling in a recessed base can degrade gaskets and cable glands faster than sun and weather alone; specify seal materials rated for glycol contact and inspect gasket condition at overhaul, not only at initial install.

Colour Consistency Along the Route, Not Just Fixture-by-Fixture
Design

Lamp or LED colour temperature can drift subtly across a long taxiway route even when every individual fixture passes chromaticity spec on its own; check colour consistency by walking a full run rather than testing fixtures in isolation.

Trend Loop Readings, Don't Just Spot-Check
Troubleshooting

Moisture ingress at an L-823 connector usually shows up first as a slow drift in loop insulation resistance, not a hard fault. Logging megger readings over successive maintenance cycles catches a failing splice months before it takes the loop down.

Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED fixture is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual LED load rather than assuming the existing transformer is "close enough."

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven brightness runway-to-runway even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about green taxiway centreline lights: colour, spacing, beam, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a green centreline light?

A green centreline light is a flush-mounted (inset) AGL luminaire installed along a taxiway centreline. It emits steady green light so pilots can hold the aircraft precisely on the centreline during ground movement, especially in low visibility.

Q2Why are taxiway centreline lights green when edge lights are blue?

Edge lights define the lateral boundaries of the taxiway, while centreline lights guide the pilot along the optimal path. Knowing you are between two edges does not show that you are safely centred, and for large aircraft wingspan and engine clearance depend on tracking the centreline closely.

Q3Where are green centreline lights used?

On taxiways at airports supporting low visibility operations, including CAT II/III precision runways, major airports with complex taxiway networks, straight sections, curves and intersections, apron taxi lanes and rapid exit taxiways.

Q4How far apart are green centreline lights spaced?

Typically 15–30 m apart on straight sections, tightening to 7.5–15 m through curves and intersections. Fixtures are recessed into a saw-cut or cored pocket along the taxiway centreline marking using a load-rated inset base can set flush with the pavement.

Q5What is the beam pattern and intensity?

Main beam intensity is 100–1,000 cd (green) per ICAO Annex 14 and IEC 61827, with a vertical main beam of 2°–10° above horizontal, peaking around 3°–4°. Horizontal coverage is omnidirectional (360°) or about ±5° either side of the centreline for uni-directional variants.

Q6How is brightness controlled?

The CCR sets five current steps from 2.8 A to 6.6 A, which correspond to roughly 20% to 100% of rated peak intensity. All fixtures on the loop change together.

Q7How much load can a centreline light take?

Typical wheel-load capacity is 100,000 kg (220,000 lbs), with IP67/IP68 protection and an operating range of −40 °C to +55 °C.

Q8Which standards apply to green centreline lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-46 (taxiway centreline lights), IEC 61827 and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED performance. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should green centreline lights be maintained?

Typical intervals are monthly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for green filter inspection and alignment checks, and annual for LED driver function checks and seal and gasket inspection. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does a centreline light look dull or shift toward yellow?

A dull green is usually tyre rubber and contamination on the lens, especially near rapid exit taxiways, fixed by cleaning with approved solvents and non-abrasive brushes. A shift toward yellow usually means UV and thermal degradation of the green filter in a halogen-based unit, which needs a photometric colour check and filter replacement.
