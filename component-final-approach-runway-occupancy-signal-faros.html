Final Approach Runway Occupancy Signal (FAROS)

An advanced airfield safety system that alerts pilots on final approach when the runway ahead is occupied. Rather than adding a new light fixture, FAROS drives the airport's existing Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) into a flashing mode when a surface-surveillance system detects a conflict, giving pilots a direct visual go-around cue without waiting on an ATC radio call.

Overview

What Is It?

The Final Approach Runway Occupancy Signal (FAROS) is an advanced airfield safety capability that provides visual alerts to pilots on final approach when a runway is occupied. Rather than being a standalone light fixture, FAROS is an add-on control function for the existing Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI): when surface surveillance detects that the runway's protected zone is occupied, the FAROS controller commands the PAPI light units to flash instead of burning steady, giving the approaching pilot a clear, unambiguous "runway not clear" cue without requiring any additional cockpit equipment or waiting on an ATC radio call.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

FAROS exists to close a specific, dangerous gap in runway safety: a pilot on short final has no reliable way to see for themselves whether the runway ahead is actually clear, since another aircraft, vehicle, or obstruction on the far end of a long runway may not be visible from the approach path, and radio communication about runway status can be delayed, missed, or misunderstood. By switching the PAPI — a light array the pilot is already looking at for glide-path guidance — into a flashing state exactly when the runway is occupied, the system gives a go-around decision cue that doesn't depend on air traffic control instruction or the pilot's own visual judgement of a runway they may not be able to fully see. Its purpose, in short, is to convert a hidden runway-occupancy hazard into a direct, self-evident visual warning at the single moment — final approach — when a wrong decision has the least time to be corrected.

Application

Where Is It Used?

FAROS systems are installed at busy airports with high traffic volumes and complex runway configurations where approach conflicts are a significant risk. They are typically used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where safety margins are critical
  • Major international airports with intersecting runways and complex approach paths
  • Runways with high traffic density where incursion risk is significant
  • Airports implementing advanced safety management systems
  • Runways with limited visibility operations where visual cues are essential
Placement

Installation & Location

FAROS is retrofitted onto the runway's existing PAPI installation — typically a 4-box L-880 (or 2-box L-881) array sited beside the runway roughly 300 m (1,000 ft) from the threshold, on the same low, frangible-mounted ground stands used for standard PAPI. No new elevated light fixture or dedicated FAROS bar is required; the FAROS driver is added inline between the PAPI's own power/lamp-control unit and its light heads so it can switch the units into a flashing state without disturbing their aiming or glide-path calibration. The system is tied into the airport's surface surveillance source (ASDE-X, ASSC, or ADS-B-based tracking) via a FAROS controller that evaluates occupancy status for the defined activation zones and issues the flash command on detection of a conflict. The controller, its power supply, and its monitoring unit are usually housed in an equipment shelter near the PAPI site, and installation is coordinated closely with the surveillance system's own commissioning to validate accurate occupancy detection before activation is enabled.

Function

How Does It Work?

The FAROS system operates through an integrated surveillance and alert mechanism:

  • Surveillance: Surface surveillance data (ASDE-X, ASSC, or ADS-B-based tracking) tracks aircraft and vehicle positions on the airfield.
  • Detection: The FAROS processing system evaluates whether a defined runway activation zone is occupied by an aircraft or vehicle.
  • Activation: When an activation zone is occupied, the FAROS controller switches the runway's existing PAPI light units from steady-burning to a flashing state.
  • Alert: The flashing PAPI gives the approaching pilot a clear, unambiguous indication that the runway is not clear for landing.
  • Deactivation: When the activation zone is clear, the PAPI reverts to its normal steady glide-path indication and the pilot proceeds with the approach.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Unlike a series-circuit AGL fixture, FAROS is not commanded by the tower or an ALCMS brightness step — it is a self-contained, sensor-driven system that decides its own state in real time and acts on the PAPI it is attached to, rather than on a fixture of its own. Surface surveillance feeds (ASDE-X, ASSC, or ADS-B-based tracking) are continuously ingested by the FAROS occupancy processor, which evaluates whether the protected runway segment is occupied and, on detecting a conflict, drives the PAPI's existing light heads into a flashing mode independent of any ATC action. Because a missed occupied-runway call is far more dangerous than an unnecessary flash, the logic is fail-safe by design: any loss of confidence in the surveillance feed, a processor fault, or a communications dropout defaults the system to the conservative "occupied" (flashing) state rather than reverting silently to steady-burning, and the fault is separately reported to the ALCMS/tower for maintenance action.

Surveillance Feed → Occupancy Processor → FAROS Driver → PAPI Light Heads Flash (fail-safe to "occupied")

Construction

Physical Construction

FAROS does not introduce a new optical fixture: the light-emitting hardware remains the runway's existing PAPI light units (typically a sealed LED or halogen luminaire in a die-cast aluminium enclosure, anodised or powder-coated, with a red/white split beam behind a toughened glass lens), mounted low to the ground on frangible couplings beside the runway per standard PAPI siting. What FAROS adds is a driver/interface module — usually a card or unit built into or wired alongside the PAPI's power and control unit (PCU) — that can override the PAPI's normal steady output and switch the light units into a flashing pattern on command. This driver electronics package is potted or conformal-coated for moisture protection and connected via sealed, direct-burial-rated cable glands to a dedicated mains feed and UPS backup circuit, since the FAROS function must remain available even through a brief power interruption. A weatherproof junction box near the PAPI site houses the interface to the surface-surveillance/RWSL, A-SMGCS, or ALCMS control link, with corrosion-resistant fasteners and hardware used throughout given the fixture's exposed, low-lying siting near the runway environment.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Surveillance Data Latency

Delays in surveillance data processing can cause the FAROS system to react slowly, potentially failing to alert pilots in time. Regular system performance monitoring and network optimization are essential.

False Alarms

The FAROS system can trigger false alarms due to surveillance errors or system faults. False alarms can desensitize pilots and reduce the effectiveness of the system. Regular calibration and system validation are critical.

Communication Infrastructure Failure

FAROS systems rely on network communication between surveillance sensors, processing units, and output devices. Network failures can result in system-wide loss of functionality. Redundant communication paths are essential.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

System Fails to Activate on Runway Occupancy
Likely Cause
Stale or dropped surveillance feed from RWSL, A-SMGCS, or ALCMS, or the activation logic did not receive a valid trigger from the processing unit.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the surveillance interface for feed currency and correct target correlation; review the processing unit event log for a missed or rejected trigger; run a simulated occupancy scenario end-to-end.
Corrective Action
Restore or re-sync the surveillance data link, correct any feed configuration mismatch, and re-validate activation with a follow-up end-to-end test.
Frequent False Alarms / Nuisance Activations
Likely Cause
Surveillance target-correlation errors, drifted calibration, or outdated firmware producing spurious occupancy detections.
Diagnostic Steps
Review activation logs to identify the pattern and source of the false triggers; confirm surveillance sensor calibration; check processing unit firmware against the manufacturer's supported version.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate the surveillance source, update firmware/control software to close known false-alarm issues, and re-run validation testing before returning the system to service.
Delayed Activation / Slow Response
Likely Cause
Surveillance data processing latency or network congestion between sensors, the processing unit, and the light heads.
Diagnostic Steps
Monitor end-to-end response time against the designed activation window; inspect network switches and communication paths for congestion or degraded links.
Corrective Action
Optimize or reroute network traffic, replace degraded network hardware, and re-measure the response window to confirm it is back within tolerance.
System-Wide Loss of Function
Likely Cause
Failure of a shared communication path or network switch between surveillance sensors, processing units, and output devices, with no redundant path available.
Diagnostic Steps
Check network cabling, switches, and redundant communication paths for a single point of failure; confirm the processing unit is reachable from all connected sensors and light heads.
Corrective Action
Restore or replace the failed link/switch, verify redundant paths are active, and re-test system-wide activation before standing the system back up.
Single Light Head Dark or Dim
Likely Cause
Failed LED module or driver at that light head, or a corroded/loose connection at the housing.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the light head is receiving an activation command from the processing unit; inspect the housing seal and connector for corrosion or moisture ingress; check driver output at the fixture.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED module or driver, re-terminate corroded connections, and confirm the housing seal is restored to its IP rating.
System Drops Out on Mains Failure
Likely Cause
UPS battery has degraded or failed to transfer load, leaving the system without backup power during a mains interruption.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the UPS under a simulated outage; check battery health and transfer-switch operation; confirm the dedicated mains supply and its protective devices are functioning correctly.
Corrective Action
Replace a degraded UPS battery or faulty transfer switch, and re-verify uninterrupted operation with a follow-up simulated outage test.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Surveillance Feed Loss or Staleness
High Severity
Root Cause
Dropped or stale data link from the RWSL, A-SMGCS, or ALCMS surveillance source, or a target-correlation error at the interface into the processing unit.
Effect
System fails to activate on genuine runway occupancy — the most safety-critical failure mode for a system whose entire purpose is a timely visual alert to the landing aircraft.
Detection Method
Surveillance interface feed-currency and correlation checks, processing unit event log review, and end-to-end simulated occupancy testing.
Calibration Drift / Firmware Fault Causing False Alarms
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Surveillance sensor calibration drifting out of tolerance over time, or outdated processing-unit firmware producing spurious occupancy detections.
Effect
Repeated nuisance activations that desensitise pilots to the alert, undermining the system's effectiveness even when it later activates for a genuine occupancy event.
Detection Method
Activation log review to identify the pattern and source of false triggers, sensor calibration verification, and firmware version audit against the manufacturer's supported release.
Communication Path / Network Switch Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Failure of a shared network link or switch between surveillance sensors, the processing unit, and the light heads, with no redundant path in place or available.
Effect
System-wide loss of function — every light head goes unreachable regardless of individual fixture health, since the entire system depends on this shared communication chain.
Detection Method
Network cabling and switch inspection for a single point of failure, and confirmation that the processing unit remains reachable from all connected sensors and light heads.
UPS Battery Degradation / Transfer-Switch Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Aging or degraded UPS battery capacity, or a transfer switch that fails to hand load over cleanly during a mains interruption.
Effect
Complete system drop-out during a mains failure, at precisely the moment continuous surveillance-driven protection is most needed.
Detection Method
Simulated outage testing of the UPS, battery health checks, and verification of transfer-switch operation as part of scheduled maintenance.
Individual Light Head LED/Driver Failure
Low Severity
Root Cause
Failed LED module or driver at a specific light head, or a corroded/loose connection at the housing.
Effect
That light head stays dark or dim on an otherwise valid activation, reducing the alert's conspicuity without affecting the rest of the system.
Detection Method
Confirmation that the head received a valid activation command, housing seal and connector inspection, and driver output verification at the fixture.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Mains Circuit, Not a Series Loop
Electrical Safety

The FAROS driver runs on a dedicated mains branch circuit rather than the runway's CCR series loop; isolate and lock out at the FAROS driver's own supply point, and don't assume the PAPI is dead just because the FAROS circuit is isolated — the PAPI light units keep their own supply (mains for Style A, CCR series loop for Style B).

Surveillance Integration Dependency
Operational

The flashing indication is driven entirely by the surface surveillance feed (ASDE-X, ASSC, or ADS-B-based tracking); any work on the FAROS driver or the PAPI light units must be followed by an integration check confirming occupancy detection still correctly triggers flashing before returning the system to service.

PAPI Site Work Coordination
Operational

Because FAROS is retrofitted onto the PAPI's own hardware, work here follows the PAPI's own access, frangibility, and aiming-preservation procedures rather than a separate approach-lighting-structure procedure, coordinated with ATC and covered by a NOTAM for the affected runway.

Equipment Shelter Access
Electrical Safety

The FAROS controller, power supply, and monitoring unit are typically housed in an equipment shelter; follow standard electrical lockout procedure at the shelter's own supply before servicing the controller, independent of the field circuit isolation.

Verify Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any field wiring or controller work, test the affected lights and confirm the surveillance interface still reports correctly before restoring the system to active monitoring.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return FAROS to service with an unverified occupancy trigger; confirm detection accuracy and flash synchronisation against the parent ALS system before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Aiming Drift Is a PAPI Calibration Issue, Not a FAROS One
Mechanical

Because FAROS rides on the existing PAPI hardware rather than a fixture of its own, any azimuth/elevation drift is a PAPI aiming and pad-settlement issue — ground movement, frost heave, or a knocked stand — not something introduced by the FAROS retrofit. Re-verify PAPI aim against the extended runway centreline on the PAPI's own periodic schedule, and don't chase a "FAROS aiming" problem that is really a PAPI calibration problem.

Test the UPS as a Real Load Transfer, Not a Status Light
Electrical Safety

The safety case for FAROS depends on the mains-to-UPS backup circuit actually switching over correctly under a genuine mains loss. A healthy standby indicator doesn't confirm that — schedule an actual load-transfer test on the UPS circuit rather than relying on its status LED alone.

A Driver Swap Can Silently Change Cadence Without Touching Intensity
Retrofit

The FAROS driver only overrides the PAPI's steady/flashing state — it doesn't regulate beam intensity, which stays governed by the PAPI's own power and control unit. After swapping a FAROS driver module, confirm flash rate and duty cycle match spec; don't assume the retrofit affects intensity, and don't skip re-checking the PAPI's own photometrics if the PCU was touched during the same visit.

Validate Through the Real Control Interface
System Design

A FAROS fixture that flashes correctly on a manual bench test can still fail its intended function if the interface to RWSL, A-SMGCS, or ALCMS doesn't correctly trigger on actual runway occupancy. Validate through the live control interface, not just the fixture in isolation.

Frangible-Stand Vibration Loosens Fasteners Before It Damages Housings
Mechanical

PAPI/FAROS units sit on ground-level frangible stands exposed to jet-blast and continuous low-level vibration, which loosens fastener torque well before it produces any visible housing damage. Check fastener torque on a defined interval rather than waiting for a visual sign of movement to prompt a check.

Wind-Driven Rain at Ground Level Still Isn't a Bench Test
Durability

The IP65/67 rating on the PAPI/FAROS enclosure reflects rain driven horizontally by wind across open airfield terrain — angles a static bench seal-check never replicates. A gasket that seals fine on the bench can still leak in service, so don't treat a bench check as representative of in-service performance.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about FAROS (Final Approach Runway Occupancy Signal): what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is FAROS?

The Final Approach Runway Occupancy Signal (FAROS) is an airfield safety capability that gives pilots on final approach a visual alert when the runway is occupied. It is an add-on control function for the existing PAPI rather than a standalone light fixture.

Q2How does FAROS alert pilots?

When surface surveillance detects that the runway's protected zone is occupied, the FAROS controller commands the PAPI light units to flash instead of burning steady. This gives the approaching pilot a clear runway-not-clear cue without extra cockpit equipment or waiting for an ATC radio call.

Q3What is the sequence of operation?

Surface surveillance (ASDE-X, ASSC or ADS-B-based tracking) tracks aircraft and vehicles, and the FAROS processing system checks whether a defined runway activation zone is occupied. If it is, the controller switches the PAPI from steady to flashing, and when the zone is clear the PAPI reverts to steady.

Q4Does FAROS add new lights?

No. It adds no new luminaire. It drives the runway's existing PAPI light units (the L-880/L-881 array), which keep their normal colour, beam pattern and intensity. Only the cadence changes, at about 60 flashes per minute adjustable at the FAROS driver.

Q5Where is FAROS installed?

At busy airports with high traffic and complex runway configurations where approach conflicts are a significant risk: precision approach runways (CAT II and III), major international airports with intersecting runways, and runways with high incursion risk.

Q6How is FAROS powered?

The FAROS driver runs on a dedicated mains branch circuit (120/240 V AC single-phase, regional variants available) with UPS backup, not a series CCR loop. The PAPI units stay on their own normal supply, and the driver has fault detection with UPS transfer and mains-loss monitoring.

Q7How does FAROS connect to other systems?

Through a low-voltage data link via a weatherproof junction box to RWSL, A-SMGCS or ALCMS, with the driver as a potted or conformal-coated interface module that overrides the PAPI's steady output and regulates flash rate. Enclosures are IP65 or IP67.

Q8Which standards apply?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-28H (PAPI systems), FAA Order JO 6850.2C (visual guidance lighting systems, including FAROS siting and integration) and FAA AC 150/5345-53 (equipment certification). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9How often should FAROS be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for PAPI lens and optics cleaning, semi-annual for surveillance interface validation and UPS and power supply checks, and annual for flash intensity and rate, activation logic test, communication link inspection, housing and mounting inspection and software and firmware update review. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems with FAROS?

Common problems are surveillance data latency or feed loss, false alarms from calibration drift or firmware faults, communication path failure, UPS battery degradation or transfer-switch failure, and individual light head LED or driver failure. Failure to activate on runway occupancy, frequent nuisance activations and delayed activation are the typical symptoms.
