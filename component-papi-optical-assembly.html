PAPI Optical Assembly

The precision optical heart of a PAPI light unit – comprising the reflector, prism, lens, and optical filters that collimate the light source and create the sharp red/white transition. This assembly determines the beam pattern, colour separation, and transition sharpness that pilots rely on for accurate glide slope guidance.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Optical Assembly is the precision optical subsystem within each PAPI light unit. It is responsible for collecting the raw light from the lamp or LED module, shaping it into a tightly collimated beam, and introducing a sharp colour transition between white (above the glide path) and red (below the glide path). The assembly is typically a modular, replaceable unit that can be removed and serviced independently of the fixture housing and electronics.

The assembly consists of a parabolic or faceted reflector, a precision‑ground prism (often a hybrid glass/polymer element), a protective optical window, and colour filters (for the red sector). The optical design is critical: it must deliver a beam with a transition sharpness of less than 3 arc‑minutes, uniform intensity across the beam, and precise alignment of the transition angle to the required glide slope (typically 3°). The assembly is factory‑calibrated and must be handled with extreme care to maintain its optical performance.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The optical assembly is what actually creates PAPI's defining feature: a boundary a pilot can trust down to the arc-minute. Every other component in the system — the power supply, the elevation and azimuth mechanisms, the control unit — exists to support this one optical element in producing a red/white split that is sharp rather than gradual, because a soft, blurred transition would turn a precise glide-slope indication into a vague zone a pilot could only estimate rather than read. Its factory calibration and delicate handling requirements follow directly from that role: any distortion introduced into the reflector, prism, or filter after calibration doesn't just reduce light output, it can shift or soften the very boundary the whole PAPI concept depends on being exact.

Optical Design

Anatomy of the Optical Assembly

The PAPI optical assembly is engineered to produce a beam with a sharp, unambiguous red/white boundary. It consists of several key elements working together:

Reflector

Parabolic or faceted reflector (aluminium with vacuum‑deposited coating) that collects and collimates light from the source into a near‑parallel beam.

Prism / Lens

Precision‑ground optical prism or aspheric lens that introduces the colour transition. The prism separates the beam into white (upper) and red (lower) sectors with a sharp boundary.

Colour Filter

Dielectric or glass filter that transmits only the red wavelengths in the lower sector, creating the red colour while maintaining high transmission efficiency.

Optical Window

High‑transmission glass or polycarbonate window with anti‑reflective coating, protecting the prism and filter from contamination while maintaining optical clarity.

Alignment Mount

Precision‑machined mount that holds all optical elements in exact alignment. This mount interfaces with the fixture housing and provides adjustment points for azimuth and elevation.

Thermal Compensation

Design features (e.g., expansion joints, specific material choices) that maintain optical alignment across the full operating temperature range (-40°C to +55°C).

Application

Where Is It Used?

The PAPI optical assembly is a core component of every PAPI system, and its performance directly impacts the safety of precision approaches. It is found in:

  • Standard 4‑unit PAPI systems – on CAT I, II, and III precision runways worldwide.
  • APAPI (2‑unit) systems – using a simplified optical assembly with similar transition characteristics.
  • Helicopter PAPI – with steeper angle optics (4°–6°) designed for rotorcraft.
  • Portable PAPI – ruggedized assemblies for temporary deployments.
  • As a replacement part – installed during maintenance or upgrade of existing PAPI fixtures.
  • In LED‑retrofit kits – where an existing halogen PAPI is upgraded to LED, the optical assembly may be replaced or adapted.
Placement

Installation & Location

The optical assembly fits into the front of the PAPI fixture housing, seated in the precision-machined alignment mount that positions it relative to the lamp or LED source behind it and locks it to the housing's own azimuth and elevation adjustment mechanism. Because the whole PAPI installation depends on each of the four units being set to the exact same glide-slope angle relative to the others, the assembly is only fitted after the fixture housing itself has been surveyed and levelled on its concrete or frangible mounting base along the wing bar, and any elevation adjustment is made through the mount rather than by tilting the fixture body. The optical window faces outward toward the approach path, sealed against the housing to keep moisture and dust off the prism and colour filter behind it, and the unit is handled and transported in its factory packaging until the moment of fitting to avoid disturbing its calibration. Replacement in the field is done as a complete assembly swap, with the new unit's alignment checked against the glide-path angle using a theodolite or the manufacturer's alignment tool before the fixture is returned to service.

Function

How Does It Work?

The PAPI optical assembly transforms the raw light from the lamp or LED into the characteristic glide slope beam:

  • Light Collection & Collimation: The reflector captures light emitted from the source and reflects it into a parallel beam. The shape of the reflector (parabolic or faceted) determines the beam spread and intensity distribution.
  • Colour Separation: The collimated beam passes through the prism. The prism is designed with a specific refractive index and angle so that light rays above a certain elevation angle (the transition angle) remain white, while rays below that angle are refracted and directed through a red filter. This creates a sharp, distinct boundary between red and white.
  • Beam Shaping: Additional optical elements (or the prism itself) shape the beam’s horizontal and vertical spread to meet ICAO/FAA requirements. The beam is typically narrow vertically (2°–3°) to provide a crisp transition, and wider horizontally (±10°–15°) to be visible from the approach corridor.
  • Transition Sharpness: The transition is engineered to be less than 3 arc‑minutes, meaning the pilot sees an abrupt change from red to white over a very small change in aircraft position. This precision is critical for accurate glide path control.
  • Intensity Uniformity: The optical design ensures that the intensity is consistent across the beam, avoiding hot spots or dim areas that could mislead the pilot.
  • Environmental Protection: The optical window and sealed housing protect the internal optics from moisture, dust, and contaminants, maintaining performance over the fixture’s lifetime.
Diagram

Where One Beam Becomes Two Colours & Control Logic

The assembly's entire job happens in the handoff from reflector to prism: the reflector turns a point source into a single collimated beam, and the prism — not a mechanical shutter or a second lamp — is what splits that one beam into white above the glide path and red below it, with the red sector passing through a dichroic filter tuned to 610–660 nm. Because the split is purely optical, the transition between the two colours can be engineered to under 3 arc-minutes: sharp enough that a pilot correcting glide path sees the change almost as a hard edge rather than a gradual fade.

Construction

Physical Construction

The assembly is built around a precision reflector or lens system housed in its own sub-frame, with a split filter or dual-segment lens — clear on the upper portion, red on the lower — positioned at the exact focal point needed to produce a sharp white-to-red transition rather than a gradual fade. Optical elements are ground or moulded glass or acrylic held to tight geometric tolerance, since the transition angle itself is the calibration reference the PAPI unit's inclination is set against. The sub-frame mounts into the fixture housing on a bracket or rail system designed for quick removal and replacement as a complete sealed module, so the optics can be serviced or swapped without disturbing the housing's tilt calibration. Gasket or O-ring seals around the module's perimeter maintain the fixture's overall IP rating once the assembly is reinstalled, and locating pins or a keyed mounting face ensure the module can only be refitted in its correct rotational position.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Lens & Prism Cleaning
Quarterly

Wipe the optical prism and window free of dust, film, and insect residue so light output and transition sharpness stay within spec.

Transition Angle Verification
Annual

Confirm the red/white transition angle remains within the sub-3-arcminute tolerance using calibrated test equipment, since a shifted transition misleads the glide path.

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify beam intensity and colour against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer to catch prism haze or lamp/LED degradation.

Red Filter Transmission Check
Semi-annual

Assess the red filter for UV-driven transmission loss or discolouration, since output below spec weakens the red sector's distinct colour.

Optical Window Seal Inspection
Annual

Check the tempered glass or polycarbonate window seal for cracking or compression set to keep the assembly watertight when installed in its housing.

Mounting Flange & Alignment Check
Semi-annual

Confirm the assembly is securely fastened to its bracket and hasn't shifted, since even a small mounting tilt alters the projected transition angle.

Reflector Condition Check
Annual

Inspect the parabolic or faceted reflector coating for pitting or delamination that could reduce reflected output.

Lamp/LED Replacement Schedule Review
Annual

Track lamp or LED hours against the manufacturer's replacement cycle so light output doesn't fall below threshold before scheduled service.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Optical Surface Contamination

Dust, rubber deposits, insect residue, and de‑icing fluids on the prism, filter, or window can scatter light, reduce intensity, and blur the transition. Routine cleaning with appropriate optical wipes and non‑abrasive solvents is critical. Never use paper towels or abrasive materials.

Prism or Filter Degradation

Over time, UV exposure and thermal cycling can cause the prism material to yellow or the dielectric filter coatings to delaminate. This reduces transmission and shifts the transition angle. Regular photometric testing can detect degradation before it affects glide path accuracy.

Misalignment During Maintenance

If the optical assembly is removed and reinstalled, even a tiny angular error (0.05°, or 3 arcmin) can shift the glide path by several feet at the threshold. Always use a theodolite or precision alignment fixture to verify the transition angle after servicing.

Condensation Inside the Assembly

Failed seals or blocked pressure vents can allow moisture ingress, causing fogging on the internal surfaces. This scatters light and ruins the transition. Inspect seals and vents regularly, and replace desiccant packs (if fitted) during maintenance.

Thermal Stress Cracking

The optical window or prism can crack due to rapid temperature changes or mechanical shock. Handle the assembly with care during installation and replacement. Inspect for cracks or chips that could affect beam quality.

Incorrect Filter Colour

If the red filter is replaced with a non‑approved type, the colour may not meet ICAO/FAA chromaticity requirements. Always use OEM‑specified filters and verify colour with a colorimeter during commissioning.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Transition Line Appears Blurred or Indistinct
Likely Cause
Contamination on the prism, filter, or window is scattering light, or condensation has formed on internal surfaces from a failed seal or vent.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect optical surfaces for dust, rubber deposits, or fogging; check seals and pressure vents for failure; run a photometric transition check.
Corrective Action
Clean optical surfaces with approved optical wipes and non-abrasive solvents, or repair the seal/vent and replace desiccant if fitted, then re-verify the transition.
Glide Path Angle Reading Off Design Value
Likely Cause
A small angular misalignment introduced during servicing or reinstallation of the optical assembly.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-verify the transition angle with a theodolite or precision alignment fixture, comparing against the designed glide path angle.
Corrective Action
Re-align the optical assembly to the specified transition angle and re-confirm with a flight inspection where required.
Reduced Intensity or Colour Shift Over Time
Likely Cause
UV exposure and thermal cycling have caused the prism to yellow or the dielectric filter coating to delaminate.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity and chromaticity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; compare against baseline commissioning data.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded prism or filter with an OEM-specified part, and re-verify colour and intensity before returning to service.
Visible Crack or Chip in the Optical Window
Likely Cause
Rapid temperature change or mechanical shock has cracked the optical window or prism.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the window and prism for cracks or chips, and check whether beam quality is affected via a photometric check.
Corrective Action
Replace the cracked component, handling the replacement carefully during installation to avoid repeat damage.
Red Sector Fails Chromaticity Check
Likely Cause
A non-approved or incorrect red filter was fitted, not meeting ICAO/FAA chromaticity requirements.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify colour with a colorimeter and compare the filter part number against the OEM specification.
Corrective Action
Replace with the correct OEM-specified filter and re-verify chromaticity with a colorimeter before commissioning.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Optical Surface Contamination
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Dust, rubber deposits, insect residue, or de-icing fluid accumulating on the prism, filter, or window over time between cleanings.
Effect
Scattered or attenuated light output, blurring the red/white transition and reducing effective glide-path visibility to approaching aircraft.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of optical surfaces and a photometric transition check during routine maintenance.
Prism / Filter Material Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure and repeated thermal cycling causing the prism to yellow or the dielectric filter coating to delaminate.
Effect
Reduced light transmission and a gradual shift in the transition angle, degrading photometric performance below ICAO/FAA thresholds.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric intensity and chromaticity testing compared against baseline commissioning data.
Alignment Drift After Servicing
High Severity
Root Cause
Small angular error introduced when the optical assembly is removed and reinstalled without a precision alignment fixture or theodolite.
Effect
The glide path angle shifts off design value, moving the effective touchdown point and potentially placing the aircraft outside the intended approach path.
Detection Method
Theodolite or precision alignment fixture verification of the transition angle, confirmed where required by flight inspection.
Seal / Vent Failure & Internal Condensation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Aging or damaged seals and blocked pressure vents allowing moisture ingress into the optical assembly housing.
Effect
Fogging on internal optical surfaces that scatters light and blurs the red/white transition, sometimes intermittently as conditions change.
Detection Method
Inspection of seals and vents, checking for internal fogging, and replacement-interval tracking of desiccant packs where fitted.
Optical Window / Prism Cracking
High Severity
Root Cause
Rapid temperature change or mechanical shock during handling, installation, or servicing of the assembly.
Effect
Cracked or chipped optics distort the beam pattern and can fail the unit outright, requiring immediate replacement.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the window and prism for cracks or chips, paired with a photometric check to confirm beam quality.
Non-Approved Filter Substitution
Low Severity
Root Cause
A non-OEM or incorrect red filter fitted during repair, not meeting ICAO/FAA chromaticity specifications.
Effect
The red sector fails chromaticity requirements, risking a misread transition colour for approaching pilots.
Detection Method
Colorimeter verification of filter colour and cross-check of the fitted part number against the OEM specification.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Handling
Electrical Safety

Isolate and lock out the fixture's supply before removing or fitting the optical assembly, and confirm the lamp or LED module behind it is de-energised, even though the assembly itself carries no power connections.

High-Intensity Beam Eye Safety
Personnel Safety

PAPI beams are designed for visibility from several miles out; never look directly into an energised unit at close range during testing or alignment checks, and warn nearby personnel before re-energising.

Halogen Lamp Heat & Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow a halogen lamp source to cool fully before working behind the assembly, and never touch the bulb envelope with bare skin; skin oils cause localised hot spots that lead to premature bulb failure.

Optical Surface Contamination
Mechanical

Handle the assembly by its housing, not the prism or window face; fingerprints, dust, or cleaning with the wrong solvent can degrade the transition sharpness this unit is factory-calibrated to deliver. Keep it in its factory packaging until the moment of fitting.

Field Service Is Swap-Only
Compliance

Do not attempt to disassemble, re-seat the prism, or adjust the internal optics in the field; the assembly is a sealed, factory-calibrated unit and any degraded or damaged unit is replaced as a complete assembly.

Frangibility of the Parent Fixture
Mechanical

Where the PAPI fixture body sits on a frangible mount along the wing bar, never over-torque or defeat that mounting while accessing the optical assembly; it must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft on a low approach or go-around.

Verify Glide-Path Alignment Across All Four Units
Compliance

After fitting a replacement assembly, check the transition angle against the glide-path using a theodolite or the manufacturer's alignment tool before returning the fixture to service; a single unit out of step with the other three in the wing bar gives pilots a misleading glide-slope indication.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Fingerprints and Micro-Scratches Degrade Transition Sharpness
Handling

The prism and colour-transition edge are precision-ground to a tight tolerance, and even a light fingerprint or a fine cleaning scratch across that edge can soften the white/red transition enough to fail a photometric check, well before it's visible to the naked eye. Handle the optical face only by its mounting edges and clean it exactly per the manufacturer's method.

Thermal Cycling Can Shift a Bonded Prism Assembly
Reliability

On assemblies using a glass/polymer hybrid prism bonded with an optical adhesive, repeated thermal cycling over years of outdoor service can very gradually shift the bond line and, with it, the transition angle. A unit that passed acceptance testing can drift out of tolerance years later without any visible damage, which is why periodic transition-angle verification belongs in the overhaul cycle.

Housing Re-Aim Doesn't Correct an Internal Optical Shift
Troubleshooting

If field checks show the glide path angle drifting on a unit whose housing aim hasn't been touched, don't assume re-aiming the housing will fix it; verify the optical assembly's internal transition angle first, since re-aiming a fixture with a shifted internal optic just moves the error rather than correcting it.

Condensation Inside the Housing Acts Like a Diffuser
Environmental

A compromised gasket that lets humid air into the housing produces condensation on the inside of the optical window, which softens the beam's transition sharpness in a way that can look like an optical fault. Check for a moisture path before assuming the prism itself has degraded.

Verify Colour Filter Fade Independently of Intensity Readings
Testing

UV exposure fades red filter material gradually, and a unit can still measure within white-sector intensity tolerance while its red sector has drifted toward pink and fallen out of chromaticity spec. Check red-sector chromaticity as its own test point, not just overall intensity.

Store Spare Assemblies in Their Original Packaging Until Use
Maintenance

A factory-calibrated spare assembly left loose in a maintenance vehicle or store room is exposed to dust, impact, and handling damage well before it's ever installed. Keep spares in their protective packaging and verify transition sharpness at receipt and again immediately before installation.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the PAPI optical assembly: reflector, prism and filter, transition sharpness, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is the PAPI optical assembly?

The precision optical subsystem in each PAPI light unit. It collects light from the lamp or LED module, shapes it into a tightly collimated beam, and creates a sharp colour transition between the red and white sectors.

Q2Why is the optical assembly so important?

It creates PAPI's defining feature, a boundary a pilot can trust down to the arc-minute. Every other part of the system exists to support this.

Q3Where is the optical assembly used?

In every PAPI system, including standard 4-unit PAPI on CAT I, II and III runways and APAPI (2-unit) systems.

Q4How does the optical assembly work?

A reflector captures the source's light into a parallel beam, then a prism and red filter divide the beam into red and white sectors at the set angle, and a protective window seals the assembly.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

A transition sharpness under 3 arc-minutes (0.05°), a transition angle of typically 3° (2.5°-4° range, factory set), a red filter transmission above 80% at 610-660 nm, an optical window transmission above 92%, and IP67/IP68 sealing when installed in its housing.

Q6What are the components made of?

An aluminium reflector with a protective coating (parabolic or faceted), a prism of high-index optical glass (such as BK7 or SF15) or precision-moulded polymer, and a tempered glass or UV-stabilised polycarbonate window.

Q7Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-28, IEC TS 61827, FAA Engineering Brief 67 (EB-67D) and ASTM E1164 (spectrophotometric colour data).

Q8How often should the optical assembly be maintained?

Quarterly lens and prism cleaning; semi-annual red filter transmission checks; and annual transition angle verification, photometric intensity checks and optical window seal inspection.

Q9Why does the transition line look blurred?

Contamination on the prism, filter or window may be scattering light, or condensation may have formed inside from a failed seal or vent. Inspect the optical surfaces and seal.
