PAPI Elevation Adjustment

The precision vertical angle mechanism that sets the beam elevation of each PAPI light unit to achieve the required glide slope. This adjustment determines the exact angle at which the red/white transition occurs, directly affecting the approach path that pilots follow during landing.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Elevation Adjustment is the mechanical system that allows the vertical (up/down) angle of a PAPI light unit to be set with extreme precision. This adjustment determines the angle of the red/white transition beam relative to the horizontal plane. For a standard fixed‑wing PAPI, the transition is set to 3° above the horizontal—the nominal glide slope. However, this angle can be adjusted to suit specific runway requirements, obstacle clearance needs, or aircraft types.

The elevation adjustment is typically a fine‑threaded mechanism integrated into the mounting base or a dedicated elevation sub‑assembly. It allows the entire PAPI unit to be tilted up or down in controlled increments, with a typical range of 2.5° to 4.5° and a resolution of 0.02° or better.

In a standard four‑unit PAPI system, each unit is set to a slightly different elevation angle to produce the characteristic red/white pattern:

  • Unit 1 (far from threshold): lowest angle (e.g., 2°30′)
  • Unit 2: 2°50′
  • Unit 3: 3°10′
  • Unit 4 (nearest threshold): 3°30′

This staggered arrangement creates the 2‑white/2‑red indication when the aircraft is on the correct 3° glide path.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The elevation adjustment is what actually encodes the glide slope into the PAPI array — without it, four identical light units would just be four identical light units, not an instrument. It is the fractions of a degree separating each unit's angle that create the boundaries a pilot reads as "too high," "on path," or "too low," so the mechanism's precision directly determines how precisely the visual indication matches the intended approach angle. A coarse or imprecise adjustment would blur those boundaries, widening the transition zone between red and white until the indication became ambiguous exactly where it needs to be most decisive — right around the correct glide path. Because the whole system's credibility rests on this handful of arc-minutes being set correctly on every one of the four units, the elevation adjustment is arguably the single mechanism responsible for whether a PAPI tells the truth about an aircraft's position on approach.

Architecture

Key Components of the Elevation Mechanism

The elevation adjustment mechanism is a precision assembly designed for repeatable, accurate angle setting:

Pivot Hinge

A precision hinge or bearing at the rear of the mounting base that allows the PAPI unit to tilt up and down around a fixed axis.

Adjustment Screw

A fine‑pitch threaded rod that raises or lowers the front of the unit. Typically M6×0.75 or M8×1.0, providing 0.02°–0.05° per full turn.

Shim System

An alternative to screws, using precision ground shims that are inserted or removed to set the angle. Provides excellent stability but requires a stock of shims.

Locking Mechanism

Clamping bolts or locknuts that secure the elevation setting after adjustment, preventing drift from vibration or thermal changes.

Angle Index

Scale markings or a vernier scale that indicates the current elevation angle, aiding in setting and verification.

Corrosion Protection

Stainless steel or coated components with anti‑seize lubricant, ensuring the mechanism remains adjustable over decades of service.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The elevation adjustment is a standard feature on all PAPI systems, with variations for different operational requirements:

  • Fixed‑wing runways – typically set to 3° (2.5°–4° range), with each unit staggered for the 4‑light pattern.
  • Helicopter PAPI – set to steeper angles, typically 4°–6°, to accommodate helicopter approach profiles.
  • Runways with obstacle constraints – adjusted to steeper angles (e.g., 3.5°–4°) to provide obstacle clearance.
  • Runways with noise abatement – steeper approaches can reduce noise footprint, requiring non‑standard elevation settings.
  • Portable PAPI – with quick‑adjust mechanisms for rapid reconfiguration at temporary airfields.
  • Calibration and maintenance – used whenever the glide slope angle needs to be verified or changed.
Placement

Installation & Location

The elevation mechanism is built into the fixture head where it pivots on the mounting base, positioned so a technician can access the adjustment screw or graduated scale without dismounting the unit from its foundation. It's set during initial commissioning, after the mounting base and wing bar are already fixed in their final surveyed position, since the elevation angle only makes sense relative to a base that's already correctly sited and levelled. Each unit on the bar is adjusted individually to its own staggered angle (each unit typically offset by a fraction of a degree from its neighbours to produce the four-light pattern), and the mechanism is locked off once set so vibration or weather doesn't drift the angle between calibration checks. On portable PAPI, the same mechanism is designed for quicker access, since the whole system may be re-levelled and re-aimed each time it's redeployed.

Function

How Does It Work?

The elevation adjustment process is a precise operation that requires care and the right tools:

  • Initial Setup: The PAPI unit is mounted on its base with the elevation adjustment screws or shims in their neutral positions. The unit is loosely secured to allow tilting.
  • Reference Measurement: A theodolite or electronic inclinometer is set up to measure the current elevation angle of the unit’s optical axis. The reference is typically the horizontal plane or a known survey point.
  • Calculating the Required Angle: The required elevation angle is determined from the airfield’s AGL documentation. For a standard 3° glide path, the unit’s transition angle must be set to 3° (or a staggered value for each unit in the array).
  • Adjustment: The adjustment screw is turned to raise or lower the front of the unit. Each full turn changes the angle by a known amount (e.g., 0.03° per turn). The adjustment is made incrementally, with the angle re‑measured after each turn.
  • Locking: Once the correct angle is achieved, the locking bolts or nuts are tightened securely. The angle is verified one final time to ensure it did not shift during locking.
  • Verification: For a complete PAPI system, the elevation of all four units is set to the staggered values. The system is then tested photometrically to ensure the red/white transition pattern is correct when viewed from the approach.

The adjustment is typically performed using a precision inclinometer or theodolite with an accuracy of ±0.01° or better, as even a 0.05° error can shift the glide path by several feet at the runway threshold.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Elevation adjustment, like azimuth, has no electrical control logic — it's a mechanical, technician-closed loop rather than an ALCMS-driven one. With the pivot hinge loosened, a theodolite or inclinometer measures the current elevation angle against the required glide-path value, and the adjustment screw is turned incrementally, with the angle re-measured after every turn, until the correct staggered value for that unit's position in the four-unit array is achieved and locked.

Because the four PAPI units in an array are each set to a slightly different elevation angle to produce the red/white transition pattern, this loop is repeated separately for every unit, and the final photometric check across all four is what actually confirms the array's electrical intensity control and the mechanical elevation settings are working together correctly.

Construction

Physical Construction

Like its azimuth counterpart, the elevation mechanism is a fine-threaded assembly integrated into the mounting base or a dedicated sub-assembly beneath the PAPI unit, rather than a separate visible fitting. A precision-ground threaded rod or worm-gear arrangement tilts the unit up or down about a horizontal pivot axis as it is turned, with the fine thread pitch giving the sub-arc-minute resolution the glide slope setting requires. A calibrated scale or vernier alongside the adjustment point lets the technician read the current angle directly, or in combination with an alignment tool such as an inclinometer for the final precise setting. Once set, a locking collar, clamping plate, or lock nut is tightened against the adjustment thread to fix the angle permanently against wind loading and vibration, and all load-bearing parts are machined from corrosion-resistant metal so the mechanism continues to move smoothly and hold its setting through years of outdoor service.

3°30′

The stagger creates a 1° spread across the four units, with 20 arcminutes (0.33°) between adjacent units. When the aircraft is on the 3° glide path, Units 2 and 3 appear white (above their transition angles), while Units 1 and 4 appear red (below their transition angles), giving the pilot the 2‑white/2‑red indication. The exact stagger values may vary depending on the manufacturer and specific installation requirements.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Adjustment Screw Seizing

Corrosion or dirt can cause the fine‑pitch adjustment screw to seize, making it impossible to adjust the elevation. Use stainless steel screws and apply anti‑seize compound during installation. Regularly exercise the screw through its full range to keep it free.

Angle Drift from Vibration

If the locking bolts are not sufficiently tightened, the elevation can drift over time due to wind or aircraft vibration. Use locking washers or thread‑locker and re‑torque bolts during routine maintenance. For critical installations, consider using a secondary locking system.

Incorrect Stagger

Setting all four units to the same angle (instead of staggered) will result in the entire array turning from red to white at the same point, losing the 4‑light indication. Always follow the manufacturer's stagger settings (typically 20 arcmin, ~0.33°, between units).

Reference Error

If the theodolite or inclinometer is not correctly referenced to a known horizontal plane, the elevation angle will be incorrect. Use a properly calibrated instrument and verify the reference using a known survey point or a precision spirit level.

Thermal Expansion Effects

Temperature changes can cause the mounting base and unit to expand or contract, slightly shifting the elevation angle. For critical calibrations, perform the adjustment when the unit is at its typical operating temperature (avoid extreme heat or cold).

Lost Adjustment Records

Without proper documentation of the elevation settings for each unit, it's difficult to know if the system has drifted. Keep a log of all adjustments, including the date, measured angle, and the name of the technician who performed the adjustment.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Elevation Adjustment Screw Won't Move
Likely Cause
Adjustment screw seizing from corrosion or dirt ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the screw threads for corrosion or debris, and check for prior application of anti-seize compound.
Corrective Action
Clean and lubricate or replace the screw with a stainless steel type and anti-seize compound; exercise it periodically to keep it free.
Unit Elevation Angle Found Drifted at Inspection
Likely Cause
Insufficient locking bolt torque allowing drift from wind or aircraft vibration.
Diagnostic Steps
Check locking bolt torque against specification and inspect for locking washers or thread-locker.
Corrective Action
Re-torque locking bolts, fit locking washers or thread-locker, and consider a secondary locking system for critical installations.
All Four Units Transition from Red to White at the Same Point
Likely Cause
Incorrect stagger — all units set to the same angle instead of the specified stagger increments.
Diagnostic Steps
Check each unit's individually measured elevation angle against the manufacturer's stagger specification (typically 20 arcmin, ~0.33°, between units).
Corrective Action
Re-adjust each unit to its correct staggered angle per the manufacturer's specification, and re-verify with flight or ground inspection.
Measured Elevation Angle Inconsistent With Survey Data
Likely Cause
Reference error — the theodolite/inclinometer not correctly referenced to a known horizontal plane.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the instrument's reference against a known survey point or precision spirit level.
Corrective Action
Re-reference the instrument correctly and repeat the elevation measurement.
Angle Reading Changes Between Morning and Afternoon Checks
Likely Cause
Thermal expansion of the mounting base and unit shifting the elevation slightly.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare readings taken at different temperatures and check whether the adjustment was performed at typical operating temperature.
Corrective Action
Perform the final calibration at the unit's typical operating temperature, avoiding extreme heat or cold.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Adjustment Mechanism Seizure
High Severity
Root Cause
Corrosion binding the screw or gear threads, or dried-out lubricant allowing dirt ingress into the adjustment mechanism.
Effect
Elevation adjustment screw won't move, preventing angle correction during a scheduled or corrective alignment visit.
Detection Method
Functional check of adjustment travel during scheduled maintenance, and re-lubrication of the mechanism per the maintenance schedule.
Locking Mechanism Creep
High Severity
Root Cause
A locking mechanism that does not fully re-engage after adjustment, or gradual loosening under wind and thermal cycling loads.
Effect
Unit elevation angle found drifted at inspection, which can shift the presented glide path if uncorrected across multiple units.
Detection Method
Periodic elevation angle verification against the approved glide path setting, and inspection of the locking mechanism for full engagement.
Multi-Unit Transition Point Misalignment
High Severity
Root Cause
One or more units drifted in elevation independently, so their red-to-white transition points no longer line up with the rest of the array.
Effect
All four units transition from red to white at the same point is no longer true, presenting pilots with an inconsistent or ambiguous glide path indication.
Detection Method
Field verification of the transition point for each unit relative to the others, using calibrated alignment equipment.
Survey Reference Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The threshold crossing height or approach surface reference used for the original setting has changed since the last formal survey.
Effect
Measured elevation angle inconsistent with survey data, even though the adjustment mechanism itself is functioning correctly.
Detection Method
Cross-check of the current elevation setting against an up-to-date approach surface survey.
Thermal Expansion Reading Variation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Differential thermal expansion of the mounting structure between a cool morning and a warm afternoon, shifting the apparent angle slightly.
Effect
Angle reading changes between morning and afternoon checks, without an actual underlying drift in the unit's setting.
Detection Method
Repeat readings at consistent times of day, and comparison against the tool's rated thermal stability specification.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Glide Path Safety Impact
Operational

Elevation angle sets the red/white transition that defines the glide slope itself; an uncorrected drift changes the vertical guidance a pilot flies on approach, so treat a confirmed mismatch as a priority correction.

Verify Transition Point Across the Array
Verification

After adjusting any single unit, confirm all units in the PAPI array still transition from red to white at the same point; a unit corrected in isolation can leave the array inconsistent even though each unit reads within its own tolerance.

Confirm Fixture State Before Adjustment
Personnel Safety

Follow the aerodrome's electrical safety procedure for the associated PAPI unit and base can before working at the mounting interface, since the adjustment mechanism is integrated into or near the fixture's mounting base.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Do not over-torque or rigidly fix the adjustment mechanism in a way that compromises the frangible mounting interface; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact per the frangibility requirement.

Verify Full Lock Engagement
Mechanical

Confirm the locking mechanism has fully re-engaged after every adjustment; partial engagement is a recurring cause of the unit's elevation drifting between scheduled checks under wind and thermal cycling loads.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Adjustment work on an in-service PAPI unit requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the unit will be off-aim or out of service during the correction.

Mechanism Care
Mechanical

Re-lubricate the adjustment mechanism per the maintenance schedule and keep it protected from dirt ingress, since corrosion and dried-out lubricant are the leading causes of the mechanism seizing when correction is needed.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify the corrected elevation against the approved glide path setting and confirm the lock is fully engaged before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Thermal Cycling Drifts Elevation Slowly, Not Suddenly
Reliability

Repeated expansion and contraction of the mounting interface across seasonal temperature swings can shift elevation angle by fractions of a degree per cycle, well below what a routine visual check would catch. Trend precision-instrument readings across seasons rather than relying on the same annual calibration date to catch this kind of slow drift.

Adjust Units in a Defined Order, Not Whichever Is Convenient
Procedure

Correcting units in the array out of sequence can leave the transition point briefly inconsistent between visits if a fault is found partway through. Work outboard-to-inboard or per the site's documented sequence so a partially completed correction never leaves an ambiguous glide indication if work is interrupted.

A Fully Locked Mechanism Can Still Be Mis-Set
Troubleshooting

Lock engagement confirms the mechanism won't move further, not that it was set to the correct angle in the first place. Always verify the actual elevation reading independently of lock status, since a confidently "locked" unit that was set wrong will hold that wrong angle just as firmly as a correct one.

Re-Survey the Reference Points Periodically
Design

The elevation setting is only as accurate as the ground survey reference it was calibrated against; settlement, resurfacing, or drainage work near the array can shift that reference over the years. Where correction requirements keep recurring at the same unit, check whether the underlying ground reference has moved before assuming the mechanism itself is at fault.

Lubricant Choice Matters More in Extreme Climates
Maintenance

A general-purpose lubricant that performs fine in temperate conditions can stiffen in cold climates or wash out faster in monsoon-exposed sites, both of which show up as a mechanism that resists fine adjustment. Match the lubrication spec to the actual climate the array operates in rather than using whatever's standard stock.

Wind-Loading Fatigue Accumulates on the Mechanism
Mechanical

Constant low-amplitude vibration from wind loading over years can loosen a mechanism that tested fine at commissioning, distinct from the more obvious impact or corrosion failure modes. Factor mechanical fatigue into the overhaul interval for exposed sites, not just visible wear or corrosion signs.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about PAPI elevation adjustment: glide slope angles, unit stagger, procedure, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is PAPI elevation adjustment?

It is the mechanical system that sets the vertical (up/down) angle of a PAPI light unit with high precision. That angle determines where the red/white transition occurs relative to the horizontal, which defines the glide slope pilots follow.

Q2How does elevation adjustment create the PAPI pattern?

In a standard four-unit PAPI each unit is set to a slightly different angle, for example 2°30′, 2°50′, 3°10′ and 3°30′ from the far unit to the one nearest the threshold, so on the correct 3° path the pilot sees two red and two white lights. It is the fractions of a degree between units that create the too-high, on-path and too-low boundaries.

Q3What angle is a PAPI normally set to?

For fixed-wing runways the nominal glide slope is 3°, typically within a 2.5° to 4° range and a tolerance of about ±0.05° (3′). Helicopter PAPI uses steeper angles, typically 4° to 6°, and runways with obstacle constraints or noise abatement needs may use 3.5° to 4° or other non-standard settings.

Q4How is PAPI elevation adjusted?

With the unit loosely secured, a theodolite or electronic inclinometer measures the optical axis angle. An adjustment screw raises or lowers the front of the unit in small steps (for example about 0.03° per turn), re-measuring after each turn. The lock is then tightened, the angle re-verified, and the whole array tested photometrically for the correct red/white transition.

Q5How accurate does the measuring instrument need to be?

Typically a precision inclinometer or theodolite accurate to ±0.01° or better, because even a 0.05° error can shift the glide path by several feet at the runway threshold.

Q6What are typical elevation adjustment specifications?

Range is 2.5° to 4.5° standard or 4° to 6° for helicopter, with resolution of 0.02° to 0.05° per screw turn and a minimum increment under 0.01° with vernier scale. Unit stagger is 20 arcmin (about 0.33°). Locking is by clamping bolts or locknuts at 20 to 40 Nm, in stainless steel, 6061-T6 aluminium or galvanised steel, from -40°C to +70°C.

Q7What happens if all units are set to the same angle?

The whole array turns from red to white at the same point, so the graduated four-light indication is lost. The correct angular stagger between units must be maintained.

Q8Which standards apply to PAPI elevation adjustment?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (elevation angle tolerances), FAA AC 150/5345-28 (installation and elevation setting procedures), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (photometric performance and verification), ISO 17123 (theodolite accuracy) and ASTM B117 (corrosion testing). Always verify the current edition.

Q9How often should PAPI elevation be verified?

Typical practice is a semi-annual check of the elevation angle and the unit stagger, an annual check that the adjustment screw is free and lubricated, and a logged record at each adjustment. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common elevation adjustment problems?

Screw seizing from corrosion or dirt, angle drift from vibration if locking bolts are under-tightened, incorrect stagger, a poorly referenced instrument, thermal expansion shifting the angle slightly, and lost adjustment records. After adjusting any unit, confirm all units in the array still transition from red to white at the same point.
