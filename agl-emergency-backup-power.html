Emergency Backup Power in AGL

UPS and generator integration for AGL systems. Emergency backup power is not simply about keeping airport equipment energized during a power failure — it must maintain the required availability of critical control, monitoring, communication and lighting functions while preventing unsafe transfer conditions, equipment damage, voltage disturbances and loss of operational control.

Overview

What Is Emergency Backup Power in AGL?

Emergency backup power is the electrical arrangement used to maintain selected airport lighting and associated systems when the normal utility power supply is interrupted, degraded or unavailable.

Depending on airport design, the backup architecture may include UPS systems, emergency generators, automatic transfer switches, static transfer switches, battery systems, emergency distribution boards, dual utility supplies, redundant control power supplies, and dedicated backup power for ALCMS, communication systems and selected AGL equipment. The exact arrangement depends on airport operational requirements, electrical architecture and applicable regulations.

The engineering objective: Utility Power Failure → Detect → Transfer → Backup Supply → Maintain Critical AGL Functions → Restore Utility → Controlled Return.

Not "Keep the Lights ON"

The real objective is to maintain the required operational capability of critical airport systems during loss of normal supply — not simply keep equipment energized.

A Complete Chain

Backup power is only as reliable as its weakest link — source, transfer, distribution, CCR, control and monitoring all have to hold together.

Verified, Not Assumed

A generator that starts, a UPS that runs, or an ATS that transfers is not automatically a successful backup system unless the required function is confirmed.

Why It Matters

Why Backup Power Matters in AGL

Airport lighting is a safety-critical system. Loss of normal electrical power can affect runway lighting, taxiway lighting, approach lighting, PAPI, airfield signs, runway guard lights, stop bars, ALCMS, CCR control, monitoring systems, communication systems and control-room equipment.

The purpose of emergency power is therefore not simply "Keep the lights ON." It is "Maintain the required operational capability of critical airport systems during loss of normal supply."

Core Distinction

UPS vs Generator

UPS and generators perform different functions. A generator is generally intended for sustained backup. A UPS is primarily intended to bridge interruptions and protect critical electronic/control loads. A properly engineered system may use both.

Application

UPS in AGL & Generator Integration

UPS in AGL

UPS systems may be used for equipment such as:

  • ALCMS servers & operator workstations
  • Control systems & PLC systems
  • Communication equipment & network switches
  • Monitoring systems & control panels
  • Instrumentation & selected electronic equipment

Whether an AGL CCR itself is supplied through a UPS depends on the specific airport electrical architecture and CCR design. A UPS should not automatically be assumed suitable for every AGL load.

Generator Integration

An emergency generator may supply selected AGL loads during utility failure. Typical arrangement:

Utility → Main Switchboard → ATS
AGL Distribution

The generator connects via its own breaker to the same ATS. It must be appropriately sized for the connected emergency load and its starting, transient and steady-state characteristics.

Switching Logic

The Automatic Transfer Sequence

The ATS provides controlled transfer between power sources. The actual transfer sequence must follow the approved electrical design and equipment configuration.

01

Normal Operation

Normal source available — load supplied by normal source.

02

Failure Detection

Normal source failure is detected.

03

Generator Start

Generator start command is issued (usually after a short start delay that rides through momentary dips); the generator must reach acceptable voltage and frequency before it is allowed to accept load.

04

Transfer

ATS transfers the load — emergency supply becomes available and AGL critical loads operate from the emergency source.

05

Restoration & Return

Utility is restored; transfer-back is delayed until the utility is confirmed stable, the load returns to the normal source, and the generator runs unloaded for its cool-down before shutting down.

Bridging the Gap

UPS + Generator Integration

A common architecture pairs both systems so the UPS bridges the interval needed for generator starting and transfer, while the generator provides longer-duration emergency power.

The UPS acts as the short-duration energy bridge while the generator provides longer-duration emergency power, so critical continuity is maintained across the transfer window.

Power Continuity Timeline — UPS Bridging the Generator Start

The UPS carries its critical electronic loads through the interruption while the generator starts and reaches acceptable condition, then the ATS transfers and the generator sustains the load

Switch-over time matters for CCRs. A CCR fed from the generator-backed distribution (not from a UPS) is interrupted for the generator start and transfer time. ICAO Annex 14, Volume I, Chapter 8 (Electrical Systems) sets maximum secondary-power switch-over times by runway type: as short as practicable for non-instrument runways, generally 15 seconds for non-precision and Category I approach runways, and 1 second for specified Category II/III lighting elements (15 seconds for the rest). A generator-and-ATS sequence alone cannot meet a 1-second requirement unless a running alternate source, UPS or rapid-transfer arrangement is used. Table numbering and the exact facilities listed vary between editions and State regulations — verify against the current edition and the applicable DGCA/national requirement.

Effect on the System

Backup Power, CCRs, Series Circuits & ALCMS

CCR Behaviour

A power interruption can cause CCR shutdown, restart, output interruption, control-communication loss, alarms and transient loss of lighting. The design must consider CCR input supply → response → restart behaviour → circuit recovery → control recovery, per the manufacturer's requirements.

Series Circuits

The AGL series circuit is specialized; backup power isn't equivalent to supplying a conventional lighting circuit. The chain remains Emergency Source → Distribution → CCR → Series Circuit → Isolation Transformer → Fixture, with every stage's requirements unchanged.

ALCMS

ALCMS must maintain operator control, CCR status, lighting status, alarms, communication, logging and emergency control functions. If ALCMS loses power while lighting stays on, operators can lose visibility — availability of lighting and of control must be evaluated separately.

Emergency Power Reaching the Series Circuit

Emergency power must flow all the way to the fixture — and ALCMS visibility must be tracked separately, since it can be lost even while lighting stays energized

Sizing & Compatibility

Generator Sizing & Source Compatibility

Generator sizing should consider more than connected kW — a generator that appears adequate on connected kW alone may not perform correctly under actual operating conditions. UPS and generator systems must also be compatible; a UPS may reject a generator supply if incoming electrical parameters fall outside its acceptable limits.

Generator Sizing Factors

Real & apparent power, power factor, motor starting loads, transformer inrush, UPS input characteristics, CCR input characteristics (power factor and harmonics can vary with CCR type and brightness step), non-linear loads, harmonic currents, load sequencing, transient response, future load and ambient conditions.

UPS–Generator Compatibility

Frequency and voltage variation, generator waveform quality, harmonics, UPS input current characteristics, generator loading, power factor, synchronization requirements and transfer timing must all be verified — never assumed.

Transfer Equipment Evaluation

Transfer time, source monitoring, interlocking, break-before-make/make-before-break arrangement, voltage/frequency sensing, generator availability, manual and maintenance bypass, and fault conditions.

The transfer system must prevent unsafe simultaneous connection of incompatible sources unless the system is specifically designed for synchronized operation.

Electrical Quality

Power Quality, Batteries & Fuel

Power Quality

Important parameters: voltage, frequency, voltage unbalance, harmonic distortion, power factor, transients, voltage dips, voltage interruptions and frequency stability. Poor power quality can affect electronic AGL equipment even while it remains energized.

Battery System Engineering

Battery chemistry, rated capacity, design life, operating temperature, charging voltage, discharge characteristics, monitoring, ventilation, maintenance, replacement interval, terminal connections, protection and enclosure. Condition should be monitored — never assume an old battery still delivers its original autonomy.

Generator Fuel System

Fuel tank capacity, required runtime, fuel quality and contamination, ageing, transfer system, leak protection, tank monitoring, refuelling arrangements and emergency access. Emergency power availability is ultimately limited by the weakest part of the backup system.

Distribution, Earthing & Protection

Emergency feeders should clearly identify normal, emergency, critical AGL, UPS, generator, control, auxiliary and maintenance circuits. Earthing, bonding, neutral management and fault-current paths must also account for the transfer arrangement — incorrect neutral switching or bonding (for example, 3-pole versus 4-pole transfer switching and where the neutral–earth bond is made) can create serious protection problems.

Performance, Not Just Power

Backup Power & Photometry

Photometry may become relevant when emergency operation affects lighting output — different CCR behaviour, reduced available intensity, incorrect intensity selection, voltage instability, fixture performance changes and control-system configuration differences can all appear during emergency operation.

The question is not only "Are the lights ON?" It is "Do the required lighting systems provide the required performance in the emergency operating condition?"

The Core Principle

The Most Important Emergency Power Principle

Emergency backup power is successful only when the complete critical system remains operational, controllable, protected and verifiable during loss of normal power.

A generator that starts but cannot accept the load is not a successful backup system. A UPS that operates but has degraded batteries is not a reliable backup system. An ATS that transfers but causes CCR or control-system instability is not a successful transfer system. And an AGL system that remains electrically energized but cannot provide the required operational function is not necessarily a successful emergency-power outcome.

Normal power supplies the airport. Emergency power protects operational continuity. UPS provides continuity through interruption, generators provide sustained backup, transfer systems manage source changeover, and AGL control and monitoring ensure the lighting system remains operationally visible and controllable.

The engineering objective is not simply Power Available. It is: Power Available → Correct Transfer → Stable Supply → AGL Operational → Control Available → Monitoring Available → Performance Verified.

Keyword Reference

Airfield Lighting Battery Backup, Emergency Repair & Emergency Procedures

Airfield lighting battery backup

Airfield lighting battery backup supplies critical loads through a UPS while the generator starts and takes over. It bridges the short gap in supply and keeps control systems, monitoring and selected lights running. Battery capacity, autonomy and condition checks are set by the project design and the manufacturer.

Airfield lighting emergency repair

Airfield lighting emergency repair restores failed lights or circuits quickly after a fault or storm. Teams make the work area safe, find the fault, repair or replace the part, test the circuit and tell the operations staff. Temporary measures, such as portable lights, should be recorded and replaced with a permanent repair.

AGL emergency procedures

AGL emergency procedures set out what staff do when power or lighting fails: who is called, how loads are transferred, what is reported to air traffic control and how the airfield is checked afterwards. They should be written, rehearsed and kept up to date with the airport's operating procedures.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is emergency backup power in AGL?

It is the electrical arrangement used to maintain selected airport lighting and associated systems when the normal utility supply is interrupted, degraded or unavailable. Depending on the airport it may include UPS systems, generators, automatic or static transfer switches, battery systems, emergency distribution boards, dual utility supplies and dedicated backup for ALCMS and communication systems.

Q2Why does backup power matter for airfield lighting?

Airport lighting is safety-critical. Loss of normal power can affect runway, taxiway and approach lighting, PAPI, airfield signs, stop bars, runway guard lights, ALCMS, CCR control, monitoring, communication and control-room equipment. The purpose is not simply to keep the lights on but to maintain the required operational capability of critical systems during loss of normal supply.

Q3What is the difference between a UPS and a generator in AGL?

A generator is generally intended for sustained, extended-duration backup. A UPS is primarily intended to bridge interruptions and protect critical electronic and control loads. A properly engineered system may use both.

Q4Is a CCR powered from a UPS?

It depends on the specific airport electrical architecture and CCR design. A UPS should not automatically be assumed suitable for every AGL load. UPS systems may be used for ALCMS servers and workstations, PLC and control systems, communication equipment, network switches and monitoring systems.

Q5How does an automatic transfer switch (ATS) sequence work?

The normal source fails and is detected, a generator start command is issued (usually after a short delay that rides through momentary dips), and the generator must reach acceptable voltage and frequency before accepting load. The ATS then transfers the load to the emergency source. When utility power returns, transfer-back is delayed until the utility is confirmed stable, the load returns to the normal source and the generator runs unloaded for its cool-down before shutting down.

Q6How do a UPS and a generator work together?

A common architecture pairs them so the UPS bridges the interval needed for generator starting and transfer, while the generator provides the longer-duration emergency power. Critical continuity is maintained across the transfer window.

Q7What is the difference between an ATS and a static transfer switch?

An ATS transfers load between the normal and emergency sources. A static transfer switch provides rapid transfer between compatible electrical sources. The actual transfer sequence must follow the approved electrical design and equipment configuration.

Q8What switch-over times does ICAO Annex 14 set for secondary power?

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I, Chapter 8 sets maximum switch-over times by runway type: as short as practicable for non-instrument runways, generally 15 seconds for non-precision and Category I approach runways, and 1 second for specified Category II/III lighting elements (15 seconds for the rest). A generator-and-ATS sequence alone cannot meet a 1-second requirement unless a running alternate source, UPS or rapid-transfer arrangement is used. Table numbering and facilities vary between editions and State regulations, so verify against the current edition and the applicable DGCA or national requirement.

Q9Is a generator that starts automatically a successful backup system?

Not by itself. A generator that starts, a UPS that runs or an ATS that transfers is not automatically a successful backup system unless the required function is confirmed. Backup power is only as reliable as its weakest link across source, transfer, distribution, CCR, control and monitoring.

Q10What happens when utility power is restored?

The return should be controlled rather than immediate. Transfer-back is delayed until the utility is confirmed stable, the load returns to the normal source, and the generator runs unloaded for its cool-down before it shuts down.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Size Autonomy for the Real Load, Not the Nameplate
UPS

Measure or calculate the actual connected load, include ageing and temperature effects on batteries, and record the assumptions so the autonomy figure can be checked at a later date.

Test the Transfer Under Load
Transfer

A generator that starts on a no-load test may behave differently when the CCRs and controls are connected. Include the complete transfer sequence in periodic tests.

Fuel Quality Is a Common Hidden Failure
Generator

Stored fuel degrades and tanks collect water. Include sampling and tank checks in the plan so the set does not fail when finally needed.

Know What the Switch-Over Interruption Means for Each Load
Interruption

CCRs, controls and monitoring respond differently to a short break. Confirm that each recovers cleanly and in the intended order.

Check Power Quality, Not Just Presence of Voltage
Quality

Harmonics, voltage and frequency excursions from a backup source can upset sensitive equipment. Monitor these during commissioning and at periodic tests.

Keep the Test Log Where Operations Can See It
Records

Backup power is only credible if its test history is visible. Record dates, loads, durations and any defects, and follow up findings.
