Externally Illuminated Windsock

A fabric wind cone illuminated by external floodlights positioned around the mast, providing clear visibility of wind direction and speed at night. This design allows for easy maintenance, lamp replacement without mast disassembly, and the use of standard light fixtures.

Overview

What Is It?

An Externally Illuminated Windsock is a wind cone assembly that uses external floodlights or spotlights mounted on the mast or nearby structures to illuminate the windsock from the outside. This is the traditional and most common method of windsock illumination. The light sources are typically LED or halogen floodlights positioned at the base of the mast or on separate poles, aimed upward to bathe the entire windsock in light. This design offers several advantages: easy lamp replacement (no need to enter the mast), the ability to use standard, off‑the‑shelf lighting fixtures, and the flexibility to adjust the beam angle and intensity to suit different wind cone sizes and locations. External illumination is also easier to inspect and maintain, as all lighting components are accessible at ground level or on the mast's exterior.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

External illumination exists to keep a windsock visible at night using simple, easily maintained equipment: because the light source sits outside the mast, technicians can inspect, adjust, or replace it from ground level without accessing the interior of the structure, which keeps maintenance straightforward and downtime short if a lamp fails. This practicality is a real safety consideration in its own right, since a windsock that goes dark at night because of a hard-to-service internal fault loses its value exactly when pilots most need a wind reference.

The flexibility to adjust beam angle and intensity also lets the lighting be matched to windsock size and site conditions, ensuring the sock stays clearly readable from the air or the runway threshold, which is the entire purpose of illuminating it in the first place.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Used at a wide range of airfields, heliports, and military installations where windsock illumination is required. Particularly common at larger airports, military airbases, and facilities where maintenance access is a priority. The external illumination design is also preferred in environments where the windsock mast is too small to accommodate internal lighting, or where existing floodlight infrastructure can be repurposed. It is also used at temporary and remote airfields where simplicity and ease of maintenance are critical.

Placement

Installation & Location

Floodlights are mounted on a ground-level pole or bracket set back from the base of the windsock mast, angled upward so the beam washes the full length of the sock without glaring toward an approaching aircraft. Fixture spacing and aiming are set during commissioning so the sock remains evenly lit from tail to throat regardless of wind direction, since the sock itself rotates freely on the mast head. Power cabling runs underground in duct from the floodlight bases back to the local distribution point, with the fixtures wired on their own circuit (often via a photocell or timer) so illumination can be verified and maintained independently of the mast structure itself. Because the light source sits at ground level, the mounting position is also chosen for straightforward access, so lamp replacement and aiming checks can be carried out without climbing the mast.

Function

How Does It Work?

The externally illuminated windsock uses one or more floodlights mounted on the mast or on separate poles. The floodlights are typically positioned at the base of the mast, aimed upward to illuminate the full length of the wind cone. Some installations use multiple floodlights arranged around the mast to ensure even illumination from all sides. The light source is usually a high‑intensity LED floodlight with a wide beam angle (60°–120°) to cover the entire windsock. A photocell or timer controls the on/off cycle. The floodlights are housed in weatherproof enclosures (IP66/IP67) with corrosion‑resistant materials. The windsock itself is made from opaque, UV‑resistant fabric (orange and white bands) that reflects the external light, making it clearly visible at night. The external design allows for easy lamp replacement, cleaning, and adjustment of the light beam.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Unlike runway and taxiway edge lights, windsock floodlights are not part of the constant current series circuit — they are standard AC fixtures wired in parallel on their own dedicated lighting circuit, switched as a group by a photocell (or timer) rather than stepped through CCR current levels. This keeps the control logic simple by design: there is no intensity-step selection tied to visibility category, no series-loop fault monitoring, and no isolating transformer in the circuit, because the windsock is a wind-direction reference rather than a category-dependent visual aid. The photocell typically drives a contactor that energises the whole floodlight group at dusk and de-energises it at dawn, with a manual override at the local distribution point for maintenance or testing; where the site has an ALCMS, the circuit's on/off state is commonly wired back as a simple status point so a failed photocell or tripped breaker shows up as a fault, but the floodlights themselves remain outside the CCR-controlled visual aid system.

Light Path

Illuminated From the Outside In

Floodlights Aim Upward — Fabric Reflects the Light

Opaque, UV-resistant orange/white fabric bounces the external beam back to the observer; nothing needs to be inside the mast.

Because every lighting component sits at the base of the mast or on nearby poles, lamp replacement and beam adjustment happen entirely at ground level — the trade-off against the Internally Illuminated Windsock is potential glare and less even coverage if the floodlights aren't carefully aimed.

Construction

Physical Construction

This configuration pairs a standard fabric wind cone assembly with one or more floodlight fixtures mounted separately from the cone itself, either on brackets fixed to the mast below the swivel point or on dedicated poles positioned around the base. The floodlight housings are weatherproof, corrosion-resistant enclosures rated to IP66/IP67, with a hardened glass or polycarbonate lens sealed against moisture and a mounting yoke that allows the beam angle to be adjusted so the full length of the cone is evenly lit without excessive spill or glare toward approaching aircraft. Because the light source sits outside the cone and mast, wiring runs from the floodlight housing down to a ground-level junction box are kept separate from the mast's internal cabling, simplifying lamp replacement and maintenance access without requiring work at height. The cone itself follows standard wind cone construction — UV-stabilised, flame-retardant polyester fabric on a ring-supported throat — while the mast is a conventional galvanized steel, aluminium, or fibreglass pole without the need for internal illumination wiring or a translucent fabric specification.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Floodlight Misalignment

Floodlights can shift due to wind or vibration, causing uneven illumination or shadows on the windsock. Regular inspection and realignment of floodlights is necessary to maintain even coverage.

Lamp/LED Failure

External floodlights are exposed to the elements and can fail due to moisture ingress, electrical faults, or lamp burnout. Regular replacement and use of high‑quality fixtures with long‑life LEDs is recommended.

Light Pollution and Glare

External floodlights can cause glare for pilots on final approach if not properly shielded. Using directional floodlights with glare shields and careful positioning can mitigate this issue.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Entire Windsock Goes Dark at Night
Likely Cause
Failed photocell or contactor leaving the whole floodlight group de-energised, or a tripped breaker on the dedicated AC lighting circuit — since all fixtures are switched together as one group.
Diagnostic Steps
Check breaker status at the local distribution point; test the photocell by covering/uncovering it to confirm it switches the contactor; verify supply voltage is present at the contactor output if the photocell itself checks out.
Corrective Action
Reset the breaker and investigate the cause of the trip, or replace the failed photocell or contactor; use the manual override to confirm the floodlight group energises correctly before returning to automatic control.
One or More Floodlights Dark, Others Working
Likely Cause
Individual lamp/LED burnout, since floodlights are wired in parallel on the shared circuit and a single fixture can fail without affecting the rest of the group.
Diagnostic Steps
Identify which specific floodlight(s) are dark during a night inspection; check the fixture's supply connection and lamp/LED module condition; inspect for moisture ingress in the housing if the lamp itself tests good.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed lamp or LED module at ground level; reseal the housing if moisture ingress is found, and re-check aim/alignment after any fixture disturbance.
Uneven Illumination or Shadows on the Sock
Likely Cause
Floodlight has shifted out of its commissioned aim due to wind or vibration, or the sock's free rotation on the mast head is creating a shadow gap as it swings into an unlit sector.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the sock through a full rotation to identify which sector is under-lit; check floodlight mounting bracket security and beam angle against the commissioning aim record.
Corrective Action
Re-aim and re-secure the floodlight to restore even coverage tail to throat across all wind directions; re-verify with a full-rotation night inspection.
Glare Reported by Approaching Aircraft
Likely Cause
Floodlight beam spilling upward toward the approach path rather than being contained on the sock, due to incorrect aim or a missing/damaged glare shield.
Diagnostic Steps
Check floodlight aim angle against the commissioning record; inspect for a missing, cracked, or displaced glare shield on the fixture.
Corrective Action
Re-aim the floodlight downward/inward to keep the beam on the sock, and fit or replace the glare shield; re-verify no spill toward the approach path from a pilot's-eye vantage point.
Photocell Switching at Wrong Time (Early/Late Dusk-Dawn)
Likely Cause
A dirty, shaded, or degraded photocell sensor lens giving a false light-level reading, or a failed timer if the site uses timed rather than photocell control.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the photocell lens for dirt or an obstruction casting a shadow on it; compare actual switching time against expected dusk/dawn times over several days; check timer settings and clock accuracy if fitted.
Corrective Action
Clean the photocell lens or relocate it away from an obstruction; replace a degraded photocell or reset/replace a faulted timer.
ALCMS Reports Circuit Fault But Sock Appears Lit
Likely Cause
The ALCMS status point is wired to the circuit's on/off state rather than confirming actual illumination, so a nuisance fault can appear from a wiring or status-point issue even while the floodlights themselves work correctly.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually confirm the floodlight group is actually lit; check the status point wiring and contactor auxiliary contact feeding the ALCMS input.
Corrective Action
Repair or re-terminate the status point wiring/auxiliary contact so the ALCMS indication matches actual circuit state.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Photocell or Contactor Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Photocell sensor end-of-life, contactor contact wear from repeated daily switching, or a failed coil.
Effect
The entire floodlight group fails to energise at dusk, leaving the windsock unlit at night, or a tripped breaker on the shared circuit.
Detection Method
Breaker status check, photocell test by covering/uncovering it, and verification of supply voltage at the contactor output.
Individual Lamp/LED Module Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Lamp burnout, LED driver failure, or moisture ingress into an individual floodlight housing exposed to the elements.
Effect
One or more floodlights go dark while the rest of the parallel-wired group continues operating, leaving part of the sock under-lit.
Detection Method
Night inspection to identify the specific dark fixture, followed by a supply connection and lamp/LED module check at that unit.
Floodlight Misalignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Wind loading or vibration gradually shifting a floodlight's mounting bracket away from its commissioned aim.
Effect
Uneven illumination or shadowing on the sock as it rotates through different wind directions, reducing night conspicuity from some approach angles.
Detection Method
Full-rotation night observation to identify the under-lit sector, and comparison of floodlight aim against the commissioning record.
Glare Shield Damage or Loss
Low Severity
Root Cause
Physical damage, weathering, or incorrect reassembly after maintenance dislodging or cracking the glare shield.
Effect
Floodlight beam spills upward toward the approach path, creating glare reported by approaching flight crews.
Detection Method
Aim-angle check against the commissioning record, and visual inspection for a missing, cracked, or displaced glare shield.
Photocell Lens Contamination or Degradation
Low Severity
Root Cause
Dirt accumulation or gradual sensor degradation biasing the photocell's light-level reading.
Effect
The floodlight group switches on or off earlier or later than actual dusk/dawn conditions warrant.
Detection Method
Inspection of the photocell lens for dirt or obstruction, and comparison of actual switching times against expected dusk/dawn over several days.
ALCMS Status Point Wiring Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A wiring fault or degraded auxiliary contact at the contactor feeding the ALCMS status input, independent of the floodlights themselves.
Effect
The ALCMS reports a circuit fault even though the floodlight group is actually lit and functioning correctly.
Detection Method
Visual confirmation the floodlight group is lit, followed by inspection of the status point wiring and the contactor's auxiliary contact.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the floodlight circuit at its local supply and verify zero energy before servicing fixtures at ground level or on the mast.

Mast-Mounted Floodlight Access
Fall Protection

Where floodlights are mounted on the mast rather than at ground level, use fall-protection equipment appropriate to the mounting height.

Beam Aim Glare
Personnel Safety

Avoid standing directly in the upward beam path during aim adjustment or functional testing to prevent glare exposure.

Wind-Speed Work Limits
Operational

Avoid mast-top work in high wind, and keep clear of the swivel assembly while the cone is moving during floodlight aim checks.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside operations before servicing a windsock relied on for night wind indication, particularly where the outage spans a period of active operations.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for electrical connections and standard airside PPE for ground-level or mast access work.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Verify the floodlight beam evenly illuminates the full cone with no dark segments before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Aim in Daylight is Meaningless
Commissioning

Floodlight beam aim always looks fine during a daytime walk-around because the eye compensates for uneven light easily; the real test is a night verification with the windsock actually flying, checked from a pilot's-eye elevation and angle, not from directly beneath the mast.

Re-Check Aim After the First Windy Season
Mechanical

Floodlight brackets that were torqued correctly at installation can creep after repeated wind loading on the mast, especially on pole-mounted units some distance from the sock itself. Build a re-aim check into the first scheduled maintenance after commissioning rather than assuming the initial aim holds indefinitely.

Shield for the Approach, Not Just for Compliance
Glare Control

A floodlight can pass a standard glare measurement taken from the ground and still put unacceptable stray light into a pilot's eyes on a specific approach path, because glare is highly angle-dependent. Where the site geometry allows, verify glare shielding from an approximate approach angle, not only from prescribed ground measurement points.

Match Beam Spread to Cone Size, Not a Standard Fixture
Design

Using the same off-the-shelf floodlight beam angle for both large and small windsocks tends to over-light small cones near the mast (causing hot spots and glare) or under-light the tip of large cones. Select or adjust beam spread against the specific cone dimensions on that mast rather than defaulting to a stock fixture spec.

Insect and Debris Buildup Degrades Aim Silently
Maintenance

Ground-mounted or low-pole floodlights accumulate insect debris and cobwebs on the lens faster than mast-height fixtures, gradually narrowing effective beam spread without any visible fault on a status monitoring system. Include a lens-clarity check in routine visits even when the fixture reports as functioning normally.

Photocell Placement Affects the Whole Circuit's Reliability
Electrical

A photocell mounted where it sees its own floodlight's reflected glow, or direct headlight/vehicle light from an access road, will switch erratically regardless of how well the wiring is installed. Site the photocell away from stray light sources first; troubleshooting timer settings afterward wastes time if the sensor location itself is the problem.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about externally illuminated windsocks: how they work, floodlight options, illuminance, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is an externally illuminated windsock?

It is a wind cone assembly lit from outside by floodlights or spotlights mounted at the base of the mast or on separate poles, aimed upward to light the whole windsock. It is the traditional and most common method of windsock illumination.

Q2Why choose external illumination?

The light source sits outside the mast, so lamps can be inspected, adjusted or replaced from ground level without entering the structure. It uses standard off-the-shelf fixtures, lets beam angle and intensity be adjusted, and keeps downtime short if a lamp fails.

Q3Where is external illumination used?

Airfields, heliports and military installations, particularly larger airports and bases where maintenance access is a priority, where the mast is too small for internal lighting, where existing floodlight infrastructure can be reused, and at temporary or remote airfields.

Q4What light sources and layout does it use?

One to three LED floodlights (50–150 W) or halogen (150–300 W), each giving 5,000–15,000 lumens with a wide 60°–120° beam. Fixtures are on a ground-level pole or bracket set back from the mast and angled upward so the sock is evenly lit from tail to throat without glaring toward approaching aircraft.

Q5How bright must the windsock be?

At least 50 lux average on the cone surface at mid-length, with a minimum point illuminance of about 20 lux (FAA AC 150/5345-27 gives 2 foot-candles, roughly 21.5 lux, at throat, trailing end and centre points tested at 30° intervals). Maximum-to-minimum uniformity is 4:1 or better.

Q6Does a windsock have to be lit at night?

Yes. ICAO requires at least one wind direction indicator to be illuminated at an aerodrome intended for use at night. A windsock that goes dark makes wind direction unavailable when pilots may need it.

Q7What are the ICAO cone and siting requirements?

A truncated cone at least 3.6 m long with a throat diameter of at least 0.9 m, extending fully in a wind of about 15 kt and indicating direction clearly at 6 kt. It is sited clear of turbulence from buildings or terrain, visible from aircraft in flight and the movement area, and marked by a 15 m circle where practicable.

Q8How is the floodlight circuit powered?

A local 230 V AC branch circuit or 24 V DC via a converter, not part of the runway series (CCR) circuit. It has MCB and RCD protection, a photocell, astronomical timer or manual switch for control, and earthing of the fixtures and mast.

Q9Which standards apply to externally illuminated windsocks?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 Vol I §5.1.1 (wind direction indicators), ICAO Doc 9157 Part 4, FAA AC 150/5345-27 (wind cone assemblies, including externally lighted Style I-A) and local electrical codes for the mains circuit. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q10How often should an externally illuminated windsock be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens and fixture cleaning and cone condition inspection, and semi-annual for floodlight function and alignment checks, photocell or timer control verification and swivel bearing lubrication. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11Why has the whole windsock gone dark at night?

A failed photocell or contactor, or a tripped breaker on the lighting circuit, since all fixtures are switched as one group. Check the breaker, test the photocell by covering it, verify supply at the contactor output and use the manual override to confirm the floodlights energise.

Q12Why are one or more floodlights dark while others work?

Individual lamp or LED burnout, since floodlights are wired in parallel and a single fixture can fail without affecting the rest. Also check for misalignment from wind or vibration, which leaves shadowed sections of the sock.
