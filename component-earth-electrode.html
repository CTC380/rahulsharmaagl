Earth Electrode

The conductive element that provides the physical connection between the earthing system and the general mass of earth. Earth electrodes are critical for dissipating fault currents, lightning surges, and static charges safely into the ground, ensuring personnel safety and the protection of Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) equipment. They are the heart of any earthing installation.

Overview

What Is It?

An Earth Electrode is a conductive rod, plate, or pipe that is buried in the ground to establish a low‑impedance electrical connection to the earth. It serves as the physical interface between the earthing system and the soil, allowing fault currents and lightning surges to dissipate safely. The most common types used in AGL installations are:

  • Copper‑bonded Steel Rods: The most widely used type, comprising a steel core with a copper coating (≥ 250 µm thick) for high mechanical strength and excellent corrosion resistance. Available in lengths of 2.4 m, 3.0 m, and 6.0 m, and diameters from 12.7 mm to 17.2 mm.
  • Solid Copper Rods: Used in highly corrosive soils where a thicker copper section is required. They offer superior conductivity and longevity but are more expensive.
  • Copper Plates: Large flat plates (typically 600 mm x 600 mm x 3 mm) buried vertically or horizontally. Used in high‑resistivity soils or where space permits, providing a large surface area for current dissipation.
  • Galvanised Iron (GI) Pipes and Rods: A more economical option, but with lower corrosion resistance; used in less aggressive soil conditions.
  • Chemical (Maintenance‑Free) Electrodes: Filled with conductive compounds (e.g., bentonite, magnesium sulphate) that reduce soil resistivity and maintain low resistance over time, even in dry or rocky soils.
  • Strip Electrodes: Flat copper or steel strips buried in trenches, often used as ring earth electrodes around buildings.

Earth electrodes are typically installed vertically by driving them into the ground, though horizontal (trenched) installations are also used, especially where rock or shallow soil conditions prevent deep driving.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The earth electrode's purpose is to give fault current and lightning surge energy somewhere safe to go — a low-resistance path into the soil itself — rather than letting that energy remain in equipment enclosures, cable trays, or metal fixtures where it could create a dangerous touch voltage or destroy sensitive electronics. Every earthed component in the AGL system, from a CCR chassis to a runway light can, ultimately relies on an electrode buried nearby doing this job reliably for decades, which is why electrode material and length are chosen specifically to resist corrosion and achieve a low enough resistance for the local soil conditions. Without an effective electrode, even a well-designed bonding and surge-protection scheme has nowhere to actually discharge the energy it is meant to divert.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Earth electrodes are fundamental to every part of the AGL earthing infrastructure:

  • CCR Rooms and Substations: Providing the main earthing for switchgear, transformers, and CCR panels.
  • ALCMS and Communication Shelters: Earthing for sensitive electronic equipment and lightning protection.
  • Lighting Control Panels (LCPs): Local earthing for outdoor panels and SPDs.
  • Airfield Lighting Circuits: Earthing of cable trays, junction boxes, and metal enclosures along the series circuits.
  • Obstruction Lighting Structures: Earthing of masts, towers, and poles.
  • Apron Floodlight Masts: Earthing for tall floodlight structures.
  • Equipment Shelters: Earthing of remote RTUs, modems, and power supplies.
  • Lightning Protection Systems: The termination point for down conductors from air terminals.
  • Runway and Taxiway Lighting Can Bases: Often each light fixture has a local earth electrode to ensure equipotential bonding and dissipation of static charges.
Placement

Installation & Location

Earth electrodes are driven or buried at each point in the network requiring a local earth reference — beside CCR rooms and substations, at the base of obstruction masts and floodlight poles, next to lighting control panels, and at intervals along runway and taxiway circuits at fixture or junction box positions. Rod electrodes are driven vertically into the soil to a depth that reaches stable, lower-resistance strata, while plate or mesh electrodes are buried horizontally where ground conditions or space make vertical driving impractical. Electrode location is chosen for soil moisture and resistivity as much as convenience, since a technically correct connection into poor soil will still fail to meet the required resistance, and multiple electrodes are bonded together where a single rod can't achieve the target value on its own.

Function

How Does It Work?

An earth electrode works by providing a low‑resistance path for electrical current to flow into the earth. The key principles are:

  • Low Resistance Dissipation: The electrode, in contact with the soil, allows fault currents (e.g., from a short circuit or lightning strike) to spread into the surrounding earth. The resistance depends on the electrode's length, diameter, and the soil resistivity. Deeper and longer electrodes generally provide lower resistance.
  • Corrosion Protection: The electrode material (e.g., copper‑bonded steel) is chosen to resist corrosion in the soil environment, ensuring a long service life (typically 25–50 years).
  • Equipotential Bonding: All connected metallic parts are brought to the same potential as the earth, preventing dangerous voltage gradients and sparking.
  • Surge Current Conduction: During a lightning strike, the high‑frequency surge current is efficiently conducted to the electrode and dissipated into the ground without causing flashover or equipment damage.
  • Soil Resistivity Improvement: In some cases, chemical backfill or soil treatments (e.g., salt, bentonite) are used around the electrode to reduce soil resistivity and improve performance.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

An earth electrode has no active control logic — it is the passive termination point of the earthing architecture, providing the physical interface to soil that every other bonded component ultimately relies on. Architecturally it sits at the bottom of the fault-current and surge-current path: equipment chassis, cable trays, and lightning down-conductors are all bonded back to it, directly or through an earth pit, so that a fault or a lightning strike anywhere in the bonded network has a single, low-resistance route to ground. There is no real-time monitoring of the electrode itself in most installations — its condition is verified periodically by earth resistance testing rather than continuously, though critical installations sometimes add an earth-fault-loop monitoring relay upstream that watches for a rise in loop impedance and raises an alarm if the electrode's effective resistance has degraded.

Construction

Physical Construction

A rod electrode is a solid or copper-bonded steel core driven vertically into the ground in sections, joined by threaded couplers so additional lengths can be added until the target resistance or refusal depth is reached; a driving stud or cap protects the top thread from mushrooming under hammer blows during installation. Copper-bonded rods carry a molecularly bonded copper coating over a steel core for mechanical strength with copper's conductivity and corrosion resistance, while solid copper or plate electrodes are used where soil conditions or design standards call for maximum conductivity. The electrode is typically surrounded by a conductive backfill — bentonite, charcoal, or a proprietary conductive compound — packed into the borehole or trench to lower the effective soil resistivity around the electrode and maintain consistent contact with the surrounding earth even as native soil dries out. The top of the electrode terminates at a connection point — a threaded coupler, exothermically welded joint, or mechanical compression lug — that ties into the earthing conductor running back to the bonding network.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

High Soil Resistivity

Dry or rocky soils can significantly increase earth resistance. In such cases, multiple electrodes, deeper driving, chemical backfill, or the use of plate electrodes may be necessary to achieve the required resistance.

Corrosion of Electrode

In aggressive soils (high salinity, acidic, or with stray DC currents), even copper‑bonded rods can corrode over time. Regular resistance testing and periodic visual inspection (via earth pits) are essential. Use of solid copper or stainless steel in severe conditions is recommended.

Inadequate Driving Depth

Driving electrodes to insufficient depth (e.g., not reaching the water table or permanent moisture level) can result in high resistance. Use of coupling sections to achieve greater depth or installing multiple electrodes in parallel can help.

Poor Connections

Loose or corroded connections between the electrode and the earthing conductor increase resistance and can cause arcing. Use exothermic welding or high‑quality mechanical connectors with proper torque and anti‑corrosion treatment.

Damage During Installation

Driving electrodes into rocky ground can damage the copper coating or bend the rod. Pre‑drilling pilot holes in rocky areas or using specialised driving tools is advisable.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Earth Resistance Above Acceptable Limit
Likely Cause
High soil resistivity (dry or rocky ground), insufficient driving depth, or corrosion at the electrode.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a fall-of-potential or clamp-on earth resistance test; check driving depth against design (e.g., whether it reaches the water table); visually inspect the electrode via the earth pit for corrosion.
Corrective Action
Drive the electrode deeper using coupling sections, add parallel electrodes, apply chemical backfill, or replace a corroded electrode, then re-test to confirm resistance is within limits.
Visible Corrosion on the Electrode
Likely Cause
Aggressive soil conditions (high salinity, acidity, or stray DC currents) attacking the copper bonding or base metal.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the electrode through the earth pit for pitting, flaking, or loss of copper coating; test resistance to see if corrosion has degraded performance.
Corrective Action
Replace with solid copper or stainless steel electrodes suited to the soil conditions, and schedule more frequent inspection intervals for that site.
High Resistance at the Connection Point
Likely Cause
Loose or corroded connection between the electrode and the earthing conductor.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the connection for looseness, corrosion, or signs of arcing; measure resistance across the joint itself.
Corrective Action
Remake the connection using exothermic welding or a properly torqued, anti-corrosion-treated mechanical connector.
Electrode Bent or Coating Damaged During Installation
Likely Cause
Driving the electrode into rocky ground damaged the copper coating or bent the rod.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the exposed portion for bending or coating loss; test resistance to confirm the damage hasn't compromised performance.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged electrode, and pre-drill pilot holes or use specialised driving tools for rocky ground on future installs.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Assume Fault or Lightning Current May Be Present
Electrical Safety

Treat an earth electrode and its connections as potentially carrying fault or lightning current at any time; do not disconnect a bonding connection without first confirming the associated system is isolated or that the disconnection is planned and safe.

Excavation & Underground Services
Operational

Locate and mark existing buried cables and services before driving or excavating for an electrode, and follow the aerodrome's permit-to-dig process for any work below grade.

Driving Tool Hazards
Personnel Safety

Use eye protection and appropriate PPE when driving electrode sections with a hammer or powered driving tool, and keep hands clear of the striking point, particularly in rocky ground where rebound or rod deflection is more likely.

Test Before Relying on a Connection
Electrical Safety

Measure earth resistance and verify continuity to the main earthing system after installing or disturbing an electrode, and do not treat a newly driven or reconnected electrode as effective protection until it has been tested.

PPE for Touch/Step Potential
Personnel Safety

Be aware that touch and step potential can develop around an earth electrode during a fault or lightning event; avoid working on the electrode or its immediate surroundings during an active electrical storm.

Secure Bonding Connections
Compliance

Torque bonding connections to the electrode to the manufacturer's specification and protect the joint from corrosion, since a loose or corroded connection can silently raise the system's overall earth resistance.

FOD Control at the Excavation
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and backfill materials accounted for at all times when the electrode is exposed, and reinstate the surrounding ground before leaving the area, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave an electrode disconnected, undersized, or with an out-of-tolerance resistance reading after maintenance; verify the measured value meets the design target before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Resistance Can Rise Long Before a Connection Looks Bad
Troubleshooting

Corrosion at a bonding lug can significantly increase resistance well before there's any visible sign of deterioration at the joint. Rely on measured resistance trends over time rather than a visual inspection alone to decide when a connection needs remaking.

Soil Moisture Swings Can Mask a Genuine Fault
Design

Earth resistance readings can vary significantly between wet and dry seasons purely from soil moisture content, independent of the electrode's actual condition. Take the season into account when comparing a reading against the design target, and don't assume a single dry-season reading represents year-round performance.

Dissimilar Metals Accelerate Corrosion at the Joint
Durability

Bonding a copper conductor directly to a galvanized or aluminium electrode without the correct bimetallic connector or compound sets up galvanic corrosion that shortens the connection's service life well below the electrode's own rating. Match connector materials to both sides of the joint, not just to the conductor.

A Single Electrode Rarely Tells the Whole Story
Design

Testing one electrode in isolation can look compliant while the broader earthing system it's bonded into is still marginal, since electrodes in an array interact with each other's resistance. Evaluate the system-level resistance the electrode contributes to, not just its standalone reading, when diagnosing a borderline result.

Rocky or Shallow Ground Changes the Whole Approach
Installation

Where bedrock or very shallow soil prevents driving an electrode to design depth, defaulting to a shorter rod without compensating elsewhere leaves the system undersized. Consider a horizontal radial, chemical electrode, or multiple shorter rods in parallel rather than accepting a compromised single-rod installation.

Step and Touch Potential Risk Doesn't End When the Storm Passes
Personnel Safety

Residual charge and ongoing fault conditions can leave hazardous potential gradients around an electrode for some time after visible lightning activity has stopped. Confirm the system is fully clear before resuming work rather than treating the end of the storm itself as the all-clear.
