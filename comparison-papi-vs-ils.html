Visual Aid vs Radio Navigation Comparison

PAPI VS ILS

PAPI and ILS both help keep an aircraft on the correct approach path, but they are not competing versions of the same thing. PAPI is a visual lighting aid giving vertical guidance only, once the runway environment is in sight. ILS is a radio-navigation system giving both lateral and vertical guidance through the aircraft's own instruments — including in conditions where the PAPI cannot be seen at all.

02 · Definitions

What Is PAPI & What Is ILS?

PAPI

PAPI is a visual approach-slope indicator made up of four light units installed in a horizontal row beside the runway. The pilot looks outside and interprets the red/white pattern to judge vertical position on approach.

What it provides

Purely visual vertical approach-path information — nothing more.

ILS

ILS is a precision radio-navigation system with two primary components: the localizer, which provides lateral guidance relative to the extended runway centreline, and the glide slope, which provides vertical guidance, typically around a 3° path.

What it provides

Lateral + vertical guidance via radio signals received and displayed on the aircraft's own instruments.

03 · How Each System Signals Position

Light Pattern vs Instrument Display

PAPI

Read across the row — white = high, red = low
4 White — too high
2 White + 2 Red — on path
4 Red — too low

ILS

Read the localizer and glide-slope needles on the flight instrument
  • Localizer needle centred — on runway centreline; deflected left or right shows lateral deviation
  • Glide-slope needle centred — on the designed vertical path; deflected up or down shows the aircraft is low or high
  • Both centred — aircraft tracking the correct course and glide path
04 · The Core Difference

Vertical-Only vs Lateral + Vertical

This is the single most important distinction between the two systems.

PAPI

Provides vertical approach-slope information only. It does not tell the pilot anything about lateral position relative to the runway centreline.

ILS

Provides both axes at once — the localizer for lateral position and the glide slope for vertical position — through a single integrated instrument display.

PAPI does not provide the lateral guidance that an ILS localizer provides. The two systems are not interchangeable in scope.

05 · Pilot Interface

Looking Outside vs Reading Instruments

PAPI

Guidance is based entirely on visual observation — the pilot looks outside the aircraft at the light units and reads the red/white combination directly.

ILS

Guidance comes from radio signals received by the aircraft and displayed on a cockpit instrument, independent of what the pilot can currently see outside.

06 · Visibility & Low-Visibility Operations

A Major Operational Difference

PAPI depends entirely on the pilot being able to visually acquire the light units. If conditions such as fog, heavy precipitation, cloud or smoke prevent that visual acquisition, PAPI simply cannot provide its normal guidance. ILS, by contrast, is specifically designed to provide reliable instrument guidance in conditions where visual references may be inadequate.

PAPI depends on visual acquisition. ILS provides instrument guidance without requiring the runway or PAPI to be visible. This is why ILS underpins low-visibility and precision instrument approach operations, while PAPI serves as a visual reference once the environment is in sight.

07 · Precision Approach

PAPI Does Not Make an Approach "Precision"

PAPI by itself does not make an approach a precision instrument approach. ILS is the instrument landing system that supports precision approach operations, according to the specific installation and applicable operational category.

Don't confuse the name with the function. The word "Precision" in PAPI refers to the design of the approach-path indicator itself — it does not mean PAPI is equivalent to an ILS precision approach system.

08 · Used Together

Can PAPI and ILS Work on the Same Runway?

Yes — and in practice they commonly complement each other. The ILS provides instrument guidance throughout the approach, including before the runway environment becomes visible. Once the PAPI is visible, the pilot can use it as an independent visual cross-check against what the ILS instruments are already showing.

Agreement between the two is a useful situational cue. Where an ILS and PAPI serve the same runway, their approach paths are ideally correlated so the visual and instrument guidance point to the same slope.

09 · Equipment & Location

Where Each System Sits

PAPI

Typically a power supply, control/CCR and four light units, usually located beside the runway near the approach end. No special aircraft equipment is required to use it.

ILS

A localizer transmitter and antenna array, generally beyond the far end of the runway, plus a glide-slope transmitter and antenna near the approach end. The aircraft also needs its own ILS receiver and display system to use it.

12 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: PAPI is a form of ILS
Fact

No. PAPI is a visual lighting system; ILS is a radio-navigation system. They work on entirely different principles.

Myth: PAPI provides centreline guidance
Fact

No. PAPI provides vertical approach-path guidance only — it says nothing about lateral position.

Myth: ILS only provides vertical guidance
Fact

No. ILS provides both — the localizer for lateral guidance and the glide slope for vertical guidance.

Myth: PAPI can replace ILS in low visibility
Fact

No. PAPI requires visual acquisition and cannot provide the instrument guidance an ILS gives in low-visibility conditions.

Myth: PAPI and ILS should show different approach slopes
Fact

No. Where the two are designed to serve the same runway, the visual approach path is intended to be correlated with the non-visual glide path, not to disagree with it.

13 · Engineering Verdict

PAPI vs ILS

Engineering Verdict

PAPI and ILS are not competing systems — they operate on different principles and serve different phases of the approach. ILS is a precision radio-navigation system giving lateral and vertical guidance through the aircraft's own instruments, usable well before the runway environment is visually acquired. PAPI is a simple visual aid giving vertical guidance only, and only once it can actually be seen.

The two are frequently installed together and are meant to complement each other: ILS carries the approach through to the point of visual acquisition, and PAPI then gives the pilot an independent visual cross-check against the instrument-derived glide path.

PAPIVisual, vertical-only, light-based, requires visual acquisition, no aircraft equipment needed.
ILSInstrument, lateral + vertical, radio-based, works in low visibility, requires an aircraft receiver.

PAPI complements ILS — it does not replace it, and it should never be treated as a substitute for instrument guidance in marginal visibility.

14 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between PAPI and ILS?

PAPI is a visual lighting aid providing vertical guidance only, read directly by the pilot looking outside. ILS is a radio-navigation system providing both lateral (localizer) and vertical (glide slope) guidance through the aircraft's instruments.

Can PAPI replace ILS in poor visibility?

No. PAPI requires the pilot to visually acquire the light units, so it cannot function as guidance when visibility conditions prevent that. ILS is specifically designed for use in such conditions.

Does PAPI provide lateral or centreline guidance?

No. PAPI gives vertical approach-path guidance only. Lateral guidance, when needed, comes from the ILS localizer or other navigation aids.

Can PAPI and ILS be installed on the same runway?

Yes, and this is common. The ILS provides instrument guidance throughout the approach, while PAPI gives the pilot a visual cross-check once the runway environment is in sight.

Does using PAPI require any equipment on the aircraft?

No. PAPI is read purely visually by the pilot. ILS, on the other hand, requires the aircraft to carry a compatible receiver and display.

Does "Precision" in PAPI mean it supports precision approaches like an ILS?

No. "Precision" in PAPI's name refers to the design of the light-pattern indicator itself, not to precision instrument approach capability, which is provided by systems like ILS.

15 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — visual approach slope indicator systems including PAPI, siting and photometric requirements.

ICAO Annex 10, Volume I

Aeronautical Telecommunications — technical specifications for the Instrument Landing System, including localizer and glide-slope performance categories.

FAA AC 150/5345-28 & FAA Order 6750.16

AC 150/5345-28, Current Edition (Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) Systems, equipment designations L-880/L-881) sets the PAPI equipment standard; FAA Order 6750.16, Current Edition (Siting Criteria for Instrument Landing Systems) sets ILS siting criteria.

Final Engineering Note

PAPI and ILS design, siting and integration must always be developed from the current governing standard, the approved airport design and project-specific operational requirements — not from a generic web comparison.

PAPI is a visual complement to ILS, not a substitute for it. A correctly designed, aligned and maintained PAPI, working alongside a correctly functioning ILS, gives the pilot both instrument and visual confirmation of the same approach path.
