1Case Study Overview

During routine night operations, Runway 09/27 edge lighting failed completely immediately after the runway lighting circuit was energized from the ALCMS.

The operations center reported:

  • Entire runway edge circuit dark.
  • CCR available but abnormal.
  • ALCMS alarm active.
  • Remote control unsuccessful.
  • No visible runway edge lights.

Because the failure affected the entire runway lighting system, a comprehensive investigation was initiated covering:

  • ALCMS commands.
  • PLC control logic.
  • SCADA communication.
  • CCR operation.
  • Primary cable integrity.
  • Insulation resistance.
  • Isolating transformers.
  • Secondary circuits.
  • L-823 connectors.
  • Individual light fittings.
  • Photometric verification.

The investigation identified multiple contributing failures, with the primary cause being a primary-circuit insulation failure combined with secondary-connector water ingress, resulting in total loss of runway edge lighting.

This case demonstrates the complete engineering methodology for investigating a full runway lighting system failure.

9Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The complete runway lighting failure resulted from combined primary-circuit insulation failure and secondary-connector water ingress, including:

  • Primary cable insulation breakdown.
  • Water-related earth leakage.
  • Open-circuit development.
  • Transformer moisture contamination.
  • Connector corrosion.
  • Secondary-circuit degradation.

The primary failure mechanism was primary-circuit failure, while secondary deterioration contributed to system instability.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: Entire runway edge circuit OFF. CCR unable to regulate current. Output voltage extremely high. ALCMS communication healthy. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace all runway light fittings.
B. Replace the CCR.
C. Verify the control system, analyze CCR behavior, test primary-circuit continuity, measure insulation resistance, perform TDR fault location, inspect transformers and connectors, and verify the complete electrical chain from the CCR to the fixture.
D. Increase CCR current.
Correct answer: C. A complete runway lighting failure requires systematic investigation from the control system through the entire electrical chain. CCR behavior (high voltage, no current regulation) indicates an open or high-impedance circuit. Following the structured methodology from ALCMS to fixture will identify the root cause without unnecessary equipment replacement.

Quick Reference: Runway Lighting CCR Current

Runway lighting CCR current is the series current the CCR holds in the loop, set by the brightness step. If the whole runway is dark, check that the CCR is on, that its output current is correct at the selected step and that its alarms are clear. The current reading tells you whether the fault is at the CCR or in the loop after it.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about complete runway lighting failure and the CCR-to-fixture methodology.

Q1What is AGL-CS-099?

AGL-CS-099 is an engineering training case study. It covers a complete Runway 09/27 edge lighting failure, investigated from the ALCMS command through to the individual fixtures. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2What investigation sequence was used?

ALCMS, SCADA, PLC, CCR, primary cable, insulation, transformer, secondary cable, connector, fixture and photometry. Working in order prevents unnecessary component replacement.

Q3Was the control system at fault?

No. The command was transmitted, the PLC received it and the CCR start command executed, with ALCMS communication healthy.

Q4What did the CCR show?

Input supply was normal, but the CCR could not regulate current and output voltage was excessively high with protection active, indicating an open-circuit or high-impedance condition.

Q5How was the fault found in the field?

Continuity testing showed an open primary circuit and insulation resistance was 0.9 MΩ against at least 100 MΩ. TDR located the fault about 1.42 km from the CCR, where excavation found a damaged cable with water ingress, sheath deterioration and earth leakage.

Q6Were other components affected?

Yes. The nearest transformer had water contamination and terminal corrosion. Secondary continuity was intermittent, and L-823 connectors showed moisture, corrosion and incomplete sealing.

Q7What was the root cause of AGL-CS-099?

Primary-circuit insulation failure combined with secondary-connector water ingress. The primary mechanism was primary-circuit failure, with secondary deterioration adding instability.

Q8How was the runway lighting restored?

The team replaced the damaged primary cable section, repaired joints and replaced the affected transformer, L-823 connectors and secondary cable sections. They restored enclosure sealing, tested insulation and CCR regulation, verified ALCMS operation and conducted photometric verification.

Q9What is the key lesson from this case?

Use a systematic top-to-bottom method. Complete failures can have several contributing faults, so test each layer instead of replacing parts by guesswork.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

A complete runway lighting failure must be investigated as an integrated system failure from the ALCMS command through the PLC, CCR, primary circuit, isolating transformers, secondary circuits, connectors, and fixtures, and effective troubleshooting requires systematic elimination of each subsystem rather than replacement of individual components.

Systematic control-system verification, CCR diagnostics, primary-circuit testing, insulation analysis, TDR fault location, transformer evaluation, connector inspection, fixture verification, and photometric testing provide a complete engineering methodology for restoring runway lighting safely and efficiently.

In AGL engineering, the runway light is only the final visible component of a much larger electrical, control, and automation system, and the engineer who understands the entire chain from the CCR to the fixture can solve failures that others can only observe.
