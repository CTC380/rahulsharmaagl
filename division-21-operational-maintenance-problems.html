OPM-506 Frequent Lamp Failure

Symptom — A fixture, group, or circuit shows a lamp replacement frequency well above the expected service life, identified through maintenance records rather than a single failure event.

Equipment / System

Incandescent/halogen lamp population across a fixture, circuit, or zone, assessed collectively via maintenance and replacement history rather than as an individual bulb fault.

Possible Causes
HighSustained overvoltage at the CCR output driving lamps beyond their rated operating voltage, shortening filament life across the whole circuit
HighVibration from ground/aircraft traffic at that specific location accelerating filament fatigue
MedIncorrect lamp wattage/voltage rating stocked and used at replacement, mismatched to the fixture's isolation transformer
MedFrequent power cycling (on/off) at that location compared to others, accelerating thermal filament stress
LowA batch of lamps from a specific supplier/lot with a systemic quality defect
LowPoor fixture ventilation/heat dissipation at that specific location shortening lamp life
Required Tools
CCR output voltage/current trend logTrue-RMS multimeter (CAT III)Lamp replacement history/work order recordsSample lamps from suspect batch for bench comparison
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before lamp replacement or fixture inspection.
  • Coordinate airside access through ATC/apron control before approaching fixtures on an active surface.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the lamp replacement history for the affected fixture/circuit/zone and compare the failure rate against the expected service life and against comparable circuits.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, verify CCR output voltage/current against design at the affected circuit, and compare to a circuit with normal lamp life.
  3. MeasurementConfirm the lamp wattage/voltage rating in use matches the fixture/transformer specification.
  4. DecisionVoltage/current confirmed elevated → investigate CCR calibration or transformer tap setting. Rating or vibration/location factor identified → address at the fixture level.
  5. Fault LocationDetermine whether the elevated failure rate is circuit-wide (pointing to CCR output) or localised to specific fixtures (pointing to vibration, ventilation, or a batch defect).
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action reduces the failure rate over the following several operating cycles rather than relying on a single replacement.
Test Points
CCR output voltage/current vs. design
Lamp rating in stock vs. fixture specification
Comparative failure rate across circuits
Failure rate trend after correction
Root Cause Verification

Track the lamp replacement rate at the affected location over the following several months to confirm it has returned to the expected baseline.

Corrective Action
  • Correct CCR output voltage/current if confirmed elevated.
  • Replace lamps with the correctly rated stock where a mismatch is found.
  • Address vibration or ventilation issues at chronically affected fixture locations.
  • Quarantine and stop using a confirmed defective lamp batch.
Preventive Action
  • Trend lamp replacement rates by circuit/zone to catch an emerging pattern before it becomes a chronic maintenance burden.
  • Verify CCR output calibration on a defined schedule.
  • Track lamp batch/lot numbers to enable early detection of a systemic quality issue.
Related References

Standards: FAA AC 150/5340-30, manufacturer lamp specification sheets

OPM-507 Frequent LED Failure

Symptom — A fixture, group, or circuit fitted with LED modules shows a module or driver failure rate well above the expected service life, identified through maintenance records.

Equipment / System

LED module and driver population across a fixture, circuit, or zone, assessed collectively via maintenance and replacement history.

Possible Causes
HighDriver overheating from inadequate heat sinking or ventilation at that fixture type/location, accelerating electronic component degradation
HighVoltage/current transients at the CCR output or a specific circuit segment stressing driver electronics beyond design tolerance
MedA batch of LED modules or drivers from a specific supplier/lot with a systemic quality defect
MedIncorrect driver current setting for the LED module installed, causing accelerated LED degradation
LowMoisture ingress at a specific location repeatedly damaging driver electronics
LowThermal cycling extremes at a particular location (e.g. high solar gain) accelerating solder joint fatigue in the driver
Required Tools
Thermal imaging cameraTrue-RMS multimeter (CAT III) with waveform/transient captureLED module/driver replacement history recordsSample modules from suspect batch for bench comparison
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before module/driver replacement or inspection.
  • Coordinate airside access through ATC/apron control before approaching fixtures on an active surface.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the LED module/driver replacement history for the affected fixture/circuit/zone and compare the failure rate against expected service life and comparable circuits.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, thermally image the driver during a live operating period to check for overheating relative to a known-good fixture.
  3. MeasurementCheck CCR output for voltage/current transients or instability on the affected circuit.
  4. DecisionThermal reading elevated → investigate heat sinking/ventilation at that fixture type/location. CCR output shows transients → route to Division 01 for source-side investigation.
  5. Fault LocationDetermine whether the elevated failure rate is circuit-wide (pointing to CCR/source) or localised (pointing to thermal or moisture factors at specific fixtures).
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action reduces the failure rate over subsequent operating cycles.
Test Points
Driver thermal signature vs. known-good fixture
CCR output stability on the affected circuit
Comparative failure rate across circuits
Failure rate trend after correction
Root Cause Verification

Track the LED module/driver replacement rate at the affected location over the following several months to confirm it has returned to baseline.

Corrective Action
  • Improve heat sinking/ventilation at chronically affected fixture locations.
  • Investigate and correct CCR output instability if confirmed.
  • Quarantine and stop using a confirmed defective module/driver batch.
  • Correct driver current setting to match the installed LED module.
Preventive Action
  • Trend LED module/driver replacement rates by circuit/zone to catch an emerging pattern early.
  • Include thermal imaging of drivers in the periodic PM cycle for fixture types with a history of failures.
  • Track LED module/driver batch/lot numbers to enable early detection of a systemic quality issue.

Equipment / System

Isolation transformer population across a fixture, circuit, or zone, assessed collectively via maintenance and replacement history.

Possible Causes
HighSustained overvoltage or current transients at the CCR output stressing transformer insulation and windings across the circuit
HighChronic moisture exposure at that location's base cans repeatedly degrading transformer insulation
MedA batch of transformers from a specific supplier/lot with a systemic quality defect
MedTransformers under-rated for the actual fixture load at that location, running consistently overloaded
LowRepeated mechanical stress (e.g. from unsupported installation per Division 17) fatiguing internal connections over time
LowAmbient temperature extremes at a particular location accelerating insulation ageing
Required Tools
CCR output voltage/current trend log1000V insulation resistance tester (megger)Transformer replacement history recordsSample transformers from suspect batch for bench comparison
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before transformer replacement or inspection.

Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the transformer replacement history for the affected fixture/circuit/zone and compare the failure rate against expected service life and comparable circuits.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, verify CCR output voltage/current against design, and check insulation resistance trend for the affected circuit.
  3. MeasurementInspect base cans at the affected location for chronic moisture or mechanical stress consistent with Division 14/17 fault patterns.
  4. DecisionCCR output confirmed elevated → investigate source-side calibration. Moisture or mechanical stress identified → address at the base-can/installation level.
  5. Fault LocationDetermine whether the elevated failure rate is circuit-wide (source-side) or localised (installation/environmental).
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action reduces the failure rate over subsequent operating cycles and re-check insulation resistance trend.

Failure rate trend after correction

Corrective Action
  • Correct CCR output voltage/current if confirmed elevated.
  • Address chronic base-can moisture or mechanical stress at affected locations.
  • Quarantine and stop using a confirmed defective transformer batch.
  • Replace under-rated transformers with the correctly specified rating.
Preventive Action
  • Trend transformer replacement rates by circuit/zone to catch an emerging pattern early.
  • Verify CCR output calibration on a defined schedule.
  • Track transformer batch/lot numbers to enable early detection of a systemic quality issue.

OPM-509 Frequent Connector Failure

Symptom — A specific circuit, zone, or fixture type shows an L-823 connector failure rate well above the expected service life, identified through maintenance records rather than a single connector event.

Equipment / System

L-823 connector population across a fixture, circuit, or zone, assessed collectively via maintenance and replacement history.

Possible Causes
HighChronic base-can flooding or drainage issues at that location repeatedly causing water/moisture-related connector failures
HighGround/aircraft traffic vibration at that specific location repeatedly loosening or damaging connectors
MedA batch of connectors from a specific supplier/lot with a systemic quality defect
MedConsistent installation-practice errors (sealing, support, torque) at that location per Division 17 findings
LowConnector type under-rated for the circuit's actual current at that location
LowRepeated mishandling during frequent maintenance access at a high-traffic-fault location
Required Tools
Connector replacement/failure history records1000V insulation resistance tester (megger)Base-can drainage inspection kitSample connectors from suspect batch for bench comparison
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before connector replacement or inspection.
  • Coordinate airside access through ATC/apron control before approaching base cans on an active surface.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the connector replacement/failure history for the affected fixture/circuit/zone and characterise the pattern — same failure mode each time, or a mix.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, inspect base-can drainage and mechanical condition at the affected location per Division 14 procedures.
  3. MeasurementIf a batch defect is suspected, bench-test sample connectors from the same lot against a known-good reference.
  4. DecisionPattern points to a location-specific drainage/mechanical condition → correct the underlying condition rather than continuing like-for-like swaps. Pattern points to a batch defect → proactively replace other connectors from that lot.
  5. Fault LocationDistinguish between a location-specific systemic cause and a component-batch-specific cause before deciding the scope of corrective action.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action addresses the systemic driver identified, and monitor the failure rate at that location afterward.
Test Points
Historical failure mode pattern from records
Base-can drainage/mechanical condition
Comparative bench test of suspect batch connectors
Failure rate trend after correction
Root Cause Verification

Place the affected location on a shortened monitoring interval for several cycles and confirm the failure pattern does not recur before returning to standard PM.

Corrective Action
  • Correct chronic base-can drainage or mechanical stress at the affected location.
  • Replace connectors with an upgraded or better-suited type if the standard part is inadequate for that location's conditions.
  • Quarantine and stop using a confirmed defective connector batch.
Preventive Action
  • Flag locations with two or more repeat connector failures for root-cause review rather than routine like-for-like replacement.
  • Track connector batch/lot numbers to enable early detection of systemic quality issues.
  • Feed recurring-failure locations into the design/engineering review process for a permanent fix.
Related References

SOP: SOP-CON-14 (Repeat Failure Root Cause Investigation Procedure), SOP-OPM-01 (Recurring Failure Rate Trending Procedure)

OPM-510 Frequent Cable Failure

Symptom — A specific circuit segment or zone shows a cable fault (insulation breakdown, open, or short) rate well above the expected service life, identified through maintenance and fault-location records.

Equipment / System

Primary series cable population across a circuit segment or zone, assessed collectively via maintenance and fault history rather than a single cable event.

Possible Causes
HighChronic mechanical stress at that location — heavy traffic loading, repeated excavation nearby, or pavement movement — repeatedly damaging cable insulation
HighCable of that age/batch nearing end of service life across the whole segment
MedChronic moisture exposure at low-lying sections of the route repeatedly degrading insulation
MedInadequate burial depth or protection at that segment per Division 17 findings, leaving it exposed to repeated damage
LowA manufacturing batch defect in the cable insulation
LowFrequent third-party excavation activity in the area repeatedly nicking or stressing the cable
Required Tools
Cable fault/failure history records1000V/2500V insulation resistance tester (megger) with trend logTDR / fault locatorRoute/burial as-built drawing for the affected segment
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before fault-locating or excavating.
  • Coordinate with other utility owners before excavating near the cable route.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the fault/failure history for the affected segment and characterise the pattern — clustered at one point, distributed along the run, or associated with specific events (excavation, weather).
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, megger the segment and use a TDR/fault locator to identify the specific fault points from this and prior events.
  3. MeasurementReview burial depth, protection, and drainage conditions along the segment per the as-built record.
  4. DecisionFaults clustered at a specific point with a mechanical/environmental cause → correct the local condition (protection, drainage, re-route). Faults distributed and age-related → plan proactive replacement of the segment.
  5. Fault LocationMap all historical fault points along the segment to identify whether they cluster or distribute.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action reduces the fault rate on that segment over subsequent seasons/cycles.
Test Points
Historical fault location pattern along the segment
Insulation resistance trend for the segment
Burial depth/protection condition vs. specification
Fault rate trend after correction
Root Cause Verification

Trend the segment's fault rate over the following operating seasons and re-megger periodically to confirm the corrective action has been effective.

Corrective Action
  • Add or repair cable protection at a clustered fault location.
  • Correct chronic drainage issues along the affected segment.
  • Plan proactive cable replacement for a segment showing distributed, age-related fault clustering.
  • Coordinate with utility owners to prevent repeated third-party excavation damage.
Preventive Action
  • Trend cable fault rates by segment to catch an emerging pattern before a hard failure occurs.
  • Prioritise ageing or previously-faulted segments for proactive replacement in capital planning.
  • Maintain accurate as-built burial and protection records to speed future fault investigation.
Related References

SOP: SOP-OPM-01 (Recurring Failure Rate Trending Procedure), SOP-TCL-11 (Guidance Circuit Fault Location Procedure)

Standards: IEC 60364, FAA AC 150/5340-30

OPM-511 Excessive Maintenance Requirement

Symptom — A specific fixture, circuit, or zone consistently consumes a disproportionate share of maintenance labour/work orders compared to the rest of the system, without any single dominant failure mode standing out.

Equipment / System

The maintenance workload profile for a specific fixture, circuit, or zone, assessed via aggregated work order records across all fault types rather than a single component.

Possible Causes
HighMultiple minor, individually-unremarkable faults (loose hardware, minor moisture, minor drift) recurring at the same location, none severe enough to trigger a dedicated root-cause investigation on its own
HighAn underlying environmental stressor at that location (traffic loading, drainage, exposure) affecting several component types simultaneously
MedOriginal design or installation at that location marginal in a way that manifests as generalised extra wear rather than one specific fault
MedThat location simply receiving disproportionate reporting/inspection attention rather than actually failing more, skewing the record
LowStaffing or procedural factors causing repeat visits for the same underlying issue rather than a single thorough repair
LowA recently changed operational pattern (e.g. new taxiway routing) increasing traffic loading at that location beyond original design assumptions
Required Tools
Aggregated work order/maintenance history report for the locationEnvironmental/traffic loading data for the location if availableDesign and installation records for the locationCross-division troubleshooting atlas reference for individual fault types found
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before any on-site investigation.
  • Coordinate airside access through ATC/apron control for the investigation visit.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the full aggregated work order history for the location across all fault types and categorise each entry by division/fault type.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated where applicable, conduct a thorough on-site inspection covering mounting, connectors, transformer, cable, and earthing rather than only the most recently reported symptom.
  3. MeasurementCompare the location's traffic loading, drainage, and environmental exposure against comparable, lower-maintenance locations.
  4. DecisionA common environmental or design factor identified across multiple fault types → address that root driver rather than each symptom individually. No common factor found → continue standard component-level troubleshooting for each new fault as it arises, but flag the location for closer monitoring.
  5. Fault LocationIdentify whether the excess maintenance burden is genuinely component failures or partly a reporting/procedural artefact.
  6. Root CauseConfirm whether addressing the identified common factor reduces the overall work order rate for that location over subsequent months.
Test Points
Work order count and categorisation by fault type over a defined period
Traffic loading/environmental exposure vs. comparable locations
Design/installation record review findings
Work order rate trend after intervention
Root Cause Verification

Track the total work order rate for the location over the following several months and compare against the historical baseline and comparable locations to confirm improvement.

Corrective Action
  • Address the identified common environmental or design factor (traffic loading, drainage, marginal installation) rather than treating each fault individually.
  • Escalate for a design/engineering review if the location's conditions exceed original design assumptions.
  • Correct any procedural gap causing repeat visits for the same underlying issue.
Preventive Action
  • Review aggregated work order data by location on a regular interval to catch an emerging high-maintenance pattern early, not just individual fault trends.
  • Feed chronic high-maintenance locations into the capital renewal/design review process.
  • Ensure work orders are closed out with full root-cause documentation to avoid masking a systemic pattern behind many small tickets.
Related References

SOP: SOP-OPM-01 (Recurring Failure Rate Trending Procedure)

Standards: FAA AC 150/5340-30, internal reliability/RCA guidance

OPM-512 Repeated Circuit Trips

Symptom — A specific series circuit trips its CCR protection repeatedly over a period, with each individual trip investigated and seemingly resolved, yet the circuit continues to trip again within a short interval.

Equipment / System

CCR overcurrent/protection function and the series circuit it protects, assessed across a pattern of repeated trip events rather than a single trip.

Possible Causes
HighA marginal or intermittent fault on the circuit that clears itself enough to pass a post-trip test but reappears under specific operating conditions (thermal, vibration, moisture)
HighCCR protection settings are marginal for the circuit's normal inrush or operating characteristics, tripping on conditions that are not actually faults
MedMultiple independent minor faults on the circuit, each individually minor, cumulatively pushing the circuit to the trip threshold
MedA repair from a previous trip investigation addressed a symptom but not the actual root cause
LowCCR itself has a marginal or drifting protection relay/sensor giving false trips
LowAn external factor (e.g. a nearby lightning-prone area, or a specific weather pattern) coinciding with each trip event
Required Tools
CCR trip/event log with timestamps and trip type1000V/2500V insulation resistance tester (megger)Series circuit fault locator / TDRCCR protection settings and calibration record
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before investigation or testing.
  • Treat a circuit with a repeated-trip history as higher priority for thorough investigation rather than another quick reset-and-monitor cycle.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the full trip history for the circuit, including timestamps, and look for a pattern correlated with time of day, weather, or operating condition.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, megger the full circuit and compare against previous test values to check for a genuine, if marginal, insulation trend.
  3. MeasurementReview the CCR's protection settings and calibration against the circuit's normal operating characteristics.
  4. DecisionInsulation trend confirms a genuine marginal fault → proceed to full segment-by-segment fault location per Division 08/14 procedures. No insulation trend found and settings are marginal → review and adjust CCR protection settings.
  5. Fault LocationUse the trip-history pattern (time correlation, weather correlation) to help narrow which segment or condition to focus fault-location effort on.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action results in no further trips over an extended monitoring period covering the conditions previously associated with trips.
Test Points
Trip event timestamps and correlation with operating conditions
Full-circuit insulation resistance vs. historical trend
CCR protection settings vs. circuit characteristics
Trip-free interval after correction
Root Cause Verification

Monitor the circuit's trip log over an extended period, ideally covering the range of conditions (weather, time of day) previously associated with trips, before closing out as resolved.

Corrective Action
  • Complete full segment-by-segment fault location and repair for a confirmed marginal insulation fault.
  • Adjust CCR protection settings if found to be marginal for the circuit's normal characteristics.
  • Recalibrate or replace a CCR protection relay/sensor found to be drifting.
Preventive Action
  • Treat any second trip on the same circuit within a defined interval as a repeated-trip investigation trigger rather than a routine reset.
  • Trend insulation resistance for chronically tripping circuits rather than relying on a single pass/fail test after each event.
  • Review CCR protection settings periodically against actual circuit operating characteristics.
Related References

SOP: SOP-CCR-05 (Repeated Trip Investigation Procedure)

Standards: FAA AC 150/5340-30, IEC 60364

OPM-513 Repeated Earth Fault

Symptom — A circuit's earth-fault/ground-fault protection trips repeatedly over a period, with insulation resistance appearing to recover between events, yet the fault recurs.

Equipment / System

Circuit earth-fault/ground-fault protection function at the CCR and the insulation system of the circuit it protects, assessed across a pattern of repeated events.

Possible Causes
HighMoisture intrusion at a specific point that dries out between rain events, temporarily restoring insulation resistance until the next wet cycle
HighA cable or connector insulation defect that only manifests as a leakage path under specific thermal or mechanical conditions
MedCumulative ageing across the circuit reducing overall insulation margin, so the circuit trips under any additional minor stress
MedEarth-fault protection threshold set marginally, tripping on borderline-acceptable readings rather than a genuine developing fault
LowA shared earthing/bonding issue elsewhere in the vault affecting the reference used for the earth-fault measurement
LowSeasonal water-table changes affecting insulation resistance at specific points along the route
Required Tools
CCR earth-fault trend log with timestamps1000V/2500V insulation resistance tester (megger)Moisture detection meterWeather/rainfall correlation data for the site
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before investigation or testing.
  • Treat a circuit with a repeated earth-fault history as a developing hazard warranting thorough investigation rather than repeated resets.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the earth-fault trip history with timestamps and correlate against weather/rainfall data to check for a moisture-related pattern.
  2. MeasurementWith the circuit isolated, megger the full circuit segment-by-segment, ideally during or shortly after a period similar to when trips have occurred, to catch the fault in its active state.
  3. MeasurementInspect base cans and connectors along the circuit for moisture at points identified as lowest-reading in the segment test.
  4. DecisionSegment testing isolates a specific low-reading point correlated with the trip pattern → proceed to targeted repair at that point. No clear segment isolation despite testing → consider the earth-fault protection threshold itself as marginal and review CCR settings.
  5. Fault LocationUse weather/timing correlation to decide when to conduct segment testing, since a dried-out fault may not show up in a test performed long after the last trip.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action results in no further earth-fault trips through at least one full wet-weather cycle before closing out.
Test Points
Earth-fault trip timestamps correlated with weather
Segment insulation resistance tested during/after a relevant weather event
Moisture presence at the lowest-reading point
Trip-free interval through a subsequent wet-weather cycle
Root Cause Verification

Monitor the circuit's earth-fault trend through at least one full wet-weather cycle after correction before closing out as resolved.

Corrective Action
  • Repair or reseal the identified moisture entry point.
  • Replace the cable segment or connector confirmed as the source of the intermittent leakage path.
  • Review and adjust CCR earth-fault protection threshold if found to be marginal rather than reflecting a genuine fault.
Preventive Action
  • Test insulation resistance during or shortly after wet weather for circuits with a history of earth-fault trips, rather than only during dry conditions.
  • Trend earth-fault readings over time rather than treating each trip as an isolated event.
  • Prioritise circuits with a repeated earth-fault pattern for proactive moisture-point sealing during PM.
Related References

SOP: SOP-CON-05 (Water Ingress Inspection and Remediation Procedure), SOP-CCR-05 (Repeated Trip Investigation Procedure)

Standards: IEC 60364, FAA AC 150/5340-30

OPM-514 Repeated CCR Fault

Symptom — A specific Constant Current Regulator unit experiences repeated internal faults (output instability, protection nuisance trips, control card errors) over a period, despite individual repairs, distinct from faults isolated to the circuit it feeds.

Equipment / System

CCR unit internals — control card, output stage, protection relays, and cooling — assessed across a pattern of repeated fault events rather than the circuit-side faults it protects.

Possible Causes
HighA marginal or ageing internal component (control card, output SCR/transistor stage) causing intermittent faults that each individual repair only partially addresses
HighInadequate cooling/ventilation for the CCR's operating environment causing repeated thermal-stress-related faults
MedInput power quality issues (voltage sags, harmonics) at that specific vault stressing the CCR repeatedly
MedA previous repair used an incorrect or non-genuine replacement part, itself prone to failure
LowFirmware/software issue on a digital CCR causing recurring control faults not resolved by a hardware-focused repair
LowThe CCR is simply approaching end of service life with generalised increasing fault frequency across multiple subsystems
Required Tools
CCR fault/event log with timestamps and fault codesThermal imaging camera for CCR cabinetInput power quality loggerManufacturer CCR service/repair history for the unit
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO and de-energise the CCR from both input and output sides before internal investigation.
  • Follow manufacturer safety procedures for capacitor discharge and internal component access.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationPull the full fault/event log for the CCR, including fault codes, and look for a pattern (same subsystem repeatedly, or a spread across different subsystems).
  2. MeasurementWith the unit isolated, thermally image the cabinet during a subsequent live period to check for a chronic hot spot.
  3. MeasurementCheck input power quality at the vault for sags, surges, or harmonics that could be stressing the CCR repeatedly.
  4. DecisionPattern points to one subsystem repeatedly → replace that subsystem/card rather than repairing again. Pattern is generalised across multiple subsystems → consider the unit approaching end of service life and evaluate replacement.
  5. Fault LocationConfirm whether the repeated fault is genuinely internal to the CCR or is a symptom of an external input power quality issue.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action results in a fault-free interval consistent with normal CCR reliability expectations before closing out.
Test Points
Fault code pattern across the event log
Thermal signature of the CCR cabinet under load
Input power quality at the vault
Fault-free interval after correction
Root Cause Verification

Monitor the CCR's fault log over an extended period after correction and confirm no further recurrence of the same fault pattern before closing out.

Corrective Action
  • Replace the specific subsystem/card responsible for a repeatedly recurring fault.
  • Improve cooling/ventilation for the CCR's operating environment.
  • Address input power quality issues at the vault if identified as the driver.
  • Evaluate full CCR replacement if the unit shows generalised end-of-life fault frequency.
Preventive Action
  • Track CCR fault codes and frequency by unit to identify an emerging repeated-fault pattern early.
  • Use only manufacturer-approved genuine replacement parts for CCR repairs.
  • Include input power quality monitoring in vaults with a history of CCR faults.
Related References

SOP: SOP-CCR-05 (Repeated Trip Investigation Procedure), SOP-CCR-06 (CCR Internal Fault Diagnosis Procedure)

Standards: Manufacturer CCR service manual, FAA AC 150/5345-10

OPM-515 Repeated Sign Failure

Symptom — A specific lighted airfield sign experiences repeated failures (dark, dim, wrong colour, or flickering) over a period, despite individual repairs, indicating a systemic rather than isolated cause at that sign location.

Equipment / System

Lighted airfield guidance sign and its internal LED modules, driver, and connections, assessed across a pattern of repeated fault events at a specific location.

Possible Causes
HighChronic moisture ingress into the sign housing at that location, repeatedly damaging internal electronics
HighRepeated impact or vibration exposure at that location (e.g. proximity to a high-traffic taxiway junction) stressing internal connections
MedA batch of LED modules or drivers from a specific supplier/lot with a systemic quality defect installed in that sign
MedInadequate ventilation/heat dissipation in that sign's housing design or installation, accelerating electronic degradation
LowPower supply instability specific to the circuit feeding that sign

  4. DecisionCircuit-level flooding only, vault unaffected → proceed with systematic circuit-by-circuit pump-out, drying, and insulation verification. Vault/CCR affected → treat as a higher-priority, system-wide event requiring full equipment assessment before any re-energisation.
  5. Fault LocationPrioritise fault investigation by criticality (e.g. runway circuits before secondary taxiway circuits) given the scale of a flood event.
  6. Root CauseConfirm every affected circuit passes insulation testing before re-energising, and monitor for the secondary fault wave that can follow as residual saturation continues to affect components over subsequent days.
Test Points
Extent of flood-affected base cans/circuits/vault
Insulation resistance for every affected circuit before re-energising
Vault/CCR equipment condition if affected
Secondary fault trend over the days following the event
Root Cause Verification

Systematically re-verify insulation resistance for every affected circuit before and after re-energising, and continue monitoring for a secondary fault wave over the following days as residual moisture continues to affect components.

Corrective Action
  • Pump out and dry all affected base cans, pull boxes, and vault areas.
  • Replace components (connectors, transformers, control cards) confirmed damaged by flood exposure rather than only drying and re-testing marginal readings.
  • Coordinate with civil/drainage teams on any temporary or permanent drainage improvement needed to prevent recurrence.
  • Prioritise restoration by circuit criticality.
Preventive Action
  • Maintain a flood emergency response procedure specific to the AGL system, including prioritisation criteria and safety steps.
  • Identify flood-prone base cans and vault areas in advance and consider protective measures (raised sills, backup pumping) proactively.
  • Review drainage design capacity against extreme rainfall/flood scenarios, not just routine rainfall.
Related References

SOP: SOP-OPM-05 (Flood Emergency Response and Recovery Procedure), SOP-CON-05 (Water Ingress Inspection and Remediation Procedure)

Standards: FAA AC 150/5340-30, local emergency management coordination requirements

OPM-525 Dust-Related Failure Pattern

Symptom — Fault reports, particularly reduced photometric output, connector contact issues, and cooling-related faults, show a clear correlation with dry/dusty conditions or specific dust-generating activity nearby, identified through trend analysis.

Equipment / System

Fixture lenses, connector contact surfaces, and CCR/equipment ventilation/cooling paths, assessed for dust accumulation correlated with a fault pattern.

Possible Causes
HighAirborne dust from nearby unpaved areas, construction activity, or seasonal dry conditions accumulating on fixture lenses, reducing measured photometric output
HighDust ingress into connector housings or base cans contributing to contact resistance issues over time
MedDust accumulation in CCR/equipment vault ventilation and cooling paths reducing cooling effectiveness and contributing to heat-related faults
MedNearby construction or ground-disturbance activity generating dust levels well above the site's normal background
LowDust combined with moisture forming a conductive or corrosive film on exposed contacts
LowSeasonal wind patterns concentrating dust exposure on specific zones of the field rather than uniformly across the site
Required Tools
Photometric field measurement equipmentLens/optic and connector cleaning kitThermal imaging camera for vault/cabinet cooling assessmentSite activity log for nearby construction/ground disturbance
Safety Precautions
  • LOTO the circuit before cleaning or inspecting fixtures/connectors.
  • Use appropriate PPE for dust exposure during cleaning and inspection work.
Diagnostic Procedure
  1. ObservationCorrelate the fault/photometric trend against dust-generating events (construction activity, dry-season wind patterns) using site activity logs.
  2. MeasurementWith circuits isolated, measure photometric output before and after lens cleaning at affected fixtures to quantify the dust contribution.
  3. MeasurementInspect connector contacts and CCR/vault cooling paths at affected locations for visible dust accumulation.
  4. DecisionPhotometric output recovers substantially after cleaning → confirm dust as the primary driver and schedule proactive cleaning. Output does not fully recover after cleaning → investigate an additional underlying cause (e.g. LED ageing) rather than attributing the full decline to dust.
  5. Fault LocationIdentify whether the dust-related pattern is site-wide (seasonal) or concentrated near a specific dust-generating activity.
  6. Root CauseConfirm the corrective action (cleaning, source control) results in sustained improvement rather than a temporary recovery that quickly re-declines.
Test Points
Photometric output before/after cleaning
Dust accumulation level on lenses, contacts, and cooling paths
Correlation with nearby dust-generating activity
Sustained output level after a subsequent period
Root Cause Verification

Re-measure photometric output and inspect for dust re-accumulation after a subsequent period to confirm whether a one-time cleaning was sufficient or a more frequent cleaning interval is needed.

Corrective Action
  • Clean affected fixture lenses, connector contacts, and CCR/vault cooling paths.
  • Coordinate with nearby construction/ground-disturbance activities to reduce dust generation where practical (e.g. dust suppression measures).
  • Increase cleaning frequency at chronically dust-affected zones.
Preventive Action
  • Schedule proactive lens/optic and cooling-path cleaning ahead of known dry-season or construction-related dust periods.
  • Track photometric output trends by zone to catch a dust-related decline before it approaches a compliance threshold.
  • Coordinate with any nearby construction activity to require dust suppression measures near the AGL system.
Related References

SOP: SOP-OPM-03 (Performance Trending Procedure)

Standards: ICAO Annex 14 Vol I (photometric requirements), FAA AC 150/5340-30

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Division 21 — Operational & Maintenance Problems in the AGL Troubleshooting Atlas.

Q1What does Division 21 of the AGL Troubleshooting Atlas cover?

Division 21 is the Operational & Maintenance Problems reference. It publishes 20 diagnostic topics, OPM-506 to OPM-525, in a single subsection.

Q2What information does each topic include?

Every topic follows the same diagnostic record: the symptom, equipment/system, possible causes ranked by likelihood (high, medium or low), required tools, safety precautions, diagnostic procedure, test points, root cause verification, corrective action, preventive action and related references (SOPs, standards, diagrams and case studies).

Q3Which topics are in Division 21?

The 20 topics are OPM-506 to OPM-525: Frequent Lamp Failure; Frequent LED Failure; Frequent Transformer Failure; Frequent Connector Failure; Frequent Cable Failure; Excessive Maintenance Requirement; Repeated Circuit Trips; Repeated Earth Fault; Repeated CCR Fault; Repeated Sign Failure; High Energy Consumption; Unexpected Shutdown; Performance Degradation; False Alarms; Seasonal Fault Pattern; Rain-Related Failure Pattern; Heat-Related Failure Pattern; Cold-Weather Failure Pattern; Flood-Related Failure Pattern; Dust-Related Failure Pattern.

Q4How does a diagnostic procedure begin in Division 21?

Each procedure starts with observation before any intervention. For example, OPM-506 (Frequent Lamp Failure) begins: Pull the lamp replacement history for the affected fixture/circuit/zone and compare the failure rate against the expected service life and against comparable circuits.

Q5What safety precautions apply in Division 21?

Each topic lists its own precautions. For example, OPM-506 (Frequent Lamp Failure) starts with: LOTO the circuit before lamp replacement or fixture inspection.

Q6Which SOPs are referenced in Division 21?

Related references in the topics include SOP-OPM-01 (Recurring Failure Rate Trending Procedure), SOP-CON-14 (Repeat Failure Root Cause Investigation Procedure), SOP-TCL-11 (Guidance Circuit Fault Location Procedure), SOP-CCR-05 (Repeated Trip Investigation Procedure), SOP-CON-05 (Water Ingress Inspection and Remediation Procedure), SOP-CCR-06 (CCR Internal Fault Diagnosis Procedure), SOP-INST-13 (Sign Installation Verification Procedure) and SOP-OPM-02 (Energy Consumption Trending and Investigation Procedure), and 4 more.

Q7Which standards are referenced in Division 21?

Where a standard applies, it appears in the topic's Related References. Those cited in this division include FAA AC 150/5340-30, FAA AC 150/5345-46, FAA AC 150/5345-26, IEC 60364, FAA AC 150/5345-10, ICAO Annex 14 Vol I and FAA AC 150/5340-18.

Q8How can I find a specific topic in Division 21 quickly?

Use the search box at the top of the page to filter the 20 published topics by code, fault name or keyword; matching topics open automatically. Each diagnostic record can also be downloaded as a Word (.doc) file.
