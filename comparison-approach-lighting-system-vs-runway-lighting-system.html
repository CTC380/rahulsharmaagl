Approach & Landing Systems Comparison

Approach Lighting System VS versus Runway Lighting System

An Approach Lighting System (ALS) and runway lighting do different jobs. The ALS starts at the landing threshold and extends outward along the extended runway centreline, helping the pilot line up with the runway during the change from instrument to visual flight. Runway lighting (edge, threshold, runway-end, centreline and touchdown-zone lights) marks the runway itself for landing, rollout and take-off. In FAA guidance they are separate systems with separate installation criteria and separate controls.

Technical review: 20 September 2026, checked against FAA Order JO 6850.2C, FAA AC 150/5340-30J and FAA AIM Chapter 2, Section 1. System names and dimensions on this page follow FAA practice; see section 22 for ICAO/DGCA differences.

02 · What Is an ALS?

What Is an Approach Lighting System?

An Approach Lighting System (ALS) is a set of signal lights arranged symmetrically about the extended runway centreline. It starts at the landing threshold and extends outward into the approach area. It gives the pilot visual information on runway alignment, height perception, roll guidance and horizon reference, and it is the basic means of moving from instrument flight to visual flight for landing (FAA AIM 2-1-1; FAA Order JO 6850.2C).

Aircraft → Approach lighting → Threshold → Runway.

Because the ALS lies outside the runway pavement, it is designed around an approach light plane at threshold elevation that must stay clear of obstructions. Runway lighting is designed around the runway geometry.

03 · What Is Runway Lighting?

What Is a Runway Lighting System?

A runway lighting system provides visual references on and along the runway itself. Edge lights mark the lateral limits of the usable landing area. Threshold and runway-end lights mark its longitudinal limits. On selected precision runways, centreline and touchdown-zone lights add guidance for landing, rollout and take-off (AC 150/5340-30J, Chapters 2 and 3).

Threshold → Runway → Runway end. The lighting tells the pilot: "This is the runway and this is where I can land, roll, or take off."

04 · Key Difference

The Simplest Difference

Approach Lighting

Before the runway: "Where is the runway and how am I aligned with it?"

Runway Lighting

On the runway: "Where are the runway limits and where is the usable runway surface?"

05 · Approach Lighting Examples

Common FAA Approach Lighting Systems

High-Intensity

  • ALSF-2: Approach Lighting System with Sequenced Flashing Lights, CAT II/III configuration. Five steady-burning brightness steps and three flasher intensities.
  • ALSF-1: legacy high-intensity configuration. It still appears on charts and at some older or military sites, but it is not among the systems described in the current FAA Order JO 6850.2C.

Medium-Intensity

  • MALSR: MALS plus RAIL (five sequenced flashers) out to 2,400 ft. The typical CAT I system.
  • SSALR: charted as a standalone system at many airports. FAA now treats it as the medium-intensity mode of the dual-mode ALSF-2/SSALR, not as a new standalone installation.
  • MALSF: 1,400 ft MALS with three sequenced flashers, for non-precision runways where the approach area is hard to identify.
  • MALS: 1,400 ft, no flashers. FAA no longer installs it as a new facility.

Related Visual Guidance Aids

  • ODALS: seven omnidirectional flashing lights (five on the extended centreline to 1,500 ft, two at the threshold) for non-precision runways. FAA no longer installs it as a new facility.
  • REIL: a pair of synchronised flashing lights, one each side of the threshold, that identify the approach end of the runway. FAA lists REIL as a separate visual guidance system, not as an ALS type, and it sits at the threshold rather than in the approach area.
  • PAPI: a vertical glide-path indicator beside the runway. It is neither an ALS nor runway lighting (FAA no longer installs VASI as a new facility).

FAA Order JO 6850.2C is the installation-criteria document for ALSF-2/SSALR, MALS, MALSF, MALSR and ODALS, and for REIL and PAPI. AC 150/5340-30J, Chapter 7 ("Economy Approach Aids") covers MALSF/MALS, ODALS, REIL and PAPI.

06 · Runway Lighting Examples

Common Runway Lighting Systems

Runway Edge Lighting

  • LIRL, Low-Intensity Runway Lights: visual runways at small airports; one brightness step
  • MIRL, Medium-Intensity Runway Lights: visual and non-precision instrument runways; three steps
  • HIRL, High-Intensity Runway Lights: precision instrument runways; five steps

Edge lights are white. On MIRL and HIRL instrument runway ends, the last 2,000 ft (610 m) of the runway, or half its length if that is less, shows yellow toward the instrument approach as a caution zone during rollout.

Other Runway Lighting

  • Threshold lights: green to arriving aircraft, red toward the runway end
  • Runway-end lights: red
  • Runway centreline lights: 50 ft (15 m) spacing; white, then alternating red and white from 3,000 ft to 1,000 ft remaining, then all red for the last 1,000 ft
  • Touchdown-zone lights: two rows of transverse three-light bars from 100 ft to 3,000 ft, or to mid-runway on shorter runways
  • Stopway lights: red, where a stopway exists

Not runway lighting: runway guard lights, stop bars and clearance bars belong to taxiway and hold-position lighting (AC 150/5340-30J, Chapter 4). They protect the runway but are neither ALS nor runway lighting.

09 · Centreline Distinction

Approach Centreline vs Runway Centreline

This is an important distinction.

Approach Centreline

Located outside the runway, on the extended centreline, starting at the threshold. Its purpose is to extend the runway direction visually into the approach area. ALSF-2 centreline bars sit on 100 ft stations; MALS/MALSR bars on 200 ft stations.

Runway Centreline

Located on the runway at 50 ft (15 m) spacing. Its purpose is lateral guidance during landing, rollout and take-off. It is required on CAT II/III runways, on CAT I runways used for landing below 2,400 ft (750 m) RVR, and on runways used for take-off below 1,600 ft (480 m) RVR unless FAA approves otherwise.

Approach centreline ≠ runway centreline. They work together but serve different phases of flight.

10 · Intensity Systems

Intensity Classification

Approach Lighting

  • ALSF-2 → High (5 steady-burning steps, 3 flasher steps)
  • ALSF-1 → High (legacy)
  • MALSR → Medium (3 steps)
  • MALSF → Medium (3 steps)
  • MALS → Medium (3 steps)

Runway Edge Lighting

  • LIRL → Low (1 step)
  • MIRL → Medium (3 steps)
  • HIRL → High (5 steps)

The words "medium" and "high" must therefore be read within each system's own classification. A medium-intensity ALS does not imply MIRL, and a high-intensity ALS does not imply HIRL.

11 · Electrical Engineering

Electrical Engineering Comparison

Series-fed airfield lighting follows one familiar chain, and much of the runway system uses it. The ALS does not always follow it.

Constant Current Regulator (CCR) → Primary series circuit → Isolation transformers → Light fixtures.

Approach Lighting

  • ALSF-2: 6.6 A or 20 A series circuits with isolation transformers (20 A units are typical), plus sequenced-flasher equipment and control cabinets
  • MALS / MALSF / MALSR: steady-burning lights typically fed from a 120/240 V constant-voltage supply (FAA MALS and MALSR use 120 V lamps), not from a CCR loop
  • Flashers: discharge-type flashing light units
  • Monitoring and remote-control interface (ALCMS where fitted)

Runway Lighting

  • HIRL / MIRL: series circuits with CCRs and isolation transformers are recommended; base-mounted fixtures must be on series circuits
  • LIRL: parallel circuits (120/240 V) are recommended; stake-mounted MIRL may also use parallel
  • Centreline and touchdown-zone lights: 6.6 A series circuit up to 30 kW total load, 20 A above that, with isolation transformers
  • Runway control and monitoring (ALCMS where fitted)

Isolation transformers are L-830 for 60 Hz systems and L-831 for 50 Hz systems, so 50 Hz networks such as India's need the L-831 type. Confirm circuit type, load and monitoring against FAA Order JO 6850.2C (ALS) and AC 150/5340-30J (runway lighting and economy aids) before design.

12 · Control Difference

Control System Comparison

ALS Control

  • Remote control is required for every FAA ALS (landline or radio; landline only for ALSF-2)
  • Sequenced flashers may run only while the steady-burning lights are on
  • ALSF-2: five steady-burning steps, three flasher intensities
  • MALSR: RAIL can be switched separately; pilots can key the radio 7, 5 or 3 times for high, medium or low, with a 15-minute timer
  • Monitoring is required on all steps for ALSF-2 (CAT II/III) and not required for CAT I systems

FAA practice controls MALSR separately from the runway edge lights. Where a runway has both an ALS and runway lighting, air-to-ground radio control of the ALS takes precedence and the runway lights sit at a preset intensity.

Runway Lighting Control

  • Edge-light intensity: 1 step (LIRL), 3 steps (MIRL), 5 steps (HIRL)
  • Centreline lights controlled independently of touchdown-zone lights and HIRL
  • Local control by switch, photocell or timer; remote control from the tower or by radio
  • Edge lighting that supports CAT II/III should be remotely monitored, with status reported to the tower

REIL, where installed, can follow the runway edge-light circuit intensity.

13 · Pilot's View

What the Pilot Sees

During Approach

Approach lights → Threshold → Runway. The ALS helps answer: "Can I visually acquire and align with the runway?"

During Landing

Threshold → Runway edge, runway centreline, touchdown zone. The runway lighting helps answer: "Where is the usable runway surface and where should I maintain alignment?"

14 · Working Together

ALS + Runway Lighting Work Together

They are not competing systems. They form one continuous visual guidance chain:

Instrument approach → Approach lighting → Threshold → Runway lighting → Touchdown → Rollout.

Where the two systems meet

  • Both place green threshold lights at the landing threshold, 2 to 10 ft before it.
  • With a displaced threshold that has runway centreline lights, the controls are interlocked. If a medium-intensity ALS is installed in the displaced area, the displaced-area centreline lights must be off whenever the ALS is on. A high-intensity ALS washes out those lights, so separate circuiting is not required.
15 · Example: CAT I Runway

CAT I Runway Example

Approach Side

MALSR (or another CAT I ALS such as ALSF-2 operating in SSALR mode) → Threshold.

Runway

HIRL and threshold/end lights on the precision runway. Centreline and touchdown-zone lights are added where landing below 2,400 ft (750 m) RVR is authorised. Centreline lights are also recommended on CAT I runways wider than 170 ft (50 m) or used by aircraft with approach speeds above 140 kt.

The ALS helps with approach and runway acquisition. The runway lighting supports landing and rollout. Runway visual range (RVR) minima decide which optional runway lights are needed.

16 · Example: CAT II/III Runway

CAT II/III Runway Example

Approach

ALSF-2 → Threshold. FAA treats ALSF-2 as the high-intensity system for CAT II and III approaches.

Runway

HIRL, runway centreline lights and touchdown-zone lights, all required for CAT II/III, with edge lighting remotely monitored to the tower.

The combination provides extensive visual information during low-visibility operations. Low-visibility taxi lighting (taxiway centreline lights, stop bars) belongs to a separate system and is not covered here.

18 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: Approach lights are runway lights
Fact

They are different visual-aid systems in different areas, installed to different FAA criteria and operated with separate controls.

Myth: An ALS replaces runway lighting
Fact

No. An ALS provides approach guidance; runway lighting provides runway guidance.

Myth: Approach lights give glide-path guidance like PAPI
Fact

An ALS gives alignment, roll, height and distance cues. Vertical guidance comes from the ILS glide slope and, visually, from PAPI.

Myth: Every runway needs an ALS
Fact

Whether an ALS is provided, and which type, depends on the approach procedure, visibility minima and operational need. Many visual runways have none.

Myth: ALS intensity determines runway lighting intensity
Fact

The two systems have separate classifications, brightness steps and controls. FAA practice controls MALSR separately from the runway edge lights.

Myth: Every ALS runs from a CCR series circuit
Fact

ALSF-2 uses series circuits, but MALS, MALSF and MALSR are typically fed from a 120/240 V constant-voltage supply. Always check the circuit type for the specific system.

20 · Engineering Verdict

Engineering Verdict

Engineering Verdict

Approach Lighting System: Provides visual guidance from the approach to the runway threshold. Examples: ALSF-2, MALSR, MALSF, SSALR (legacy or no longer installed new: ALSF-1, MALS, ODALS).

Runway Lighting System: Defines the usable runway and guides the pilot on it. Examples: LIRL, MIRL, HIRL, threshold and end lights, runway centreline lights, touchdown-zone lights.

Keep Their Roles Separate in Design and Maintenance
Concept

The approach system helps acquisition and alignment; runway lighting supports landing and roll. Faults in each affect different phases.

Separate Circuits Help Isolation
Electrical

Distinct circuits allow one part to be worked on without taking down the other. Check the segmentation.

Inspection

Walking the pattern in darkness shows gaps and dim units that daytime checks miss.

Coordinate Outages and NOTAMs
Operations

A problem in either system may affect operations. Plan work and notices together.

23 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

This page is educational. For an actual project, confirm the current revision of each document and follow the approved project specification and drawings.

FAA Order JO 6850.2C

Visual Guidance Lighting Systems (30 Aug 2022). Installation criteria for ALSF-2/SSALR, MALS, MALSF, MALSR, ODALS, REIL and PAPI.

FAA AC 150/5340-30J

Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids (12 Feb 2018). Runway and taxiway edge lighting, runway centreline and touchdown-zone lighting, taxiway lighting and economy approach aids. Mandatory for AIP- and PFC-funded projects; check faa.gov for a newer revision.

FAA AIM, Chapter 2, Section 1

Pilot-facing description of approach lights, REIL, runway edge, centreline and touchdown-zone lights.

FAA Equipment Specifications

AC 150/5345-46 (light fixtures), AC 150/5345-10 (CCRs), AC 150/5345-47 (isolation transformers), AC 150/5345-56 (ALCMS) and AC 150/5340-26 (maintenance of visual aids).

ICAO and DGCA

ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, ICAO Aerodrome Design Manual (Doc 9157) Part 4, and DGCA CAR Section 4, Series B, Part I (Aerodrome Design and Operations) for Indian aerodromes.

Final Engineering Note

Approach lighting and runway lighting are separate systems that work together as one continuous visual guidance chain. Verify the exact configuration, circuit type and brightness steps against the current applicable standard and the approved project drawings.

Approach lighting helps the pilot find and align with the runway; runway lighting helps the pilot land, roll and operate on the runway.
