REL-01 Asset Reliability Fundamentals
Purpose

Asset reliability is the ability of an AGL asset or system to perform its required function consistently and without unacceptable failure over the required operating period.

Reliability Management Should Consider
  • Asset function
  • Failure probability
  • Failure modes
  • Operating exposure
  • Operating conditions
  • Maintenance history
  • Failure history
  • Criticality
  • Consequence of failure
  • Design characteristics
  • Environmental conditions

REL-02 MTBF Analysis
Purpose

MTBF — Mean Time Between Failures is used to understand the average operating time between failures for repairable equipment, when the underlying data and calculation assumptions are appropriate.

MTBF = TOTAL RELEVANT OPERATING TIME ÷ NUMBER OF RELEVANT FAILURES
AGL Applications
  • CCRs
  • PAPI systems
  • AGL fixtures
  • Signs
  • Control equipment
  • Other repairable assets
MTBF Should Always Be Accompanied By
  • Defined asset population
  • Defined failure criteria
  • Defined observation period
  • Operating exposure
  • Failure count
  • Data-quality assessment
Principle

A raw MTBF number without a defined dataset can be misleading.

REL-03 MTTR Analysis
Purpose

MTTR — Mean Time To Repair/Restore, depending on the organization's defined terminology, measures the typical time required to return an asset to the required operational condition after a failure.

MTTR = TOTAL RELEVANT RESTORATION/REPAIR TIME ÷ NUMBER OF RELEVANT FAILURES
Track
  • Failure time
  • Response time
  • Diagnosis time
  • Repair time
  • Testing time
  • Restoration time
  • Operational return time
Why MTTR Matters

Two assets can have similar failure frequencies but very different operational impacts if one takes substantially longer to restore.

REL-04 Failure-Rate Analysis
Purpose

Failure-rate analysis examines how frequently assets fail in relation to their relevant exposure or population.

Possible Measures
  • Failures per asset
  • Failures per operating hour
  • Failures per circuit
  • Failures per year
  • Failures by equipment type
  • Failures by manufacturer/model
Analyze By
  • Asset type
  • Manufacturer
  • Model
  • Age
  • Location
  • Circuit
  • Operating environment
  • Operating hours
  • Failure mode
Important

Failure-rate calculations should use clearly defined exposure data wherever possible.

  • Operating-condition issue
REL-06 Repeat-Failure Analysis
Purpose

Repeat failure analysis identifies assets or components that fail repeatedly after corrective action.

Example
FIXTURE FAILUREREPLACEMENTSAME FIXTURE/CIRCUIT FAILS AGAINREPLACEMENTTHIRD FAILURE
Repeat-Failure Investigation — The Organization Should Ask
  • Is the replacement part correct?
  • Is the root cause known?
  • Is the circuit condition normal?
  • Is the transformer healthy?
  • Is the cable healthy?
  • Is the environment contributing?
  • Is the equipment being operated correctly?
  • Is there a design issue?
Principle

Repeated replacement without eliminating the underlying cause is not reliability improvement.

REL-07 Failure-Mode Analysis
Purpose

Failure-mode analysis identifies how an asset can fail and what effect that failure has.

Typical AGL Failure Modes

AGL fixture: No illumination, intermittent operation, reduced output, optical degradation, physical damage, water ingress, electrical connection failure.

CCR: No output, incorrect output, control failure, alarm failure, protection trip, over-temperature condition.

Record
  • Asset
  • Component
  • Failure mode
  • Failure cause
  • Failure effect
  • Detection method
  • Corrective action
  • Preventive action
Downstream Link

This creates the bridge between the AGL Failure Database and reliability engineering.

REL-08 Root-Cause Analysis
Purpose

Root-Cause Analysis (RCA) should determine why the failure occurred, not merely identify what component failed.

RCA Should Distinguish
  • Symptom: What was observed?
  • Failure mode: How did the asset fail?
  • Immediate cause: What directly caused the failure?
  • Underlying cause: What condition allowed the failure?
  • Root cause: What fundamental issue must be addressed to prevent recurrence?
Example
SYMPTOM: FIXTURE NOT OPERATINGFAILURE MODE: TRANSFORMER SECONDARY FAILUREIMMEDIATE CAUSE: ELECTRICAL BREAKDOWNUNDERLYING CAUSE: ABNORMAL CIRCUIT CONDITIONROOT CAUSE: REQUIRES ENGINEERING INVESTIGATION
Principle

The final root cause should only be recorded when supported by adequate evidence.

REL-09 Reliability KPIs
Purpose

Reliability KPIs convert failure information into measurable performance indicators.

Recommended KPI Categories
  • MTBF — Failure-free operating performance
  • MTTR — Restoration effectiveness
  • Failure rate — Failure frequency
  • Repeat-failure rate — Recurring-problem identification
  • Unplanned downtime — Operational impact
  • Reliability trend — Long-term performance
  • Critical-asset failures — Safety/operational risk
  • RCA completion — Failure-learning effectiveness
  • Corrective-action effectiveness — Improvement effectiveness
Important

The exact KPI definitions should be standardized so that different reports use the same calculation basis.

REL-10 Reliability Benchmarking
Purpose

Reliability benchmarking compares actual reliability against an approved reference.

Benchmark Sources

Internal: Previous year, previous project, similar asset, same manufacturer/model, same airport.

External: Approved industry information, manufacturer information, contractual performance requirements, appropriate engineering benchmarks.

Example — Compare CCR Model A vs. CCR Model B For
  • Failure rate
  • MTBF
  • MTTR
  • Repeat failures
  • Maintenance burden
  • Lifecycle cost
Benchmarking Should Account For Differences In
  • Operating exposure
  • Environment
  • Asset age
  • Maintenance strategy
  • Operating conditions

REL-12 Risk-Based Reliability Planning
Purpose

Reliability effort should be prioritized according to risk and consequence, not simply the number of failures.

Consider
  • Safety criticality
  • Operational criticality
  • Regulatory criticality
  • Failure probability
  • Failure consequence
  • Detectability
  • Redundancy
  • Recovery time
Example

A low-frequency failure in a safety-critical runway system may deserve more engineering attention than a high-frequency failure in a low-consequence asset.

Downstream Link

This directly connects Division 11 with Division 03 — Asset Criticality & Risk Assessment — and Division 09 — Risk-Based Maintenance.

REL-13 Critical Asset Reliability
Purpose

Critical assets require enhanced reliability management.

Monitor
  • Failure frequency
  • MTBF
  • MTTR
  • Availability
  • Repeat failures
  • Failure modes
  • RCA status
  • Corrective actions
  • Spare availability
  • Obsolescence
  • RUL
Potential Critical AGL Assets

Depending on the airport's approved criticality assessment: runway lighting systems, PAPI, critical CCRs, critical series circuits, critical cable sections, ALCMS components and other safety/operationally critical assets.

Principle

The more critical the asset, the less acceptable uncontrolled reliability deterioration becomes.

REL-14 Reliability-Centred Maintenance
Purpose

Reliability-Centred Maintenance (RCM) provides a structured approach for determining the most appropriate maintenance strategy based on asset function, functional failure, failure modes, failure effects, failure consequences, failure detectability and maintenance task effectiveness.

Strategic Connection

RCM asks: What must we do to ensure this asset continues to perform its required function?

Possible Outcomes Can Include
  • Preventive maintenance
  • Condition monitoring
  • Predictive maintenance
  • Functional testing
  • Failure-finding
  • Corrective maintenance
  • Redesign
  • No scheduled maintenance where justified
Important

Division 11 should establish the RCM strategy and decision framework. The detailed maintenance procedures belong in the AGL Maintenance Handbook.

REL-15 Failure-Cost Analysis
Purpose

Failures should be evaluated not only technically but also economically.

Failure Cost May Include
  • Spare part
  • Labour
  • Contractor
  • Test equipment
  • Access arrangements
  • Operational disruption
  • Runway/taxiway closure
  • Emergency response
  • Downtime
  • Secondary damage
  • Investigation
  • Repeat repair
  • Replacement
Important Distinction

A low-cost component failure can create a very high operational cost.

COMPONENT COST ≠ FAILURE CONSEQUENCE COST
Supports Decisions Such As
  • Product replacement
  • Design modification
  • Maintenance strategy change
  • Spare holding
  • Redundancy
  • Capital renewal

Improvement Cycle

Division 11 — Core Principle

A failure is not the end of the maintenance process; it is the beginning of the reliability-learning process.

The complete reliability lifecycle is: Failure Occurs → Detected → Recorded → Failure Mode Identified → Cause Investigated → Root Cause Established → Corrective Action → Reliability Impact Measured → MTBF/MTTR/Failure Trend Updated → Reliability Improvement Decision → Maintenance/Design/Procurement/Operational Change → Failure Recurrence Monitored.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Reliability Engineering & Failure Management in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide.

Q1What is Division 11 — Reliability Engineering & Failure Management in the AGL Asset Lifecycle Guide?

Division 11 is the reliability-intelligence and failure-management layer, connecting directly with the AGL Failure Database. It covers 16 topics on asset reliability fundamentals, MTBF, MTTR, failure-rate and failure-trend analysis, repeat-failure analysis, failure-mode analysis, root-cause analysis, reliability KPIs, benchmarking, improvement programmes, risk-based reliability planning, critical asset reliability, reliability-centred maintenance, failure-cost analysis and improvement tracking.

Q2What is asset reliability in AGL systems?

Asset reliability is the ability of an AGL asset or system to perform its required function consistently and without unacceptable failure over the required operating period. It relates to asset function, failure probability, failure modes and operating exposure.

Q3What are MTBF and MTTR?

MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) describes the average operating time between failures for repairable equipment such as CCRs, PAPI systems, fixtures and signs, when the underlying data and calculation assumptions are valid. MTTR measures the typical time to repair or restore an asset to operation, depending on the organization's defined terminology.

Q4How are failure rates and failure trends analyzed?

Failure-rate analysis examines how often assets fail relative to exposure or population, for example failures per asset, per operating hour, per circuit or per year. Failure trends may be increasing, decreasing, stable, seasonal, or concentrated in specific equipment or locations.

Q5What is the difference between repeat-failure analysis and root-cause analysis?

Repeat-failure analysis identifies assets or components that fail repeatedly after corrective action, asking whether the replacement part was correct and whether the root cause is known. Root-cause analysis should determine why the failure occurred, not merely which component failed.

Q6What is failure-mode analysis?

It identifies how an asset can fail and what effect that failure has, recording the asset, component, failure mode, cause, effect and detection method.

Q7What is Reliability-Centred Maintenance (RCM)?

RCM is a structured approach for determining the most appropriate maintenance strategy based on asset function and functional failure. Outcomes can include preventive maintenance, condition monitoring, predictive maintenance and functional testing.

Q8How do reliability KPIs, benchmarking and improvement programmes work?

Reliability KPIs such as MTBF, MTTR and failure rate convert failure information into measurable indicators, and benchmarking compares them against an approved reference. Where problems are significant a structured improvement programme should be set up, and improvement must be measurable. Division 11 connects with the AGL Failure Database.
