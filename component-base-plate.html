Base Plate

A sturdy, precision‑machined mounting plate that provides the structural foundation for elevated AGL fixtures, junction boxes, and other airfield equipment. Designed to be embedded in concrete or asphalt, it ensures accurate alignment, secure fixing, and long‑term stability under heavy aircraft loads and harsh environmental conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Base Plate (also known as a mounting plate, fixture plate, or foundation plate) is a robust metal plate that serves as the primary structural interface between an elevated AGL fixture (or other airfield equipment) and the pavement or foundation. It is designed to be securely anchored into the concrete or asphalt, providing a stable, level, and accurately positioned mounting surface for the fixture.

Key characteristics of base plates include:

  • Precision Machining: The plate is machined to tight tolerances to ensure that the fixture is perfectly aligned (vertical and horizontal) and that the beam pattern is correctly oriented.
  • High Strength: Made from high‑grade steel (often galvanised or stainless) to withstand heavy aircraft loads, impact, and vibration without deformation.
  • Corrosion Protection: Hot‑dip galvanised, stainless steel, or coated with anti‑corrosion paint to resist the harsh airfield environment (moisture, de‑icing fluids, fuels).
  • Mounting Holes: Pre‑drilled or tapped holes that match the fixture's bolt pattern, allowing for quick and secure attachment.

Base plates are used for a variety of AGL equipment, including:

  • Elevated light fixtures: Runway edge lights, taxiway edge lights, approach lights, and obstruction lights.
  • Junction boxes and distribution panels: Providing a mounting surface for underground or above‑ground enclosures.
  • Signage: For airfield signs and guidance markers.
  • Sensor housings: For weather sensors, RVR sensors, and other instrumentation.

The plate is typically embedded in a concrete foundation or directly into the asphalt pavement, with anchor bolts or expansion bolts securing it in place. The top surface of the plate is machined flat and level, and often includes a central hole or conduit entry for cable routing.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

An elevated fixture's photometric performance depends on it being installed at a specific height, tilt, and orientation — figures decided on a drawing long before the equipment reaches site. The base plate's purpose is to make that specification physically enforceable: a level, precisely machined interface that fixes the fixture's angle and position at installation and keeps it there under the wind loading, vibration, and thermal cycling the fixture experiences afterward. Because the plate carries the full mechanical load from the fixture down into the foundation, it also protects the fixture itself — its housing, optics, and internal wiring are never expected to bear structural loads they were not designed for. In practical terms, the base plate is what turns an engineering drawing's height and angle tolerances into something a technician can actually deliver and trust on site.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Base plates are used throughout the airfield wherever elevated fixtures or equipment require a secure, level, and durable mounting foundation, including:

  • Runway Edge Lighting: Providing the foundation for elevated runway edge light fixtures mounted on frangible poles.
  • Taxiway Edge Lighting: Mounting elevated taxiway edge light fixtures, especially on curves or where flush mounting is not possible.
  • Approach Lighting: Supporting elevated approach light fixtures, barrettes, and flashing light units.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Mounting obstruction lights on poles, towers, or building rooftops.
  • Airfield Signage: Providing the base for mandatory instruction signs, direction signs, and information signs.
  • Junction Boxes and Distribution Panels: Supporting above‑ground enclosures that house cable terminations, connectors, or control equipment.
  • Sensor Mounting: For anemometers, wind cones, visibility sensors, and other airfield instrumentation.

The plate is installed during the pavement construction or as a retro‑fit by cutting a recess into the existing pavement. It is leveled using grout or shims, and anchor bolts are torqued to the specified value. The fixture is then bolted to the plate using the pre‑drilled holes. The plate's design ensures that the fixture can be easily removed for maintenance or replacement without disturbing the foundation.

Placement

Installation & Location

The base plate sits on top of the foundation or base can, bolted down onto anchor bolts embedded in the concrete, with a levelling check performed before final torque so the pole or fixture mounted on it stands plumb rather than leaning. Plate position and orientation are set out from the fixture's survey point, with any directional fixture's plate keyed or marked so the pole can only be bolted on facing the correct direction. Grouting or shimming under the plate is used where the foundation surface isn't perfectly flat, since an unsupported corner of the plate becomes a stress concentration point under wind or impact loading.

Function

How Does It Work?

The base plate functions as the critical load‑bearing and alignment interface between the fixture and the pavement. Its design ensures that the fixture remains stable, level, and correctly oriented throughout its service life:

  • Load Distribution: The plate distributes the weight of the fixture (and any additional loads, such as wind or ice) evenly over a larger area of the pavement, preventing localised stress or cracking.
  • Precise Alignment: The machined top surface provides a true reference plane, ensuring that the fixture is perfectly vertical and that the light beam is aimed correctly (e.g., towards the runway centreline or approach path).
  • Secure Fixing: Anchor bolts embedded in the foundation secure the plate, while the fixture bolts into the plate's threaded holes. This provides a robust connection that can withstand aircraft jet blast, vibration, and maintenance activities.
  • Frangible Feature (if applicable): In some designs, the base plate includes a frangible coupling or shear‑off feature that allows the fixture to break away cleanly on impact, minimizing damage to the aircraft.

The installation process typically involves:

  • Setting the plate: The plate is positioned in the wet concrete or asphalt, or bolted into a pre‑formed recess using epoxy anchors.
  • Levelling: The plate is adjusted to the correct height and inclination using a spirit level or digital level.
  • Grouting: A non‑shrink grout is injected under the plate to fill any voids and provide a solid, level bearing surface.
  • Torquing: The anchor bolts are torqued to the manufacturer's specified value to ensure proper clamping force.
  • Fixture mounting: The fixture is placed on the plate and secured using the fixture mounting bolts.

The plate is designed to withstand vertical loads (aircraft weight) and horizontal loads (braking, turning, and jet blast) without shifting or deforming. It is typically designed for a minimum service life of 20 years, with regular inspection for corrosion, loose bolts, or damage.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The base plate is a purely mechanical interface with no electrical or control role — it sits between the fixture or pole above and the anchor bolts and foundation below, and its job is to spread the loads from above (dead weight, wind, and any impact) evenly into the bolt pattern rather than concentrating them on one or two fasteners. Its thickness, footprint, and bolt-hole pattern are matched to the specific fixture it carries and to the load case at that installation, which is why it is not a generic, interchangeable part between fixture families even where the anchor bolt spacing happens to match.

Construction

Physical Construction

A base plate is cut from structural steel, aluminium, or stainless steel plate stock to the thickness required for its rated load, then machined or laser-cut with a bolt hole pattern matched to both the fixture's mounting footprint on top and the anchor bolt layout set into the pavement below. The plate's top and bottom surfaces are finished flat within a tight tolerance so the fixture sits level and true once installed, since even a small deviation can throw off the fixture's beam alignment. Corrosion protection is applied as a hot-dip galvanised coating or powder coat finish over the full plate surface, with threaded holes or captive nuts often protected separately to prevent the coating from interfering with bolt engagement. Where the plate incorporates a frangible coupling interface for elevated fixtures, a weakened section or shear-designed connection point is machined into the plate itself, engineered to fail predictably under an aircraft impact load while retaining full strength under normal wind and vibration loading. The plate is bolted down onto the anchor bolts protruding from the pavement, with levelling nuts or shims used to fine-tune its final position before grouting or backfilling around the base.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Bolt Hole Pattern Match
Mechanical

Confirm the plate's bolt hole pattern matches both the fixture's mounting footprint above and the anchor bolt layout below before it is set down, since the two must align simultaneously.

Flatness & Level Verification
Mechanical

Check the plate sits flat and level within tolerance once positioned, since even a small deviation can throw off the fixture's beam alignment once mounted.

Non-Shrink Grout Selection & Placement
Civil

Use a non-shrink grout mix rated for the load and inject it fully beneath the plate so no voids remain that could allow settlement under repeated loading.

Anchor Bolt Torque
Mechanical

Torque anchor bolts to the value specified for their diameter and grade only after grout has cured, so the plate is not disturbed while the load path is still setting.

Frangible Interface Preservation
Mechanical

Where the plate incorporates a frangible coupling interface, avoid overtightening or damaging the weakened shear section during installation, since it must still fail predictably under an aircraft impact load.

Coating Touch-Up at Threads
Mechanical

Touch up any galvanised or powder coating damaged around threaded holes or captive nuts during installation, keeping full bolt engagement while restoring corrosion protection.

Pre-Fixture Sign-off
Documentation

Record plate level, grout placement, and torque values as-built before the fixture is mounted on top.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Anchor Bolt Torque Verification
Annual

Re-torque the anchor bolts securing the plate to spec so it maintains its rated load capacity and stays firmly seated against the foundation.

Flatness Check
Annual

Verify the plate remains within its ≤0.5 mm flatness tolerance; a warped plate can introduce uneven stress on the mounted fixture and its coupling.

Corrosion / Coating Check
Annual

Inspect galvanised or powder-coated surfaces for coating breakdown or pitting, particularly around bolt holes where moisture tends to collect.

Bolt Hole Wear Check
Annual

Inspect bolt holes for elongation or cracking under repeated vibration loading, which can gradually loosen the fixture connection over time.

Foundation Interface Check
Annual

Check for cracking or settlement in the surrounding concrete or pavement that could affect plate levelness and load transfer.

Deformation / Load Rating Check
Annual

Look for bending or deformation under load, particularly on thinner plates near the upper end of their load rating.

Alignment Check
Annual

Confirm the bolt hole pattern remains correctly aligned with the fixture mounting holes, especially after any resurfacing or fixture replacement.

Vibration Looseness Check
Semi-annual

Check for play or rocking between the plate and mounted fixture, an early sign of bolt or hole wear before failure occurs.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Plate and Bolts

Base plates are exposed to moisture, de‑icing chemicals, and fuels. Galvanised coatings can be damaged during installation, leading to rust. Use stainless steel plates or apply additional protective coatings. Regularly inspect and touch up damaged areas.

Loose Anchor Bolts

Vibration from aircraft and thermal cycling can cause anchor bolts to loosen, resulting in fixture misalignment or instability. Use torque wrenches during installation and schedule periodic re‑torquing as part of maintenance.

Poor Levelling

If the plate is not level, the fixture's beam pattern will be incorrect, reducing visibility for pilots. Use a precision level and shim the plate as needed. Grout under the plate to ensure full contact and support.

Inadequate Grouting

Voids under the plate can lead to stress concentrations, causing the plate to crack or the pavement to fail. Use non‑shrink grout and ensure it is fully injected to fill all voids.

Misalignment with Fixture

If the plate's bolt holes do not align with the fixture, the fixture may be forced into position, causing stress or incorrect orientation. Verify the bolt pattern before installation and use adjustable plates where necessary.

Damage from Snowploughs or Maintenance Vehicles

Base plates installed on runways or taxiways can be struck by snowploughs or maintenance vehicles. Use recessed or low‑profile plates, and install protective covers if possible.

Incorrect Anchor Bolt Depth

If the anchor bolts are not embedded to the required depth, they may not provide sufficient pull‑out strength. Follow the manufacturer's specifications for embedment depth and torque.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Visible Rust on Plate or Bolts
Likely Cause
The galvanised coating was damaged during installation — a scratch, weld splatter, or impact — exposing bare steel to moisture, de-icing chemicals, and fuel exposure.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the plate and bolts for rust bloom or coating breaks; compare the extent of corrosion against the last inspection record if available.
Corrective Action
Clean the corroded area and apply a cold-galvanising or equivalent touch-up coating, or replace the plate/bolts with stainless steel if corrosion has progressed beyond surface level.
Fixture Loose or Misaligned
Likely Cause
Vibration from aircraft operations and thermal cycling has loosened the anchor bolts securing the plate.
Diagnostic Steps
Check anchor bolt torque against specification; look for visible movement or a gap between the plate and pavement surface.
Corrective Action
Re-torque anchor bolts to specification using a calibrated torque wrench, and add the joint to the periodic re-torquing schedule.
Fixture Beam Aimed Incorrectly
Likely Cause
The base plate is not level, so the fixture mounted on it is tilted and its beam pattern is off from the intended aim.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the plate for level with a precision level in both axes; measure any gap between the plate and pavement that indicates shimming has settled or was never done.
Corrective Action
Re-shim the plate to bring it level, and re-grout underneath to ensure full, stable contact before re-torquing the fixture down.
Plate Cracking or Pavement Failure Around the Base
Likely Cause
Voids under the plate from inadequate or incomplete grouting are creating stress concentrations under aircraft loading.
Diagnostic Steps
Sound or tap-test the area around the plate for hollow spots indicating voids; inspect for visible cracking in the plate or surrounding pavement.
Corrective Action
Re-grout with non-shrink grout, ensuring it is fully injected to fill all voids beneath the plate, and monitor the area at the next inspection.
Fixture Forced or Stressed During Installation
Likely Cause
The plate's bolt hole pattern does not align with the fixture being mounted, often from a plate/fixture mismatch or a plate that was not verified before installation.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the bolt hole pattern on the plate against the fixture's mounting pattern before attempting to force a fit; check for visible stress marks or an out-of-square fixture if already installed.
Corrective Action
Replace the plate with one matching the fixture's bolt pattern, or install an adjustable plate where the standard pattern cannot be matched.
Plate Damaged or Displaced
Likely Cause
Impact from a snowplough blade or maintenance vehicle has struck a plate that was not sufficiently recessed or protected.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the plate for impact damage, deformation, or displacement from its original position; check whether it sits proud of the surrounding pavement.
Corrective Action
Replace a damaged plate with a recessed or low-profile design, and add a protective cover where snowplough or vehicle traffic is routine.
Anchor Bolts Pulling Loose or Failing Under Load
Likely Cause
The anchor bolts were not embedded to the manufacturer's specified depth during installation, reducing their pull-out strength.
Diagnostic Steps
Where accessible, verify embedment depth against the manufacturer's specification; check for any sign of the anchor working loose from the surrounding pavement or concrete.
Corrective Action
Re-anchor to the correct embedment depth and torque per the manufacturer's specification; this typically requires re-drilling and re-setting the anchor rather than a surface repair.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Corrosion of Plate and Bolts
High Severity
Root Cause
A damaged or absent corrosion coating, or prolonged exposure to moisture at the pavement or ground interface.
Effect
Visible rust develops on the plate or bolts, progressively weakening the structural connection between fixture and foundation.
Detection Method
Corrosion/coating check as part of scheduled inspection, comparing against the plate's as-installed coating condition.
Loose Anchor Bolts
High Severity
Root Cause
Insufficient torque at installation, or vibration from nearby traffic gradually working the anchor bolts loose over time.
Effect
The fixture is loose or misaligned, and under continued load the anchor bolts can pull loose or fail entirely.
Detection Method
Anchor bolt torque verification and a vibration looseness check as part of scheduled maintenance.
Poor Levelling
Medium Severity
Root Cause
The plate not brought to level during installation, or ground movement shifting it out of level afterward.
Effect
The fixture beam is aimed incorrectly, since even a small deviation in plate level translates directly into a beam-aiming error.
Detection Method
Levelling verification against the design tolerance, and an alignment check of the fixture relative to the plate.
Inadequate Grouting
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Grout placed incompletely under the plate, or grout that hasn't been given adequate time or conditions to cure to full strength.
Effect
Plate cracking or pavement failure around the base as the plate lacks uniform bearing support across its footprint.
Detection Method
Foundation interface check confirming full, sound grout bearing beneath the plate.
Damage from Snowploughs or Maintenance Vehicles
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Incidental contact from ground equipment operating near an elevated fixture's base plate.
Effect
The plate is damaged or displaced, and the fixture may be forced or stressed during any subsequent re-installation attempt.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the plate for impact damage, and a deformation/load rating check where damage is suspected.
Misalignment with Fixture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A bolt hole pattern or plate orientation that doesn't correctly match the fixture being installed on it.
Effect
The fixture is forced or stressed during installation to make the bolt pattern fit, pre-loading the connection incorrectly from the outset.
Detection Method
Dimensional and bolt hole pattern verification against the fixture's specification before installation.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Verify Isolation Before Removal
Electrical Safety

Where the base plate carries the fixture's bonding/earth connection, de-energise and lock out the associated circuit and verify zero energy before disconnecting or lifting the plate.

Correct Fastener Torque
Mechanical

Torque mounting bolts to specification; an under-torqued plate can work loose under vibration and shift fixture aim, while over-torquing can distort the plate or interfere with a frangible coupling's designed shear threshold.

Frangibility Not to Be Compromised
Mechanical

Never add reinforcement, shims, or a stiffer plate than specified where the base plate forms part of a frangible assembly; doing so can raise the impact force needed to break away and defeat the safety design.

Level & Alignment Check
Compliance

Verify the plate is level and correctly orientated before final tightening, since a tilted base plate carries through to fixture aim and can put the light out of photometric tolerance.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Coordinate with ATC/airside and raise a NOTAM where the fixture will be out of service or out of tolerance during the work.

FOD Control
Operational

Account for all fasteners, shims, and washers on the movement area, and clear the work site fully before reinstating the fixture.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools where the plate is part of a live-circuit bonding path, and confirm bonding continuity is restored before reconnecting the fixture.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Shimming Corrects Level But Can Mask an Underlying Foundation Problem
Alignment

Adding shims to level a base plate after installation is a legitimate fix for minor foundation irregularity, but repeated or heavy shimming at the same location often signals a settlement or pour-quality issue that will keep recurring. Investigate the foundation itself if shimming needs are increasing over time.

Re-Verify Machined Tolerance After Any Rework
Precision

If a base plate is drilled, welded, or otherwise modified in the field to fit a non-standard situation, its original machined flatness and hole-position tolerances are no longer guaranteed. Re-check alignment tolerances after any field modification rather than assuming factory precision still holds.

Check the Underside, Not Just the Visible Top Surface
Corrosion

The underside of a base plate sits against the foundation or can and traps moisture against bare or damaged coating far longer than the exposed top surface; corrosion often starts there and isn't visible until the plate is lifted during other maintenance work.

Confirm Bonding Continuity After Any Plate Replacement
Electrical

Where the base plate forms part of the fixture's bonding/earthing path, a replacement plate installed with fresh paint or coating at the bonding point can silently break electrical continuity even though it looks correctly bolted down. Verify continuity with a meter after any plate swap.

Torque Pattern Matters as Much as Torque Value
Mechanical

Tightening base plate bolts in the wrong sequence can induce a slight warp in the plate even when every bolt reaches the correct final torque value. Follow a cross-pattern tightening sequence, especially on larger plates with more than four fixing points.

Record As-Installed Shim Configuration for Future Reference
Documentation

When shims are used to achieve level, document their thickness and position at commissioning; this baseline makes it much faster to distinguish new settlement from the original, accepted correction during a future inspection.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the base plate: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a base plate in airfield ground lighting?

A base plate (also called a mounting plate, fixture plate or foundation plate) is a robust metal plate that forms the structural interface between an elevated AGL fixture, or other airfield equipment, and the pavement or foundation. It gives a stable, level and accurately positioned mounting surface.

Q2What does a base plate do?

It distributes the fixture's weight and any wind or ice loads over a larger area of pavement, provides a machined reference plane so the fixture is vertical and its beam is correctly aimed, and is secured by anchor bolts embedded in the foundation.

Q3Where are base plates used?

They are used under elevated runway edge light fixtures on frangible poles, elevated taxiway edge lights, elevated approach lights, and other elevated fixtures and equipment that need a secure, level, durable mounting foundation.

Q4What are the typical dimensions and materials?

Thickness is typically 10 mm to 25 mm and size 150 mm x 150 mm to 400 mm x 400 mm depending on the fixture. Material is structural steel (S235JR or S355JR), galvanised or 316L stainless, with anchor bolts from M12 to M24 and hole patterns such as 4-hole or 6-hole to match the fixture.

Q5How flat must a base plate be?

Flatness tolerance is typically 0.5 mm or better over the plate area. Poor levelling or inadequate grouting can leave the fixture out of alignment.

Q6What load can a base plate carry, and how is it protected from corrosion?

Typical vertical load capacity is 10 kN to 100 kN depending on plate size and anchoring. The finish is hot-dip galvanising of at least 85 µm or powder coating, with salt spray resistance of at least 500 hours for galvanised and 1,000 hours for stainless.

Q7Which standards apply to base plates?

The main references are EN 10025 (structural steel), ASTM A123 (galvanised coatings), FAA AC 150/5345-42 (light bases and accessories) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a base plate be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover anchor bolt torque, flatness, corrosion and coating, bolt hole wear, foundation interface, deformation and load rating, and alignment, with a semi-annual vibration looseness check. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common problems with base plates?

Common problems are corrosion of the plate and bolts, loose anchor bolts, poor levelling, inadequate grouting, misalignment with the fixture, damage from snowploughs or maintenance vehicles and incorrect anchor bolt depth. A loose or misaligned fixture, or a badly aimed beam, is a sign to check the plate and its anchoring.
