Earth Resistance Calculator

Turn Wenner readings into soil resistivity, design an earth electrode to a target resistance, and check a fall-of-potential test.

About this calculator

Three tools for airfield earthing work. Use the soil resistivity tab to convert tester readings into ρ, send the average to the rod design tab, and use the fall-of-potential tab to confirm that an installed electrode was tested with correctly placed probes. Results are planning estimates and do not replace a calibrated earth tester or the project design.

Method & reference

How the three tools work

The formulas below are the ones used on the other tabs. Results are estimates for planning and verification.

Soil resistivity — Wenner 4-pin

Four pins are driven in line at equal spacing a. The tester injects current through the outer pins and reads the voltage across the inner pins. Resistivity is calculated at each spacing.

ρ = 2 · π · a · R

Readings that rise strongly with spacing suggest a higher-resistivity deeper layer. Falling readings suggest a wetter or more conductive layer. A spread of more than 50% across the spacings is flagged as layered or non-uniform soil.

Single earth rod

A single vertical rod with its top at ground level uses Dwight's formula. The design ρ is the entered ρ multiplied by the seasonal multiplier.

R₁ = ρ / (2πL) · [ ln(8L/d) − 1 ]

Rods in a line

For n rods in a straight line at spacing s, the group resistance adds the mutual resistance between every pair of rods (Sunde's expression for equal rods with tops at ground level). The tool finds the smallest n up to 60 that meets the target.

Rₙ = (1/n²) · [ n·R₁ + Σᵢ≠ⱼ Rₘ(|i−j|·s) ]
Rₘ(x) = ρ/(4πL) · [ 2·asinh(2L/x) − √(4 + (x/L)²) + x/L ]

Connection resistance and cable are not included.

Fall-of-potential, 62% rule

The potential probe is placed at 52%, 62% and 72% of the distance to the current probe. If the spread between the highest and lowest reading is 5% or less of the 62% reading, the probes sit outside each other's resistance areas and the 62% value is valid. If not, move the current probe further out and repeat.

Spread = (max − min) / R₆₂ × 100 %

FAQ

Earth Resistance Calculator: Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers on soil resistivity, earth rod design and the fall-of-potential test.

What does the Earth Resistance Calculator do?

It has three tools for airfield earthing work. The soil resistivity tab turns Wenner 4-pin tester readings into soil resistivity, the earth rod design tab finds how many rods are needed to reach a target earth resistance, and the fall-of-potential tab checks whether an installed electrode was tested with correctly placed probes.

How do I calculate soil resistivity from Wenner readings?

Drive four pins in a line at equal spacing a, read the resistance R on the tester, and calculate ρ = 2πaR at each spacing. For example, a = 4 m and R = 25 Ω gives about 628 Ω·m. Keep the pin depth under a/20. The method follows IEEE Std 81. The average resistivity can be sent straight to the rod design tab.

How does the calculator know if the soil is layered?

It compares the resistivity at each spacing. A spread of more than 50 % across the spacings is flagged as layered or non-uniform soil. Readings that rise strongly with spacing suggest a higher-resistivity deeper layer, and falling readings suggest a wetter or more conductive layer.

How is the resistance of a single earth rod calculated?

A single vertical rod with its top at ground level uses Dwight's formula, R1 = ρ ÷ (2πL) × [ln(8L ÷ d) − 1], where L is the rod length and d the rod diameter. The design ρ is the entered soil resistivity multiplied by the seasonal multiplier, which covers the dry-season rise.

How many earth rods do I need, and how far apart should they be?

For rods in a straight line at spacing s, the group resistance includes the mutual resistance between every pair of rods (Sunde's expression for equal rods). The tool finds the smallest number of rods, up to 60, that meets your target. Spacing of at least the rod length, ideally about twice, keeps interference between rods low, and closer spacing makes extra rods much less effective. Connection resistance and cable are not included.

What target earth resistance and seasonal multiplier should I use?

Both are your project's own inputs. Set the target from your project specification or the applicable standard. For reference, FAA AC 150/5340-30 shows a maximum of 25 Ω for airfield lighting counterpoise and ground rods, so check the current edition, and note that lightning protection or NAVAID facilities may need lower values. The seasonal multiplier is applied to ρ to cover the dry-season rise, and the calculator starts at 1.3. Use 1.0 if the soil survey was done in the driest period.

What is the 62 % fall-of-potential check?

The potential probe is placed at 52 %, 62 % and 72 % of the distance to the current probe, and the three readings are entered. If the spread, (max − min) ÷ the 62 % reading, is 5 % or less, the probes sit outside each other's resistance areas and the 62 % reading is a valid earth resistance. If not, move the current probe further out and repeat. The current probe should be at least 5 times the electrode's longest dimension from the electrode. The rule suits a single rod or small electrode, and a large grid or a long counterpoise run needs a much longer probe distance.

Can I use the results as the final installed earth resistance?

No. Results are estimates for planning and verification. Always confirm the final installed value with a calibrated earth tester, and follow the resistance limit given in your project specification, the aerodrome manual or the applicable standard. The tool does not replace the project design.
