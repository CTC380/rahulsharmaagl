Overview

Emergency Power & Lighting Systems for Airports

Airport lighting cannot be treated like ordinary commercial lighting. Runway edge, threshold and end lights, centreline, touchdown-zone and approach lighting, PAPI, stop bars and essential taxiway lighting may perform safety-critical functions. A power loss has to be managed so the required visual aids are restored within the applicable switch-over time.

In aerodrome engineering the more precise ICAO term is generally secondary power supply. ICAO allows it to come from an independent public power source or standby units such as engine generators and batteries. The goal is not merely lights during a blackout. It is the required continuity, availability, changeover performance and operational integrity of essential visual aids.

A modern airport power architecture may combine

Normal utility powerIndependent feederUPSDiesel generatorAutomatic transfer / changeoverAGL distributionMonitoring
Requirements differ by runway type, operational category and authority. Use the current applicable requirement and the approved aerodrome documentation for any project.
Terminology

Emergency Power vs Secondary Power Supply

Emergency power

A broad engineering term for backup electrical systems used when normal power fails.

Secondary power supply

The aerodrome-specific ICAO concept for supplying facilities that need continued service after failure of the primary supply.

Standby power

Power from a secondary source such as a generator or battery system.

UPS

An uninterruptible power system that holds selected loads without the interruption of a conventional generator start and changeover.

The terms are related but not exact synonyms. The distinction matters when writing airport technical specifications.
Why Backup

Why Airport Lighting Needs Backup Power

Generator only

Utility failure → generator starts → transfer → load restored. This can take several seconds, which can be operationally unacceptable for some visual aids.

UPS bridging to a generator

Utility → online UPS → AGL load, with UPS → DG as the longer-duration path. On utility failure the UPS carries the load at once, the DG starts, becomes available and then supplies the UPS. This gives fast continuity and extended backup.

Current AAI aeronautical information shows this kind of architecture in India. Hyderabad, for example, states that critical AGL circuits are supported by UPS and that a standby DG takes over automatically after the specified UPS backup period if commercial power is not restored.

      Equipment

      UPS, Diesel Generator and the Combined Architecture

      Online UPS

      Normal: utility → rectifier → DC link / battery → inverter → AGL load. On utility failure: battery → inverter → AGL load. The protected output continues without waiting for the generator, which suits very short power-restoration times.

      DG generator

      Supplies longer outages: essential airport loads, AGL, and support to UPS systems. A set includes engine, alternator, fuel system, starting batteries, control panel, protection, exhaust, cooling, fuel tanks and paralleling equipment where needed. Large airports may use several sets for redundancy and load distribution.

      Combined, the UPS is the bridge and the DG is the long-duration source. Current AAI information for Delhi describes multiple DG sets for airfield lighting with UPS-backed AGL, showing how large airports distribute and duplicate their power.

      Switch-Over Time

      ICAO Maximum Switch-Over Times

      Switch-over time is not one value for every light. It depends on runway type and operational category. Annex 14 Table 8-1 sets maximum values by visual aid. The extract below is for precision approach Category II/III.

      Visual aid (Cat II/III)Maximum switch-over time
      Inner 300 m of approach lighting1 second
      Other approach lighting15 seconds
      Runway threshold1 second
      Runway end1 second
      Runway centreline1 second
      Runway touchdown zone1 second
      Stop bars1 second
      Essential taxiway15 seconds
      Abridged. The full table lists further aids and categories, such as runway edge and obstacle lights. For precision approach Category I the cited values for listed aids are 15 seconds. Always check the current Annex 14 table.

      Do not apply one value to every airport

      Requirements depend on runway classification, approach category, take-off RVR requirements, procedures, terrain, lighting configuration and authority requirements. The right question is “what switch-over performance does each protected visual aid need under the applicable category?”

      How switch-over is measured

      Switch-over time is defined by the behaviour of the actual light intensity: the time for measured intensity to fall to 50% and recover to 50% in the relevant direction. Generator start time is not AGL switch-over time, and UPS transfer time is not automatically the same as measured light recovery.

      Visual Aids

      Backup Power for Each Lighting System

      Runway lighting

      Often split into critical and less-critical loads. Critical: edge, threshold, end, centreline, touchdown zone and selected approach lighting. Loss of one source must not cause unacceptable loss of visual guidance.

      Taxiway lighting

      Not all taxiway lighting has the same requirement. ICAO separates essential taxiway functions. Classify each circuit by operational importance rather than putting the whole network on one backup arrangement.

      PAPI

      Visual approach slope indicators fall within the secondary-power framework. Include PAPI in the visual-aid power analysis and do not treat it automatically as critical or non-critical.

      Stop bars

      Safety-critical control lights at runway holding positions. For Category II/III the cited maximum is one second, so they need a very reliable and fast power and control architecture.

      ALCMS

      If the lights stay on but control loses power, remote switching, brightness control, monitoring, alarms, stop-bar control, route guidance and Follow the Greens are lost. Study power to the light and power to the control system.

      A-SMGCS and Follow the Greens

      Depend on surveillance, processing, networks, ALCMS and field controllers. Decide which equipment is UPS-protected, how stop bars and green routes behave in a power failure, what ATC sees and what the pilot sees. Validate in integrated commissioning.

      Scope

      Emergency Lighting vs AGL Secondary Power

      AGL emergency / secondary power

      Keeps aerodrome visual aids such as runway and taxiway lighting.

      Terminal emergency lighting

      Escape routes, stairs, corridors, critical spaces, fire-safety systems and exits. Governed mainly by building, fire, electrical and life-safety requirements, not AGL provisions.

      Evacuation, apron and ATC lighting

      Evacuation routes, operational or emergency-response apron areas and essential controller illumination where required.

      Fire and rescue

      ICAO lists essential equipment and facilities for aerodrome emergency-response agencies among loads that can need secondary power: fire station systems, emergency communications, access lighting, selected alarms. The list is airport-specific.

      Engineer these as separate but coordinated systems.

      Sizing

      Generator, UPS and Battery Sizing

      Generator sizing considerations

      Do not size by adding nameplate wattages.

      • Actual connected load and demand factor
      • Motor starting current
      • UPS input characteristics and harmonics
      • CCR characteristics and non-linear loads
      • Simultaneous operation and future expansion
      • Redundancy, derating, altitude, temperature
      • Fuel strategy

      UPS sizing considerations

      Size for the actual protected AGL load and required autonomy, not an arbitrary percentage.

      • Load kW and kVA, power factor, efficiency
      • Autonomy and battery ageing
      • Temperature and future load growth
      • Redundancy, recharge time, fault current
      • Bypass arrangements

      Online vs offline UPS

      An online UPS supplies the load continuously through the inverter: no conventional transfer delay, power conditioning and a stable output. An offline or standby UPS switches to the inverter after detecting failure, which introduces a transfer event. Choose from the required switch-over performance and the project specification.

      Battery technology

      VRLA, flooded lead-acid, lithium-ion or other approved types. Consider autonomy, temperature, fire protection, ventilation, maintenance, life cycle, replacement, monitoring and environment. Base selection on the manufacturer’s certified system design and airport electrical requirements.

      Redundancy

      Independent Supply, Feeders and Generator Redundancy

      Independent public power

      ICAO allows an independent public power source as the secondary supply, provided it is sufficiently independent and simultaneous failure is extremely remote. Feasibility depends on the airport’s utility infrastructure.

      Genuine feeder independence

      Two cables leaving one vulnerable point are not independent. Consider separate routes, switchgear, protection and substations, common-cause failure, fire separation and physical separation.

      Generator redundancy

      N, N+1, dual-source, geographically separated or dedicated runway-area generators, so one failure does not take out the protected lighting. Set the level from the airport’s reliability and operational design.

      Common-cause check

      Supposedly redundant systems may share switchgear, a cable route, control power, a fuel system or cooling. True redundancy covers the whole chain.

      Monitoring

      Power Monitoring and ALCMS Status

      Backup power only helps if the airport knows it is available. Monitor utility (voltage, frequency, breakers), UPS (mode, battery state, inverter, bypass, alarms, battery health), DG (running status, fuel, oil pressure, coolant, battery, breaker, load, faults) and AGL (CCR and circuit status, alarms, serviceability). An integrated ALCMS can show one common status picture and flag a problem before a lighting outage.

      Low Visibility

      Backup Power and Low-Visibility Procedures

      At airports operating LVP, backup power becomes an operational readiness item, not only an electrical-maintenance matter. AAI information describes engineering staff verifying secondary-power availability before or during LVP and monitoring AGL status, with inspections shortly before LVP and at set intervals while it remains in force.

      Possible sequence during a power failure

      Normal power fails

      UPS takes load

      AGL stays available

      DG starts and supplies

      Engineering confirms status

      ATC acts per LVP

      If backup is unavailable, the approved LVP procedure may require restrictions or suspension of affected operations. AAI publications document such decisions where UPS or DG unserviceability affects LVP status.
      Failure Modes

      Common Emergency Power Failures

      Click a failure to see what to investigate.

      UPS battery failure

      The UPS may work electrically but lack autonomy:

      • Battery ageing
      • Capacity not tested
      • Temperature effects

      DG starting failure

      The generator does not start when needed:

      • Weak starting battery
      • Fuel problem
      • Starter or control fault
      • Insufficient maintenance

      ATS failure

      The generator runs but the critical load does not transfer:

      • Transfer device fault
      • Control logic or interlock
      • Return-to-normal failure

      Overload

      The generator or UPS trips when all loads energize:

      • Poor load segregation
      • Inrush or starting current
      • Undersized source

      Poor power quality

      Sensitive equipment is affected:

      • Voltage or frequency deviation
      • Harmonics
      • Generator–UPS interaction

      Incorrect load segregation

      A backup-protected circuit holds loads that should be on another source:

      • Wrong bus allocation
      • Undocumented changes
      • Load schedule not kept current

      Control system failure

      Power is available but ALCMS or field controls do not operate:

      • Server or network not on UPS
      • Field controller unprotected
      • Alarms lost

      Common-cause failure

      Two redundant systems fail together:

      • Shared switchgear or cable route
      • Shared control power
      • Shared fuel or cooling

      Wider Systems

      Renewables, Microgrids and Control-Room Power

      Renewables and storage

      Solar PV, battery storage, microgrids and smart energy management can improve resilience. But renewable generation does not automatically make a supply suitable as a safety-critical secondary source. Critical AGL needs availability, stability, switch-over performance, fault behaviour and approved operating requirements, with a dedicated secondary-power strategy.

      Control rooms and field controls

      ATC, ALCMS, A-SMGCS, communications and surveillance should be considered together. Keeping the light but losing control or monitoring is a problem, and so is the reverse. Protect the ALCMS server, workstation, field controllers, network, CCR interfaces and PLC/RTU, and plan functional continuity, not only equipment continuity.

      CCRs and isolating transformers

      Test the whole path: backup source → CCR → primary circuit → transformer → light. Do not test each block alone and assume the result. Judge the light itself, not only the CCR output reading.

      Full chain

      Power source → switchgear → UPS / DG → CCR → primary cable → isolating transformer → connector → AGL light. A defect anywhere affects the final visual aid.

      Safety

      Working Safely on Emergency Power Systems

      Hazards include high voltage, electrical isolation, generator backfeed, UPS stored energy, batteries, fuel, rotating equipment, airside vehicle movements, runway and taxiway access, live circuits, lockout/tagout and permit systems. Do generator and UPS maintenance only under appropriate electrical safety procedures.

      Standards

      Standards and Design Baseline for India

      ICAO

      Annex 14 Volume I is the core international reference. The ICAO Store lists the 9th Edition, July 2022, including Amendment 18. Its secondary-power provisions allow an independent public source or standby units and set category-dependent switch-over times. ICAO guidance also says power, lighting and control for very low RVR operations must be designed so that an equipment failure does not leave the pilot with inadequate or misleading visual guidance.

      • AAI requirements
      • Aerodrome operating procedures
      • Project specifications
      • Current ICAO provisions as applicable

      Projects

      New Airports, Upgrades and Gap Assessment

      New airports

      Set the power philosophy before finalizing AGL circuit design: operational category → visual-aid requirement → critical / essential load classification → power architecture → UPS / DG sizing → AGL circuit design → ALCMS integration → switch-over testing → photometry → commissioning. This avoids costly redesign after circuits are installed.

      Existing airports

      Start with an audit of utility feeds, DG and UPS capacity, battery age, CCR configuration, circuit segmentation, switchgear, ATS, cable routes, ALCMS, actual measured changeover time and current operating category. Find the gap between existing and required capability.

      FAQ

      Emergency Power & Lighting FAQ

      Q1What is emergency power in an airport?

      Backup electrical infrastructure used to maintain critical airport facilities when the normal source fails. For aerodrome visual aids ICAO commonly calls it a secondary power supply.

      Q2What is the difference between UPS and a diesel generator?

      A UPS gives rapid or uninterrupted power to protected loads. A diesel generator gives longer-duration standby power. They are often used together.

      Q3Why is UPS used for runway lighting?

      A UPS can hold the protected AGL load through the transition caused by failure of the normal source, helping the lighting meet the applicable switch-over requirement.

      Q4Does every airport need the same backup-power system?

      No. The architecture depends on runway category, operational conditions, visual aids, airport configuration and applicable regulations and specifications.

      Q5What is the required AGL switch-over time?

      There is no single value. ICAO Table 8-1 gives category- and visual-aid-specific maximums. For example it gives one second for the inner 300 m of approach lighting and for all stop bars in precision approach Category II/III.

      Q6Is 30 minutes of UPS backup required by ICAO?

      No. Thirty minutes appears in some Indian airport configurations, such as Hyderabad, but it is an airport-specific implementation detail.

      Q7Can an independent utility feeder be used instead of a generator?

      Yes. ICAO allows an independent public power source as a secondary supply when it meets the required independence conditions.

      Q8Does generator start time equal AGL switch-over time?

      No. Switch-over is evaluated at lighting-system level, from the measured light intensity response through the power transition.

      Q9Does PAPI need emergency power?

      It may, depending on runway category and applicable requirements. Include it in the airport’s visual-aid power analysis.

      Q10Does emergency power need to supply ALCMS?

      Include the control and monitoring system in the continuity analysis wherever its loss would affect the required operation of critical AGL or safety-related guidance functions.

      Q11What happens if UPS fails during low visibility?

      The airport’s approved LVP procedure decides the response. AAI information shows examples where UPS or DG unserviceability can lead to restrictions or suspension of affected low-visibility operations.
