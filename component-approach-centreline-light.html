Approach Centreline Light

A series of high‑intensity lights installed along the approach centreline, providing pilots with precise lateral guidance and distance‑to‑runway cues during final approach. These lights are a fundamental element of precision and medium‑intensity approach lighting systems (ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSF, etc.), helping pilots maintain accurate alignment and glide‑slope awareness in low‑visibility conditions.

Overview

What Is It?

An Approach Centreline Light is a high‑intensity light fixture installed on the approach centreline, typically spaced at regular intervals (e.g., 30 m or 60 m) as part of an approach lighting system. These lights form a continuous row that extends from the runway threshold into the approach area, providing pilots with a clear visual reference for lateral alignment and distance‑to‑go. They are often steady‑burning white lights, but may also flash in some configurations to enhance conspicuity.

Approach centreline lights are a standard component of both precision (ALSF‑1/2) and medium‑intensity (MALSF/MALSR) approach lighting systems. They work in conjunction with barrettes, crossbars, and sequenced flashing lights to create a comprehensive visual guidance system, particularly critical during low‑visibility (CAT I/II/III) operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The approach centreline light exists to give a pilot a continuous, un-interrupted line to fly before the runway itself is close enough to provide any visual reference at all. In the seconds after breaking out of cloud or fog on an instrument approach, this row of lights is often the very first thing a pilot sees, and it needs to answer two questions immediately: am I aligned, and how far do I still have to go? Lateral alignment comes from the row appearing straight ahead rather than skewed to one side; distance comes from the perceptual effect of the evenly spaced lights appearing to spread apart as the aircraft closes in, which a trained eye reads almost automatically as closure rate. Because it forms the visual spine that barrettes, crossbars, and flashing lights are all built around, its role is less about any single light and more about maintaining that unbroken reference line all the way to the runway environment.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Approach centreline lights are deployed on precision and non‑precision approach runways and are used in:

  • CAT I precision approach runways (ALSF‑1) – providing essential centreline guidance for approaches with 200 ft decision height.
  • CAT II and III precision approach runways (ALSF‑2) – critical for low‑visibility operations with decision heights as low as 100 ft or less.
  • Medium‑intensity approach lighting systems (MALSF, MALSR) – providing enhanced centreline cues for non‑precision runways with higher traffic volumes.
  • Runways with low‑visibility operations (RVR below 800 m) – ensuring pilots have continuous centreline alignment cues.
  • Major international airports – where high traffic volumes and adverse weather demand reliable approach guidance.
  • Military airfields – requiring all‑weather precision approach capability.
Placement

Installation & Location

Barrettes are set out on the extended runway centreline on the approach lighting pier or gantry structure, spaced at 30 m intervals from 300 m out to 900 m from the threshold (per ICAO Annex 14 CAT II/III precision approach lighting; FAA ALSF configurations typically extend 2,400–3,000 ft, roughly 730–915 m, depending on terrain), each mounted on its own frangible pole and pier foundation. Where the approach terrain is level, piers stand individually; where the approach crosses low-lying ground or water, piers are raised on a stilted platform or bridge structure to keep the light row at the correct beam elevation. Cabling runs along the pier line back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with each barrette's isolating transformer at its pole base, and pier setout tied to the ILS/runway centreline survey so the row stays true to the extended centreline across the full approach length.

Function

How Does It Work?

Approach centreline lights function as a continuous visual reference along the approach path:

  • Configuration: A row of lights is installed along the approach centreline, typically spaced at 30 m or 60 m intervals, extending from the runway threshold to the outer end of the approach lighting system (e.g., 720 m or 900 m).
  • Lighting: The lights are high‑intensity white, steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by the CCR (5‑step). In some systems, the centreline lights may flash in sequence with other components to create a "running" effect.
  • Lateral Guidance: The row of lights provides a clear centreline reference. If the aircraft is off‑centreline, the lights will appear to shift left or right, indicating the need for lateral correction.
  • Distance Reference: The spacing of the lights provides distance‑to‑go information. As the aircraft approaches, the lights appear to spread out, helping the pilot judge descent rate and remaining distance.
  • Integration: Approach centreline lights are integrated with barrettes, crossbars, side row bars, and sequenced flashing lights to form a complete visual approach system. They are typically the most numerous lights on the approach lighting structure.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Approach centreline lights are not individually addressable — steady-burning intensity is controlled at the loop level through the constant current series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR or ATC instruction. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A), and every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, burns at the same intensity simultaneously. Where a row of centreline lights is layered with a sequenced flashing "running" effect, that timing is a separate control path: the ALCMS or an operator command starts the Flasher Control Unit's sequence, which triggers each Sequenced Flashing Light in turn, independent of the steady-burning current loop beneath it. Fault detection on the steady-burning loop remains loop-based — a voltage rise at constant current signals an open-circuit lamp or failed isolating transformer, reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Centreline Fixture (with loop-level feedback)

Diagram

Two Cues in One Row of Lights

A single row of centreline lights, spaced evenly at 30 m or 60 m intervals, gives the pilot two independent visual cues at once: the row's straightness (or apparent lean) shows lateral position relative to the extended runway centreline, while the increasing apparent spacing between lights — a simple perspective effect — conveys distance-to-go and helps judge descent rate as the threshold nears.

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture is typically a die-cast aluminium housing, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, mounted on a frangible pole (elevated) or set into an L-850-series base can flush with the approach area grade (inset). The optical assembly consists of a high-intensity lamp or LED array behind a borosilicate glass or polycarbonate lens/prism, gasket-sealed to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating against rain, dust, and de-icing fluid. Elevated units use a frangible coupling engineered to shear cleanly under aircraft impact, mounted atop a base can set in the approach light bridge or stand structure; inset units seat directly in a load-rated base can recessed into the approach area surface. Internal wiring terminates at sealed L-823 primary connectors that mate to the isolating transformer's secondary leads, with the transformer typically housed in its own base can nearby. Stainless-steel fasteners, corrosion-resistant hardware, and a drainage provision in the base can are standard to cope with the exposed, often unpaved, approach area environment.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Precise Alignment and Spacing

Approach centreline lights must be installed with exact alignment along the centreline and consistent spacing. Any deviation can create confusing visual cues for pilots. Regular surveys using theodolites or laser alignment tools are essential, especially after pavement resurfacing or maintenance.

Lens Contamination and Light Output

Centreline lights are exposed to jet blast, rubber deposits, and de‑icing fluids. Contamination can reduce light output and affect the clarity of the centreline reference. Routine cleaning and photometric checks are critical, especially before low‑visibility seasons.

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Elevated centreline lights are subject to wind loading and thermal cycling, which can fatigue the frangible couplings. Regular inspection and replacement per manufacturer schedules ensure the lights remain safe and properly positioned.

Flashing Synchronisation Failure

In systems where centreline lights flash in sequence, timing errors can occur due to control circuit issues. This can disrupt the "running" effect and confuse pilots. Periodic testing of the flashing sequence and synchronisation is recommended.

Field Notes

Troubleshooting Guide

Centreline Cue Appears Misaligned to Pilots
Likely Cause
Installation deviation or pavement/ground settlement has shifted one or more fixtures off the precise centreline alignment.
Diagnostic Steps
Conduct an alignment survey with a theodolite or laser alignment tool along the affected section and compare against design centreline.
Corrective Action
Re-position the affected fixture(s) to restore alignment and spacing, and re-survey after any pavement work in the area.
Reduced Light Output or Unclear Centreline Reference
Likely Cause
Lens contamination from jet blast, rubber deposits, and de-icing fluids has reduced output on one or more fixtures.
Diagnostic Steps
Take photometric readings and compare to rated output; inspect lenses for visible contamination, especially ahead of low-visibility season.
Corrective Action
Clean lenses with approved solvents and re-check output; schedule a full-line clean before the low-visibility season begins.
Fixture Displaced or Fails to Break Away Cleanly
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling fatigue from sustained wind loading and thermal cycling on elevated centreline fixtures.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect couplings for cracking, corrosion, or visible fatigue against the manufacturer's inspection schedule.
Corrective Action
Replace the coupling per the manufacturer's replacement interval, and confirm breakaway performance is not compromised by over-tightening or corrosion.
Flashing Sequence Loses Its "Running" Effect
Likely Cause
Timing errors in the control circuit have desynchronised the flash sequence across the centreline lights.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the flashing sequence and timing against the design pattern; check the flasher control unit's timing output for drift.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or repair the flasher control timing, and re-verify the running effect is restored across the full sequence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Array / Isolating Transformer Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or a manufacturing defect in the LED array, or moisture-driven insulation breakdown in the isolating transformer winding.
Effect
A single unit in the row goes dark, breaking the continuous, unbroken reference line the approach centreline light exists to provide during the critical seconds after breakout.
Detection Method
Test activation of the centreline run, loop-resistance testing from the CCR output, and megger testing of the individual transformer.
Row Alignment Drift From Structure Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Settlement or frost heave of an individual approach light structure shifting one unit's position slightly off the design line.
Effect
The row no longer reads as perfectly straight ahead, degrading the lateral-alignment cue a pilot reads almost automatically in the first seconds after breakout.
Detection Method
Position and level survey of the centreline run against the approach lighting design drawing during scheduled inspection.
Uneven Intensity Degradation Across the Row
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Inconsistent lens haze, ageing, or partial LED degradation across different units in the row rather than a single unit failing outright.
Effect
The evenly-spread intensity gradient a pilot reads as closure rate becomes uneven, subtly distorting the distance-to-go perception the row is designed to give.
Detection Method
Comparative photometric survey across the row rather than single-fixture testing, flagging units that fall outside the expected intensity band relative to their neighbours.
Frangible Support Fatigue / Impact Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading fatiguing the frangible coupling, or a strike from wildlife, debris, or a low approach given the fixture's location directly in the approach path.
Effect
Unit leans, misaligns, or separates from its support, breaking the row's visual continuity at exactly the phase of flight it matters most.
Detection Method
Post-strike inspection procedure, and coupling condition check during scheduled maintenance of the approach lighting array.
Junction Box Connector / Series Circuit Insulation Breakdown
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress at the approach light junction box feeding the unit, or a corroded splice from years of ground moisture and temperature cycling.
Effect
Intermittent operation or a partial ground fault on the circuit segment serving that section of the centreline row.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit segment, and inspection of the junction box seal and splices during maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Approach centreline light circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the associated CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening its housing or handling cable connectors.

Elevated Tower & Off-Runway Access
Personnel Safety

Centreline lights are mounted along the extended runway centreline, often on elevated frangible towers over terrain that may be uneven or off the paved movement area; use fall protection when climbing towers and assess ground conditions before positioning vehicles or ladders.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling or tower base with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the mount must still break away cleanly on aircraft impact within the runway safety area.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Work on approach lighting affects an active runway's usable minima; obtain ATC/airside coordination and issue a NOTAM before any centreline light is taken out of service, and keep personnel and vehicles clear of the approach surface during aircraft operations.

FOD Control Along the Approach
Operational

Account for tools, fasteners, and hardware at every fixture worked on, and remove or secure loose material before leaving the site, since debris in the approach area can be ingested or strike an aircraft on short final.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the fixture and tower/base are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a light out of aim tolerance, at reduced intensity, or otherwise non-compliant after maintenance; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Longitudinal Spacing Tolerance Compounds Across a Long Approach
Design

A small spacing error at each stringer position along a long approach lighting run can accumulate into a noticeably irregular centreline by the time it reaches the threshold. Verify cumulative spacing against the overall design layout, not just station-to-station tolerance.

Off-Runway Terrain Settlement Shifts Alignment Over Time
Mechanical

Approach lighting masts often sit on softer, less-compacted terrain off the runway strip than fixtures on the paved surface; ground settlement over years of service can tilt masts out of aim tolerance gradually. Include mast-plumb checks in the routine inspection cycle, not just after a reported impact.

Wind Loading Design Should Account for the Full Mast, Not Just the Fixture
Structural

Approach lighting fixtures are often mounted on elevated masts with significant exposed structure; wind load calculations for the mounting and foundation need to account for the full mast assembly's wind profile, not treat it as a point load equivalent to the fixture alone.

Verify Alignment Relative to the Extended Runway Centreline, Not Just the Local Row
Verification

Individual centreline fixtures can be well-aligned within their own local row while the row as a whole is offset from the true extended runway centreline. Check alignment against a surveyed extended centreline reference, not only against neighbouring fixtures.

Ice Accretion on Elevated Masts Changes Both Load and Aim
Seasonal

Ice building up on an elevated approach light mast adds structural load and can also subtly shift fixture aim as the ice forms asymmetrically. In icing-prone climates, factor ice load into structural design margin and include post-thaw aim verification in the seasonal maintenance routine.

Trend Loop Readings Over the Full Approach Run
Troubleshooting

Approach lighting loops often run longer cable distances than runway edge loops; moisture-related insulation degradation can develop gradually over a long run before it causes a hard fault. Log megger readings across maintenance visits to catch a slow-developing fault early.
