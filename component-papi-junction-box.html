PAPI Junction Box

A robust, weatherproof enclosure that provides the electrical interface between the primary series circuit and the individual PAPI light units. Housing the isolating transformers and cable terminations, the junction box ensures safe, reliable power distribution and protects critical connections from environmental damage.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Junction Box (also known as a transformer housing or base can) is a key infrastructure component in the PAPI lighting system. It serves as the physical and electrical interface point between the underground primary series cable and the elevated PAPI light unit. Typically installed at the base of the frangible pole or below grade, the junction box houses the isolating transformer, primary and secondary cable terminations, and provides a secure, weatherproof enclosure for these critical connections.

The junction box is designed to:

  • House the isolating transformer – stepping down the primary circuit current (6.6A) to the secondary circuit feeding the individual PAPI unit.
  • Provide cable termination points – for both primary (incoming and outgoing) and secondary cables, with proper strain relief.
  • Protect connections – from moisture, dirt, corrosion, and mechanical damage, ensuring long‑term reliability.
  • Enable maintenance – with a removable cover for easy access to the transformer and terminations.
  • Ensure electrical safety – with proper earthing and insulation to prevent hazards.

Junction boxes are available in a variety of materials (cast iron, aluminium, polymer) and configurations to suit different installation environments—underground, surface‑mounted, or elevated.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The junction box exists to protect the single most exposed and vulnerable point in the PAPI's electrical chain — the transition between buried primary cable and the elevated unit sitting out in the weather. Moisture ingress, corrosion, or a loosened termination at this joint can take an entire PAPI unit offline, and because the four-unit array only works as a coherent set, losing even one unit compromises the whole indication rather than just reducing it slightly. By housing the isolating transformer and all cable terminations in a sealed, earthed enclosure with proper strain relief, the junction box is what keeps years of rain, ground moisture, vibration, and temperature cycling from silently degrading a connection until it fails — and its removable cover means that when maintenance is needed, it can be done without disturbing the buried primary circuit that serves every other unit on the loop.

Architecture

Key Components & Construction

A typical PAPI junction box consists of several functional elements working together:

Housing

Rugged enclosure made of cast iron, aluminium alloy, or engineering polymer. Provides mechanical protection, weather sealing, and corrosion resistance.

Isolating Transformer

Step‑down transformer (typically 200W or 300W, sized to the lamp load) that connects the primary 6.6A series circuit to the secondary circuit powering the PAPI light unit.

Primary Terminals

Heavy‑duty terminals for connecting the incoming and outgoing primary cable. Often fitted with L‑823 connectors or screw terminals for secure termination.

Secondary Terminals

Terminals for the secondary cable connection to the light unit. May include a secondary connector (L‑823) for the fixture lead.

Earthing Point

Internal and external earthing terminals for grounding the transformer, housing, and cable screens to ensure electrical safety and lightning protection.

Sealing & Drainage

O‑ring seals, gaskets, and drainage holes to prevent water ingress and allow any moisture that does enter to escape. Pressure equalisation valves are also common.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The PAPI junction box is an essential component of every PAPI installation, and is found in a variety of configurations:

  • Permanent PAPI systems – on precision runways (CAT I, II, III) with underground cabling.
  • Helicopter PAPI – with similar junction box requirements, often with smaller transformer ratings.
  • Portable PAPI – using lightweight, surface‑mount junction boxes with quick‑connect cables.
  • Retrofit installations – where existing junction boxes are replaced or upgraded during system overhauls.
  • Military airfields – with reinforced enclosures for harsh environments.
  • Offshore helipads – with corrosion‑resistant materials for marine environments.
Placement

Installation & Location

The junction box is sited at or near each mounting base along the wing bar, providing the splice point between the underground primary cable and the isolating transformer feeding that unit, or between the transformer and the fixture's secondary leads. On permanent installations it sits in a handhole or pit set to grade beside the base, buried and sealed the same way as junction boxes elsewhere in the runway lighting network, with its position tied into the same survey used to set out the wing bar itself. Portable PAPI uses a lighter, surface-mounted equivalent positioned next to the unit rather than buried, connected with quick-connect cabling so the whole system can be assembled and struck quickly. In both cases, box placement keeps cable runs to the nearest fixture short while remaining accessible for maintenance without disturbing the wing bar's alignment.

Function

How Does It Work?

The PAPI junction box performs several critical electrical and mechanical functions:

  • Power Distribution: The primary series cable (carrying 6.6A constant current) enters the junction box and connects to the primary side of the isolating transformer. The transformer steps down the voltage (typically from ~30‑50V to ~12‑24V) while maintaining the constant current characteristic. The secondary side of the transformer connects to the secondary cable, which feeds the PAPI light unit.
  • Circuit Continuity: The primary circuit passes through the junction box—incoming and outgoing primary cables are connected via the transformer primary or, in some configurations, through a bypass terminal. This ensures the series loop is continuous, even if one junction box is opened for maintenance.
  • Protection: The junction box enclosure provides IP67 or IP68 protection, shielding the transformer and connections from water, dust, and physical impact. The transformer itself provides electrical isolation between the primary and secondary circuits, preventing faults from propagating.
  • Earthing: The metal housing and transformer core are connected to the airfield earth grid via the earthing terminal, ensuring safety in case of insulation faults or lightning strikes. The cable screen, if present, is also terminated at the earthing point.
  • Maintenance Access: The cover is removable (often with a hinge or captive screws) to allow access to the transformer, terminals, and connections for testing, replacement, or inspection.
  • Moisture Management: The junction box may include a desiccant pack or a pressure equalisation valve to prevent condensation. Drainage holes at the lowest point allow any accumulated water to escape.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The junction box has no active control logic of its own — like a connector, its role in the system's architecture is structural: it houses the isolating transformer and the primary/secondary joints that the CCR's control and fault-detection logic depends on to reach the PAPI light unit. The one piece of built-in logic it does provide is passive rather than electronic — a bypass terminal that keeps the primary series loop continuous even when the box is opened for maintenance, so servicing one junction box doesn't interrupt every other fixture on the same loop.

Because the box sits between the CCR and the fixture rather than issuing any commands itself, a fault inside it (a failed transformer, a degraded seal, or a bypass that doesn't hold) is detected the same way any other loop fault is — as a voltage deviation at the CCR — rather than through any monitoring built into the box.

Primary Cable → Junction Box (Transformer + Bypass) → Secondary → Light Unit (loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The enclosure is a cast or moulded housing — cast iron, aluminium alloy, or a glass-reinforced polymer — with a removable, gasketed lid bolted or clamped down to compress an O-ring seal against the housing lip, giving the closed box its weatherproof rating. Inside, the isolating transformer sits on a mounting bracket or base plate, with the primary cable entering through one gland and the outgoing primary cable through another so the series loop passes straight through, while the secondary leads route to a separate terminal block or L-823 connector for the fixture cable. Cable entries are individually glanded and sealed, and an internal earthing stud bonds the transformer core, housing, and any cable screens to the earthing system before the lid is closed. A drainage hole or pressure-equalisation valve at the lowest point of the housing lets any moisture that does get in escape rather than pool around the transformer, and the whole assembly — whether buried in a base can, set at grade, or bolted at the base of the frangible pole — is oriented so the lid remains accessible for maintenance without disturbing the cable connections beneath it.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Box Siting
Civil

Set the enclosure in the base can, at grade, or bolted at the base of the frangible pole per the design, orienting it so the lid stays accessible for maintenance without disturbing cable connections.

Series Loop Duct Route
Electrical

Lay duct so the incoming and outgoing primary cable pass straight through the box's separate glands, keeping the series loop intact through the enclosure.

Isolating Transformer Mounting
Electrical

Fit the isolating transformer to its mounting bracket or base plate inside the housing, confirming its rating matches the connected PAPI light unit before wiring.

Cable Gland Sizing & Sealing
Electrical

Confirm primary and secondary cable diameters match the individual gland sizing before sealing each entry, since an oversized or undersized entry compromises the seal.

Earthing Stud Bonding
Electrical

Bond the transformer core, housing, and any cable screens to the internal earthing stud, connecting through to the site earthing system before the lid is closed.

Lid Seal Fitting
Mechanical

Bolt or clamp the gasketed lid to compress the O-ring seal against the housing lip, confirming the closed box achieves its rated IP67/IP68 protection.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record box position, cable routing, transformer rating, and earthing connection as-built before backfill or final closure.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test primary and secondary circuits through the box against earth and against each other before backfill, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Earth Continuity & Bonding Check
Pre-Energisation

Verify continuity from the internal earth stud through to the external earth connection, confirming the box is properly bonded to the site earthing system.

Terminal Torque Check
Pre-Energisation

Torque all primary and secondary screw terminals or L-823 connector glands to spec, since loose terminals are a common source of intermittent series-circuit faults.

Transformer Ratio / Secondary Voltage Check
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at the terminal block matches the installed transformer's rated ratio for the connected PAPI light unit.

Enclosure Seal & IP Rating Check
Pre-Energisation

Inspect gasket seating and cable gland seals to confirm the box's rated IP67/IP68 protection is achieved before it is buried or exposed to standing water.

Cable Entry & Gland Sizing Check
Pre-Energisation

Confirm primary and secondary cable diameters match the gland sizing and that no oversized or undersized entries compromise the seal.

Post-Backfill Insulation Resistance Re-Test
Final Acceptance

Where the box is below-grade, re-megger after backfill and compaction to confirm no insulation damage occurred during installation.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation resistance, earth continuity, and secondary voltage readings alongside as-built position in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Water Ingress

Failed seals, damaged gaskets, or missing drain plugs can allow water to enter the junction box. This leads to corrosion of terminals, transformer failure, and short circuits. Regularly inspect seals, ensure drain holes are clear, and replace damaged gaskets.

Connector Corrosion

The L‑823 connectors (especially on the secondary side) are prone to corrosion in marine or industrial environments. Apply dielectric grease to contacts during installation and inspect annually. Replace corroded connectors promptly.

Transformer Overheating

Overloaded transformers (due to incorrect lamp wattage) or poor ventilation can cause overheating, leading to insulation breakdown. Ensure the transformer is correctly sized and that the junction box has adequate airflow (if vented).

Loose Terminations

Vibration from aircraft or ground vehicles can loosen screw terminals over time. Loose connections cause arcing, overheating, and intermittent faults. Use torque wrenches for final tightening and perform periodic re‑torquing during maintenance.

Earthing Failure

Corrosion or breakage of the earth connection can leave the junction box and transformer unearthed, creating a shock hazard and increasing lightning damage risk. Inspect earth connections regularly and verify earth resistance is below 10Ω.

Mechanical Damage

Ground‑level junction boxes can be struck by maintenance vehicles or snowploughs. Use protective bollards or guard posts in high‑risk areas, and inspect the housing for cracks or deformation after any incident.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Corrosion or Short Circuit Found Inside the Junction Box
Likely Cause
Water ingress from failed seals, damaged gaskets, or a blocked drain plug.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect seals and gaskets for damage, check that drain holes are clear, and look for signs of moisture or corrosion inside the box.
Corrective Action
Replace damaged gaskets/seals, clear the drain holes, and dry out and clean the box before re-energising.
Intermittent Secondary Circuit Fault
Likely Cause
Corrosion at the L-823 connector contacts, particularly in marine or industrial environments.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect connector contacts for corrosion or pitting and check for dielectric grease application.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace corroded connectors, apply dielectric grease, and inspect annually going forward.
Transformer Running Hot or Failing Prematurely
Likely Cause
Transformer overheating from incorrect lamp wattage or poor ventilation.
Diagnostic Steps
Verify the transformer is correctly sized for the fitted lamp/LED module and check for adequate airflow in the box.
Corrective Action
Correct the wattage/transformer match, improve ventilation, and replace the transformer if insulation has degraded.
Loose Connection Causing Arcing or Intermittent Fault
Likely Cause
Vibration-loosened screw terminals inside the junction box.
Diagnostic Steps
Check terminal torque against specification and look for arcing marks or heat discolouration at terminals.
Corrective Action
Re-torque terminals to specification using a torque wrench, and schedule periodic re-torquing during maintenance.
Elevated Shock Risk or Lightning Damage
Likely Cause
Earthing failure from corrosion or breakage of the earth connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure earth resistance at the junction box and inspect the earth connection for corrosion or breaks.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the earth connection, and confirm earth resistance is below 10Ω before returning to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Gasket Seal Degradation / Short Circuit
High Severity
Root Cause
Gasket compression set over years of thermal cycling, or a lid that no longer closes flush after repeated access for maintenance splicing.
Effect
Corrosion or a short circuit found inside the junction box, risking a ground fault or loss of the PAPI secondary circuit.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at each opening, and insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit as part of scheduled maintenance.
Intermittent Secondary Circuit Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
A splice connector that was under-crimped or under-torqued at installation, or corrosion building up on the conductor at the termination point over time.
Effect
Intermittent secondary circuit fault causing erratic lamp/module operation on the affected PAPI unit.
Detection Method
Loop resistance testing across the circuit, and continuity testing at each splice during scheduled maintenance.
Transformer Overheating
High Severity
Root Cause
Restricted ventilation inside the box, or an oversized/mismatched transformer generating more heat than the enclosure was designed to dissipate.
Effect
Transformer running hot or failing prematurely, shortening its service life relative to the rated expectation.
Detection Method
Internal temperature check during maintenance, and thermal imaging of the transformer under load.
Loose Connection Arcing
High Severity
Root Cause
A termination that loosened over time from thermal cycling, or was under-torqued at the last service visit.
Effect
Loose connection causing arcing or an intermittent fault, with risk of localised heat damage inside the box.
Detection Method
Thermal imaging of terminations under load, and torque-check of connections during scheduled maintenance.
Shock Hazard from Storm Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A lightning-induced transient that damaged the box's bonding/grounding path or created an unexpected voltage on the enclosure.
Effect
Elevated shock risk to maintenance personnel, or lightning damage to the internal splices and transformer.
Detection Method
Post-storm bonding/grounding continuity check, and insulation resistance testing before the box is next opened for service.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Before Opening
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the associated CCR/circuit before opening the box for splicing, transformer, or inspection work, and verify zero energy at the terminals before handling internal connections.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work that takes a PAPI unit out of service or off aim/intensity tolerance requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM, with vehicle and personnel movement kept clear of the movement area per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Gasket & Seal Reinstatement
Mechanical

Inspect the lid gasket for compression set or damage every time the box is opened, and replace it if it no longer seats flush; a poor seal is the leading path to moisture ingress and the short-circuit failure mode.

Transformer Heat & Ventilation
Personnel Safety

Allow the internal transformer to cool before handling after an active period, and confirm ventilation paths are clear on reassembly; a mismatched or poorly ventilated transformer can run hot enough to shorten its service life or pose a burn risk.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any splice, transformer, or cable work inside the box, megger test the affected secondary circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can cause erratic operation or trip the CCR.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on live or recently energised connections, and confirm the box's bonding/grounding path is intact and re-verified after any storm event before assuming it is safe to open.

Torque Check on Terminations
Electrical Safety

Verify splice connectors and terminations are torqued to specification on every access, since a loose or under-crimped connection is a direct precursor to arcing and localised heat damage inside the enclosure.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the box open, the seal compromised, or the circuit unverified after maintenance; confirm insulation resistance and lid closure before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Gasket Compression Set Is Progressive, Not Sudden
Reliability

A gasket that looks intact can already have lost enough compliance to leak under wind-driven rain, well before it visibly cracks or deforms. Replace on a defined interval based on climate exposure rather than waiting for visible degradation, especially at monsoon-exposed or coastal sites.

Transformer Sizing Interacts With Box Ventilation
Design

A correctly rated transformer can still run hotter than expected if the box's ventilation path has been partially blocked by re-sealing work, added cabling, or debris. Check actual internal temperature against ambient during a maintenance visit, not just the transformer's rated capacity against the fixture load.

Recurring Moisture Faults Point to the Seal, Not the Splice
Troubleshooting

Where the same box keeps developing insulation faults after repeated splice repairs, the actual root cause is often a persistent seal or ingress path that keeps reintroducing moisture, not a series of unrelated splice failures. Address the seal and drainage around the box before assuming another splice repair will hold any better than the last.

Re-Torque After Thermal Cycling, Not Just on First Install
Maintenance

Terminations that were torqued correctly at commissioning can loosen slightly after repeated heating and cooling cycles from the internal transformer, particularly on connectors with dissimilar thermal expansion rates. Include a re-torque check in the routine visit schedule rather than treating initial installation torque as permanent.

Verify Bonding After Every Storm, Not Just on Schedule
Electrical

A lightning event nearby can degrade a bonding connection without leaving any obvious external sign, leaving the box's earth path compromised until the next scheduled check happens to catch it. Treat post-storm bonding verification as a standing trigger for inspection, not only the routine calendar interval.

Cable Slack Inside the Box Prevents the Next Repair From Being Harder
Installation

Leaving generous service loops on both incoming and outgoing conductors during original installation makes the next splice repair or transformer swap far easier years later. A box wired with no slack turns a routine repair into one that requires extending cable, so this is worth getting right at first install rather than fixing under time pressure during a fault.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the PAPI junction box: function, ratings, sealing, standards, maintenance and faults.

Q1What is a PAPI junction box?

A PAPI junction box, also called a transformer housing or base can, is the weatherproof enclosure that forms the physical and electrical interface between the underground primary series cable and the elevated PAPI light unit. It houses the isolating transformer and the cable terminations.

Q2Why does a PAPI need a dedicated junction box?

It protects the most exposed and vulnerable point in the PAPI's electrical chain, the transition from buried primary cable to the elevated unit in the weather. Moisture ingress, corrosion or a loosened termination here can take a unit out of service.

Q3Where is the PAPI junction box used?

In every PAPI installation, including permanent systems on precision runways (CAT I, II and III) with underground cabling, and helicopter PAPI installations with similar junction box requirements.

Q4What does the junction box do electrically?

The primary series cable, carrying 6.6 A constant current, enters the box and connects to the primary side of the isolating transformer. The transformer's secondary output feeds the PAPI light unit.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

A cast iron, aluminium alloy (LM6) or glass-reinforced polymer housing rated IP67 or IP68, a 200 W or 300 W transformer, 30-50 VAC primary and 12-24 VAC secondary, primary terminals of 2 x L-823 connectors or heavy-duty screw terminals, and an operating temperature of -40°C to +70°C.

Q6What insulation and earthing requirements apply?

At least 50 MΩ insulation resistance at 500 VDC between primary, secondary and ground, a dielectric withstand of at least 2,000 VAC, and internal and external M6 or M8 earth studs bonded to the local earth grid.

Q7Which standards apply?

ICAO Annex 14, FAA AC 150/5345-28 (PAPI systems), FAA AC 150/5345-42 (Airport Light Bases, Transformer Housings, Junction Boxes, and Accessories), IEC 60529 (IP code) and FAA AC 150/5345-26 for the L-823 secondary lead termination.

Q8How often should a PAPI junction box be inspected?

Semi-annually for transformer thermal condition, and annually for seals and glands, connector corrosion, termination torque and earthing continuity.

Q9What causes corrosion or a short circuit inside the box?

Water ingress from failed seals, damaged gaskets or a blocked drain plug. Inspect the seals and gaskets and check that the drain holes are clear.
