Airside Safety Procedures for Engineers

Airside is one of the most sensitive environments in any airport. Engineers working on AGL, electrical systems, or maintenance activities are directly exposed to live aircraft movement, high-voltage circuits, and strict operational timelines — and safety here is procedural, disciplined, and continuous.

RS

Rahul Sharma

Manager – Technical & Commercial · AGL Photometry Specialist

March 28, 2026

Airside operations are one of the most sensitive environments in any airport. Engineers working on Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL), electrical systems, or maintenance activities are directly exposed to live aircraft movement, high-voltage circuits, and strict operational timelines.

Safety in this environment is not optional. It is procedural, disciplined, and continuous. This article covers the key airside safety procedures every engineer should follow.

Key Takeaway

Airside safety is the result of Procedure + Awareness + Accountability. Completing the work is important — but completing it safely, without impacting flight operations, is the real measure of successful execution.

01

Airside Entry & Permit Control

Before entering the airside operational area, engineers must ensure that all required authorizations are in place.

  • A valid aerodrome entry permit (AEP, or the airport’s equivalent pass) is mandatory, with a separate vehicle permit for vehicles
  • Work must be supported by an approved permit
  • Clearance from ATC / Airfield Operations is required

No engineer should enter an operational area without formal authorization. Unauthorized access is a serious violation and can create significant operational and safety risks.

02

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Appropriate PPE is essential for protecting engineers during airside work. Minimum requirements generally include:

Hi-Vis JacketHigh-visibility reflective jacket
Safety ShoesProtective, slip-resistant footwear
HelmetRequired for active work zones
Insulating GlovesRated for the circuit voltage and within test date
Insulation PPEAdditional insulation where required
Arc ProtectionDepending on circuit and work conditions

Hearing and eye protection are added where the task or aircraft noise requires. PPE should be considered a basic safety requirement, not an optional precaution.

03

Coordination with ATC & Operations

Airside is a live operational environment. Aircraft, vehicles, maintenance teams, and other airport activities may be operating simultaneously. Engineers must:

  • Maintain communication with ATC / Apron Control
  • Follow approved work time windows strictly
  • Immediately vacate the area when instructed

For runway-related activities, strict adherence to the approved runway closure or work window is particularly critical, including the agreed time to vacate the runway on instruction.

Good coordination ensures that engineering work does not interfere with aircraft operations. On completion, hand the area back to ATC / Operations only after the FOD sweep, with lighting circuits restored and the state of equipment confirmed.

04

Vehicle & Equipment Safety

Only authorized vehicles should be permitted to operate airside. Airside vehicles must have:

Amber flashing beacon ON and two-way radio fitted (or an authorised escort)
Proper marking and identification
Compliance with designated airside driving routes
Strict adherence to speed limits

Engineers and vehicle operators must never cross an active taxiway or runway without the required clearance. Vehicle discipline is essential to maintaining safe aircraft movement areas.

05

Electrical Safety — Critical for AGL Engineers

Electrical safety is one of the most critical aspects of AGL maintenance and installation work. AGL circuits typically operate as series circuits with high voltage, so engineers must follow proper electrical isolation procedures before starting work. Key precautions include:

  • Always isolate the circuit before starting work
  • Follow proper Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) procedures
  • Verify the absence of voltage before handling cables
  • Use insulated tools and connectors
  • Use connectors specified for the circuit (for example FAA L-823 type) and keep them correctly assembled

Never assume that a circuit is de-energized. The circuit must be properly isolated and the absence of voltage verified before work begins. Never open or disconnect an energised series loop, and remember that a CCR can be restarted remotely from the control room or ALCMS: lock out at the CCR itself, and where the aerodrome procedure requires, earth the circuit and discharge cable capacitance before touching conductors.

06

Tool & FOD Control

Foreign Object Debris (FOD) is a major hazard in airport operations. Tools, fasteners, cable pieces, packaging materials, and other loose objects can become dangerous hazards if left on the airside. Engineers should:

  • Maintain proper tool accountability
  • Avoid leaving loose materials on the airside
  • Clean the entire work area before leaving

Even a small object can cause serious aircraft damage. Tool control and FOD prevention must remain part of every airside work activity.

07

Weather & Visibility Conditions

Weather conditions can directly affect both engineering safety and airport operations. Engineers should:

  • Avoid working during low-visibility conditions such as fog (Low Visibility Procedures in force) unless specifically permitted
  • Suspend outdoor work on AGL circuits during thunderstorms or lightning warnings under the aerodrome’s lightning procedure: lightning can induce dangerous voltages on long airfield cables, even some distance away
  • Apply the required earthing and bonding during electrical work

Changing visibility or severe weather can affect personnel movement, aircraft operations, electrical work, and overall site safety — weather should always be considered before and during airside activities.

08

Emergency Preparedness

Every engineer working airside should be familiar with the airport's emergency arrangements, including:

  • Emergency contact numbers
  • Nearest assembly points
  • Fire and rescue procedures

In case of an incident:

01Stop WorkStop work immediately
02InformInform ATC / Operations
03Follow ProtocolFollow the established emergency protocol. For an electric shock, isolate the supply before touching the casualty and call the airport emergency number

Emergency preparedness helps ensure that personnel can respond quickly and correctly when an unexpected situation occurs.

09

Training & Compliance

Airside safety requires continuous training and compliance. Regular airside safety training is mandatory, and engineers must follow the applicable:

  • ICAO Annex 14 and PANS-Aerodromes provisions on work in the movement area
  • Airport-specific SOPs
  • DGCA / AAI regulations, and applicable Indian electrical safety requirements (for example CEA safety regulations)

Safety compliance should not be treated as a one-time checklist. It is a continuous process involving training, awareness, discipline, and accountability.

10

Key Takeaways

  • No engineer should enter airside without a valid pass, an approved permit, and ATC/Operations clearance
  • PPE — hi-vis jacket, safety shoes, helmet, gloves, and arc/insulation protection for AGL work — is a baseline requirement, not optional
  • Continuous coordination with ATC/Apron Control and strict adherence to the approved runway work window keep engineering work from interfering with aircraft operations
  • A circuit should never be assumed de-energized — isolate, follow LOTO, and verify absence of voltage every time
  • Tool accountability and FOD control matter because even a small object can cause serious aircraft damage
  • Weather, visibility, and grounding discipline must be factored into every airside activity
  • Knowing emergency contacts, assembly points, and the stop-inform-follow-protocol sequence enables a fast, correct response
  • Compliance with ICAO provisions, airport SOPs, and DGCA/AAI regulations is continuous, not a one-time checklist

What Makes Airside Work Safe

Procedure + Awareness + Accountability = Reliable Airside Safety

Conclusion

Working airside demands a different level of discipline compared to normal industrial environments. For engineers involved in AGL, electrical systems, installation, commissioning, or maintenance, the responsibility is not simply to complete the assigned job.

The work must be completed safely, systematically, and without impacting flight operations. A safe airside environment is the result of Procedure + Awareness + Accountability.

Every engineer working at an airport has a responsibility to follow established procedures, communicate effectively, control hazards, and maintain the highest level of safety awareness.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is needed before an engineer can enter the airside?

A valid aerodrome entry permit (AEP, or the airport's equivalent pass), with a separate vehicle permit for vehicles, plus an approved work permit and clearance from ATC / Airfield Operations. No engineer should enter an operational area without formal authorisation, and unauthorised access is a serious violation.

What PPE do AGL engineers need airside?

Minimum requirements generally include a hi-vis jacket, safety shoes, a helmet for active work zones, insulating gloves rated for the circuit voltage and within test date, additional insulation PPE where required, and arc protection depending on circuit and work conditions. Hearing and eye protection are added where the task or aircraft noise requires. The article treats PPE as a basic requirement, not an optional precaution.

How should engineers coordinate with ATC and operations?

Airside is a live operational environment, so engineers must maintain communication with ATC / Apron Control, follow approved work time windows strictly and vacate the area immediately when instructed. For runway activities, adherence to the approved runway closure or work window is particularly critical. On completion, the area is handed back only after the FOD sweep, with lighting circuits restored and equipment state confirmed.

What are the airside vehicle rules?

Only authorised vehicles may operate airside. They must have an amber flashing beacon on and a two-way radio fitted (or an authorised escort), proper marking and identification, and must follow designated airside driving routes and speed limits. Nobody may cross an active taxiway or runway without the required clearance.

What electrical safety precautions apply to AGL work?

Always isolate the circuit, follow Lockout/Tagout (LOTO), verify the absence of voltage before handling cables, use insulated tools and connectors, and use connectors specified for the circuit (for example FAA L-823 type). Never assume a circuit is de-energised and never open or disconnect an energised series loop. A CCR can be restarted remotely from the control room or ALCMS, so lock out at the CCR itself and, where the aerodrome procedure requires, earth the circuit and discharge cable capacitance before touching conductors.

Why do tool control and FOD matter?

Foreign Object Debris such as tools, fasteners, cable pieces and packaging can become a dangerous hazard if left on the airside, and even a small object can cause serious aircraft damage. Engineers should keep tool accountability, avoid leaving loose materials and clean the entire work area before leaving.

How do weather and visibility affect airside AGL work?

Engineers should avoid working during low-visibility conditions such as fog (Low Visibility Procedures in force) unless specifically permitted. Outdoor work on AGL circuits should be suspended during thunderstorms or lightning warnings under the aerodrome's lightning procedure, because lightning can induce dangerous voltages on long airfield cables, even some distance away. Required earthing and bonding must be applied during electrical work.

What should an engineer do in an emergency?

Stop work immediately, inform ATC / Operations and follow the established emergency protocol. For an electric shock, isolate the supply before touching the casualty and call the airport emergency number. Every engineer should already know the emergency contact numbers, nearest assembly points and fire and rescue procedures.
