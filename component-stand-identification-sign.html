Stand Identification Sign

A visual guidance sign installed at individual aircraft parking stands, providing pilots and ground crew with clear identification of the stand number and any aircraft size or type restrictions. Essential for efficient aircraft parking and turnaround operations.

Overview

What Is It?

A Stand Identification Sign is a visual guidance sign located at each aircraft parking stand (also known as a parking bay or position) on the apron. These signs display the stand number and often include additional information such as the maximum aircraft wingspan, length, or type (e.g., "Code E", "B747-400"). They are typically yellow with black text, and may be internally illuminated for night operations. Stand identification signs are a critical component of the apron signage system, ensuring that pilots and ground crew correctly identify the assigned parking position and are aware of any operational restrictions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The stand identification sign exists to remove any doubt, for both the flight crew taxiing in and the marshaller or docking system guiding them, about which specific parking position they are approaching. Confirming the correct stand number prevents an aircraft from occupying a bay it was not allocated, which could otherwise trigger gate conflicts, ground-handling misassignment, or fouling of an adjacent stand. Where the sign also states a code letter or maximum type, it gives the crew an independent check that the aircraft's wingspan and length fall within the stand's certified clearance envelope before they commit to the final approach line, complementing the wingtip and tail clearance limits enforced by the apron layout itself.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Stand identification signs are installed in various locations around aircraft parking stands, including:

  • At the Stand Entrance: At the taxi lane entrance to the stand, clearly visible to pilots as they turn into the parking position.
  • On the Stand Surface: Painted or mounted at the front of the stand, near the stop position, for final identification.
  • On Visual Docking Guidance Systems (VDGS): Often integrated with or placed adjacent to VDGS displays, providing stand number and aircraft type information.
  • At Multi‑stand Areas: At gates with multiple stands, each stand has its own identification sign to differentiate positions.
  • Cargo and Freight Stands: In cargo aprons, stand signs help identify specific cargo parking positions for freight aircraft.
Placement

Installation & Location

Stand entry signs are founded on a post at the taxi-lane entrance to the stand, set back clear of wingtip clearance for the stand's design aircraft, while stand-surface markers are set flush or low-profile near the stop position where a post would foul the docking manoeuvre. Where the sign is integrated with a VDGS, it shares that unit's mast, foundation, power, and data cabling rather than a standalone post and cable run; standalone entry signs are fed from the apron electrical distribution board where illuminated. Because the entry sign and the stop-position marker serve two different moments in the docking manoeuvre, their positions are set out and inspected against the stand's own docking-guidance drawing rather than treated as a single location.

Function

How Does It Work?

Stand identification signs function by providing clear, unambiguous visual identification of the parking position. The sign face typically displays the stand number in large, high-contrast text, often with additional information such as aircraft size code or type restrictions. Many signs are internally illuminated using LED or fluorescent lighting, ensuring visibility in low-light conditions. The signs are mounted on frangible poles or on the terminal building structure, oriented to be visible from the taxi path and from the cockpit during parking. In some cases, stand signs are integrated with the airport's gate management system, allowing for dynamic updates to display real-time flight information or stand changes.

A single sign at the stand entrance would be too far away to double-check against once the aircraft is committed to the turn, so identification is split across the manoeuvre rather than posted once: confirm the stand while there's still room to correct course, then confirm again right before stopping. That's a different problem from the Gate Sign's aircraft-type compatibility check, even though the two sign types share the same yellow/black format and are often mounted at the same location.
Construction

Physical Construction

The cabinet is aluminium or stainless steel, sized to keep the stand number legible from the cockpit at the marshalling distance, with a UV-resistant acrylic face bearing the black legend on a yellow (or reversed white-on-dark for illuminated variants) background. Illumination is an internal LED array or fluorescent tube, typically run on 230V AC from the apron mains supply given the sign's location near existing terminal electrical infrastructure — though some installations feed the fixture from the airfield CCR series circuit via an isolating transformer instead, for consistency with adjacent AGL equipment — through a sealed cable entry, giving the enclosure an IP54–IP65 rating against apron dust, fuel spillage, and driving rain. The panel bolts to a frangible pole set in a concrete-anchored base, to a building-mounted bracket where the stand sits against a terminal or hangar facade, or to a ground-level pedestal — the mounting choice driven by the specific stand's layout rather than the sign construction itself, which stays the same across all three options.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Setting-Out & Siting Survey
Civil

Survey and mark the sign position from the design/siting drawing before excavation, confirming clearance from the taxiway/runway edge safety area and any frangibility height limit.

Foundation / Base Plate Installation
Civil

Excavate and set the base plate or footing to design depth, keeping the finished mount level and within the frangible height limit for the sign's location.

Frangible Mount Fitting
Mechanical

Fit the frangible pole coupling, building fixing, or ground-level pedestal mount and torque to the specified value, confirming it shears as designed under lateral load and does not bind.

Cable Route & Connector Termination
Electrical

Lay the duct/cable route to the panel and terminate the L-823 connector per manufacturer procedure, keeping the splice fully submersible-rated before backfill.

Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer wattage and ratio match the panel and the series circuit's rated current step before connection.

Panel Legend & Content Setting
Quality

Before the panel is fixed and the position closed out, confirm the stand number and any wingspan or aircraft-type restriction on the panel match the apron allocation record.

Retroreflective Sheeting Application
Mechanical

Apply and inspect the retroreflective sheeting for correct grade, alignment, and freedom from bubbles or edge lifting before the panel is mounted.

Pre-Backfill Sign-off
Documentation

Record base/footing position, frangible coupling torque, cable route, and panel content as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the isolating transformer and internal wiring before the circuit is energised, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum ahead of backfill.

Loop Continuity & Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Verify series loop continuity through the sign and record loop resistance, ruling out open circuits or high-resistance splices before first energisation.

Primary/Secondary Voltage Verification
Energisation

With the CCR energised, confirm secondary voltage at the L-823 connector matches the transformer's rated ratio at the commissioned current step.

CCR Brightness Step Response

Luminance & Legibility Test

Legend & Content Verification

Frangible Coupling & Mounting Verification

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Illumination Failure

Internal LED or fluorescent lighting can fail due to power supply issues, damaged wiring, or driver failure. Regular inspection of illumination is critical to maintain night visibility at stands.

Sign Face Degradation

UV exposure, chemical spills (de‑icing fluids, jet fuel), and physical damage can cause the sign face to fade, crack, or become illegible. Using UV‑resistant materials and regular replacement of damaged faces is essential.

Obstruction by Ground Equipment

Stand signs can be obscured by ground support equipment, passenger boarding bridges, or parked aircraft. Proper siting and regular apron layout reviews are needed to maintain visibility.

Frangible Coupling Corrosion

In harsh environments, the frangible coupling on poles can corrode and fail prematurely, causing the sign to fall. Regular inspection and replacement are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Stand Number Dark or Illegible at Night
Likely Cause
Failed LED module, fluorescent lighting, or driver, or a loose wiring connection at the base.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm supply voltage is present at the fixture; inspect and re-torque wiring terminations; isolate the LED module/driver or ballast if voltage is present but the sign stays dark.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED module, driver, or ballast, or re-terminate corroded connections with fresh crimps and sealant; re-test illumination once repaired.
Sign Face Faded, Cracked, or Stained
Likely Cause
UV exposure, de-icing fluid or jet fuel contact, or physical impact has degraded the face material.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the face panel for crazing, discolouration, chemical staining, or cracking; compare legibility against a reference panel or the applicable retroreflectivity standard.
Corrective Action
Replace the sign face with UV- and chemical-resistant material rated for the local environment, and log the replacement date to track service life.
Sign Reported as Not Visible from the Approach
Likely Cause
Ground support equipment, a passenger boarding bridge, or a parked aircraft is obstructing the sightline to the sign.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the reported viewing angle against the sign's mounted position and height; confirm whether the obstruction is a one-off equipment placement or a recurring apron-layout conflict.
Corrective Action
Coordinate with ground operations to keep the sightline clear, or relocate/raise the sign if the obstruction recurs as part of normal stand operations.
Sign Down or Leaning on the Pole
Likely Cause
The frangible coupling has corroded or fatigued and sheared prematurely, or the mounting pole/pedestal base has loosened.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coupling for corrosion, cracking, or a clean-but-premature shear; check base bolts and pedestal footing for looseness or movement.
Corrective Action
Replace the frangible coupling, re-level and re-secure the sign, and increase inspection frequency at that location if corrosion is the recurring cause.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Panel / Internal Light Source Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal cycling or driver-circuit fault in the LED panel of an illuminated unit, or a manufacturing defect in the light source.
Effect
Sign face goes dark or dim on the illuminated variant, removing the sign's night/low-visibility conspicuity while the panel itself remains structurally intact.
Detection Method
Night or low-light visual check against the panel's expected output, and driver-circuit voltage check at the housing.
Retroreflective Sheeting Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
UV exposure, surface abrasion, or accumulated grime/rubber film reducing the sheeting's coefficient of retroreflection over time.
Effect
Reduced legibility under aircraft or vehicle landing-light illumination at night, most critical for non-illuminated units that depend entirely on retroreflectivity for visibility.
Detection Method
Scheduled retroreflectivity measurement against ICAO Annex 14 / FAA minimums, and visual inspection for sheeting delamination or contamination.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Impact Damage
High Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic wind loading fatiguing the frangible coupling over time, or a direct strike from a vehicle, aircraft component, or ground equipment.
Effect
Sign leans, misaligns, or separates from its post, degrading legibility and, if debris remains in the movement area, creating a foreign-object hazard.
Detection Method
Post alignment and coupling condition check during scheduled inspection, and immediate post-incident inspection following any reported strike.
VDGS-Integrated Sign Loses Shared Power/Data Feed
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Fault in the mast-mounted VDGS unit's shared power or data cabling, on installations where the stand sign is integrated with the VDGS rather than run as a standalone post and cable.
Effect
Sign goes dark or shows stale information as a side effect of a VDGS fault, even though the sign hardware itself may be undamaged, since it depends entirely on the VDGS's shared infrastructure.
Detection Method
Fault isolation check to confirm whether an affected sign's supply is standalone or VDGS-shared before troubleshooting the sign itself.
Legend Mismatch After Stand Reassignment or Restriction Change
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Stand number or aircraft size/type restriction on the sign face not updated to match a subsequent stand reassignment or restriction revision.
Effect
Sign displays a stand identifier or restriction that no longer matches current operational assignment, risking an incorrect docking or a missed restriction.
Detection Method
Cross-check of stand sign legends against the current stand assignment and restriction plan whenever a reassignment or revision occurs.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit Isolation Before Access
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the feeder circuit at the CCR or panel, then verify zero energy at the base can before opening the housing or handling cable connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near the movement area requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the sign will be dark, obscured, or out of tolerance, with vehicle and personnel movement kept clear of the taxiway or runway strip per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

FOD Control at the Base
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the base can or housing is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the sign, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or panel work, megger test the affected circuit before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the feeder circuit or damage healthy units on the same loop.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on circuit connections, and confirm the sign housing and base can are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the circuit.

Coordinate VDGS-Integrated Servicing
Operational

Where the sign shares a power or data feed with a visual docking guidance system, coordinate any isolation or servicing with the VDGS maintainer so the docking system is placed in a safe state and the stand is closed to arrivals for the duration of the work.

Engineering & Expert Notes

Build VDGS Coordination Into the Work Order Template
Electrical

Rather than relying on individual technicians to remember to call the VDGS maintainer, add a standard coordination check-in to the work order template for any stand-ID sign sharing infrastructure with a docking guidance system.

Treat Restriction Text Changes With Full Change Control
Compliance

An aircraft size or type restriction on the sign face carries the same safety weight as the stand number itself — an outdated restriction is a safety issue disguised as a labelling detail.

Weight Illumination Repair Priority by Stand Utilisation
Operational

A dark stand-ID sign at a high-turnaround stand should outrank the same fault at an infrequently used remote stand in the repair queue.

Trigger a Legend Check Whenever the Stand Boundary Is Re-Marked
Maintenance

An apron re-marking project should trigger an explicit stand-sign legend check, since the sign and the paint need to describe the same stand geometry.

Expect Faster Coupling Wear at High-Traffic Stands
Mechanical

Frangible coupling fatigue concentrates at stands with frequent aircraft movements and GSE traffic — adjust inspection frequency by stand utilisation rather than one airfield-wide interval.

Budget Shorter Face-Replacement Intervals for Heavily Serviced Stands
Durability

Fuel and hydraulic fluid exposure ages a sign face faster at working stands with heavy servicing activity than at remote parking stands — don't apply one blanket replacement schedule across both.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the stand identification sign: purpose, siting, power, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a stand identification sign?

A stand identification sign is a visual guidance sign at each aircraft parking stand (also called a parking bay or position) on the apron. It shows the stand number and often extra information such as maximum wingspan, length or aircraft type (for example Code E or B747-400), so pilots and ground crew correctly identify the assigned position and any restrictions.

Q2What colour is a stand identification sign?

Stand identification signs are typically yellow with black text, and may be internally illuminated for night operations. The sign face usually shows the stand number in large, high-contrast text.

Q3Where are stand identification signs installed?

At the taxi lane entrance to the stand so pilots can see it as they turn in, on or at the front of the stand near the stop position for final identification, and often integrated with or beside the Visual Docking Guidance System (VDGS) display. They are mounted on frangible poles or the terminal building structure, oriented to be visible from the taxi path and the cockpit.

Q4What size is a stand identification sign and how far can it be read?

Standard sizes run from 600 mm × 400 mm to 1200 mm × 800 mm, mounted 1.5 to 3.0 m above ground level, with housings rated IP54 to IP65. Typical visibility is at least 200 metres by day and at least 100 metres at night.

Q5How is an illuminated stand identification sign powered?

The illumination is LED or fluorescent, typically fed from 230 V AC apron mains, although some airports that integrate the sign into the AGL system feed it from a CCR-fed series circuit through an isolating transformer. A VDGS-integrated sign takes its power and data from the VDGS feed.

Q6Which standards apply to stand identification signs?

ICAO Annex 14 covers stand identification sign colour, size and placement. FAA AC 150/5345-44 is the fixture specification applied where the apron sign is illuminated, IEC 60598 covers luminaire requirements, and FAA Engineering Brief #67 covers LED sign performance and durability.

Q7What are the photometric requirements for a stand identification sign?

On the illuminated variant the yellow background should average at least 30 cd/m² with uniformity no worse than 10:1 and an opaque black legend giving a contrast ratio of at least 5:1. The non-illuminated variant needs a retroreflective coefficient of at least 20 cd/lux/m² for yellow per ASTM D4956 Class 1 or 2, with luminance tolerance of ±25% across the face.

Q8How often should stand identification signs be inspected?

Typical intervals are monthly for illumination and driver checks, quarterly for sign-face inspection (UV fading, cracking, or de-icing fluid and jet fuel damage) and for a visibility and obstruction survey, semi-annual for base and pole stability, and annual for frangible coupling and cable and connector checks.

Q9What causes a stand identification sign to become hard to see?

Illumination failure, a faded, cracked or stained sign face, and obstruction by ground equipment are the recurring field issues. The sign face can also suffer UV fading or chemical damage from de-icing fluid and jet fuel, and a sign can be hidden by parked aircraft, ground support equipment or boarding bridges, which is why a visibility and obstruction survey is done quarterly.

Q10How does a stand identification sign differ from an apron sign?

A stand identification sign identifies an individual parking stand and its size or type restrictions. An apron sign is the broader category covering stand numbers, gate positions, apron taxi-lane guidance and designated operational areas across the apron.
