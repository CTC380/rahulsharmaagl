PAPI / APAPI Failures

The dedicated failure-classification section for Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) and Abbreviated PAPI (APAPI) systems — covering not only PAPI hardware failures, but also the alignment, elevation, azimuth, beam-angle and photometric failures that can leave a PAPI unit technically energized yet operationally incorrect. “Light is ON” does not necessarily mean “PAPI is performing correctly.”

Initial Issue

Key principle — Division 11 separates equipment failure from guidance-performance failure. A PAPI can be energized and apparently operational while its optical alignment or photometric output has already deteriorated. Every record should answer: what failed → how did it fail → why did it fail → what evidence confirmed it → what was the effect on approach guidance → how was it corrected → how can recurrence be prevented?

PAF-01 PAPI Unit Failure
Failure Definition

Failure of a complete PAPI unit resulting in loss of its intended lighting or indication function.

Possible Causes
  • Lamp/LED failure
  • Driver failure
  • Power-supply failure
  • Internal wiring failure
  • Optical failure
  • Moisture ingress
  • Control failure
  • Mechanical damage
Possible Consequences
  • Loss of one PAPI unit
  • Incorrect PAPI indication
  • Reduced system availability
  • Photometric non-compliance
Investigation
  • Unit power
  • Lamp/LED condition
  • Driver
  • Internal wiring
  • Optical system
  • Alignment
  • Housing/sealing
  • Photometric output

PAF-14 Beam-Angle Deviation
Failure Definition

The actual PAPI/APAPI beam angle deviates from the intended/required beam-angle setting.

Possible Causes
  • Elevation error
  • Azimuth error
  • Optical damage
  • Mechanical movement
  • Incorrect adjustment
  • Foundation settlement
Consequences
  • Incorrect visual approach guidance
  • Incorrect PAPI indication
  • Photometric/alignment non-compliance
Important Distinction

Alignment error (PAF-11) describes the physical alignment condition. Beam-angle deviation describes the resulting optical/angular performance. Both can therefore be recorded in the same failure chain.

D. PAPI RCA Hierarchy

For PAPI/APAPI, the database distinguishes between equipment failure and guidance-performance failure. For example:

EQUIPMENT-LEVEL: LED FAILURE UNIT-LEVEL: PAPI UNIT FAILURE OPTICAL-LEVEL: REDUCED OPTICAL OUTPUT PERFORMANCE-LEVEL: PHOTOMETRIC-PERFORMANCE DEGRADATION OPERATIONAL-LEVEL: INCORRECT PAPI INDICATION / DEGRADED APPROACH GUIDANCE

This distinction is important because “light is ON” does not necessarily mean “PAPI is performing correctly.”

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging:

Critical / Safety-Critical — immediate operational or safety risk

Major / Operationally Critical — significant availability impact

Moderate — reduced performance, not yet critical

Minor / Non-Critical — limited or no operational effect

Degradation / Intermittent / Latent — developing or hidden fault

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about PAPI and APAPI failures, as covered in Division 11 of the AGL Failure Database.

Q1What is Division 11 — PAPI / APAPI Failures in the AGL Failure Database?

Division 11 covers failures of Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) and Abbreviated PAPI (APAPI) systems. It contains 22 published topics (PAF-01 to PAF-22) spanning unit, electrical, optical, alignment, structural, environmental, control and performance failures.

Q2Which PAPI and APAPI failures does Division 11 cover?

PAPI unit failure, APAPI unit failure, lamp, LED, driver, power-supply, cable and connector failure, optical-window contamination, optical damage, alignment, elevation and azimuth error, beam-angle deviation, structural movement, foundation settlement, moisture ingress, internal wiring failure, communication/control failure, incorrect PAPI indication, intermittent PAPI operation and photometric-performance degradation.

Q3How are the 22 PAPI/APAPI topics grouped?

They fall into six groups: PAPI/APAPI Equipment Failures (2 topics), Electrical & Component Failures (6), Optical & Alignment Failures (6), Structural & Environmental Failures (3), Control & Operational Failures (4) and Performance Failure (1).

Q4Does a lit PAPI mean it is performing correctly?

No. The database distinguishes equipment failure from guidance-performance failure because “light is ON” does not necessarily mean “PAPI is performing correctly”. The hierarchy runs from equipment level (for example LED failure) through unit level, optical level (reduced optical output) and performance level (photometric-performance degradation) to operational level (incorrect PAPI indication or degraded approach guidance).

Q5What PAPI failure chains does Division 11 capture?

In the alignment chain, foundation settlement leads to structural movement, elevation error, beam-angle deviation, incorrect PAPI indication and approach-guidance degradation. In the optical chain, dust or salt contamination leads to optical-window contamination and reduced optical transmission.

Q6Where should the investigation start for a PAPI/APAPI failure?

The diagnostic matrix assigns a primary investigation to each topic. PAPI and APAPI unit failures start with complete unit condition, lamp and LED failures with output, driver failure with driver input and output, power-supply failure with the supply path, cable failure with continuity and insulation, and connector failure with connection and contact.

Q7How are failure severity and operational impact classified?

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging. The classes are Critical / Safety-Critical (immediate operational or safety risk), Major / Operationally Critical (significant availability impact), Moderate (reduced performance, not yet critical), Minor / Non-Critical (limited or no operational effect), and Degradation / Intermittent / Latent (a developing or hidden fault).

Q8How does the Failure Database differ from the Troubleshooting Atlas?

The Troubleshooting Atlas answers “I have a fault now — how do I diagnose and fix it?” The Failure Database answers “this failure happened — what happened, why, what evidence proves it, what was done, and how do we stop it recurring?” Kept strictly separate, then linked end to end, the two form an AGL Failure Intelligence Engine rather than a simple fault list.

A PAPI or APAPI fault can come from lamp/LED degradation, unit settlement, elevation drift or power supply issues — real troubleshooting means understanding glide path geometry and unit construction well enough to find why the beam moved.
