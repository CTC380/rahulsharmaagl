Overview

PAPI Calibration, Aiming and Alignment

PAPI calibration and aiming ensure the system gives the intended visual approach-slope indication. Performance depends on more than the units being operational: each must be at the correct location and elevation, aligned to the runway and set to the right vertical aiming angle.

A healthy PAPI can still need recalibration

Foundation movement, accidental impact, mechanical adjustment, runway or pavement works, threshold changes, relocation, optical-component or equipment replacement, and deterioration or movement of mounting hardware.

Calibration vs maintenance

Maintenance covers cleaning, physical, connector, transformer and electrical checks and general serviceability, and may reveal the need for recalibration. Calibration measures angles, sets vertical aiming and horizontal alignment, verifies elevation and unit-to-unit geometry, and checks optical performance.

AAI maintenance documents show regular PAPI angle checks and periodic calibration-setting checks with sighting sticks, clinometers and spirit levels. Treat these as examples; frequency comes from the airport's approved maintenance programme and contract.
Two Activities

Ground Calibration vs Flight Inspection

Ground calibration / aiming verification

Done on the airfield with surveying and aiming equipment. It establishes the physical and optical settings.

Flight inspection / flight calibration

Airborne verification by the competent authority or an approved flight-inspection organisation, where required.

AAI's operational circular on PAPI calibration (as cited) states that ground calibration is carried out before flight calibration, that flight inspection of a new PAPI installation covers items such as range, setting angles, brilliancy control and compatibility with the ILS glide path, and that calibration is required after major maintenance or developments affecting the system. AAI also operates a Flight Inspection Unit that can inspect visual glide slope indicators. Confirm against the current circular and your authority's procedure.

Ground aiming is not flight inspection. They are complementary.
Setting Angles

PAPI Aiming Angle and the 3° Geometry

The aiming angle is the elevation each light unit is set to. The four units are not set identically; different angles create the sequence 4 White → 3W/1R → 2W/2R → 1W/3R → 4 Red.

On-course sector

The pilot's on-course indication is the angular relationship between the relevant unit beams, conventionally about 20′ of arc. ICAO-derived guidance allows widening to 30′ where harmonisation with an electronic glide path requires it. That is a design decision, not a routine maintenance change.

PAPI angle is not always 3°

3° is common but runway-specific. Indian AAI eAIP entries publish approach angle and MEHT per runway. Records should state the approved approach angle and the measured unit angles, not just "PAPI = 3°".

The 3° figures are the conventional ICAO-derived arrangement as cited. Do not copy them into a project: unit settings must come from the approved aerodrome design and the current applicable requirement. Unit labels also vary by drawing and manufacturer, so record your numbering convention on the calibration sheet.

Vertical Aiming

Vertical Angle, Equipment and Levelling

Clinometer / inclinometer

Measures the vertical aiming angle. AAI documents call for calibration settings to be checked with clinometers and spirit levels, and FAA maintenance guidance also checks vertical aiming with a clinometer. Use an instrument suited to the required accuracy.

Sighting stick

Used for routine angle verification where the maintenance procedure specifies it, with the device correct for the PAPI model.

Spirit level and levelling

Check the mechanical reference before measuring: spirit level, manufacturer levelling device, mounting reference, tilt indication. Aiming without levelling gives unreliable readings.

Other equipment

Survey equipment (coordinates, elevation, distance, runway relationship, foundation levels), optical or photometric equipment (intensity, distribution, colour, alignment), and the manufacturer-specific aiming tool where one is specified.

Electronic vs manual

Electronic inclinometers give digital readings, high resolution, data logging and easier documentation. Sighting sticks, mechanical clinometers and spirit levels remain useful for field verification. Choose by tolerance and manufacturer procedure.

Any instrument must be verified and within calibration. An unverified instrument makes the whole calibration result questionable.
Horizontal Alignment

Horizontal Alignment, Spacing and Position

Vertical aiming alone does not complete alignment. The units must also have the correct horizontal relationship with the runway centreline, the PAPI row reference and the intended approach direction. Errors come from foundation movement, impact, loose hardware, maintenance, civil works or incorrect installation.

ParameterICAO-derived design guidance (as cited)FAA AC 150/5340-30 (see Field Guide)
Unit spacingAbout 9 m between units20 to 30 ft (6 to 9 m), ±1 ft
Inner edge from runway edgeAbout 15 m ±1 mAt least 50 ft (15 m); 30 ft for small non-jet runways
The two columns differ, which is exactly why values must not be mixed. The figures are cited from secondary ICAO-derived material, so verify against ICAO Annex 14 / Doc 9157 Part 4 and your State's requirements, and let the approved project drawing control. Position also depends on runway geometry, approach angle, eye/wheel height, TCH, obstacles, clearances and runway elevation.

Verify at each visit

Unit spacing unchanged, units not moved, foundations stable, PAPI row undisturbed. Aiming cannot compensate for a wrongly positioned PAPI, so check distance from the threshold, lateral offset, elevation, runway relationship and approved coordinates.

Unit-to-unit relationship

Calibrate the four units as a system. The question is not only "is Unit 2 at its angle?" but "is Unit 2 correctly related to Units 1, 3 and 4?". Never calibrate only the unit that seems faulty.

Elevation

Elevation, Foundations, TCH and MEHT

Verify foundation elevation, unit elevation, relative elevation between units, threshold elevation and the runway reference. An angle is only meaningful when the mechanical reference is right. If the foundation settles, the unit can move vertically while an instrument zeroed to a local reference still reads correctly, changing the PAPI-to-threshold relationship.

Moving the PAPI or changing runway levels can require a complete engineering review. Indian AAI aeronautical information publishes MEHT per runway PAPI, which is why "set every PAPI to 3°" is inadequate.

Tolerances come from the applicable standard, manufacturer and project. FAA and ICAO-derived material specify their own aiming tolerances, so do not mix them. For an Indian airport use the current DGCA, AAI or project requirement.
Optical Verification

Photometric and Colour Verification

Aiming is not complete until optical performance is verified where required. A correctly aimed unit can still have an optical defect, and a photometrically healthy unit can still be wrongly aimed, so consider aiming + photometry together. Photometry may check intensity, colour, chromaticity, distribution, transition, coverage and alignment.

Beam transition

Establish that the white/red transition falls within the required angular region. Problems include a transition too high, too low, irregular or inconsistent between units, any of which should trigger optical and aiming investigation.

Colour

Visual inspection, chromaticity measurement and unit-to-unit comparison, depending on equipment and standard. AAI field-photometry specifications include colour and chromaticity in AGL and PAPI testing.

The strongest verification workflow

Mechanical calibration + survey verification + photometric verification + functional verification. Aiming alone is not enough, photometry alone is not enough, and electrical testing alone is not enough. Together they give a much stronger indication of PAPI condition.

Recalibration

When to Recalibrate and the Recalibration Workflow

Recalibration may be needed after major maintenance, unit or optical-component replacement, foundation work, physical impact, runway rehabilitation, threshold or PAPI relocation, any change affecting geometry, or abnormal photometric results. A small routine correction is not necessarily a full recalibration: the level of verification depends on what changed, why, its effect on geometry, airport procedure and authority requirement. A major change can need formal ground and flight verification.

Records

Calibration Report and Maintenance Schedules

Report contents

Project (airport, runway, PAPI ID, date, conditions); reference data (threshold, position, elevation, approved angle, unit settings); survey (coordinates, elevations, spacing, horizontal alignment); aiming (required, measured, difference, adjustment); photometry; functional test; corrective work (defect, action, retest); final status (accepted, accepted with observations, or corrective work outstanding). Do not pre-fill a universal angle into forms.

AAI schedule examples

One cited AAI schedule has routine serviceability and visual checks for damage or displacement, weekly angle checks with a sighting stick, half-yearly calibration-setting checks with clinometer and spirit level, and earthing, cable-insulation and isolating-transformer connection checks. Another includes recurring angle calibration and checks of wiring, earthing and remote control. Use these as examples only.

FAQ

PAPI Calibration FAQ

Q1How is PAPI calibrated?

Calibration verifies the approved position, elevation, horizontal alignment, unit spacing and individual vertical aiming angles, followed by photometric and functional verification. Formal flight inspection, where required, is separate.

Q2What equipment is used to calibrate PAPI?

Typically a calibrated clinometer or inclinometer, spirit level, sighting stick, survey equipment and suitable photometric equipment, plus the manufacturer's aiming equipment where specified.

Q3What is the PAPI angle?

There is no single universal angle. 3° is common, with a conventional ICAO-derived four-unit arrangement of 3°30′, 3°10′, 2°50′ and 2°30′. Project settings come from the approved design.

Q4Are all four PAPI units set at the same angle?

No. Their vertical aiming angles are deliberately different to create the white/red sequence.

Q5What is the standard angle for a 3° PAPI?

The conventional ICAO-derived settings are 3°30′, 3°10′, 2°50′ and 2°30′, from the unit nearest the runway to the farthest.

Q6Can the PAPI angle be changed during maintenance?

Only under the approved maintenance and design procedure, never just to make the lights look aligned.

Q7What is PAPI horizontal alignment?

The positioning and orientation of the PAPI row and individual units relative to the runway reference.

Q8What happens if one PAPI unit is misaligned?

Its white/red transition occurs at the wrong elevation, changing the indication presented to the pilot.

Q9Does PAPI calibration include photometry?

A complete verification should include photometry where required, but they measure different things: aiming establishes geometry and photometry verifies optical performance.

Q10When should PAPI be recalibrated?

After major maintenance, relocation, runway or threshold developments, physical impact, foundation changes or other work affecting geometry. AAI's circular calls for calibration after major maintenance or developments affecting PAPI.

Q11Is PAPI ground calibration the same as flight inspection?

No. Ground calibration sets the physical and optical settings. Flight inspection is airborne verification where the authority or procedure requires it.

Q12Does PAPI need calibration after runway resurfacing?

It may. Any change affecting runway elevation, threshold geometry or the PAPI-to-runway relationship should trigger an engineering assessment.

Q13What is the difference between PAPI aiming and alignment?

Aiming is mainly the vertical optical angle of each unit. Alignment is broader: horizontal orientation, position, spacing and the relationship between the four units.
