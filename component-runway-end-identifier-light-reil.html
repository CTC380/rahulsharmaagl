Runway End Identifier Light (REIL)

A high-intensity flashing light system installed at the runway threshold to provide immediate and unmistakable identification of the runway end. REIL systems are critical for visual acquisition of the runway, especially during night operations and in areas with surrounding urban light clutter.

Overview

What Is It?

A Runway End Identifier Light (REIL) is a pair of high-intensity flashing lights installed on either side of the runway threshold. These lights produce synchronized or alternating flashes (typically white) that are visible from a great distance, providing pilots with an unmistakable visual identification of the runway end. REIL systems are particularly valuable for non-precision runways and airports located in areas with significant background lighting where traditional steady-burning threshold lights might be less conspicuous.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

REIL exists to solve a specific problem that steady-burning threshold lights don't fully address: making the runway threshold instantly conspicuous against background clutter, whether that's the ambient city lighting surrounding many airports or the general absence of surrounding visual references at an unlit rural strip. A flashing high-intensity light is inherently more attention-grabbing to the human eye than a steady one at the same distance, which is precisely why REIL is used to positively identify the threshold from far out on approach, before the pilot is close enough to resolve the steady threshold and edge lighting in detail. Its purpose is therefore concentrated on the early, long-range phase of a non-precision approach, where confirming the correct runway and threshold as early as possible reduces the risk of confusion with nearby lighting, roads, or another runway.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed at the threshold of runways requiring enhanced identification, particularly:

  • Non-precision runways where additional visual cues are beneficial
  • Runways in urban areas with significant background light clutter
  • Runways with difficult approach paths where runway acquisition is challenging
  • Military airfields requiring rapid runway identification
  • Remote or offshore airports where the runway needs to be distinguished from surrounding terrain
Placement

Installation & Location

A pair of REIL units is installed symmetrically on either side of the runway threshold, roughly 10–15 m outward from the runway edge, positioned in line with the threshold lights so the flash is visible on the extended runway centreline from the approach. Each unit mounts on a frangible stem set in its own base can, fed by cable run in a trench or duct back to the REIL control unit, which houses the flash-tube power supply, synchronisation circuitry (for unidirectional systems), or independent flash control (for omnidirectional systems), together with any pilot-activated or ATC-controlled switching. The control unit is typically located in an equipment enclosure near the threshold, separate from the general runway lighting vault, since REIL is usually run on its own dedicated power supply rather than the runway series circuit. Placement keeps the units within the obstacle-limitation surface for the runway category, with omnidirectional units favoured where the runway serves varied approach directions.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each REIL unit consists of a high-intensity light source (typically xenon flash tube or high-power LED array) with a parabolic reflector to project a concentrated beam. The two units are installed approximately 10-15 metres (30-50 feet) from the runway edge, aligned with the threshold. They flash in a synchronized or alternating pattern (typically 60-120 flashes per minute). The system is powered from the airfield electrical supply, with a dedicated controller that provides flash pattern control and intensity adjustment. Unlike most AGL fixtures, REIL systems are typically not powered from the series circuit but from a separate power source.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Unlike loop-controlled AGL fixtures, REIL is not addressed through the airfield series circuit at all — it runs off a dedicated supply with its own controller, so its control logic is a separate command chain rather than a branch of the CCR network. Activation typically comes from ATC or, at smaller aerodromes, pilot-controlled lighting (PCL) keyed from the aircraft radio; either input is decoded by the REIL controller, which generates the flash timing (60-120 flashes per minute), selects synchronized or alternating operation between the two units, and — on systems with adjustable output — sets flash intensity independently of any RVR-linked step table. Because each unit has its own flash-tube (or LED) drive circuit rather than sharing a single loop current, the two units can, and in alternating-mode systems must, be driven out of phase with each other. This also changes fault handling: the controller monitors each drive circuit separately, so a failed left or right unit is reported and isolated on its own, without darkening the other unit or being masked as a generic loop fault the way a CCR-driven fixture failure would be.

Command chain runs controller → per-unit drive circuit → unit, independent of the CCR series-loop logic used by most AGL fixtures.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each unit is a pole-mounted, elevated fixture with a die-cast aluminium alloy or high-impact polycarbonate housing, weatherproofed and UV-stabilised to withstand continuous outdoor exposure at the runway end. The xenon flash tube or LED array sits behind a clear, high-strength polycarbonate dome or lens, gasket-sealed to achieve an IP65/IP67 rating against wind-driven rain and dust. The mounting pole is typically a low-impact, frangible or break-away design so the fixture yields safely if struck, and the base is anchored on a concrete footing set back from the runway end per the applicable siting criteria. A weatherproof enclosure at the pole base or nearby houses the flash-tube power supply and controller circuitry, since REIL units run on a dedicated mains feed rather than the series circuit used by most other AGL fixtures, and are cabled back to that supply through sealed, direct-burial-rated connectors.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Flash Intensity Check
Annual

Verify flash output and effective intensity against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer; reduced output usually points to flash tube aging or optical window haze.

Flash Tube Inspection
Semi-annual

Check xenon flash tubes for discoloration or output drop as they approach their 3,000-5,000 hour service life, and replace or convert to LED before failure.

Capacitor & Flash Circuit Check
Annual

Inspect high-voltage capacitors and flash circuit components for degradation, since aging capacitors are the leading cause of reduced or erratic flashing.

Optical Window Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean the glass optical window of dust, salt spray, and bird droppings, which can significantly reduce flash output if left unattended.

Flash Rate & Synchronization Test
Semi-annual

Confirm both units flash in sequence at the correct rate, since desynchronization or drift undermines the identification cue REIL is designed to provide.

Seal & Gasket Inspection
Annual

Check enclosure O-rings and gaskets for cracking or compression set, and re-torque fasteners to spec to keep the housing watertight.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Housing & Base Inspection
Annual

Check the base and surrounding pavement or turf for settlement, cracking, or debris that could affect fixture alignment or drainage.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Flash Tube Degradation

Xenon flash tubes have a limited lifespan (typically 3,000-5,000 hours) and degrade gradually, reducing output intensity. Regular replacement or conversion to LED technology is recommended to maintain performance.

Capacitor Failure

The flash circuit relies on high-voltage capacitors that can degrade over time, causing reduced flash intensity or erratic flashing. Regular maintenance and capacitor replacement are essential for reliable operation.

Optical Window Contamination

The glass optical window can become contaminated with dust, salt spray, or bird droppings, significantly reducing light output. Regular cleaning with approved glass cleaners is required.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

One Unit Dark, Other Unit Flashing Normally
Likely Cause
Failed flash tube or LED array on that unit, or an open/failed capacitor in that unit's independent drive circuit — since each REIL unit has its own drive circuit, a fault stays isolated to one side.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the REIL controller's per-unit fault status to confirm which drive circuit is reporting the failure; inspect the flash tube/LED array and trigger capacitor on the affected unit; verify supply voltage is reaching that unit's drive circuit.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed flash tube, LED array, or capacitor on the affected unit only; re-verify flash output and rate before returning it to synchronized/alternating operation with the other unit.
Both Units Fail to Activate on ATC/PCL Command
Likely Cause
Fault in the REIL controller's ATC or pilot-controlled lighting (PCL) input decoding, or loss of the dedicated REIL power supply upstream of both drive circuits.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the ATC/PCL activation signal is reaching the controller input; check the dedicated REIL supply feed and its protective device; issue a local manual test activation at the controller to isolate a signal-decoding fault from a power fault.
Corrective Action
Restore the REIL supply feed if lost, or repair/replace the controller's ATC/PCL input decoding circuit; confirm both units respond correctly to a full test activation before returning to service.
Units Desynchronized or Drifting Out of Phase
Likely Cause
Timing drift in one unit's independent drive circuit, or a controller fault in the synchronized/alternating flash-timing logic that coordinates the two separately-driven units.
Diagnostic Steps
Time both units through several flash cycles to confirm which one is drifting; check the controller's synchronization/alternating-mode output to each drive circuit; substitute a known-good drive circuit to isolate a unit-side fault from a controller-side fault.
Corrective Action
Replace the drifting unit's drive circuit component, or repair the controller's timing logic if both units are individually healthy; re-verify synchronized flash rate and phase before returning to service.
Reduced Flash Intensity on One or Both Units
Likely Cause
Ageing xenon flash tube approaching end of rated life, a degrading trigger capacitor no longer reaching full charge, or a contaminated optical window.
Diagnostic Steps
Check logged flash-tube hours against the 3,000-5,000 hour rated life; measure capacitor charge/discharge behaviour if intensity is low despite a fresh tube; inspect the optical window for dust, salt spray, or bird-dropping contamination.
Corrective Action
Clean the optical window first as the simplest fix; replace the flash tube if near or past rated life, or the trigger capacitor if it fails a charge test; consider LED conversion if xenon tube replacement becomes frequent.
Erratic or Random Flashing
Likely Cause
Degraded high-voltage capacitor causing inconsistent trigger timing, or a loose/corroded connection in that unit's power or control cabling.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect and re-torque power and control cable connections at the affected unit; test the capacitor bank for consistent charge/discharge cycling; observe flash pattern over several minutes to characterise the irregularity.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate corroded or loose connections, or replace the degraded capacitor; confirm stable, consistent flashing over an extended test period before returning to service.
Water Ingress or Housing Fault at One Unit
Likely Cause
Failed O-rings or gaskets on the enclosure allowing moisture into the flash-tube/capacitor compartment, or base settlement affecting the frangible stem and fixture alignment.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect enclosure seals and fastener torque for compression set or cracking; check for condensation or corrosion inside the housing; check the base can and surrounding pavement/turf for settlement affecting alignment.
Corrective Action
Renew O-rings and gaskets and re-torque fasteners to spec; dry out and inspect internal components for corrosion damage; correct any base settlement and re-verify fixture alignment.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Flash Tube / LED Array Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
End-of-rated-life wear on the xenon flash tube (typically 3,000-5,000 hours), electrode erosion from repeated high-voltage discharge, or LED array degradation on converted units.
Effect
The affected unit goes dark or drops below minimum flash intensity while the other unit, driven by its own independent circuit, continues flashing normally.
Detection Method
Controller per-unit fault status, logged flash-tube hours against rated life, and visual/photometric inspection of the affected unit's output.
Trigger / Storage Capacitor Degradation
High Severity
Root Cause
Dielectric breakdown from repeated charge/discharge cycling, thermal stress inside the enclosure, or ordinary capacitor end-of-life wear.
Effect
Erratic or random flashing, reduced flash intensity, or complete failure of that unit to trigger despite a healthy flash tube.
Detection Method
Capacitor charge/discharge testing, observation of flash pattern consistency over an extended period, and controller fault indication.
Drive Circuit Fault (Single Unit)
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Component failure within one unit's independent drive circuit, timing-reference drift, or a loose/corroded power or control connection to that circuit.
Effect
The affected unit desynchronizes or drifts out of phase with the other, or fails to activate at all, while the paired unit is unaffected.
Detection Method
Timing both units through several flash cycles, substituting a known-good drive circuit to isolate the fault, and inspecting cable terminations at the unit.
Controller ATC/PCL Input or Supply Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Failure in the controller's ATC or pilot-controlled lighting (PCL) input decoding, or loss of the dedicated REIL power supply feeding both drive circuits.
Effect
Both units fail to activate on command, even though each unit is individually healthy when tested locally.
Detection Method
Confirming the activation signal reaches the controller input, checking the dedicated supply feed and its protective device, and issuing a local manual test activation.
Optical Window Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Accumulation of dust, salt spray, or bird droppings on the glass optical window from prolonged outdoor exposure between cleaning cycles.
Effect
Significantly reduced light output and diffused beam pattern, even though the flash tube and drive circuit are operating correctly.
Detection Method
Visual inspection of the optical window during scheduled maintenance and periodic photometric intensity checks.
Enclosure Seal / Moisture Ingress
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Compression set or cracking of enclosure O-rings and gaskets, or fasteners that have loosened below spec torque, letting moisture reach the flash-tube/capacitor compartment.
Effect
Internal condensation and corrosion that can degrade capacitor and connection reliability, leading to erratic operation or accelerated component failure.
Detection Method
Inspection of enclosure seals and fastener torque, and checking for condensation or corrosion inside the housing during scheduled maintenance.
Frangible Stem / Base Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Fatigue or partial shearing of the frangible stem from impact or weathering, or settlement of the base can and surrounding pavement/turf due to poor sub-grade compaction or drainage failure.
Effect
Fixture alignment drifts out of tolerance, and cable connections at the base can come under added mechanical stress.
Detection Method
Physical inspection of the frangible stem for cracking or play, and a check of the base can and surrounding surface for settlement or heave during annual maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Dedicated Mains Supply, Not a Series Loop
Electrical Safety

REIL typically runs on its own dedicated power supply rather than the runway series circuit; isolate and lock out at the REIL control unit's own supply point, and don't assume the unit is dead just because the runway CCR is isolated.

Flash-Tube Capacitor Discharge Hazard
Electrical Safety

The flash-tube power supply stores energy in high-voltage capacitors that can remain charged after the unit is switched off; follow the manufacturer's discharge procedure and confirm the capacitors are bled down before opening the control unit or handling the flash head.

High-Intensity Flash Exposure
Personnel Safety

REIL produces high-intensity flashes designed to be visible from a great distance; avoid direct close-range viewing of an energised flash head, and warn nearby personnel before re-energising a unit under test.

Frangible Stem Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible stem mounting with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; it must still shear cleanly on impact.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

REIL sits within the obstacle-limitation surface near the threshold; work here requires ATC coordination and a NOTAM where the units will be dark or out of service.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a unit dark, unsynchronised (for unidirectional systems), or with a weakened flash output after maintenance; verify flash rate, synchronisation, and intensity before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Watch the Flash Tube's Colour Drift, Not Just Failure
Maintenance

A xenon flash tube nearing end of life often shows a visible shift toward a duller, warmer flash and a gradual drop in peak intensity well before it fails outright; logging intensity readings across maintenance visits catches this ageing trend earlier than waiting for a hard failure or a pilot report.

Capacitor Bank Ageing Changes Flash Timing First
Electrical

As the energy-storage capacitors in the flash power supply age, the discharge characteristic can shift before intensity noticeably drops, throwing off flash duration or synchronisation timing between units. Treat drift in flash rate or sync as an early capacitor-health indicator, not just a control-unit fault.

Re-Check Lens Aim After Any Frangible Mount Disturbance
Alignment

On unidirectional REIL units, even a mount that has been nudged and re-seated without visibly shearing can shift the lens aim enough to narrow the effective beam coverage seen by an approaching aircraft. Re-verify aim with a proper alignment tool after any disturbance, not just after a confirmed frangible break.

Synchronisation Controller Timing Drifts With Temperature
Troubleshooting

Flash-sequencing electronics in the master/slave synchronisation controller can drift slightly with ambient temperature swings, producing an intermittent sync fault that only shows up in hot afternoons or cold pre-dawn checks. Test synchronisation across a range of operating temperatures rather than relying on a single daytime bench check.

Photocell Threshold Deserves Its Own Calibration Check
Calibration

Where REIL intensity or activation is tied to an ambient-light photocell, dust film and lens haze on the sensor itself can skew the day/night threshold independently of the flash head's own condition. Clean and verify the photocell separately during overhaul rather than assuming it tracks the main lens condition.

High-Voltage Connectors Corrode Quietly in Coastal Sites
Durability

Salt-laden air accelerates corrosion at the high-voltage connections feeding the flash head well before any visible external damage appears, and a partially corroded connector can still pass a quick continuity check while degrading under load. Inspect and re-terminate these connections on a tighter cycle at coastal or humid airfields.
