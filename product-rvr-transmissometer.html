RVR / Transmissometer

Principle of Operation

How a Transmissometer Works

A transmissometer measures the attenuation of light travelling through a defined atmospheric path. An optical transmitter and receiver are positioned at a known separation. Fog, mist, dust, smoke and other suspended matter reduce the light reaching the detector. The equipment processes this attenuation into an atmospheric visibility parameter. Configured for RVR, the visibility information can be combined with other runway and meteorological inputs to give runway visual range for operational reporting.

Many airports also use forward-scatter visibility sensors as the RVR input, which ICAO Doc 9328 recognises alongside transmissometers. A transmissometer measures over a fixed baseline, while a forward-scatter sensor measures a small sample volume. Select the sensor type from the airport's RVR requirement, the runway category and the aviation authority's approval basis.

RVR Computation

From Transmittance to Runway Visual Range

The transmissometer measures transmittance (T) over a fixed baseline (L). From this the system derives the extinction coefficient (σ), and meteorological optical range (MOR) follows from it. RVR is then calculated for the pilot's viewing conditions, not just reported as MOR:

σ = −ln(T) / L  ·  MOR ≈ 3 / σ

By day

RVR is computed from the extinction coefficient and the background luminance (Koschmieder's law, for the visibility of a dark object against the horizon sky). Runway lights matter less in strong daylight.

By night and at low light

RVR is computed from the extinction coefficient and the intensity of the runway lights in use (Allard's law, for the range at which lights are seen). The processor needs the actual runway light brightness step from the constant current regulator (CCR) or the lighting control system.

AGL link: if the brightness step reaching the RVR processor does not match the step set on the CCR, the reported RVR will be wrong. Commissioning must verify this interface end to end, and it must be re-verified after any change to the lighting control system or CCR replacement. Confirm that the step table in the RVR processor matches the actual intensity of the approach, edge and centreline lights in use on that runway.

System Components

Main System Components

Transmitter UnitGenerates the controlled light beam for the measurement. Installed at one end of the path and kept aligned with the receiver.
Receiver UnitDetects the optical signal after it crosses the path. The signal is processed to determine atmospheric attenuation.
Optical Measurement PathThe path between transmitter and receiver forms the measurement volume. It must give representative measurements while meeting siting and operational requirements.
Controller / ProcessorProcesses sensor measurements and provides outputs to the meteorological system. May be in the field equipment or a separate RVR/AWOS processing unit.
Communication InterfaceCarries visibility measurements, status, alarms and diagnostics. Coordinate with the existing AWOS, RVR, ATIS, SCADA or airport network.
Background Luminance SensorMeasures ambient light for the day/night computation of RVR. Usually mounted separately from the transmissometer, with a clear view of the sky and no shadow or reflected light.
Mounting StructureMechanically stable and, within the operational area, frangible and mounted as low as the function allows. It must keep optical alignment and allow maintenance access.
RVR System Integration

A Complete RVR Measurement System

A transmissometer is one component of a complete RVR system. Depending on the airport configuration, RVR determination may require integration with:

  • Visibility measurement
  • Runway light intensity setting (brightness step from the CCR or lighting control system)
  • Background luminance (ambient light) sensor
  • Runway configuration
  • Sensor location
  • Relevant meteorological parameters
  • RVR processing algorithms
  • Operational reporting interfaces

Engineer the RVR architecture as a complete system, not the transmissometer as an isolated visibility sensor.

Installation

Installation Requirements

Correct installation is essential for reliable visibility measurement. The design should consider:

  • Transmitter and receiver separation
  • Optical alignment and sensor height
  • Runway geometry and sensor location relative to the runway
  • Obstructions within or near the optical path
  • Aircraft and vehicle clearance, including runway strip and obstacle-limitation surfaces
  • Frangible mounting of equipment within the operational area (Annex 14, Vol. I, Chapter 9)
  • Lateral offset from runway centreline and height above runway, to suit the sensor position
  • Effects of jet blast, spray, de-icing fluid and snow clearance on the optical windows
  • Maintenance access
  • Electrical power availability
  • Communication cable routing
  • Earthing and surge protection
  • Environmental exposure
  • Protection against accidental impact
  • Airport construction and safety restrictions

Establish exact sensor location from the applicable airport design criteria and operational requirements. Typical ICAO guidance puts sensors close to the runway (of the order of 120 m from the centreline or less) at about 2.5 m height, with the touchdown-zone sensor about 300 m from the threshold. Check these figures against Annex 3 Appendix 3 and Doc 9328 for your edition.

Optical Alignment

Optical Alignment

Transmissometer performance depends heavily on correct optical alignment. During installation and maintenance:

  1. Confirm the transmitter and receiver mounting positions.
  2. Verify mechanical stability of both structures.
  3. Align the optical axes according to the manufacturer's procedure.
  4. Check the optical measurement path for obstructions.
  5. Confirm signal strength and measurement stability.
  6. Record alignment and commissioning measurements.
  7. Repeat verification after major structural work or equipment relocation.

Follow the manufacturer's approved maintenance documentation.

FAQ

RVR / Transmissometer — Frequently Asked Questions

What is an RVR / transmissometer system?

It provides automated measurement of atmospheric visibility for airport meteorological monitoring and runway visual range assessment. An optical measurement path determines atmospheric extinction, and the processed data goes to an airport weather observation system, RVR display, ATIS interface or other approved reporting infrastructure.

How does a transmissometer work?

It measures the attenuation of light travelling through a defined atmospheric path between an optical transmitter and receiver at a known separation. Fog, mist, dust, smoke and other suspended matter reduce the light reaching the detector, and the equipment processes this attenuation into a visibility parameter.

How is RVR calculated from the transmissometer reading?

The transmissometer measures transmittance (T) over a fixed baseline (L), from which the extinction coefficient is σ = −ln(T) / L and meteorological optical range follows as MOR ≈ 3 / σ. RVR is then calculated for the pilot viewing conditions: by day from the extinction coefficient and background luminance (Koschmieder), and at night from the extinction coefficient and runway light intensity (Allard).

Why does RVR depend on the runway light brightness step?

At night and in low light the processor needs the actual runway light brightness step from the CCR or lighting control system. If the step reaching the RVR processor does not match the step set on the CCR, the reported RVR will be wrong, so commissioning must verify this interface end to end and re-verify it after any change to the lighting control system or CCR replacement.

Can a forward-scatter sensor be used instead of a transmissometer?

Many airports use forward-scatter visibility sensors as the RVR input, which ICAO Doc 9328 recognises alongside transmissometers. A transmissometer measures over a fixed baseline, whereas a forward-scatter sensor measures a small sample volume. Select the type from the airport RVR requirement, the runway category and the aviation authority approval basis.

Where should RVR sensors be located?

Establish the exact location from the applicable airport design criteria. Typical ICAO guidance puts sensors close to the runway, of the order of 120 m from the centreline or less, at about 2.5 m height, with the touchdown-zone sensor about 300 m from the threshold. Check these figures against Annex 3 Appendix 3 and Doc 9328 for your edition. Equipment within the operational area should be frangibly mounted.

What are the typical RVR reporting rules?

General ICAO guidance gives a reported range of approximately 50 m to 2,000 m, with steps of 25 m below 400 m, 50 m from 400 to 800 m and 100 m above 800 m, and averaging of one minute for local reports and ATS displays and ten minutes for METAR/SPECI. RVR is required on CAT II/III runways. Confirm against the current Annex 3, Doc 9328 and DGCA/IMD requirements before using these as a specification.

Why is optical alignment important?

Transmissometer performance depends heavily on correct optical alignment. Confirm the mounting positions and mechanical stability of both structures, align the optical axes per the manufacturer procedure, check the path for obstructions, confirm signal strength and stability, record the results and repeat verification after major structural work or equipment relocation.

What is the role of the background luminance sensor?

It measures ambient light for the day and night computation of RVR. It is usually mounted separately from the transmissometer with a clear view of the sky and no shadow or reflected light. A wrong day or night changeover can point to a dirty, shaded or faulty sensor.

What maintenance and troubleshooting does it need?

Periodic optical inspection, alignment verification, window and lens cleaning, diagnostic review, communication testing and calibration or verification using manufacturer-approved procedures. For unstable measurement check contamination and alignment, for no data check supply and communication, and if RVR does not change with the lighting brightness setting check the CCR feedback and the processor step table.

Which standards apply to RVR systems?

References include ICAO Annex 3 and its Appendix 3, ICAO Annex 14 Volume I, ICAO Doc 9328, ICAO Doc 9837, WMO-No. 8, and DGCA India and IMD requirements for aerodrome observing systems. Confirm the applicable edition for the project. See also the Wind & Visibility Display page for presenting RVR to operations staff.

What should I confirm before ordering?

Confirm the runway and installation location, required visibility measurement range, RVR requirement, number of measurement positions, optical path arrangement, accuracy, data outputs, communication interface, AWOS and ATIS integration, compatibility with the existing RVR processor, power supply, environmental requirements, mounting, surge and lightning protection, calibration and documentation.

Engineering Note

An RVR / transmissometer installation should be treated as part of the complete airport meteorological measurement and reporting system. Sensor selection alone does not establish RVR compliance. RVR is calculated from transmittance, background luminance and the runway light intensity in use, and the sensor positions must represent the touchdown zone, midpoint and stop-end as required for the runway category.

Final equipment selection should consider the runway operational category, sensor location, optical measurement arrangement, runway lighting information, processing architecture, reporting requirements, applicable aviation regulations and the manufacturer's current technical documentation.
