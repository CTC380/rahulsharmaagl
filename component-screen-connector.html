Screen Connector

A specialised connector used to terminate and bond the metallic screen or shield of shielded AGL cables to earth, equipment chassis, or junction boxes. Provides a low‑impedance path for electromagnetic interference (EMI), reduces capacitive coupling, and ensures safety by preventing electrostatic discharge and minimising interference with sensitive AGL control and monitoring systems.

Overview

What Is It?

A Screen Connector (also known as a shield connector, screen termination, or cable screen clamp) is a specialised termination device used to connect the metallic screen (shield) of shielded AGL cables to earth, equipment chassis, or junction box earth terminals. The screen connector provides a low‑resistance, low‑inductance path to earth for induced currents and electromagnetic interference (EMI), ensuring the integrity of signal and power circuits in AGL systems.

Shielded cables are used in AGL systems where electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) is critical—such as communication links, control circuits, sensitive sensor wiring, and connections between CCRs and ALCMS systems. The screen connector:

  • Terminates the cable screen: Provides a secure mechanical and electrical connection to the cable's metallic screen (braid, foil, or spiral wrap).
  • Bonds to earth: Connects the screen to the earthing system, providing a low‑impedance path for interference currents.
  • Reduces EMI: Prevents the cable screen from acting as an antenna, reducing radiated and conducted emissions.
  • Prevents electrostatic discharge (ESD): Safely dissipates static charges that can build up on the cable screen.

Screen connectors are available in various configurations, including clamp‑on types, crimp‑on lugs, compression glands with integral screen termination, and solder‑type terminations. They are typically made of tinned copper, brass, or stainless steel for corrosion resistance and low contact resistance.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

A shielded cable's screen only works if it is actually connected to earth at every termination point, and the screen connector is the component that makes that connection real rather than theoretical. Without it, the metallic screen is simply a floating conductor that can accumulate static charge or, worse, act as an unintended antenna that re-radiates the interference it was meant to block. By providing a secure, low-impedance bond from the screen to earth, the connector completes the circuit path that carries induced currents away safely, which is what allows the shielded cable elsewhere on the page to deliver its EMI protection in practice rather than only on paper.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Screen connectors are used throughout the airfield wherever shielded cables are employed and screen termination is required, including:

  • AGL Control and Monitoring Cables: Terminating the screens of control cables between CCRs, ALCMS workstations, and field equipment to prevent EMI interference.
  • Communication Links: Screen termination for fibre optic cable metallic members, telephone lines, and data communication cables.
  • Sensor Cables: Terminating screens on weather sensors, RVR sensors, and other sensitive instrumentation cables.
  • Junction Boxes and Distribution Panels: Providing a screen termination point within junction boxes and panels for incoming shielded cables.
  • CCR Output Cables: For shielded primary cables used in environments with high EMI (e.g., near radar or high‑power transmitters).
  • Retrofit and Upgrades: Adding screen termination to existing installations when upgrading to shielded cables for improved EMC performance.

The connector is selected based on the cable screen type (braid, foil, or combination), the screen material (copper, tinned copper, or aluminium), the cable diameter, and the required termination method (crimp, clamp, or solder). Proper screen termination is essential for ensuring the effectiveness of the cable shielding.

Placement

Installation & Location

The connector is installed at each point along a shielded cable's run where the screen needs to be bonded to earth, typically at junction boxes, equipment enclosures, and cable termination points rather than at intermediate positions along a continuous run. The cable's outer jacket is stripped back to expose the screen (braid, foil, or combination) without damaging the underlying insulated conductors, and the connector is then clamped, crimped, or soldered onto the screen to create a secure mechanical and electrical bond. From there, a short bonding lead connects the screen connector to the local earth bar or chassis earth point, kept as short and direct as practicable, since a long or coiled bonding lead adds inductance that reduces its effectiveness at higher frequencies. Where a cable run enters multiple enclosures, the screen is typically terminated at each entry point rather than left floating partway along the route, so no section of the shield is left unbonded.

Function

How Does It Work?

A screen connector provides a secure, low‑impedance electrical connection between the cable screen and the earthing system. Its design ensures that the screen is effectively bonded to earth, preventing the screen from becoming an antenna that radiates or picks up interference. The functional principles vary by connector type:

  • Clamp‑on Screen Connectors: A split ring or clamp that wraps around the cable screen, with a tightening screw or bolt that compresses the clamp onto the screen. A tail or lug provides a connection point for the earth wire. This type is easy to install and allows for screen termination without cutting the screen.
  • Crimp‑on Screen Lugs: A lug with a barrel that accepts the gathered screen braid. The braid is inserted into the barrel and crimped using a standard crimping tool. The lug is then bolted to the earth terminal. This provides a permanent, low‑resistance connection.
  • Compression Glands with Screen Termination: Cable glands that incorporate a screen termination ring or spring. When the gland is tightened, the ring compresses against the cable screen, providing a 360° conductive path to the gland body, which is then earthed.
  • Solder‑type Screen Terminations: The screen braid is tinned and soldered directly to a terminal or lug. This provides a very low‑resistance connection but requires skilled labour and is less common in field installations.

Key performance requirements for a screen connector include:

  • Low Contact Resistance: The connection must have a resistance of a few milliohms to ensure effective EMI suppression.
  • Low Inductance: A short, direct path to earth minimises the inductive impedance at high frequencies, which is critical for effective EMI suppression.
  • Mechanical Integrity: The connection must withstand vibration, thermal cycling, and mechanical stress without loosening or breaking.
  • Corrosion Resistance: The connector materials must resist corrosion to maintain long‑term low resistance.

The screen connector is typically connected to a dedicated earth terminal or to the equipment chassis, which is itself bonded to the main earthing system. The connection should be as short and direct as possible to minimise inductance.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The screen connector sits outside the constant current series circuit and its ALCMS/CCR control loop — it bonds a cable's screen or braid to earth for EMI suppression and carries no lighting current in normal operation. Because it plays no part in the current the CCR regulates, its condition produces no signal the loop-level fault detection can see: a loosened clamp or a degraded crimp does not register as a loop voltage anomaly or open circuit on the CCR. Its consequences are instead indirect and slow to surface — a compromised screen bond degrades EMI suppression on control and monitoring cabling, which can manifest as intermittent noise or unreliable signals on the ALCMS's own monitoring links rather than as a defined fault the system can localise. Verifying the connection therefore falls to periodic continuity and bonding-resistance checks carried out during scheduled maintenance, not to anything the lighting control system reports during normal operation.

Construction

Physical Construction

Form follows the termination method chosen. A clamp‑on screen connector is a small split‑band or jaw of tinned copper, brass, or stainless steel that closes directly around the exposed screen (braid, foil, or spiral wrap) once the outer cable jacket has been stripped back, with a bolt or screw drawing the band tight to make solid metal‑to‑metal contact across the full circumference of the screen rather than a single point. A crimp‑on lug instead gathers the screen conductors — folded back over the jacket or twisted into a pigtail — into a barrel that is crimped in the same manner as a standard cable lug, with a tongue for bolting to the earth bar. Compression glands with an integral screen termination combine both jobs in one fitting: the gland body seals and strain‑relieves the cable entry into an enclosure while an internal cone or ring clamps the screen against the gland body itself, bonding it to the (earthed) enclosure automatically as the gland is tightened. Whichever method is used, a short, direct earth tail then runs from the connector to the local earth bar or bonding point, kept as short as practicable to preserve the low‑impedance path the screen depends on.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Contact Resistance Test
Annual

Measure screen-to-earth contact resistance at each termination and compare against the ≤1mΩ specification, flagging any rise that points to corrosion or a loosened clamp.

Corrosion Inspection
Annual

Check the braid and connector body for surface corrosion, especially in moist or chemically aggressive locations, and re-apply anti-oxidant compound where needed.

Clamp / Crimp Torque Check
Annual

Re-torque clamp-on connectors and inspect crimped joints for looseness, since improper clamping is a leading cause of high contact resistance.

Pigtail Length Check
Semi-annual

Confirm the screen termination stays short (ideally under 50mm) with no looping, since a long pigtail raises inductance and degrades high-frequency EMI suppression.

Earth Bond Verification
Annual

Verify the connection routes to the main earthing system with resistance ≤10Ω, and confirm it is not tied to a non-dedicated "dirty" earth point.

Seal Inspection
Annual

Check heat-shrink or self-amalgamating tape seals over the connection for cracking or lifting, re-sealing to keep the joint at its rated IP class.

Braid Preparation Check
Annual

Where accessible, verify the screen braid is still cleanly gathered and free of dirt or oxidation at the termination point.

Material Compatibility Check
Annual

Confirm connector and screen materials remain suited to the operating environment and temperature range, replacing any component showing incompatible-metal corrosion.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Corrosion of Screen and Connector

The cable screen (often tinned copper) and the connector can corrode, especially in moist or chemically aggressive environments. This increases contact resistance and reduces EMI suppression effectiveness. Use corrosion‑resistant materials (tinned copper, stainless steel) and apply anti‑oxidant compound. Seal the connection with heat‑shrink or self‑amalgamating tape.

High Contact Resistance

Improper clamping or crimping can result in high contact resistance, reducing the effectiveness of the screen termination. Use the correct tooling (crimping dies, torque wrenches) and follow the manufacturer's installation instructions. Test the continuity of the screen connection after installation.

Inadequate Screen Preparation

If the screen braid is not properly gathered, twisted, or cleaned before termination, the connection may be poor. Ensure the screen is free of dirt, oil, and oxidation, and that the braid is properly gathered to fill the connector barrel or clamp.

Long Screen Pigtails

Leaving a long pigtail (unterminated screen) before the connector increases inductance and reduces high‑frequency EMI suppression. Keep the screen termination as short as possible (ideally < 50mm) and connect directly to the earth terminal without looping.

Incorrect Earth Connection

If the screen connector is not connected to a proper earth (low‑impedance path), the screen may not provide effective shielding. Ensure the earth connection is to the main earthing system with a resistance of ≤ 10 Ω. Avoid connecting the screen to a "dirty" earth (e.g., a non‑dedicated earth point).

Mechanical Damage to Screen

During installation, the screen braid can be damaged (cut or frayed), reducing its effectiveness. Handle the cable carefully, use proper stripping tools, and avoid nicking the screen braid.

Multiple Screen Connections

When multiple shielded cables are terminated in the same junction box, ensure each screen is connected to a common earth point (star configuration) to avoid creating ground loops. Use a dedicated earth bar for screen terminations.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

High Resistance or Ineffective Screen Termination
Likely Cause
Corrosion at the screen-to-connector interface, or an improperly clamped/crimped termination that never achieved a low-resistance contact.
Diagnostic Steps
Measure contact resistance across the screen termination and compare against the manufacturer's acceptance value; visually inspect for corrosion, discolouration, or a loose clamp.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the corroded termination, re-terminate with correct tooling and torque, and re-verify contact resistance before sealing.
EMI/Noise Reappears Despite Shielded Cable
Likely Cause
A long unterminated screen pigtail, or an open/floating screen connection, is defeating the high-frequency shielding the connector is meant to provide.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the pigtail length at the termination (should be well under 50mm); test screen continuity end to end; check that the screen is actually bonded to the connector, not just resting against it.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with a short, direct connection to the earth terminal (no looping or long pigtail), and re-test for the noise symptom after the fix.
Screen Connection Fails Continuity Test
Likely Cause
Mechanical damage to the screen braid during installation (nicked, cut, or frayed strands), or corrosion has broken the electrical path.
Diagnostic Steps
Perform a continuity/resistance test along the full screen path from cable to earth point; visually inspect the braid at the termination point for damage.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate on undamaged braid if enough length remains, or replace the affected cable section; re-test continuity before returning to service.
Ground Loop Noise on Multi-Cable Runs
Likely Cause
Multiple shielded cables terminated in the same junction box without a proper star-earthing configuration, creating a ground loop between screens.
Diagnostic Steps
Trace how each screen in the junction box is earthed; check whether they share a common dedicated earth bar or are bonded through different paths.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate all screens to a single dedicated earth bar in a star configuration, removing any unintended multi-point bonding.
Screen Termination Corroded or Loose at the Connector
Likely Cause
Inadequate sealing has allowed moisture into the termination, corroding the connection over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the heat-shrink or self-amalgamating tape seal for cracking or gaps; check the termination underneath for corrosion once opened.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the corroded termination and re-seal properly with heat-shrink or self-amalgamating tape rated for the environment.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

De-Energise Before Terminating
Electrical Safety

De-energise the associated circuit and verify zero energy before opening a cable end to terminate or re-terminate the screen; a shielded control or signal cable can still share a duct with, or run near, energised power circuits.

Single-Point (Star) Earthing
Workmanship

Terminate the screen to a single dedicated earth point per the system's star-earthing design; earthing the same screen at both ends or bonding multiple screens together in a junction box creates a ground loop that degrades signal quality.

Careful Braid Handling
Workmanship

Strip and fold back the screen braid carefully to avoid nicking or fraying strands, and keep the pigtail short and fully bonded rather than left long or floating, which defeats the shielding effect.

Seal the Termination
Workmanship

Fully seal the finished termination with heat-shrink or self-amalgamating tape rated for the environment; an incomplete seal lets moisture corrode the connection over time.

Verify Continuity Before Closing Out
Compliance

Perform an end-to-end screen continuity test and confirm the screen is genuinely bonded to the connector before closing the enclosure, since a floating or damaged screen is not always visually obvious.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate PPE when working in junction boxes or near energised power circuits, and confirm bonding/grounding of the enclosure itself before reconnecting the shielded cable.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a screen termination corroded, loose, unsealed, or multi-point earthed; verify and document the connection before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Second Earth Point Can Be Worse Than None
Compliance

Technicians sometimes add a "belt and braces" second earth point on a screen thinking it improves safety; in a star-earthed system this creates a ground loop that degrades signal quality without necessarily improving safety, so any second bonding point must be a deliberate design decision, not an ad hoc addition.

A Floating Screen Can Still Pass a Casual Visual Check
Troubleshooting

A screen braid that's been folded back but not actually bonded to the connector can look correctly terminated at a glance; only an end-to-end continuity test confirms genuine bonding, since a floating screen is not visually distinguishable from a properly terminated one.

Braid Fraying During Strip-Back Reduces Shield Effectiveness Gradually
Workmanship

A few broken strands lost during a rushed strip-back won't cause an immediate fault, but shield effectiveness degrades incrementally as more strands are lost across repeated re-terminations at the same cable end; handle the braid carefully every time, not just when it matters most.

An Incomplete Heat-Shrink Seal on a Screen Termination Fails Slowly
Durability

Moisture wicking into a partially sealed screen termination corrodes the braid gradually rather than causing an immediate signal fault, so a termination can appear to work for a long time before failing; verify full, even shrinkage and a visible adhesive bead at every service.

Screened Cable Routing Near Power Circuits Still Needs Separation
Design

Even a well-terminated screen can't fully compensate for a signal cable routed in close, sustained parallel with a high-current power circuit; maintain designed separation distances during any re-routing rather than relying on the screen alone to reject interference.

Document Which End of a Screen Is the Designated Earth Point
Compliance

Because star-earthing requires exactly one bonding point per screen, clear documentation of which end is designated prevents a well-meaning technician at the other end from adding a second bond during unrelated work.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about the screen connector: what it is, how it works, specifications, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a screen connector?

A screen connector (also called a shield connector, screen termination or cable screen clamp) connects the metallic screen of a shielded AGL cable to earth, equipment chassis or a junction box earth terminal. It gives induced currents and electromagnetic interference a low-resistance, low-inductance path to earth.

Q2Why do shielded AGL cables need screen connectors?

Without a proper termination the screen can act as an antenna that radiates or picks up interference. Screen connectors protect the integrity of signal and power circuits where electromagnetic compatibility is critical, such as communication links, control circuits and sensor wiring.

Q3Where are screen connectors used?

On AGL control and monitoring cables between CCRs, ALCMS workstations and field equipment, on communication links, and on sensor cables, wherever shielded cable is used and the screen must be terminated.

Q4What types of screen connector are there?

Termination types are clamp-on, crimp-on, compression gland and solder. A clamp-on connector wraps a split ring around the screen and tightens on it without cutting the screen, while crimp-on lugs use a barrel crimped onto the screen.

Q5What are the typical specifications?

Contact resistance is typically 1 mΩ or less for clamp-on types and inductance 10 nH or less for short direct connections. Cable diameter compatibility is 5 mm to 50 mm and screen cross-section 6 mm² to 50 mm². Ratings are IP68 buried or IP65 above ground with an enclosure.

Q6Is a screen connector part of the series lighting circuit?

No. It is a bonding point from the cable screen to earth, not part of the series power loop, and the screen bond is kept electrically separate from the primary series conductors.

Q7Which standards apply to screen connectors?

The main references are IEC TR 61000-5-2 (EMC installation and mitigation), IEEE 1100, FAA AC 150/5345-7F (L-824 cable including screen termination) and BS EN 50289. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q8How often should a screen connector be inspected?

Typical annual checks cover contact resistance, corrosion, clamp or crimp torque, earth bond, seals, braid preparation and material compatibility, with a semi-annual pigtail length check. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9Why does interference persist despite a shielded cable?

Likely causes are a long or floating screen pigtail, poor or corroded screen termination, or multi-point earthing that creates ground loops. Check continuity from screen to earth, pigtail length and earthing arrangement.

Q10What are the common problems with screen connectors?

Common problems are corrosion of the screen and connector, high contact resistance, inadequate screen preparation, long screen pigtails, incorrect earth connection, mechanical damage to the screen and multiple screen connections.
