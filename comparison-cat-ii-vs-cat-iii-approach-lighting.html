Approach & Landing Systems Comparison

CAT II VS CAT III

Precision Approach Lighting for Progressively Lower-Visibility Operations — CAT III is not simply a brighter or longer version of CAT II. The approach lighting system is one part of a much broader low-visibility landing system that also includes runway lighting, navigation/landing equipment, monitoring, power supply, aircraft equipment and operational procedures.

02 · Definition

What Is CAT II?

Category II is a precision approach category intended for operations in conditions where the pilot may not obtain the required visual references until significantly closer to the runway. The FAA defines CAT II as a facility providing operation down to a 100 ft decision height, with RVR not less than 1,200 ft / 366 m.

The visual system needs to provide strong references for

  • Runway alignment
  • Centreline
  • Height perception
  • Roll guidance
  • Horizontal position
  • Threshold identification
  • Touchdown area
03 · Definition

What Is CAT III?

Category III is designed for operations in substantially lower visibility than CAT II. It is divided into different operational subcategories, and the exact requirements depend on the applicable regulatory framework and approval. The FAA identifies CAT III as a facility supporting operations with no decision-height limit and very low RVR conditions, subject to the applicable CAT III criteria.

This changes the role of the visual system: the pilot may have little or no external visual reference during the final stage of landing. CAT III therefore depends much more heavily on the integrity, monitoring and availability of the complete landing system.

04 · The Key Difference

Decision Height, Visibility & System Dependence

CAT II

Low visibility + decision height + enhanced visual reference.

CAT III

Very low visibility + highly reduced visual reference + highly reliable landing system.

As visibility decreases across CAT I → CAT II → CAT III, the dependence on instrument guidance, runway centreline lighting, touchdown zone lighting, approach lighting, monitoring, redundancy, power supply and system availability all increase.

05 · Configuration

CAT II/III Approach Lighting Configuration

Under ICAO terminology, a precision CAT II/III approach lighting system uses an extended centreline with side rows and crossbars, typically comprising:

  • White centreline lighting
  • Red side-row lighting
  • White crossbars
  • Sequenced flashing lights where applicable
  • Threshold lighting

ICAO material describes CAT II/III approach lighting as extending approximately 900 m from the threshold. For FAA ALSF-2, the approach lighting area typically spans 2,400–3,000 ft, depending on installation conditions.

06 · Common Misread

Does CAT III Use a Different ALS From CAT II?

Not necessarily. For the FAA configuration, both CAT II and CAT III visual guidance are typically provided by ALSF-2 — the FAA specifically describes ALSF-2 as serving Category II/III instrument approaches.

It is incorrect to build a simple comparison such as "CAT II = ALSF-2, CAT III = ALSF-3" — that is not correct FAA terminology. For an ICAO-based project, terminology and configuration should be taken from the applicable ICAO and national requirements.

08 · Runway Lighting

Runway Lighting Becomes Critical

Approach lighting alone cannot make an airport CAT II or CAT III capable — the runway lighting system also becomes critical. FAA guidance identifies runway centreline lights and touchdown zone lights as required for CAT II and CAT III runways.

Approach Area

  • High-intensity approach lighting
  • Centreline lights
  • Side-row lights
  • Crossbars
  • Sequenced flashers

Runway & Control

  • High-intensity runway edge lights
  • Runway centreline lights
  • Touchdown zone lights, threshold & runway end lights
  • CCRs, ALCMS & monitoring
  • Fault indication, automatic control & emergency power
09 · Touchdown Zone Lighting

CAT II vs CAT III: Touchdown Zone Lighting

The FAA describes touchdown zone lighting as two rows of transverse light bars positioned symmetrically about the runway centreline, extending from the threshold into the touchdown zone, to give the pilot a strong visual reference for the touchdown area.

CAT II

TDZ lighting provides an important visual reference during the final approach and landing.

CAT III

TDZ lighting remains important, but the overall CAT III system cannot depend solely on the pilot visually identifying the touchdown zone.

10 · Runway Centreline Lighting

Runway Centreline Lighting

Runway centreline lighting provides a longitudinal reference during final approach, flare, touchdown and rollout. The FAA states that runway centreline and touchdown zone lights are required for CAT II and CAT III runways.

For CAT III operations, centreline lighting becomes particularly important during rollout because external visual references can remain extremely limited.

11 · Electrical Engineering

Electrical Engineering Perspective

From an AGL engineering perspective, the difference becomes particularly important in system reliability and continuity.

Simplified architecture: Main power → emergency/alternate power → CCR → primary series circuit → isolation transformers → approach/runway fixtures → monitoring & control.

The system should be designed so that critical lighting functions remain available within the applicable transfer and serviceability requirements.

12 · Power Supply

Power Supply Reliability

CAT II and CAT III operations require much greater attention to power continuity, which may involve main power, alternate power, emergency power, automatic transfer, separate critical circuits, CCR redundancy, monitoring and control-system redundancy.

FAA AC 150/5340-30J identifies CAT II lighting facilities requiring rapid transfer arrangements — including CAT II high-intensity runway lighting, runway centreline lighting and touchdown zone lighting — among systems requiring particularly fast transfer. For CAT III, applicable operational approval and national requirements must be checked carefully, since availability and redundancy become even more critical.

13 · Monitoring

Monitoring Requirements

CAT II

Monitoring should provide adequate information about critical system status and faults, typically including:

  • CCR status
  • Approach & runway centreline circuit status
  • TDZ circuit status
  • Flasher & power status
  • Control-system faults

CAT III

Monitoring becomes even more critical — the system may need to identify failures rapidly enough to support the required operational decision-making and maintenance response. Exact serviceability and monitoring criteria should be taken from the applicable CAT III approval and governing standard.

14 · Photometry

Photometric & Functional Performance

Both CAT II and CAT III systems require appropriate photometric performance across approach lighting (intensity, beam distribution, colour, alignment, crossbar and centreline performance), runway lighting (edge, centreline, TDZ and threshold intensity), and functional performance (intensity steps, sequenced flashers, circuit switching, monitoring, control response).

Engineering point: A CAT II/III lighting system can be electrically healthy but still fail to provide the required visual performance because of incorrect aiming, damaged optics, contamination or photometric degradation.

16 · Why CAT III Is More Demanding

The Key Issue: Visual Reference Availability

CAT II

The pilot may still obtain the required visual reference before landing — instrument guidance + approach lighting + runway lighting + pilot visual reference.

CAT III

Depending on the operation, the pilot may have extremely limited external visual reference — highly reliable instrument/landing system + highly reliable runway lighting + monitoring + redundancy + very limited external visual reference.

Therefore, CAT III relies heavily on the integrity of the complete landing system, not just the pilot's own eyes.

17 · Common Misconceptions

Myths vs Facts

Myth: CAT III needs a longer approach lighting system than CAT II
Fact

Not necessarily — in FAA practice, ALSF-2 provides visual guidance for both CAT II and CAT III approaches.

Myth: CAT III means ALSF-3
Fact

This is not correct FAA terminology — FAA identifies ALSF-2 as serving CAT II/III.

Myth: CAT III is just a brighter CAT II
Fact

CAT III is fundamentally an operational and system-integrity requirement, not simply a lighting-intensity upgrade.

Myth: Approach lighting alone determines the approach category
Fact

The approach category depends on the complete approved system — navigation/landing equipment, runway lighting, monitoring, power, procedures and operational criteria.

Myth: CAT III means the pilot lands without any lighting
Fact

CAT III runways still have extensive lighting systems — the issue is that the pilot may have very limited visual reference during critical phases of the operation.

18 · Engineering Workflow

CAT II/III Approach-Lighting Project Workflow

Sequence: Define CAT II/CAT III operational requirement → identify governing standard → determine approach lighting configuration → design ALS circuits → design runway centreline lighting → design TDZ lighting → design runway edge & threshold lighting → CCR & series circuit design → emergency/alternate power design → control & monitoring design → installation → electrical testing → photometric testing → alignment verification → power transfer testing → integrated system testing → operational approval.

19 · Engineering Verdict

CAT II vs CAT III Approach Lighting

Engineering Verdict

CAT III is not simply a different approach-lighting fixture configuration from CAT II. In FAA practice, ALSF-2 serves CAT II/III approach visual guidance.

The major difference between CAT II and CAT III is the overall operational and system-integrity requirement, particularly as visibility becomes extremely low.

CAT IILower visibility + 100-ft-class decision height + enhanced visual references + highly reliable lighting.
CAT IIIVery low visibility + potentially no decision height + highly reliable landing system + extensive monitoring and redundancy.

Simple AGL memory rule — CAT II: strong visual guidance + low-visibility runway lighting. CAT III: maximum system integrity + very low-visibility operation + minimal dependence on external visual references.

20 · FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest difference between CAT II and CAT III?

CAT II still depends significantly on the pilot acquiring visual references at around 100 ft. CAT III is designed for operations where visual reference may be extremely limited or absent, so system integrity, monitoring, power continuity and redundancy become far more critical than the approach-lighting hardware itself.

Does CAT III always use a different approach lighting system from CAT II?

Not necessarily. In FAA practice, ALSF-2 serves both CAT II and CAT III visual guidance — it should not be assumed that CAT III automatically means "ALSF-3."

Is touchdown zone lighting mandatory for CAT III?

Yes, TDZ lighting remains required and important, though the CAT III system as a whole cannot rely solely on the pilot visually identifying the touchdown zone.

Does upgrading approach lighting alone make a runway CAT III capable?

No. CAT III capability depends on the complete approved low-visibility landing system — navigation/landing equipment, runway lighting, monitoring, power reliability, procedures and operational approval.

Why does CAT III need more redundancy than CAT II?

Because the pilot may have extremely limited or no external visual reference during the final phase of landing, so the electrical, monitoring and control systems must be far more reliable to compensate.

Does CAT III have a decision height?

Depending on the CAT III subcategory and applicable operational approval, CAT III operations may have no decision-height limit — this must be confirmed against the governing regulatory framework for the specific operation.

21 · Standards & Reference Basis

Governing Documents

For an actual project design, always verify the current edition of the applicable documents.

FAA AC 150/5340-30

Design and Installation Details for Airport Visual Aids — approach lighting systems, ALSF-2 configuration, installation, photometric requirements and electrical systems.

ICAO Annex 14, Volume I

Aerodrome Design and Operations — precision approach categories, decision height and RVR criteria, CAT II/III approach lighting configuration and operational requirements.

DGCA / AAI Requirements

For Indian airport projects, also verify applicable DGCA requirements, AAI requirements, airport-specific technical specifications, project drawings and contract specifications.

Final Engineering Note

Do not design a CAT II or CAT III system from a generic web diagram. The final installation drawing should always be developed from the current governing standard, approved aerodrome design, approach category and project-specific requirements.

CAT II and CAT III should be compared as complete low-visibility landing systems, not only as approach-lighting layouts. CAT II still depends significantly on the pilot acquiring visual references; CAT III is designed for operations where visual reference may be extremely limited, so system integrity, monitoring, power continuity and redundancy become increasingly critical.
