AGL-CS-020 — Different IR Results From Two Laboratories

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A diagnostic interpretation case where two independent laboratories produced conflicting IR results for the same AGL transformer — from test methodology review through time-dependent analysis to standardized procedures.

Case ID

AGL-CS-020

Category

Insulation Resistance Testing & Laboratory Diagnostics

Level

Expert

Case Type

Engineering Simulation

Primary Skill

Interpretation of conflicting laboratory insulation resistance results

Important: This is an engineering simulation created for training. It is not presented as an actual airport incident. All readings, values, dates and outcomes are illustrative, and no airport, organisation, person or manufacturer is identified or implied.

1Case Study Overview

An airport authority submitted a suspected defective AGL isolating transformer and associated cable sample to two independent electrical testing laboratories for insulation resistance evaluation.

The results were unexpectedly different.

  • Laboratory A: Greater than 1000 MΩ
  • Laboratory B: 95 MΩ

Both laboratories used calibrated insulation resistance testers, and both reports were technically valid.

The maintenance team questioned whether one laboratory had made an error or whether the equipment condition had changed.

A detailed investigation revealed that test methodology, temperature, humidity, stabilization time, and measurement procedures significantly influenced the reported IR values.

This case demonstrates that different IR results do not necessarily mean that one laboratory is incorrect.

2Learning Objectives

After completing this case study, the learner should be able to:

  • Understand why different laboratories may obtain different IR results.
  • Evaluate laboratory test methodology.
  • Interpret temperature and humidity effects.
  • Understand polarization and absorption behavior.
  • Compare short-duration and stabilized IR measurements.
  • Assess the reliability of laboratory reports.
  • Make engineering decisions using multiple test results.

3System Background

Insulation resistance testing evaluates the dielectric integrity of:

  • AGL primary cables.
  • Cable joints.
  • Isolating transformers.
  • Secondary circuits.
  • Insulation systems.

The measured value is affected by:

  • Test voltage.
  • Temperature.
  • Humidity.
  • Surface contamination.
  • Moisture content.
  • Test duration.
  • Instrument characteristics.
  • Sample conditioning.

Therefore, IR is not a perfectly fixed value.

4Problem Statement

The reported laboratory results were:

Laboratory A Greater than 1000 MΩ
Laboratory B 95 MΩ

Both reports indicated:

  • Calibrated instruments.
  • Standard test procedures.
  • No obvious measurement error.
Engineering Question

How can the same AGL equipment produce two substantially different insulation resistance values?

5Initial Observations

The equipment under investigation was:

  • One 6.6 A isolating transformer.
  • One associated cable section.
  • Removed from service after monsoon-related maintenance.

The laboratories tested the equipment one week apart.

6Initial Engineering Hypotheses

The maintenance team considered:

Hypothesis A

One laboratory instrument was inaccurate.

Hypothesis B

The equipment dried between tests.

Hypothesis C

Different test voltages were used.

Hypothesis D

Different test durations were used.

Hypothesis E

Temperature and humidity influenced the measurements.

7Review of Laboratory Reports

Laboratory A

  • Test voltage: 5 kV
  • Temperature: 32°C
  • Relative humidity: 38%
  • Stabilization time: 10 minutes
  • Sample dried before testing

Result: Greater than 1000 MΩ

Laboratory B

  • Test voltage: 5 kV
  • Temperature: 21°C
  • Relative humidity: 72%
  • Stabilization time: 1 minute
  • Sample tested immediately after unpacking

Result: 95 MΩ

8Why IR Values Can Differ

Insulation resistance is affected by several components:

Measured IR = True insulation resistance - Absorption effects - Polarization effects - Surface leakage - Environmental influence

A short-duration measurement may differ substantially from a stabilized measurement.

9Test Equipment

The investigation reviewed:

  • Laboratory calibration certificates.
  • Test procedures.
  • Environmental conditions.
  • Sample preparation records.
  • Test duration records.
  • Instrument specifications.

10Investigation

Step 1: Instrument Verification

Both laboratories had:

  • Valid calibration certificates.
  • Appropriate test voltage.
  • Acceptable instrument accuracy.

Instrument error was unlikely.

Step 2: Temperature Analysis

Insulation resistance generally changes with temperature.

The transformer tested at:

  • 21°C
  • 32°C

Temperature correction alone could not explain the entire difference.

Step 3: Humidity Analysis

Laboratory B tested the sample at 72% relative humidity.

Surface leakage was significantly higher.

Moisture on insulation surfaces reduced the measured IR.

Step 4: Stabilization Time

This became the most important finding.

Laboratory B recorded the value after 1 minute.

Laboratory A recorded the value after 10 minutes.

The transformer exhibited dielectric absorption and polarization.

The measured resistance increased significantly with time.

Step 5: Repeat Controlled Testing

The same transformer was retested under identical conditions.

Results:

30 seconds 82 MΩ
1 minute 95 MΩ
5 minutes 420 MΩ
10 minutes Greater than 1000 MΩ

The transformer was not changing rapidly. The measurement was time-dependent.

11Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The different laboratory results were caused primarily by:

  • Different stabilization times.
  • Different humidity conditions.
  • Different sample conditioning procedures.

Both laboratories produced technically valid measurements, but they were measuring different stages of insulation polarization behavior.

12Engineering Interpretation

Laboratory A represented:

  • Dried insulation.
  • Stabilized measurement.
  • Maximum insulation performance.

Laboratory B represented:

  • Field-like moisture condition.
  • Early measurement.
  • Initial leakage behavior.

For maintenance decision-making, both results were valuable.

13Corrective Action

The engineering team:

  1. Standardized the IR test procedure.
  2. Defined a minimum stabilization time.
  3. Required temperature recording.
  4. Required humidity recording.
  5. Implemented temperature correction where appropriate.
  6. Introduced PI (Polarization Index) testing.
  7. Established laboratory comparison procedures.
  8. Updated transformer acceptance criteria.
  9. Trained maintenance staff on interpreting time-based IR trends rather than single-point readings.

14Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about conflicting insulation resistance results between laboratories, based on this case study.

Q1What is AGL-CS-020?

AGL-CS-020 is an engineering training case study on why two independent laboratories produced conflicting insulation resistance results for the same AGL transformer and cable sample. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2What were the two laboratory results?

Laboratory A reported greater than 1000 MΩ and Laboratory B reported 95 MΩ. Both used calibrated insulation resistance testers, both reports were technically valid, and the tests were performed one week apart.

Q3Was one of the laboratories wrong?

No. Instrument error was unlikely, since both laboratories had valid calibration certificates, appropriate test voltage and acceptable accuracy. The results differed because of test conditions and methodology.

Q4What caused the different results?

The main causes were different stabilization times, different humidity conditions and different sample conditioning procedures. The laboratories were measuring different stages of insulation polarization behaviour.

Q5Why did stabilization time matter so much?

Laboratory B recorded its value after 1 minute and Laboratory A after 10 minutes. Because the transformer showed dielectric absorption and polarization, measured resistance increased significantly with time.

Q6Did temperature and humidity play a part?

Yes. Insulation resistance changes with temperature, and the samples were tested at 21°C and 32°C, but temperature correction alone could not explain the gap. Laboratory B tested at 72% relative humidity, where higher surface leakage reduced the measured IR.

Q7What corrective actions did the engineering team take?

They standardized the IR test procedure, defined a minimum stabilization time and required temperature and humidity recording. They also applied temperature correction where appropriate, introduced Polarization Index (PI) testing, set up laboratory comparison procedures, updated transformer acceptance criteria and trained staff to read time-based IR trends.

Q8What is the key lesson for maintenance decisions?

A conflicting insulation resistance reading is not automatically a faulty test. Check that stabilization time, temperature and humidity were controlled and recorded consistently, and read IR trends, including Polarization Index, rather than a single snapshot value.

Standardise Test Duration and Reading Time

Method

Different stabilisation times give different values. Define the time at which the reading is taken and record it.

Control Humidity and Sample Conditioning

Conditions

Moisture content changes insulation behaviour. Agree conditioning procedures before comparing laboratories.

Align the Laboratories Before Judging the Result

Coordination

Differences between labs usually come from method. Compare procedures and instruments first.

Report Method With the Number

Reporting

A figure without its method is hard to interpret. Include voltage, time, temperature and instrument details.

15Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

A conflicting insulation resistance reading is not automatically a faulty test. Before assuming an instrument or laboratory error, check whether stabilization time, temperature, and humidity were controlled and recorded consistently between the two measurements.

Standardizing test procedure and duration, and reading IR trends (including Polarization Index) rather than a single snapshot value, prevents unnecessary transformer replacement and improves confidence in insulation diagnostics.
