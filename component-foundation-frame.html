Foundation Frame

A rugged, welded steel frame that provides a stable, level, and robust foundation for heavy AGL equipment such as Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), distribution panels, and control cabinets. Designed to be bolted to a concrete plinth or slab, it ensures secure mounting, precise alignment, and long‑term durability in the harsh airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

A Foundation Frame (also known as an equipment base frame, mounting frame, or plinth frame) is a heavy‑duty, welded steel structure that provides a secure, level, and stable foundation for large AGL equipment such as Constant Current Regulators (CCRs), distribution panels, control cabinets, UPS units, and other electrical enclosures. It is designed to be bolted to a concrete plinth, slab, or pedestal, ensuring that the equipment is correctly positioned, aligned, and protected from ground moisture and vibration.

Key characteristics of foundation frames include:

  • Heavy‑Duty Construction: Made from high‑strength structural steel (typically channel, angle, or box section) with welded joints, designed to support the weight of heavy equipment (up to several tonnes).
  • Corrosion Protection: Hot‑dip galvanised or epoxy‑coated to resist corrosion from moisture, de‑icing chemicals, and fuels.
  • Precision Levelling: Includes adjustable levelling feet or grout cavities to allow precise levelling during installation, ensuring the equipment is perfectly horizontal.
  • Cable Management: Often designed with cable entry openings, conduit knockouts, or cable tray supports to facilitate neat and organised cable routing.

Foundation frames are used for the following equipment:

  • Constant Current Regulators (CCRs): Supporting the heavy transformers and electronics of CCRs (typically 10‑50 kVA).
  • Distribution Panels: Providing a base for main distribution panels, feeder panels, and branch circuit panels.
  • Control Cabinets: Supporting ALCMS control cabinets, PLC cabinets, and communication enclosures.
  • UPS Systems: Providing a foundation for uninterruptible power supply units and battery cabinets.
  • Generator Interface Panels: Supporting generator transfer switches and control panels.

The frame is typically installed on a concrete plinth or slab that is cast specifically for the equipment. The frame is bolted to the concrete using anchor bolts, and the equipment is then bolted to the frame using the equipment's mounting feet or base channels.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The foundation frame exists to protect the reliability of the electrical and control equipment that the entire airfield lighting circuit depends on. CCRs, distribution panels, and ALCMS control cabinets are safety-critical: if one fails or is knocked out of alignment, an entire circuit of runway or taxiway lights can go dark. By providing a rigid, level, corrosion-resistant base, the frame prevents the equipment from settling, vibrating loose, or suffering moisture ingress from the floor, and it keeps sensitive electronics and liquid-filled components (such as transformers) perfectly horizontal so they perform as designed. Its secondary function — organised cable entry and routing — reduces the risk of cable damage and keeps maintenance access clear, both of which shorten the time needed to diagnose and fix a fault. In short, the frame's purpose is to keep the equipment that powers and monitors the lighting system stable, protected, and serviceable.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Foundation frames are used in all airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters, and outdoor equipment pads where heavy AGL equipment is installed, including:

  • CCR Rooms: Supporting Constant Current Regulators in airfield electrical rooms or dedicated CCR shelters.
  • Distribution Centres: Providing foundations for main and sub‑distribution panels that power AGL circuits.
  • ALCMS Equipment Rooms: Supporting control cabinets, servers, and communication equipment for the Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System.
  • Generator Areas: Providing bases for generator control panels and transfer switches.
  • Outdoor Equipment Pads: Supporting outdoor enclosures for remote CCRs, UPS units, and distribution boxes.
  • Communication Hubs: Supporting fibre optic termination cabinets, communication gateways, and network switches.

The frame is designed to match the specific footprint of the equipment, with mounting holes aligned to the equipment's base channels. The frame is typically 100‑200mm high, providing clearance for cable entry from below or through the side. The frame is also designed to provide a uniform load distribution across the concrete plinth, preventing localised stress and cracking.

Placement

Installation & Location

The frame is set and levelled on a cured concrete plinth or floor slab, anchored with expansion or chemical anchor bolts sized to the equipment's total weight and any seismic or vibration requirements for the room. Levelling shims are used under the frame feet so the completed frame presents a flat, true surface for the equipment above it, since an unlevel base can strain cabinet doors, busbar connections, or optical alignment features on the equipment it supports. Cable entry cut-outs or knockouts in the frame are positioned to line up with the trench, duct bank, or floor penetration serving that room, and the frame is bonded to the room's earthing system so the equipment it carries has a continuous path to ground.

Function

How Does It Work?

The foundation frame functions as a rigid, load‑bearing interface between the AGL equipment and the concrete foundation. Its design ensures that the equipment is securely mounted, correctly levelled, and protected from environmental factors:

  • Load Distribution: The frame distributes the weight of the equipment evenly over the concrete plinth, preventing point loads that could cause cracking or spalling. The frame's structural members are designed to carry the full weight of the equipment without deflection.
  • Levelling: The frame incorporates adjustable levelling feet or is grouted in place to achieve a perfectly horizontal mounting surface. This is critical for equipment that contains sensitive electronics, liquid‑filled components, or moving parts.
  • Vibration Isolation: The frame provides a rigid, low‑vibration mounting platform, reducing the transmission of vibration from the equipment to the building structure and vice versa. For sensitive equipment, vibration‑damping pads can be added between the frame and the equipment.
  • Cable Management: The frame is designed with cable entry openings, conduit knockouts, or cable tray supports to allow cables to be routed neatly and safely to the equipment. This ensures that cables are protected from damage and that maintenance access is not obstructed.
  • Height Clearance: The frame elevates the equipment above the floor, providing clearance for cable entry from below, reducing the risk of water ingress, and facilitating cleaning and maintenance.

The installation process typically involves:

  • Plinth Preparation: A concrete plinth or slab is cast, with anchor bolts embedded in the correct positions.
  • Frame Positioning: The foundation frame is lifted into position and aligned with the anchor bolts.
  • Levelling: The frame is levelled using adjustable feet or by grouting the base. The top surface is checked with a precision level.
  • Securing: The frame is bolted to the concrete using the anchor bolts, torqued to the specified value.
  • Equipment Mounting: The equipment is lifted onto the frame and bolted to the frame's mounting holes using the equipment's base channels.
  • Grouting (if applicable): The area under the frame is grouted with a non‑shrink grout to provide full support and to fill any voids.

The foundation frame is designed to provide a service life of 30+ years, with regular inspection for corrosion, bolt tightness, and grout integrity.

Construction

Physical Construction

The frame is fabricated from welded structural steel — typically channel, angle, or box section — sized and cross-braced to carry the static weight of the mounted equipment plus wind and seismic loading without racking. Anchor points are pre-drilled or fitted with cast-in anchor bolts matching the concrete plinth's setout, and levelling pads or jacking bolts at each corner allow the frame to be shimmed true before final grouting. The complete weldment is hot-dip galvanised after fabrication for long-term corrosion resistance, since it typically sits in an outdoor or semi-exposed equipment yard. Equipment is bolted to mounting rails or a drilled top plate welded into the frame, with slotted holes on at least one axis to accommodate manufacturing tolerance in the equipment's own footprint, and cable entry is arranged through a cut-out or gland plate consistent with the duct route from the equipment room or pit below.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Concrete Plinth & Anchor Setout
Civil

Cast the concrete plinth with cast-in anchor bolts matching the frame's setout, or pre-drill for post-installed anchors, to design position and elevation.

Levelling Pad / Jacking Bolt Adjustment
Mechanical

Use the levelling pads or jacking bolts at each corner to shim the frame true before final grouting, confirming level and orientation on the plinth.

Grouting
Civil

Grout beneath the frame once levelled, confirming full, void-free bearing and allowing the grout to reach design cure strength before loading.

Anchor Bolt Torquing
Mechanical

Torque anchor bolts to the specified value in the correct sequence once the grout has cured, ensuring even load distribution across the frame base.

Earthing & Bonding
Electrical

Bond the frame to the site earth grid, confirming continuity resistance meets the design requirement before equipment is installed.

Cable Entry Coordination
Electrical

Align the cut-out or gland plate with the duct route from the equipment room or pit below, confirming cable entry is ready before equipment is bolted to the frame.

Pre-Loading Sign-off
Documentation

Record levelling, grout cure results, and anchor torque values as-built before equipment is loaded onto the frame.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Inaccurate Anchor Bolt Placement

If the anchor bolts are not positioned accurately during the concrete pour, the frame may not align with the bolts. Use a template or jig to position the bolts precisely. If misalignment occurs, slotted holes in the frame can provide some adjustment (typically ±10mm).

Inadequate Levelling

If the frame is not levelled correctly, the equipment will be tilted, which can affect the operation of transformers (oil level), electronics, and moving parts. Use a precision level and grout under the frame to ensure full support and correct levelling.

Corrosion of Frame and Bolts

Foundation frames are often located in equipment rooms or outdoor pads where moisture and chemicals can cause corrosion. Use hot‑dip galvanised or epoxy‑coated frames and stainless steel bolts. Regularly inspect and touch up damaged coatings.

Insufficient Grout Support

If the frame is not fully grouted under the base, voids can cause stress concentrations, leading to cracking or settling. Use non‑shrink grout and ensure it is fully injected to fill all voids under the frame.

Inadequate Cable Entry Clearance

If the cable entry openings are not aligned with the equipment's cable entry points, cable routing may be difficult or may require tight bends. Coordinate the frame design with the equipment manufacturer's cable entry locations.

Loose Equipment Mounting Bolts

Vibration from the equipment (e.g., transformer hum) can cause the mounting bolts to loosen over time. Use lock washers, thread‑locking compound, and schedule regular bolt torque checks as part of the maintenance program.

Incorrect Frame Height

If the frame is too short, cable entry from below may be difficult or the equipment may be too close to the floor. If the frame is too tall, it may be unstable. Select the frame height based on the cable entry requirements and the equipment's centre of gravity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Equipment Tilted or Out of Level
Likely Cause
Frame not levelled correctly during installation, or settling of the pad/foundation over time, sometimes worsened by voids left under an incompletely grouted base.
Diagnostic Steps
Check frame level with a precision level in both axes; inspect under the frame base for grout voids or signs of settlement; check equipment-specific level indicators (e.g. transformer oil level sight glass).
Corrective Action
Re-shim and re-level the frame, inject non-shrink grout to fill any voids under the base, and re-verify level before returning equipment to service.
Frame Doesn't Line Up with Anchor Bolts
Likely Cause
Anchor bolts positioned inaccurately during the concrete pour, exceeding the frame's slotted-hole adjustment allowance (typically ±10mm).
Diagnostic Steps
Measure the actual anchor bolt spacing against the frame's bolt pattern and slotted-hole range; check whether the mismatch exceeds the adjustment available in the slots.
Corrective Action
If within tolerance, use the slotted holes to adjust and secure the frame; if the mismatch exceeds adjustment range, core-drill new anchor points or install a template-guided epoxy anchor set.
Visible Corrosion on Frame or Fasteners
Likely Cause
Damaged or degraded galvanised/epoxy coating exposing bare metal, common in equipment rooms or outdoor pads with moisture or chemical exposure.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coating for chips, scratches, or peeling; check fasteners for pitting or rust bleed; identify the source of moisture or chemical exposure if corrosion is progressing quickly.
Corrective Action
Clean and touch up damaged coating areas, replace corroded fasteners with stainless steel, and address the moisture/chemical source if practical to slow further degradation.
Mounting Bolts Loosening Over Time
Likely Cause
Vibration from the mounted equipment (e.g. transformer hum) gradually backing off fasteners not fitted with locking hardware.
Diagnostic Steps
Check bolt torque against the specified value at each mounting point; look for witness marks indicating prior movement; confirm whether lock washers or thread-locking compound were used originally.
Corrective Action
Retorque all mounting bolts, fit lock washers or thread-locking compound if not already present, and add bolt torque checks to the regular maintenance schedule.

Medium Severity
Root Cause
Frame not levelled correctly during installation, or settling of the pad over time, often worsened by voids left under an incompletely grouted base.

Bolt torque check at each mounting point, and inspection for witness marks indicating prior movement.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Suspended Load / Rigging Hazard
Personnel Safety

Use rated lifting equipment and correct rigging when placing the frame or lowering heavy equipment (CCRs, panels, UPS units) onto it, and keep personnel clear of the load path until it is safely landed and secured.

Frame Must Be Fully Secured Before Loading
Mechanical

Do not mount or energise equipment on the frame until it is fully bolted to its concrete plinth or slab and confirmed level and stable; a frame that is only partially anchored can shift or tip once equipment weight is added.

Bonding and Earthing Continuity
Electrical Safety

Verify continuity across the frame's earthing connection once equipment is mounted, since the frame often forms part of the equipment's bonding path to the site earthing system.

Anchor Bolt Torque and Load Rating
Mechanical

Torque anchor bolts to the manufacturer's specification and confirm the plinth's load rating matches the frame and equipment combination; an under-rated foundation can settle or crack under the combined static and wind/seismic load.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep fasteners, lifting hardware, and packaging accounted for at all times during installation, particularly where the frame is sited within or near the movement area.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the frame is level, fully anchored, and bonded before energising or bringing into service any equipment mounted on it.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Template the Anchor Bolts Before the Pour, Not After
Civil

Anchor bolt misalignment is far cheaper to prevent than to correct: a proper setting template locks bolt positions accurately during the concrete pour, while relying on slotted-hole adjustment afterward only accommodates minor error and can leave bolts loaded off-centre in a way that isn't obvious until the frame is under full equipment weight.

Grout Voids Are a Delayed-Onset Failure
Structural

A frame that sits level and passes inspection immediately after grouting can still have unfilled voids underneath that only reveal themselves as cracking or settling months later, once vibration and thermal cycling find the unsupported area. Verify full grout injection with a tap test or inspection ports at commissioning, since visual levelness alone doesn't confirm complete bearing support.

Re-Torque After the First Thermal Cycle, Not Just at Install
Mechanical

Anchor and mounting bolts torqued correctly during installation can lose preload as the steel frame and concrete settle and thermally cycle through their first season. Schedule a re-torque check after the frame has been through at least one full seasonal cycle, in addition to the standard annual check, rather than treating the initial torque as permanent.

Frame Height Trades Off Cable Routing Against Stability
Design

Specifying frame height purely to simplify below-frame cable entry can push the equipment's centre of gravity high enough to compromise stability under wind or vibration, especially for tall enclosures. Balance cable entry convenience against the equipment's actual centre-of-gravity height when selecting frame height, rather than optimising for one factor alone.

Coating Damage During Installation is the Most Common Corrosion Start Point
Corrosion

Galvanised or epoxy-coated frames most often begin corroding not from general weathering but from a scratch or weld repair made on-site during installation that was never touched up. Inspect and touch up any coating damage introduced during handling and installation before the frame is grouted and becomes difficult to access.

Confirm Bonding Continuity After, Not Just Before, Equipment Installation
Electrical

Earthing and bonding continuity verified on the bare frame can be compromised once equipment is mounted, if a coated or painted mounting surface prevents true metal-to-metal contact at the bonding point. Re-verify continuity after the equipment is fully installed and bonded, not only as a standalone frame test before equipment arrives.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about foundation frames for AGL equipment: materials, load capacity, installation, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a foundation frame?

A foundation frame, also called an equipment base frame, mounting frame or plinth frame, is a heavy-duty welded steel structure that gives large AGL equipment such as CCRs, distribution panels and control cabinets a secure, level and stable base.

Q2Why is a foundation frame important?

CCRs, distribution panels and ALCMS cabinets are safety-critical, so the equipment must not be knocked out of position or damaged by movement. The frame protects the reliability of the electrical and control equipment the airfield lighting circuit depends on.

Q3Where are foundation frames used?

In airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters and outdoor equipment pads, including CCR rooms.

Q4How does a foundation frame work?

It is a rigid, load-bearing interface between the equipment and the concrete foundation. It distributes load, holds the equipment level and secure, and lifts it off the floor to protect it from environmental factors.

Q5What materials and sizes are typical?

Structural steel (S235JR or S355JR), hot-dip galvanised to a minimum of 85 µm or epoxy-coated, in channel, angle or box-section form. Heights are typically 100-200 mm and lengths and widths are custom to the equipment footprint.

Q6What load can a foundation frame carry?

Typically 2,000-10,000 kg depending on frame size and steel section, with a flatness tolerance of 1 mm or less over the frame area after levelling.

Q7How is a foundation frame levelled and anchored?

Using adjustable levelling feet (M16-M24) or grout-in-place, with anchor bolts into the concrete. Slot or drilled holes (M12-M24) match the equipment base channels, and cable entry cut-outs of 50-150 mm allow conduit entry.

Q8Which standards apply?

EN 10025 / ASTM A36 (structural steel), ASTM A123 (hot-dip galvanising), AISC 360 / EN 1993-1-1 (structural steel design) and ACI 318 Ch.17 / EN 1992-4 (anchorage to concrete). FAA AC 150/5345-series documents apply to certified fixtures, not generic equipment-room frames.

Q9How often should a foundation frame be inspected?

Semi-annually for levelling; annually for anchor bolt and frame corrosion, grout condition, mounting bolt torque and cable entry clearance.

Q10Why is equipment tilted or out of level?

The frame may not have been levelled correctly at installation, or the pad may have settled, sometimes worsened by voids under an incompletely grouted base.
