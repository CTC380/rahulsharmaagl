How a Single Light Bulb Impacts the Safety of a 300-Ton Aircraft Landing in the Fog

A passenger aircraft weighing more than 300 tons can land safely in near-zero visibility conditions.

Not because the pilot can see the runway clearly.

But because a carefully designed Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) system guides the aircraft during the most critical seconds of flight.

And sometimes, the reliability of that entire operation depends on a single light fitting working correctly.

Key Takeaway

01

Every Light Has a Specific Purpose

Every airfield light fitting has a defined operational function:

  • Runway edge lights define the runway boundaries
  • Runway centerline lights guide directional alignment
  • Touchdown zone lights help identify the landing area
  • Approach lights provide the transition from instrument flight to visual landing
  • Threshold lights identify the beginning of the runway

When visibility drops, these lights become the pilot's external reference system.

02

What Happens When One Light Fails?

At first glance, one failed light may appear to be a minor issue, and standards do tolerate isolated failures within defined serviceability limits. However, during a foggy approach, failed fittings, especially clustered ones or those in critical positions, can create:

  • Visual gaps
  • False perception of alignment
  • Reduced depth judgment
  • Confusion during flare or rollout
  • Increased pilot workload

A pilot approaching through dense fog does not see the entire runway at once. The runway gradually appears from darkness. The first few lights seen by the pilot are extremely important because they confirm:

Runway PositionWhere the runway actually is
Aircraft AlignmentOn centreline or off track
Descent ContinuityVisual picture matches a stable approach, with PAPI/ILS
Runway OrientationHeading relative to the runway
Remaining DistanceHow much runway is left
03

Why Lighting Standards Are So Strict

This is why ICAO and FAA lighting standards are extremely strict about:

  • Light intensity
  • Colour accuracy
  • Beam spread
  • Photometric performance
  • Circuit redundancy (for example interleaved circuits and secondary power supply)
  • Fixture spacing

Even a small intensity deviation can affect visibility range in fog conditions.

04

A Light Can Be "ON" but Still Be Unsafe

A light that appears to be working from the ground may still fail to provide correct visual guidance to pilots if:

Incorrect beam angle
The light points somewhere other than where the pilot needs it.
Below-standard intensity
Reduces the distance at which the pilot can actually see it.
Lens contamination
Dirt, dust, or grime cuts down the light output that reaches the cockpit.
Disturbed photometric alignment
Installation drift shifts the beam away from its designed aim point.

That is why photometric testing is critical in AGL systems. Visual inspection alone cannot verify whether the pilot is receiving the correct optical guidance.

05

The Electrical Safety Chain Behind Every Light

Behind every airfield light, there is an entire electrical safety chain working continuously. This includes:

01CCRConstant Current Regulators
02Isolating TransformersPer-fitting isolation
03Series CircuitsUniform current delivery
04MonitoringALCMS control & fault monitoring

A secondary power supply with fast switch-over also protects the chain against mains failure. The goal is simple: the pilot must never lose essential visual guidance during landing.

06

Why This Matters to Passengers

Passengers may never notice runway lights during a flight. But during low-visibility operations, those lights become one of the most important safety systems at the airport.

A single fitting may look small from the ground. But for a pilot landing a heavy aircraft through fog at high speed, that one light can be the difference between uncertainty and safe visual guidance. That is the engineering responsibility behind every airfield light installed on a runway.

Key Takeaways

  • After decision height in fog, pilots rely on the first few lights they see to confirm position, alignment, and remaining distance
  • Failed fittings, especially clustered or in critical positions, can create visual gaps and false perception of alignment during the most critical seconds of approach
  • ICAO and FAA standards exist because small deviations in intensity or beam spread materially change visibility range in fog
  • A light can be electrically "ON" and still be optically unsafe — only photometric testing confirms correct guidance
  • CCRs, isolating transformers, and series circuits form a continuous electrical chain that must never let guidance drop

Conclusion

A 300-ton aircraft landing safely in fog is not a matter of luck — it is the outcome of precisely designed lighting, verified photometric performance, and a continuous electrical chain that never lets the pilot lose guidance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does a single runway light matter when landing in fog?

In fog, pilots rely on visual references from runway lighting after reaching decision height, and the runway gradually appears from darkness. The first few lights seen confirm runway position, aircraft alignment, descent continuity, runway orientation and remaining distance, so a single fitting can be the difference between uncertainty and safe visual guidance.

What happens when one airfield light fails?

Standards tolerate isolated failures within defined serviceability limits, but failed fittings, especially clustered ones or those in critical positions, can create visual gaps, false perception of alignment, reduced depth judgment, confusion during flare or rollout and increased pilot workload.

What function do different airfield lights serve?

Runway edge lights define the runway boundaries, centreline lights guide directional alignment, touchdown zone lights help identify the landing area, approach lights provide the transition from instrument flight to visual landing and threshold lights identify the beginning of the runway. When visibility drops they become the pilot's external reference system.

Why are lighting standards so strict?

ICAO and FAA standards are strict about light intensity, colour accuracy, beam spread, photometric performance, circuit redundancy (for example interleaved circuits and secondary power supply) and fixture spacing. Even a small intensity deviation can affect visibility range in fog conditions.

Can a light be on and still be unsafe?

Yes. A light that appears to work from the ground may still give incorrect guidance if its beam angle is wrong, its intensity is below standard, its lens is contaminated or its photometric alignment has drifted. Visual inspection alone cannot verify this, which is why photometric testing is critical in AGL systems.

What electrical safety chain supports every airfield light?

The chain includes Constant Current Regulators, isolating transformers providing per-fitting isolation, series circuits delivering uniform current and ALCMS control and fault monitoring. A secondary power supply with fast switch-over also protects the chain against mains failure, so the pilot never loses essential visual guidance during landing.

Do lights still matter in CAT III automatic landings?

Yes. In CAT III operations the aircraft may land automatically with no decision height, but the lights remain critical for the final approach, rollout and taxi guidance.
