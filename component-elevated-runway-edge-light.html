Elevated Runway Edge Light

The most common runway edge light configuration, mounted on frangible poles or base plates above the runway shoulder. Provides high visibility and precise beam control for all weather operations, with a breakaway design to protect aircraft on impact.

Overview

What Is It?

An Elevated Runway Edge Light is a visual aid mounted on a small pole or pedestal above the runway surface (typically 300–500 mm high). It is the standard configuration for runways where the shoulder is soft (grass or gravel) or where a raised light offers the best optical performance. The fixture includes a frangible coupling that shears on impact, reducing the risk of damage to an aircraft that veers off the runway. Elevated lights are used on both precision and non-precision runways, with white or yellow (last 600 m / 2,000 ft) beam outputs.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The elevated configuration exists because raising the light head above grade generally gives the best possible optical performance for edge delineation, projecting a cleaner, more visible beam to an approaching or departing pilot than a flush fixture can achieve, and it is the default choice wherever the shoulder surface allows it. This benefit only holds where an aircraft veering off the paved surface won't be put at greater risk by striking the fixture, which is why the elevated light is fitted with a frangible coupling engineered to shear at a controlled, predetermined force: the design deliberately trades fixture survivability for aircraft safety in an off-runway excursion. Consequently, the elevated light's role is to be used wherever soft shoulders (grass, gravel) or unconstrained ground clearance make its superior optical output and frangible safety behaviour the right combination, reserving the inset alternative for surfaces where an elevated stem would itself be the hazard.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Installed on the runway shoulders, typically 1–3 metres outside the paved edge, at regular intervals (60 m / 200 ft for CAT I and above, up to 100 m / 300 ft for non-precision). Used on all airfields with grass, gravel, or compacted soil shoulders where an inset fixture is not feasible. Also common on taxiway edges and as standalone approach lighting support structures.

Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are set out along both runway edges at the design spacing, positioned on the shoulder just outside the paved surface and clear of the graded runway strip's load-bearing requirements. Each stem mounts on a frangible coupling atop an L-867 base can set flush with grade, with overall fitting height kept within the obstacle-limitation surface for the runway category so the raised head never intrudes on the clearance envelope. The base can is fed by armoured cable run in a trench back to the isolating transformer pit, with the transformer housed in its own secondary base can adjacent to the fixture to keep the series-circuit joint clear of standing water. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline survey so the edge line stays parallel and true along the full runway length, and elevated fixtures are typically substituted with inset units within the runway end safety area, blast pads, and other zones where a raised obstacle cannot be tolerated.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each elevated light is powered from the series circuit via an isolating transformer buried near the fixture. The lamp or LED module projects light through a precision prism and lens assembly to create the required beam pattern (symmetric or asymmetric, depending on installation orientation). The intensity is controlled by the CCR's current steps. The frangible coupling is designed to break at a predetermined shear force (typically 250–450 kg), allowing the light head to detach cleanly without causing significant damage to an aircraft's wing or engine.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Elevated runway edge lights are not individually addressable — control is exercised at the loop level through the constant current series circuit. An Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS), operated by the tower or an automated visibility-linked routine, selects the required brightness step based on prevailing RVR, ATC instruction, or (at smaller aerodromes) pilot-controlled lighting input. This command is passed to the CCR, which regulates the entire loop to the corresponding fixed current (one of 5 steps between 2.8A and 6.6A). Every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, draws from the same current and therefore burns at the same intensity simultaneously — there is no way to dim or brighten a single fixture independently of the rest of the circuit. Fault detection is likewise loop-based: the CCR continuously monitors loop voltage against the commanded current, and a voltage rise at constant current signals increased circuit impedance, typically an open-circuit lamp or a failed isolating transformer, which is reported to the ALCMS as a loop fault rather than pinpointed to an exact fixture without further sectionalised testing. Because the elevated fitting sits on a frangible coupling, a sheared coupling looks electrically identical to any other open-circuit fault on the loop — the ALCMS reports it the same way, and only a field inspection confirms whether the cause was impact damage or a failed component.

ALCMS → CCR → Series Circuit Loop → Isolating Transformer → Elevated Fixture (with loop-level feedback)

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture head is a die-cast aluminium alloy housing, anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, mounted atop a short stem or pedestal (typically 300–500 mm) that raises the optics clear of grass or gravel shoulders for a cleaner beam path. The optical assembly sits under a borosilicate glass or high-strength polycarbonate prism, gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring or silicone seal for an IP67/IP68 rating against rain, dust, and fuel exposure. The stem attaches to the base through a frangible coupling, an aluminium or composite fitting engineered to shear cleanly at a defined load if struck by an aircraft, wheel, or ground vehicle, minimising damage to both the aircraft and the fixture's electrical connections below the break point. The coupling in turn seats on an L-867 base can set flush with the surrounding grade. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors, sealed for direct burial and continuous immersion, mating to the isolating transformer's secondary in an adjacent base can, with stainless-steel fasteners used throughout and a drainage provision in the base can to keep the connector joint clear of standing water.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Frangible Coupling Fatigue

Repeated snowplow impacts or vibration from nearby aircraft can weaken the frangible coupling. It is recommended to replace couplings every 5 years or after any significant impact event to ensure breakaway performance.

Prism / Lens Contamination

Elevated fixtures are exposed to dust, mud, and bird droppings. Annual cleaning with approved optics-safe solutions is necessary to maintain photometric performance and avoid intensity degradation.

Moisture Ingress via Cable Gland

Water ingress through the base cable gland is a common failure point. Periodic inspection of gland seals and re-torquing of the base connection can prevent internal corrosion and short circuits.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Circuit Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module/lamp, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary link or failed secondary winding) inside the pole base.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity is unbroken through the transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the base connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the base cable gland before closing up the pole.
Entire Series Circuit Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault in the series loop, often at a sheared frangible coupling that has also parted the internal cable connection.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; walk the row for any leaning, missing, or knocked-over fixtures; loop-resistance test to isolate the faulted segment.
Corrective Action
Replace the sheared coupling and reconnect the internal cable, or repair the faulted cable section; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress through a worn cable gland or base seal has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or partial ground fault.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for a loose gland, cracked base seal, or condensation inside the pole.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture, renew the gland seal and re-torque the base connection, and confirm insulation resistance is back above minimum before re-energising.
Reduced or Uneven Light Output
Likely Cause
Prism contaminated with dust, mud, or bird droppings; an ageing LED array; or the fixture aim drifted off tolerance.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds; inspect and clean the prism with an optics-safe solution; verify beam aim against the approach/runway path.
Corrective Action
Clean the prism, replace a degraded LED module, or re-aim the fixture; re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Fixture Leaning, Loose, or Height Out of Tolerance
Likely Cause
Frangible coupling has begun to fatigue or partially shear, or the pole/pedestal mounting has loosened or settled in the surrounding ground.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the coupling for cracking, corrosion, or play; measure mounting height against the 300–500 mm design range; inspect the base for settlement or corrosion.
Corrective Action
Replace the coupling if fatigued, re-level and re-set the fixture to correct height, and address any base or subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Coupling Shears Below Expected Impact Force
Likely Cause
Coupling material has become brittle from age, weather exposure, or accumulated micro-fractures from repeated low-level impacts, dropping breakaway force below the 250–450 kg design range.
Diagnostic Steps
Examine the sheared coupling face for corrosion or fatigue striations; review the fixture's service history and last replacement date; check nearby fixtures for similar ageing.
Corrective Action
Fit a new coupling to design spec, and bring forward the replacement interval for other couplings from the same batch or installation date.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Module / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from thermal cycling, moisture ingress at the LED board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single fixture goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the series circuit continues to operate normally.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the base connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress into the pole base leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing winding defect, or a blown primary link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop, darkening the single fixture or, if the open extends further, taking down the entire circuit.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
High Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic loading from vehicle or plough contact, material fatigue over the service interval, or corrosion at the coupling threads.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or shears off entirely, often parting the internal cable connection and taking down the full series loop.
Detection Method
Physical inspection of the coupling for cracking, corrosion, or play, and a walk of the row to spot any leaning or knocked-over fixtures.
Premature Coupling Shear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Coupling material has become brittle from age, weather exposure, or accumulated micro-fractures, dropping the breakaway force below the 250–450 kg design range.
Effect
Coupling shears under lighter-than-intended contact, causing nuisance outages and unplanned fixture replacement.
Detection Method
Examination of sheared coupling faces for corrosion or fatigue striations, and a service-history review of the coupling's batch and last replacement date.
Base Seal / Cable Gland Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A worn or loosened cable gland, or a cracked base seal, that lets moisture track into the pole from ground level.
Effect
Degraded insulation resistance producing a short or partial ground fault, and internal corrosion of the base connection.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger testing to identify the low-resistance segment, and a visual check of the gland and base seal at suspect fixtures.
Prism Contamination or Aim Drift
Low Severity
Root Cause
Dust, mud, or bird droppings accumulating on the prism, or gradual aim drift from vibration or minor impacts.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output and a beam pattern that can fall out of alignment with the approach/runway path.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity testing, visual prism inspection, and a beam-aim check against the design path during scheduled maintenance.
Pole / Pedestal Mounting Settlement
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Loosened mounting hardware, or the surrounding ground settling due to poor sub-grade compaction, frost heave, or drainage failure.
Effect
Fixture height drifts out of the 300–500 mm design range, or the pole leans, both of which push the beam out of tolerance.
Detection Method
Mounting-height measurement against design range, and a visual check of the base and surrounding ground for settlement.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on healthy circuits.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Elevated edge lights sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling connectors.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near an active runway requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance, with vehicle and personnel movement kept clear of the runway strip.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage to an aircraft that veers off the runway.

Hot Lamp / Housing Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the fixture housing and lamp/LED module to cool before handling after an active period; incandescent units retain significant heat and can cause burns during lens or lamp replacement.

Mower & Ground-Vehicle Strike
Operational

Because the fixture stands 300–500 mm proud of grade, brief mowing and snow-clearance crews on its location and keep the pole height within the design limit so it stays visible and shears rather than becomes a hidden strike hazard.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture out of aim tolerance, leaning, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; verify photometric and positional conformance before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
LED Retrofit Step Matching
Retrofit

When retrofitting an LED head onto an existing elevated stem, check that the fixture's brightness-step curve actually tracks the CCR's current steps the way the original incandescent lamp did; a mismatched curve leaves the edge line looking uneven between steps even though every fixture measures within spec at each step individually.

Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED head is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual LED load rather than assuming the existing transformer is close enough.

Frangible Couplings Age, Even Without Impact
Mechanical

UV and thermal cycling can shift the shear characteristics of a composite or aluminium frangible coupling over years of service. A coupling that looks visually fine can no longer shear at its rated load, so periodic torque/shear verification belongs in the overhaul cycle, not just the post-impact inspection.

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven edge-light brightness runway-to-runway even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

A Sheared Coupling Reads the Same as Any Other Open Circuit
Troubleshooting

Because the elevated fitting sits on a frangible coupling, a sheared coupling looks electrically identical to any other open-circuit fault to the CCR and ALCMS — both report as a loop voltage rise at constant current. Don't assume impact damage or a failed lamp from the control-room alert alone; a field inspection is what actually distinguishes the two.

De-Icing Fluid Ages Seals Faster Than UV Alone
Durability

Repeated exposure to glycol-based de-icing fluid degrades gaskets and lens seals faster than sun and weather alone, especially on stems near taxiway/runway intersections that see heavy de-icing traffic. Specify seal materials rated for glycol contact and inspect gasket condition at overhaul, not only at initial install.
