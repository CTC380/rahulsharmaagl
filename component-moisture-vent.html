Moisture Vent

A critical environmental control device installed in AGL fixture housings that allows moisture to escape, preventing condensation and ensuring optical clarity. The moisture vent works passively to maintain a dry internal environment, protecting optical components and electronics from corrosion and fogging.

Overview

What Is It?

A Moisture Vent (also called a breather vent or desiccant vent) is a passive component installed in sealed AGL enclosures to allow the escape of internal moisture while preventing liquid water ingress. Unlike a pressure valve, which primarily equalises pressure, a moisture vent focuses on removing water vapour that has entered the fixture (e.g., during maintenance or through imperfect seals) or has condensed from temperature cycling. The vent typically incorporates a hydrophobic membrane that allows water vapour (gas) to pass but blocks liquid water. Some vents also include a desiccant material to absorb residual moisture. By preventing condensation on lenses, prisms, and internal optics, the moisture vent ensures that the light output and beam pattern remain unaffected by fogging or water droplets, which is critical for maintaining photometric performance and meeting ICAO/FAA specifications.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The moisture vent exists because even a well-sealed AGL fixture will eventually accumulate internal water vapour — from temperature cycling that draws in humid air, or from tiny imperfections in seals over years of service — and if that vapour has nowhere to go, it condenses on the internal optics exactly where it will do the most damage to the fixture's light output. Left unaddressed, that fogging or droplet formation distorts the beam pattern in a way that's invisible from the ground but very real to an approaching pilot relying on that light.

By letting water vapour escape while still blocking liquid water intrusion, the vent solves this without compromising the fixture's overall environmental sealing — a passive, low-maintenance way to protect the photometric performance that ICAO and FAA certification depends on, without requiring active drying equipment or frequent internal inspection.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Moisture vents are used in all sealed AGL fixtures and enclosures where condensation could compromise performance. Key applications include:

  • Runway & Taxiway Light Fixtures: Inset and elevated lights with sealed optical chambers.
  • Threshold & End Light Housings: Where lens clarity is vital for approach guidance.
  • Approach Lighting Fixtures: Barrettes, ALS units, and strobe housings.
  • PAPI & VASI Optical Units: Precision visual glide slope indicators where condensation would cause false indications.
  • Obstruction Light Housings: Sealed beacons and LED units.
  • Isolating Transformer Housings: To prevent internal corrosion and insulation degradation.
  • Junction Boxes & Control Panels: To protect terminals and electronic components from moisture.
Placement

Installation & Location

The vent fits into a threaded or gasketed port in the fixture housing wall, sized and positioned so it sits above any water ingress point but low enough that the membrane doesn't block the optical path or interfere with the lamp/LED assembly. Orientation matters more than for most fixture hardware: the vent is fitted facing downward or to the side rather than directly upward wherever the fixture geometry allows, so standing water on top of the housing cannot pool against the membrane. Because the vent is a passive, unwired component, no cabling is involved; installation is limited to correct torque on the threaded fitting and a check that the surrounding gasket seats fully so the vent doesn't become an alternate leak path.

Function

How Does It Work?

The moisture vent works on the principle of vapour pressure differential and membrane technology:

  • Hydrophobic Membrane: A microporous PTFE (or similar) membrane allows water vapour molecules to pass through while blocking liquid water due to surface tension and pore size.
  • Vapour Pressure Gradient: When internal humidity exceeds external humidity, water vapour migrates outward through the membrane, reducing internal moisture content.
  • Condensation Prevention: By keeping internal humidity below the dew point (relative to the internal temperature), the vent prevents condensation from forming on cool surfaces like lenses and prisms.
  • Desiccant Option: Some vents incorporate a desiccant (e.g., silica gel) that actively absorbs moisture, providing an additional layer of protection.
  • IP Rating Maintenance: The vent is designed to maintain the enclosure's IP67/IP68 rating, allowing vapour escape without compromising watertight integrity.

The vent is typically installed in a threaded port on the fixture housing, often at the highest point to allow moisture vapour to rise and escape. It is a passive, maintenance‑free device, though some designs include a replaceable desiccant cartridge.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Unlike the networked components elsewhere in the AGL system, the moisture vent has no electronic control logic of its own — it is a passive part of a fixture or enclosure's mechanical architecture rather than a node on the ALCMS network. Its place in the system is defined by where it sits in the sealed housing: it forms one boundary of the enclosure, alongside the gasket, cable glands, and (in a fixture) the optical window, and it is the only one of those boundaries deliberately designed to let vapour pass while blocking liquid water and dust. When the enclosure heats and cools through its duty cycle, internal air expands and contracts; the vent equalises that pressure difference through the enclosure wall so the seals elsewhere aren't forced to do it, and it lets any moisture that does condense inside migrate back out as vapour. There is no signal or status associated with it — its condition (clear membrane versus clogged or degraded) is checked only through physical or visual inspection during maintenance, not through the monitoring architecture that covers the electronic components in the same enclosure.

Construction

Physical Construction

The vent body is machined from stainless steel (316L), nickel-plated brass, or UV-stabilised polyamide, threaded to match the fixture's mounting port (metric or NPT) and fitted with an O-ring or bonded gasket at the seating face to maintain the enclosure's IP67/IP68 rating once installed. The hydrophobic PTFE membrane is heat-staked or ultrasonically welded across the internal vent channel, backed by a perforated support disc that protects the membrane from mechanical damage during installation while still allowing free vapour passage. Where a desiccant cartridge is included, it sits in a small chamber behind the membrane, accessible for replacement on models designed for periodic servicing, and sealed on non-serviceable models. The external face includes a wrench-flat or knurled grip for hand-tightening into the housing, and the thread sealing relies on either a captive gasket or PTFE tape applied during installation rather than adhesive, so the vent can be removed and replaced without damaging the enclosure port.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Membrane Clogging with Contaminants

In dusty or industrial environments, the hydrophobic membrane can become clogged with dust, salt, or oil aerosols, reducing vapour transmission. This can lead to moisture build‑up and condensation. Regular inspection and cleaning (or replacement) of the vent is recommended.

Desiccant Saturation (if fitted)

Vents with desiccant cartridges have a finite absorption capacity. Once saturated, they no longer absorb moisture, and the vent may become ineffective. Desiccant should be replaced periodically based on humidity exposure and inspection.

Improper Installation Orientation

Moisture vents should be installed with the vent opening facing downward or sideways to prevent water pooling and to allow vapour to escape. Incorrect orientation can lead to water ingress or reduced vapour transmission. Always follow the manufacturer's installation instructions.

Corrosion of Metal Housing

In coastal or marine environments, metal vent housings can corrode if not made of stainless steel or adequately plated. Use stainless steel vents in such conditions.

Damage from High‑Pressure Water Jets

During runway washing or de‑icing operations, high‑pressure water jets can force liquid water through the membrane if the pressure exceeds the water entry pressure rating. Protect vents with splash guards or install them in locations not directly exposed to high‑pressure jets.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Condensation or Fogging Found Inside the Enclosure
Likely Cause
The hydrophobic membrane has clogged with dust, salt, or oil aerosols, reducing vapour transmission below what's needed to relieve internal humidity, or a fitted desiccant cartridge has become saturated and stopped absorbing moisture.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the membrane for visible surface contamination or discolouration; check desiccant colour-change indicator (if fitted) to confirm saturation.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace the clogged membrane vent; replace saturated desiccant cartridges on a schedule matched to the site's humidity exposure rather than waiting for a fault to appear.
Water Found Pooling Inside the Enclosure Near the Vent
Likely Cause
The vent was installed with the opening facing upward rather than downward or sideways, allowing water to pool against it rather than shed away.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the vent's installed orientation against the manufacturer's installation instructions.
Corrective Action
Reposition the vent to the correct downward or sideways orientation per manufacturer instructions; dry out the enclosure and check for any resulting corrosion before closing up.
Metal Vent Housing Showing Corrosion
Likely Cause
The vent housing isn't stainless steel or adequately plated for a coastal or marine environment, allowing corrosion to set in over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Identify the housing material and confirm whether it matches the corrosion-resistance requirement for the site's environment.
Corrective Action
Replace with a stainless steel vent rated for the site's coastal/marine exposure; check the enclosure seal integrity for any ingress that may have occurred through the corroded housing.
Water Ingress Following Runway Washing or De-icing
Likely Cause
A high-pressure water jet exceeded the membrane's water entry pressure rating during runway washing or de-icing operations, forcing liquid water through the vent.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the timing of the ingress against recent washing or de-icing activity; check whether the vent's location is directly exposed to jet spray paths.
Corrective Action
Fit a splash guard over the vent or relocate it out of direct jet exposure; dry out the enclosure and inspect internal components for water damage before returning to service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Opening the Enclosure
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the circuit the enclosure belongs to and verify zero energy before removing or servicing a moisture vent fitted to a fixture, base can, or cabinet, since accessing the vent usually means opening a live enclosure.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep the vent, its retaining hardware, and any desiccant cartridge accounted for at all times during servicing; these are small parts that are easily dropped and left behind inside the enclosure or on the pavement.

Confirm Correct Orientation and Seal on Refit
Compliance

Refit the vent in its correct orientation with the hydrophobic membrane properly seated, since an incorrectly fitted vent can let liquid water in rather than just venting vapour, defeating its purpose.

Desiccant Cartridge Handling
Personnel Safety

Where the vent incorporates a replaceable desiccant cartridge, handle it per the manufacturer's guidance and dispose of spent cartridges appropriately; avoid contact with eyes if the desiccant material is disturbed or damaged.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the enclosure reseals correctly around the vent and re-energise only once the housing is fully closed up, to avoid re-introducing the moisture the vent is meant to manage.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Orientation is the Single Most Common Installation Error
Installation

A moisture vent installed facing upward instead of downward or sideways can allow standing water to pool directly against the membrane, eventually forcing liquid through under gravity alone even at pressures well below the rated water entry pressure. Verify orientation against the manufacturer's diagram as a specific commissioning checklist item, since this single detail accounts for a disproportionate share of vent-related moisture ingress.

Membrane Clogging Reduces Function Long Before It's Visible
Maintenance

A hydrophobic membrane can lose significant vapour transmission capacity from a light film of dust, salt aerosol, or oil residue well before it looks visibly blocked. Test actual vapour transmission or pressure equalisation response periodically rather than relying on a visual "looks clean" check, particularly at sites with heavy de-icing fluid or airborne particulate exposure.

High-Pressure Washing Can Defeat a Correctly Rated Vent
Operational

Runway or fixture washing with pressure-washing equipment can exceed a moisture vent's rated water entry pressure even though the vent performs correctly under normal rain and weather exposure. Site vents away from direct pressure-washer contact where possible, or fit a splash guard, rather than assuming the standard IP rating covers deliberate high-pressure cleaning.

Desiccant Saturation Fails Silently
Durability

A saturated desiccant cartridge doesn't announce itself — the vent continues to look intact and installed correctly while providing essentially no additional moisture absorption. Replace desiccant on a defined schedule based on known humidity exposure rather than waiting for a fogging complaint to trigger replacement.

Vent Failure is Usually Diagnosed by Its Symptom, Not Itself
Troubleshooting

Internal fogging or condensation is almost always traced back to the moisture vent or a general seal failure elsewhere on the enclosure, so when fogging appears, check both the vent's condition and the fixture's other seals together rather than assuming the vent is automatically at fault or automatically innocent.

Size the Vent to the Enclosure's Actual Thermal Cycling, Not a Generic Spec
Design

An enclosure that experiences large day-night temperature swings pumps more moist air in and out through its vent than a more thermally stable one, so a vent sized adequately for one site's climate can be undersized for a hotter, more variable one. Match vent sizing to the specific fixture's expected thermal cycling range rather than defaulting to a single standard part across all sites.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL moisture vents: how they work, specifications, orientation, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a moisture vent in an AGL fixture?

A moisture vent, also called a breather vent or desiccant vent, is a passive component in sealed AGL enclosures that lets internal water vapour escape while blocking liquid water. It uses a hydrophobic membrane, and some designs add a desiccant to absorb residual moisture.

Q2Why do sealed AGL fixtures need a moisture vent?

Even a well-sealed fixture accumulates water vapour over time, from temperature cycling that draws in humid air or from tiny seal imperfections. If the vapour has nowhere to go it condenses on lenses and prisms, distorting the beam pattern in a way that is invisible from the ground but real to an approaching pilot.

Q3How does a moisture vent work?

A microporous PTFE membrane passes water vapour but blocks liquid water because of surface tension and pore size. When internal humidity exceeds external humidity, vapour migrates outward, keeping internal humidity below the dew point so condensation does not form. The vent is designed to maintain the enclosure's IP67/IP68 rating.

Q4What is the difference between a moisture vent and a pressure valve?

A pressure valve mainly equalises pressure. A moisture vent focuses on removing water vapour that has entered the fixture, for example during maintenance or through imperfect seals, or that has condensed from temperature cycling.

Q5Where are moisture vents used?

They are used in sealed runway and taxiway fixtures, threshold and end light housings, approach lighting fixtures, PAPI and VASI optical units, obstruction light housings, isolating transformer housings, and junction boxes and control panels.

Q6What are typical moisture vent specifications?

Typical vents use a hydrophobic PTFE membrane in a 316L stainless steel, nickel-plated brass or UV-stabilised polyamide housing, with metric (M8, M10, M12) or NPT (1/8, 1/4, 3/8 inch) threads. Water entry pressure is at least 0.5 bar, the temperature range is -40°C to +120°C, and membrane service life is typically 5 to 10 years.

Q7How should a moisture vent be oriented and mounted?

It fits into a threaded or gasketed port in the housing wall, positioned above any water ingress point without blocking the optical path. It should face downward or to the side to prevent water pooling and let vapour escape. Incorrect orientation compromises its function, so check it on every refit.

Q8Which standards apply to moisture vents?

The main references are IEC 60529 (IP code), IEC TS 61827 (inset and elevated AGL luminaires, including environmental sealing), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (sealing is tested as part of the qualified fixture, vent included) and ASTM B117 (salt spray testing). Always verify the current edition.

Q9Does a moisture vent need maintenance?

It is a passive, largely maintenance-free device, but typical checks are semi-annual for membrane inspection and cleaning, and annual for desiccant saturation (where fitted), orientation and mounting, and housing corrosion. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common moisture vent problems?

The common issues are a membrane clogged by dust, salt or oil aerosols, saturated desiccant, incorrect orientation, corrosion of metal housings in coastal or marine environments (use stainless steel there), and high-pressure water jets from runway washing or de-icing forcing water through the membrane above its entry pressure rating.
