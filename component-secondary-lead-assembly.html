Secondary Lead Assembly

A pre‑terminated cable assembly that connects the secondary side of an isolating transformer to the AGL light fixture, sign, or beacon. Featuring flexible, finely stranded conductors and factory‑fitted waterproof connectors on both ends, it provides a reliable, plug‑and‑play low‑voltage connection that simplifies installation and reduces field termination errors in the airfield environment.

Overview

What Is It?

The Secondary Lead Assembly (also known as a transformer lead, secondary whip, or fixture lead) is a factory‑assembled cable assembly that provides the electrical connection between the secondary side of an AGL isolating transformer and the light fixture, sign, or beacon. It is a complete, ready‑to‑install unit that includes the secondary cable with pre‑terminated connectors on both ends, eliminating the need for field termination and significantly reducing installation time and potential wiring errors.

Key characteristics of secondary lead assemblies include:

  • Pre‑Terminated Connectors: Factory‑fitted waterproof connectors (often the L‑823 or secondary‑specific connector types) on both ends, ensuring a reliable, weatherproof connection with consistent quality.
  • Flexible Cable Construction: The cable features finely stranded, annealed copper conductors with flexible insulation (PVC, EPR, or TPU), allowing easy routing through fixture bases, junction boxes, and conduit.
  • Low‑Voltage Rating: Designed for the stepped‑down secondary voltage from the transformer (typically 2.5V to 48V AC), with insulation rated for 600V or 1kV.
  • Colour‑Coded Conductors: The conductors are colour‑coded (e.g., black/white, red/black) to identify polarity and simplify connection to the transformer and fixture terminals.
  • Variety of Lengths and Configurations: Available in standard lengths (e.g., 1m, 2m, 3m, 5m) and with various connector combinations (plug, receptacle, or bare ends) to suit different transformer and fixture types.

The assembly is manufactured to meet the rigorous quality and performance standards of airfield ground lighting, ensuring reliable operation in the harsh outdoor environment. It simplifies maintenance and replacement, as the entire lead can be quickly swapped out without re‑terminating cables in the field.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The secondary lead assembly exists to remove field termination — and the variability that comes with it — from the connection between a transformer and its fixture. A hand-terminated joint made in the field, often in a cramped pit or handhole, carries real risk of an inconsistent crimp or a seal that isn't quite watertight, and either failure mode can introduce a fault into a circuit that otherwise would have performed reliably for years.

By pre-terminating both ends at the factory to a consistent quality standard, the assembly turns a connection point that used to depend on field conditions and installer skill into a simple, reliable plug-and-play component. This also speeds up maintenance directly: when a lead does eventually need replacing, a technician swaps the whole assembly rather than re-terminating cable in the field, cutting both the time a fixture is out of service and the chance of introducing a new field-termination error.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The secondary lead assembly is used throughout the airfield for all transformer‑to‑fixture connections, including:

  • Runway Lighting Fixtures: Connecting runway edge lights, centreline lights, threshold lights, end lights, and touchdown zone lights to their isolating transformers.
  • Taxiway Lighting Fixtures: Connecting taxiway edge lights, centreline lights, stop bars, clearance bars, and guidance signs.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Connecting ALSF‑1, ALSF‑2, MALSR, and other approach lighting fixtures to their transformers.
  • Obstruction Lighting: Connecting obstruction lights on structures to their power sources.
  • Airport Beacons: Connecting rotating beacons and heliport beacons to their transformers.
  • Signs and Markers: Connecting illuminated signs, guidance signs, and other visual aids to their power supplies.
  • Retrofit and Replacement: Replacing damaged or aged secondary leads during maintenance or system upgrades.

The assembly is available for various fixture types, with conductor sizes typically ranging from 1.5mm² to 4mm² (16 AWG to 12 AWG). It is designed for use in temperatures ranging from -40°C to +85°C and is resistant to oils, fuels, and de‑icing chemicals.

Placement

Installation & Location

The lead assembly runs from the isolating transformer's secondary terminals to the fixture's own lamp/LED connector, typically inside the same base can or a short length of adjacent duct, kept short since it operates on the low-voltage secondary side rather than the field's constant-current primary. Connections at both ends are made with waterproof, submersible connectors (commonly L-823 or similar) rather than taped joints, since the assembly frequently sits in a base can that can flood; enough slack is left at the fixture end to allow the fixture to be lifted clear of its base can for maintenance without disconnecting the lead. Routing keeps the secondary lead separate from the transformer's primary-side series-circuit cable within the same can, so a secondary-side fault is isolated from the primary loop.

Function

How Does It Work?

The secondary lead assembly provides a pre‑manufactured, reliable, and waterproof connection between the isolating transformer and the light fixture. Its design and construction ensure efficient power delivery and long‑term reliability:

  • Cable Conductor: High‑conductivity annealed copper with fine stranding (Class 5 or 6) provides excellent flexibility, allowing the lead to be routed through tight spaces and around bends without damage. The stranding also reduces fatigue from vibration and thermal cycling.
  • Insulation: Flexible PVC, EPR, or TPU insulation provides electrical isolation and mechanical protection. The insulation is rated for the secondary voltage and is resistant to moisture, oils, and chemicals commonly found on airfields.
  • Connectors: Factory‑terminated connectors (typically L‑823 or secondary‑specific types) are moulded or crimped onto the cable ends. These connectors are designed to mate with the transformer's secondary terminals and the fixture's input socket, providing a secure, waterproof connection with low contact resistance.
  • Strain Relief: The assembly includes integrated strain relief at the connector junctions, preventing the conductors from pulling out of the connector under tension.
  • Colour Coding: The conductors are colour‑coded (e.g., black and white, or red and black) to indicate polarity, ensuring correct connection to the transformer and fixture terminals.

In operation, the isolating transformer steps down the primary current to a lower voltage and feeds it through the secondary lead assembly to the light fixture. The lead assembly carries this low‑voltage power to the fixture's lamp or LED driver. The pre‑terminated connectors ensure a consistent, low‑resistance connection that minimises voltage drop and heating, critical for maintaining the performance of the AGL system.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The secondary lead assembly carries no control logic of its own, but it sits on the far side of the isolating transformer from the loop the CCR actually regulates — the primary side carries the fixed loop current the ALCMS commands, while the secondary lead simply delivers whatever current that transformer's turns ratio produces to the fixture. This is the point of the isolating transformer: an open circuit anywhere in the secondary lead (a broken conductor, a failed connector) still allows the transformer's primary winding to remain part of an unbroken loop, so it does not, by itself, interrupt current to any other fixture on the circuit. Electrically, though, an open secondary lead presents the same impedance-rise signature at that transformer's primary as a burnt-out lamp or a failed transformer would — the CCR sees a loop voltage rise and reports a loop fault, with no way from the ALCMS console to distinguish a bad secondary lead from a bad lamp or a bad transformer at that position. Isolating the actual cause is a field task: checking the secondary lead's continuity and connector condition at that specific fixture once the loop fault has narrowed the search to it.

The isolating transformer keeps a secondary-side fault from breaking the primary loop for every other fixture — but from the ALCMS, that same fault still reads only as "loop voltage rise," so pinning it to the secondary lead specifically is a field check once the loop fault narrows the search to that fixture.
Operating Principle

Power Path: Transformer to Fixture

The assembly's job is to carry the transformer's stepped-down output to the fixture without giving any of it back as voltage drop or heat — the fine-stranded conductor, moisture-rated insulation, and factory connectors all serve that one low-resistance, low-maintenance path.
Construction

Physical Construction

The secondary lead assembly consists of a length of secondary AGL cable, sized for the fixture's low-voltage, higher-current secondary circuit, factory-terminated at both ends with moulded or crimped connectors — typically an L-823-style secondary plug at the transformer end and a fixture-specific connector or bare lead lugs at the light end. The cable insulation and jacket are rated for direct burial or wet-location use within the base can, and the moulded connector bodies are over-moulded or potted around the crimp joints to create a factory-sealed, watertight termination that eliminates the need for field splicing. Strain-relief boots at each connector body protect the cable-to-connector transition from flexing and pulling stress during installation and maintenance. Assemblies are supplied in standard pre-cut lengths to suit typical base-can-to-fixture and transformer-to-base-can distances, with connector keying that prevents incorrect mating to other circuit types.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Connector Corrosion

The connectors on the lead assembly can corrode in saltwater or chemically aggressive environments, increasing contact resistance. Regular inspection and cleaning of connectors, along with application of dielectric grease, are recommended.

Cable Damage During Installation

The flexible cable can be damaged by sharp edges or abrasion during routing. Use protective conduit or grommets where the cable passes through metal openings, and avoid sharp bends.

Incorrect Connector Orientation

Some connectors are polarised and must be oriented correctly for proper mating. Ensure the connector is aligned correctly before tightening the coupling ring.

Over‑Tightening of Coupling Ring

Excessive torque on the coupling ring can damage the connector threads or O‑rings. Use the manufacturer's recommended torque (typically 3.5–5.5 N·m).

Incorrect Lead Length Selection

Choosing a lead that is too short may place tension on the connectors, while one that is too long can create loops that are susceptible to damage. Measure the distance accurately and select the appropriate length.

Moisture Ingress at Connector Junction

If the connector is not fully mated or the O‑rings are damaged, moisture can enter the connector, causing corrosion and short circuits. Inspect O‑rings regularly and replace if damaged.

Incorrect Conductor Polarity

Reversing the polarity (connecting the live conductor to the neutral terminal) can damage LED drivers or cause lamps to operate incorrectly. Always follow the colour‑coding or markings on the lead and the equipment terminals.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Fixture Dark Despite Healthy Transformer
Likely Cause
The secondary lead assembly connector has failed open, or the lead is damaged along its run.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm secondary voltage is present at the transformer output; test continuity through the lead from transformer to fixture; visually inspect the lead for cuts or abrasion.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged lead assembly with the correct length for the run, routed to avoid sharp edges.
Connector Shows Corrosion or High Resistance
Likely Cause
Saltwater or chemically aggressive site conditions have corroded the connector contacts.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the connector pins/contacts for corrosion or discoloration; measure resistance across the mated connector.
Corrective Action
Clean the connector and apply dielectric grease, or replace the lead assembly if corrosion has progressed, then re-verify resistance.
Connector Won't Fully Mate
Likely Cause
Incorrect connector orientation on a polarised connector, preventing proper mating.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the connector's polarisation key/orientation against the mating half before forcing the coupling ring.
Corrective Action
Realign the connector correctly and hand-tighten the coupling ring to the manufacturer's recommended torque.
Coupling Ring Damaged or O-Rings Compromised
Likely Cause
Excessive torque was applied when tightening the coupling ring, damaging threads or O-rings.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the coupling ring threads and O-rings for damage or deformation.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged connector or O-rings, and re-torque to the manufacturer's specified value (typically 3.5-5.5 N·m).
Moisture Found Inside the Connector Junction
Likely Cause
The connector was not fully mated, or the O-rings were damaged, allowing moisture ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Disconnect and inspect for moisture or corrosion inside the connector; check O-ring condition.
Corrective Action
Dry the connector, replace damaged O-rings, and confirm a full, correctly torqued mate before re-energising.
LED Driver Damaged or Lamp Operating Incorrectly
Likely Cause
Reversed conductor polarity at the lead assembly, connecting the live conductor to the neutral terminal.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the lead's colour-coding or markings against the equipment terminals; verify polarity with a meter before re-energising.
Corrective Action
Correct the polarity connection, and replace the LED driver if it was damaged by the reversed polarity.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the series circuit, then verify zero energy at the lead before disconnecting or replacing it.

Verify Polarity Before Re-Energising
Electrical Safety

Confirm the lead's colour-coding or markings match the transformer and fixture terminals, and check polarity with a meter before restoring power; a reversed connection can damage LED drivers or cause incorrect lamp operation.

Connector Seating & O-Ring Condition
Mechanical

Inspect the O-ring for damage and ensure the connector is fully seated before closing the base can; a compromised seal at either end of the lead allows moisture ingress that leads to corrosion or a short circuit.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the affected series loop after any lead replacement before restoring power, since a residual fault can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and old connector material accounted for whenever the base can is open, and secure loose material before reinstating the fixture.

PPE

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm secure, correctly polarised, and properly sealed connections at both ends of the lead before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Reversed Polarity Rarely Trips an Alarm Immediately
Diagnostics

A secondary lead reconnected with reversed polarity on a fixture that tolerates it electrically can still pass a basic continuity check while causing subtle downstream issues, such as incorrect photometric behaviour on some LED fixtures. Verify polarity explicitly, not just continuity, after any lead disconnection.

A Failed Seal at One End Ages the Whole Lead
Durability

Moisture that enters through a degraded seal at one connector can wick along the conductor and affect insulation resistance readings measured from the other end, making the fault location less obvious than the resistance reading alone suggests. Inspect both terminations, not just the end nearer the reading.

Insulation Resistance Drift Predicts Failure Better Than a Single Reading
Troubleshooting

A lead assembly reading just inside the acceptance threshold on a single test can still be on a clear downward trend from a previous baseline. Logging readings against the specific lead's history, rather than only against the pass/fail limit, catches a developing fault earlier.

Mismatched Connector Types Cause Recurring Field Faults
Installation

Mixing connector styles or ratings between a replacement lead and the fixture it serves is a common, easily overlooked cause of a connection that looks secure but degrades quickly in service. Match connector type and rating to the original specification, not just physical fit.

Thermal Cycling at the Connector Loosens Crimps Over Time
Maintenance

Repeated heating and cooling of a fixture connection through normal operation can gradually loosen a crimped termination that was correctly made at installation. Periodic re-torque or crimp inspection at overhaul catches this before it becomes an intermittent field fault.

Tag Leads by Circuit Position, Not Just Fixture
Process

Where a lead assembly is swapped between fixtures during troubleshooting, tag it by its circuit position and test history rather than only by the fixture it currently serves, so a recurring lead-specific issue doesn't get mistaken for a series of unrelated fixture faults.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about secondary lead assemblies: construction, ratings, connectors, standards and common problems.

Q1What is a secondary lead assembly?

A secondary lead assembly, also called a transformer lead, secondary whip or fixture lead, is a factory-assembled cable that connects the secondary side of an AGL isolating transformer to the light fixture, sign or beacon. It has waterproof connectors pre-terminated on both ends, so no field termination is needed.

Q2Why use a pre-terminated lead instead of terminating in the field?

A hand-terminated joint made in a cramped pit or handhole risks an inconsistent crimp or a seal that isn't quite watertight. Factory termination gives consistent quality, and when a lead needs replacing the whole assembly is swapped, which shortens fixture outage time and avoids new field-termination errors.

Q3Where are secondary lead assemblies used?

For transformer-to-fixture connections on runway lights (edge, centreline, threshold, end, touchdown zone), taxiway lights, stop bars, clearance bars and guidance signs, approach lighting, obstruction lights, airport beacons and illuminated signs. They are also used to replace damaged or aged leads.

Q4What voltage and current does a secondary lead carry?

It works on the low-voltage secondary side, typically 2.5 V to 48 V AC, with insulation rated 600 V or 1 kV. Continuous current ratings are typically 15 A for 1.5 mm², 22 A for 2.5 mm² and 30 A for 4 mm², matched to the transformer secondary wattage.

Q5What is a secondary lead assembly made of?

The conductor is finely stranded (Class 5 or 6) annealed copper for flexibility and vibration resistance, with PVC, EPR or TPU insulation and an oil- and chemical-resistant PVC or TPU sheath. Conductors are colour-coded for polarity, and connectors have integrated strain relief.

Q6What connectors and protection rating does it have?

Typically L-823 plugs or receptacles, or secondary- or fixture-specific connectors, at each end. The mated assembly is IP68 (submersible, continuous), with an operating range of -40°C to +85°C. Standard lengths are 1, 2, 3, 5 and 10 m, with custom lengths available.

Q7Which standards apply to secondary lead assemblies?

The main references are IEC 60228 (conductor stranding and sizes), FAA AC 150/5345-47 (isolation transformers, including secondary lead requirements), FAA AC 150/5345-26 (L-823 connectors) and UL 62 (flexible cords and cables). Always verify the current edition.

Q8How often should a secondary lead be inspected?

Typical practice is an annual check of connector condition and corrosion, connection orientation, polarity and coupling, moisture ingress at the connector junction, and cable condition and lead length. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q9What are the common secondary lead problems?

Connector corrosion in saltwater or chemically aggressive environments, cable damage from sharp edges during routing, incorrect connector orientation, over-tightening the coupling ring (recommended torque typically 3.5 to 5.5 N·m), wrong lead length, moisture ingress from a partly mated connector or damaged O-rings, and reversed conductor polarity.

Q10What should I do when replacing a secondary lead?

De-energise and lock out the CCR feeding the series circuit and verify zero energy. Check the O-ring and connector seating, confirm polarity against the transformer and fixture terminals, and megger test the affected series loop before restoring power.
