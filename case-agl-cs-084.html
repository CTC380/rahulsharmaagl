AGL-CS-084 — Sequenced Flashing Light System Failure

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A sequenced flashing light (SFL) investigation where synchronization loss caused out-of-sequence flashing and continuous illumination — from power verification through controller diagnostics, timing analysis, trigger-pulse measurement, module replacement, and operational validation.

1Case Study Overview

During night operations, pilots reported that the sequenced flashing light system was operating abnormally, with several flash units flashing out of sequence and some units remaining permanently illuminated.

The steady-burning approach lights were operating normally, the Constant Current Regulator (CCR) was healthy, and no major circuit failure was present.

Because the failure affected the flashing sequence rather than the steady-burning circuit, a detailed investigation was initiated covering:

  • Flash controller operation.
  • Synchronization timing.
  • Trigger pulse distribution.
  • Power supplies.
  • Communication circuits.
  • Flash-unit control modules.
  • Sequence timing verification.

The investigation identified failure of the synchronization output module within the flash controller, resulting in incorrect triggering of multiple flash units and loss of sequential flashing operation.

This case demonstrates that sequenced flashing light failures are control-system synchronization problems requiring analysis of timing, triggering, and sequence logic rather than only electrical power circuits.

3System Background

An SFL system consists of:

  • Flash controller.
  • Synchronization module.
  • Timing generator.
  • Trigger distribution circuits.
  • Flash power units.
  • Individual flash units.
  • Communication interfaces.
  • Monitoring circuits.

Correct operation requires:

  • Accurate timing.
  • Correct sequence order.
  • Uniform flash intervals.
  • Synchronization between units.
  • Stable trigger pulses.
  • Correct power distribution.

SFL failures may include:

  • No flashing.
  • Continuous flashing.
  • Random flashing.
  • Out-of-sequence flashing.
  • Partial sequence failure.
  • Synchronization loss.
  • Controller failure.
  • Trigger-distribution failure.

Approach guidance depends on correct sequential flashing.

10Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

The SFL failure was caused by failure of the synchronization output module, resulting in:

  • Clock instability.
  • Missing trigger pulses.
  • Incorrect sequence timing.
  • Loss of flash synchronization.

The power system, communication system, and flash units remained healthy.

The primary failure mechanism was controller synchronization failure.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: Steady-burning approach lights ON. Flash units flashing randomly. Some units continuously illuminated. CCR healthy. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace all flash lamps.
B. Increase flash intensity.
C. Verify flash-controller synchronization, measure trigger pulse timing, test sequence generation, and inspect the synchronization output module.
D. Replace the CCR.
Correct answer: C. Random flashing and continuous illumination with a healthy CCR indicate a control-system synchronization problem. Timing analysis and controller diagnostics will identify the cause without unnecessary lamp or CCR replacement.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about sequenced flashing light (SFL) synchronization failure.

Q1What is AGL-CS-084?

AGL-CS-084 is an engineering training case study. It covers a sequenced flashing light system where flash units fired out of sequence or stayed on, while the steady-burning approach lights worked normally. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2What was abnormal in the flash sequence?

Units F1–F5 flashed correctly, F6–F9 flashed randomly and F10–F12 stayed continuously on.

Q3Why is this a control problem and not a power problem?

Main, flash and controller power supplies were all normal, and the steady-burning circuit worked. The fault was in timing and triggering, not power.

Q4What did controller diagnostics and trigger measurements show?

The controller reported a sequence synchronization error, a trigger-output fault and a timing-module alarm. Oscilloscope measurements showed correct pulse width but an unstable pulse interval and intermittent synchronization.

Q5Was communication at fault?

No. Communication testing showed no errors, correct unit addressing and stable links.

Q6What was the root cause of AGL-CS-084?

Failure of the synchronization output module in the flash controller, causing clock drift, missing trigger pulses and incorrect sequence timing. The power system, communication and flash units stayed healthy.

Q7How was the fault confirmed?

Replacing the synchronization output module restored the correct sequence, uniform timing and synchronized flashing, and eliminated the continuous-ON condition.

Q8What corrective actions were taken?

The team isolated the controller, replaced the synchronization module and verified timing accuracy. They also tested trigger pulse distribution, sequence order and flash intervals, checked controller diagnostics, performed ICAO sequence verification and updated records.

Q9What is the key lesson from this case?

For sequencing faults, analyse timing, triggering and sequence logic. Replacing individual flash units or checking only power would miss the cause.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

Sequenced flashing light system failures are primarily synchronization and timing-control problems caused by controller-module defects, trigger-distribution abnormalities, and sequence-generation errors, and effective diagnosis requires analysis of timing signals, synchronization pulses, controller logic, and operational sequence verification rather than only power-circuit troubleshooting.

Systematic controller diagnostics, timing analysis, trigger-pulse measurement, synchronization verification, module replacement, and post-repair operational validation provide a complete engineering methodology for restoring ICAO-compliant sequenced flashing light performance.

In AGL engineering, the most important aspect of a flashing light system is not that the lights flash; it is that they flash together in exactly the right order and at exactly the right time.
