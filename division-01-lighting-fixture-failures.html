Lighting Fixture Failures

The first and most fundamental failure category in the AGL Failure Database — capturing failures directly associated with the actual airfield light fixtures and their internal, optical and mechanical components. Runway fixtures, taxiway fixtures and component-level failures, including LED driver failure, lamp burnout, moisture ingress, optical damage, seal failure, corrosion and loose internal connections.

Key principle — This division is not merely a list of failed lights. Every record should answer: what fixture failed → how did it fail → why did it fail → what evidence confirmed the failure → how was it repaired → how can recurrence be prevented? LTF-01 Runway Edge Light Failure
Failure Definition

Failure of a runway edge-light fixture resulting in loss or degradation of the required runway-edge visual guidance.

Typical Failure Symptoms
  • Complete light outage
  • Intermittent operation
  • Reduced brightness
  • Incorrect intensity
  • Uneven illumination
  • Flickering
  • One light operating differently from adjacent fixtures
  • Photometric degradation
Possible Failure Causes
  • LED module failure
  • Lamp burnout
  • LED driver failure
  • Secondary lead failure
  • Internal connection failure
  • Connector problem
  • Moisture ingress
  • Seal deterioration
  • Optical degradation
  • Corrosion
  • Housing damage
  • Cable/transformer problem
  • Fixture misalignment
Investigation Points
  • Confirm primary series-circuit condition
  • Verify isolating transformer output
  • Inspect secondary connection
  • Check fixture input
  • Inspect LED/lampholder/driver
  • Inspect optical system
  • Check moisture or water traces
  • Check internal terminals
  • Check fixture alignment
  • Compare with adjacent fixtures
Evidence to Record
  • Fixture photographs
  • Nameplate information
  • Failure condition
  • Secondary voltage/current
  • Transformer condition
  • Internal component condition
  • Photometry result where applicable
  • Damaged-component photographs

Division-Wide Requirements

Standard record structure, classification, RCA linkage, evidence layer and severity scheme that apply across all 24 Division 01 failure topics.

A. Recommended Division 01 Failure Record

Every Division 01 failure should ideally be converted into a standard failure record with the following fields:

  • Failure ID
  • Fixture Type (Runway / Taxiway / Other)
  • Fixture Category (Edge / Centreline / Threshold / TDZ etc.)
  • Manufacturer
  • Model
  • Location
  • Circuit (series-circuit ID)
  • Date
  • Operating Hours
  • Failure Mode
  • Symptoms
  • Initial Condition
  • Test Results (electrical/optical)
  • Physical Condition
  • Environmental Condition
  • RCA
  • Corrective Action
  • Preventive Action
  • Retest
  • Evidence (photos/readings/reports)
  • Severity
  • Operational Impact
  • Repeat Failure (Yes/No)
  • Related SOP
  • Lessons Learned

B. Failure Mode vs. Root Cause

The database distinguishes failure mode from root cause — the same visible symptom (light OFF) can have entirely different causes:

Case 1

Failure mode: LED driver failure

Root cause: Thermal overstress

Contributing factor: Inadequate heat dissipation

Corrective action: Driver replacement

Preventive action: Review thermal performance

Case 2

Failure mode: Light outage

Actual root cause: Loose internal connection

Case 3

Failure mode: LED failure

Root cause: Moisture ingress

Underlying cause: Seal failure

C. Failure → RCA → Action → Reliability Loop

Every fixture failure should eventually become engineering intelligence:

FAILURE ID. INITIAL INSPECTION ELEC/OPTICAL/MECH TESTING EVIDENCE COLLECTION FAILURE MODE ID. ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS CORRECTIVE ACTION POST-REPAIR TEST PREVENTIVE ACTION LESSONS LEARNED RELIABILITY TREND

D. RCA Connection

Division 01 links every failure to the Root Cause Analysis Database. The most relevant RCA categories for fixture failures are:

E. Failure Severity

Every failure receives a severity classification, plus operational-impact tagging:

Critical / Safety-Critical — immediate operational or safety risk

Major / Operationally Critical — significant availability impact

Moderate — reduced performance, not yet critical

Minor / Non-Critical — limited or no operational effect

Degradation / Intermittent / Latent — developing or hidden fault

F. Evidence Layer

Visual evidence

  • Failed fixture photo, nameplate, internal assembly
  • LED module, driver, lens, prism, seal
  • Corrosion, water ingress, wiring, connector, housing
  • Frangible component, alignment condition

Technical evidence

  • Circuit current, secondary voltage, insulation resistance
  • Continuity, transformer output, driver output
  • Photometric measurement, maintenance history, operating hours

Failure Database vs. Troubleshooting Atlas

The Troubleshooting Atlas answers “I have a fault now — how do I diagnose and fix it?” The Failure Database answers “this failure happened — what happened, why, what evidence proves it, what was done, and how do we stop it recurring?” Kept strictly separate, then linked end-to-end, the two form a genuine AGL Failure Intelligence Engine rather than a simple fault list.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about lighting fixture failures in the AGL Failure Database.

Q1What is Division 01 — Lighting Fixture Failures in the AGL Failure Database?

Division 01 is the first and most fundamental failure category in the AGL Failure Database. It captures failures directly associated with the actual airfield light fixtures and their internal, optical and mechanical components, across 24 published topics (LTF-01 to LTF-24) covering runway fixtures, taxiway fixtures and component-level failures.

Q2Which fixture failures does Division 01 cover?

Runway fixtures include edge, threshold, end, centreline, touchdown zone and runway guard lights. Taxiway fixtures include edge and centreline lights, clearance bars, exit lights and rapid-exit taxiway lights.

Q3Which component-level failures are covered?

LED module failure, lamp burnout, LED driver failure, optical lens damage, prism damage, seal failure, moisture ingress, corrosion, loose internal connections, secondary-lead failure, housing damage, frangible-component failure and fixture misalignment.

Q4What is the difference between a failure mode and a root cause?

The database distinguishes the two because the same visible symptom, such as a light being off, can have entirely different causes. For example, an LED failure may be caused by moisture ingress, with seal failure as the underlying cause. Likewise, a light outage may trace back to a loose internal connection rather than the LED itself.

Q5What should be recorded for a fixture failure?

Each failure should ideally become a standard failure record. Fields include failure ID, fixture type and category, manufacturer, model, location, series-circuit ID, date and operating hours, failure mode, symptoms, test results, physical and environmental condition, RCA, corrective and preventive action, retest, evidence, severity, operational impact, repeat-failure flag, related SOP and lessons learned.

Q6How is a fixture failure investigated?

Each failure follows a loop from failure ID and initial inspection through electrical, optical and mechanical testing, evidence collection and failure-mode identification to root cause analysis. It continues with corrective action, post-repair test, preventive action, lessons learned and reliability trend.

Q7Why check whether the fault is really in the fixture?

A fixture outage may have its actual cause elsewhere. For runway-end lights, for example, the investigation should determine whether the failure is fixture-related, transformer-related, cable-related, circuit-related, environmental or maintenance-induced. For exit lights, the fixture itself should be verified before the failure is assigned to the circuit or transformer.

Q8How are moisture ingress, seal failure and corrosion related?

Seal failure is recorded as both a failure mode and a possible root cause. The typical chain runs from environmental exposure to seal failure, moisture ingress, corrosion, electrical failure and finally light outage.

Q9Is a loose connection the final root cause?

Not automatically. A loose connection should not be classified as the final root cause; the investigation should ask why it became loose, for example incorrect torque, vibration, thermal cycling, poor assembly, ageing, maintenance error or a component defect.

Q10How are failure severity and evidence classified?

Failures are classed as Critical / Safety-Critical, Major / Operationally Critical, Moderate, Minor / Non-Critical, or Degradation / Intermittent / Latent, with operational-impact tagging. The evidence layer combines visual evidence, such as photos of the fixture, LED module, driver, lens, seal and corrosion, with technical evidence, such as circuit current, insulation resistance, continuity, transformer and driver output, and photometric measurements.

Q11How does the Failure Database differ from the Troubleshooting Atlas?

The Troubleshooting Atlas answers how to diagnose and fix a fault happening now. The Failure Database answers what happened, why, what evidence proves it, what was done and how to stop it recurring. Kept separate but linked end to end, they form the AGL Failure Intelligence Engine.
