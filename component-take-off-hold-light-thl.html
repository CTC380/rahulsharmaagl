Take-Off Hold Light (THL)

A critical runway safety lighting system that provides visual indications to pilots awaiting take-off clearance. THL systems are an integral component of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system, displaying red lights on the runway to indicate when it is unsafe to begin a take-off roll.

Overview

What Is It?

A Take-Off Hold Light (THL) is a runway safety lighting system that provides visual indications to pilots at the runway holding position. The THL system consists of a double row of red lights embedded in the runway surface at the take-off/"line up and wait" position, beginning approximately 114 m (375 ft) beyond the runway threshold and extending roughly 457 m (1,500 ft) down the runway. When illuminated, these lights indicate to the pilot that it is unsafe to commence the take-off roll due to runway occupancy by another aircraft or vehicle. The THL system is part of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system and is designed to prevent runway incursions and collisions during take-off operations.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The THL exists to give a pilot lined up for takeoff a direct, independent confirmation that the runway ahead is actually clear, rather than relying solely on an air traffic control clearance that could be based on incomplete information or delivered just before a conflicting aircraft or vehicle enters the runway. Because the lights are embedded in the pavement directly in the pilot's line of sight at the takeoff position, illuminated red lights communicate "do not begin the roll" in a way that requires no radio call and no interpretation, closing the reaction-time gap between a developing runway conflict and the pilot's decision to hold. This makes the THL a direct layer of incursion protection specifically at the highest-consequence moment of ground operations: the instant before a departing aircraft commits to its takeoff roll.

Application

Where Is It Used?

THL systems are deployed as part of the FAA's Runway Status Light (RWSL) program at a defined set of high-traffic, towered airports selected on the basis of operations volume and runway incursion history, rather than any single runway's instrument approach category. They are used in conjunction with runway entrance lights, runway intersection lights, and runway guard lights to provide comprehensive runway safety. THL systems are typically installed at:

  • Runway take-off ("line up and wait") positions, beginning approximately 114 m (375 ft) beyond the runway threshold and extending about 457 m (1,500 ft)
  • Intersecting runways where conflicts may occur during take-off
  • Runways with high traffic volume where incursion risk is significant
  • Major international airports implementing advanced surface movement control
Placement

Installation & Location

Fixtures are installed in two rows flanking the runway centreline at the take-off position, beginning approximately 114 m (375 ft) beyond the runway threshold and extending about 457 m (1,500 ft) down the runway, with each row set a fixed offset from the centreline so the red lights are visible to a pilot lined up for departure without being mistaken for centreline lighting. Each unit mounts on an L-868 base can recessed flush with the runway pavement, rated for the full wheel-load and jet-blast environment of the take-off roll, with cabling run in pavement ducting back to the THL control cabinet. The control cabinet houses the interface to the Runway Status Light surveillance feed, which illuminates the row only when the runway is occupied or a conflict is detected, and is typically co-located with or networked to the adjacent runway guard light controllers so the two subsystems act in a coordinated sequence. Setting-out is tied to the runway centreline survey so the light rows remain parallel and symmetrical about the centreline for the full length of the take-off hold zone.

Function

How Does It Work?

The THL system operates as part of the Runway Status Light (RWSL) system:

  • Surveillance: Radar, ADS-B, and surface movement radar data track all aircraft and vehicles on the airfield.
  • Detection: The RWSL processing system detects when a runway is occupied by an aircraft or vehicle.
  • Activation: When the runway is occupied, the THL system illuminates red lights on the runway at the take-off position, indicating to the pilot that it is unsafe to start the take-off roll.
  • Deactivation: When the runway is clear, the THL system deactivates, allowing the pilot to proceed with take-off.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

THL is not a standalone system — it is one output function of the wider Runway Status Light (RWSL) architecture, sharing its surveillance and processing layer with the Runway Entrance and Runway Intersection Light functions. Surface surveillance data (ASDE-X/ASSC radar, multilateration, and ADS-B) is fused continuously in the RWSL processing system, which runs a dedicated THL algorithm evaluating the runway ahead of each departure position for occupancy by a landing, departing, or crossing aircraft or vehicle. When a hazard is predicted, the algorithm issues an illuminate command to the light control and monitoring subsystem, which energises the in-pavement THL fixture array at the take-off position; when the runway clears, the same path issues the extinguish command. Because the decision is made entirely within the processing system, illumination happens in well under a second and does not wait on, or route through, air traffic control — the pilot sees the red lights as a direct, independent cue rather than a controller instruction. The light control and monitoring subsystem still reports THL circuit and fixture health back to the tower's ATC display and to maintenance, so status is visible even though command authority isn't.

Surveillance Sensors → RWSL Processing System (THL Algorithm) → Light Control & Monitoring → THL Fixture Array (with health feedback to ATC display)

Construction

Physical Construction

The fixture housing is a heavy-duty die-cast aluminium or ductile-iron body machined to seat directly into an L-868 base can and finished to resist the fuel, hydraulic fluid, and de-icing chemical exposure typical of the take-off position. Because the unit lies flush with the pavement directly in the path of the main gear, the top plate is engineered to the full ≈27,000 kg (59,400 lb) wheel-load rating — governing case: fully loaded A380-800 tyre, per FAA Engineering Brief 83A and set with a slight crown to shed water and limit standing-fluid film over the lens. The red uni-directional beam is formed by an LED array or halogen lamp behind a toughened glass or polycarbonate lens, gasket-sealed to the housing with an O-ring or silicone seal to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating so the unit remains watertight under standing water and de-icing fluid immersion. Internal wiring terminates at L-823 primary connectors potted for direct burial, which mate to the secondary leads of the isolating transformer housed in an adjacent base can, keeping the series-circuit splice out of the wheel-load path and clear of surface water. Stainless-steel fasteners and a torque-rated top-cap seal are used throughout, and a dedicated interface cable runs from the fixture circuit back to the THL control cabinet that carries the RWSL activation command.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Lens Contamination from Rubber Deposits

THL lights are exposed to tyre rubber deposits from aircraft at the take-off position. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and visibility.

RWSL Communication Failure

THL lights rely on communication with the RWSL system. Network failures can cause the lights to fail to activate or deactivate at the correct time. Redundant communication paths and regular system checks are essential.

LED Driver Failure

LED drivers in THL lights can fail due to thermal stress or power surges. Regular inspection and replacement of faulty drivers is essential to maintain system reliability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

THL Row Fails to Illuminate When Runway Is Occupied
Likely Cause
Loss of the illuminate command from the RWSL processing system's THL algorithm, or a fault in the light control and monitoring subsystem between the processing layer and the fixture array.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the RWSL processing system's THL algorithm status and command log for this position; confirm the light control and monitoring subsystem is receiving and relaying the illuminate command; force a manual test activation to isolate processing-side vs fixture-side faults.
Corrective Action
Restore the processing-to-control-subsystem link if the command is not reaching the array, or repair/replace the control and monitoring subsystem interface; escalate a persistent algorithm-side fault to RWSL system maintenance.
Single Fixture Dark Within the THL Row
Likely Cause
Failed LED module or isolating transformer on that unit, or an open series-loop connection isolating just that fixture from the rest of the row.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm the rest of the row illuminates correctly on a test activation; check series loop continuity through the affected fixture's transformer; inspect L-823 connectors at that unit for corrosion or an open crimp.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed LED module or transformer, or re-terminate the faulted connector; re-verify with a follow-up test activation before returning the row to service.
Lights Remain Illuminated After Runway Is Clear
Likely Cause
The extinguish command from the RWSL processing system is not reaching the fixture array, or surveillance data is still reporting a stale occupancy condition for that runway.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the RWSL processing system's current occupancy determination against actual runway status; issue a manual extinguish command through the light control and monitoring subsystem and observe response; check for a latched fault in that subsystem.
Corrective Action
Reset or replace the latched control and monitoring subsystem interface; if the surveillance feed itself is stale, escalate to RWSL system maintenance to clear or restart the affected sensor/processing path.
Health Status Not Reporting to ATC Display
Likely Cause
Broken or misconfigured feedback link between the light control and monitoring subsystem and the tower's ATC display, independent of whether the THL fixtures themselves are functioning.
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm THL fixture operation is unaffected by activating the row manually; check the monitoring subsystem's reporting link and configuration to the ATC display; test with a known fixture-health event to see if it registers at the tower.
Corrective Action
Restore or reconfigure the reporting link between the monitoring subsystem and the ATC display; treat this as a monitoring-only fault and continue operating the THL row while the reporting path is repaired, per RWSL fault procedure.
Reduced Light Output / Rubber-Contaminated Lens
Likely Cause
Tyre rubber deposits from repeated take-off rolls building up on the lens, or a hazed/eroded lens from prolonged jet-blast and wheel-load exposure at the flush-mounted position.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for rubber film or surface degradation; run a photometric intensity check against ICAO/FAA thresholds if contamination alone doesn't explain the reduced output.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and a soft brush; replace the lens or fixture if photometric output remains below threshold after cleaning.
Series Circuit Fault or Insulation Resistance Drop at THL Position
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at the base can, gaskets, or connectors under the runway pavement, or wheel-load/jet-blast stress on the ducted cable run to the fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the isolation transformer and series circuit for the affected segment; inspect base can seals, top-cap O-rings, and connector torque; check the pavement and duct run for settlement or damage.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected transformer/connector, renew gaskets and O-rings, and confirm insulation resistance is restored above the acceptable minimum before re-energising the circuit.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Loss of RWSL Illuminate/Extinguish Command
High Severity
Root Cause
Failure or degradation of the communication path between the RWSL processing system's THL algorithm and the light control and monitoring subsystem, or a fault within the algorithm's occupancy determination itself.
Effect
THL row fails to illuminate for an occupied runway (loss of a safety-critical warning) or fails to extinguish once clear, either of which undermines pilot confidence in the system.
Detection Method
RWSL processing system command/status log review, periodic manual test activation of the row, and monitoring of the communication link's health indicators.
LED Module / Isolating Transformer Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Thermal stress or power-surge damage to the LED driver/module, or moisture-driven insulation breakdown in the isolating transformer winding.
Effect
Single fixture goes dark within the THL row, reducing the row's warning conspicuity even while the RWSL command chain is functioning correctly.
Detection Method
Test activation to confirm which unit fails to light, series loop continuity check through the fixture's transformer, and megger testing of the winding.
Health/Status Monitoring Link Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Broken or misconfigured reporting link between the light control and monitoring subsystem and the tower's ATC display, independent of fixture health.
Effect
Loss of fixture-health visibility at the tower even though the THL row itself continues to operate correctly, delaying awareness of a genuine fixture fault.
Detection Method
Manual row activation to confirm fixture operation is unaffected, followed by a known fixture-health test event to verify it registers correctly at the ATC display.
Lens Contamination or Erosion
Low Severity
Root Cause
Accumulated tyre rubber deposits from repeated take-off rolls, or a hazed/eroded lens from prolonged jet-blast and wheel-load exposure at the flush-mounted position.
Effect
Reduced or uneven light output that can fall below ICAO/FAA photometric thresholds, weakening the row's visual warning at the take-off position.
Detection Method
Scheduled visual inspection for rubber film or lens degradation, backed by photometric intensity testing when contamination alone doesn't explain the shortfall.
L-823 Connector / Series Circuit Insulation Breakdown
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress at the base can, gaskets, or connectors under the runway pavement, compounded by wheel-load and jet-blast stress on the ducted cable run to the fixture.
Effect
Intermittent operation or a partial ground fault that degrades insulation resistance on the series circuit serving the THL position.
Detection Method
Megger testing of the isolation transformer and series circuit segment, and visual inspection of base can seals, top-cap O-rings, and connector torque.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

THL fixtures sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the base can before opening it or handling connectors.

Take-Off Roll Zone Coordination
Operational

These fixtures sit directly in the take-off roll path; work here requires full runway closure with ATC coordination and a NOTAM, not a live-traffic gap, since the location offers no separation margin from a departing aircraft.

Surveillance Interlock Dependency
Operational

The red hold indication is driven entirely by the RWSL surveillance feed; any work on the fixture or control cabinet must be followed by an integration check confirming the interlock still correctly activates before returning the system to service.

Wheel-Load & Jet-Blast Rating
Mechanical

The inset fixture must sit precisely flush with the pavement and be rated for the full wheel-load and jet-blast environment of the take-off roll; a proud edge or depression here is a high-speed FOD and wheel-impact risk.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or leave a gap in the hold-light row.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not return the fixture to service with an unverified surveillance interlock; confirm activation timing, coordination with the adjacent runway guard lights, and photometric conformance before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Sub-Second Timing Is a Health Metric in Its Own Right
Verification

Because illumination has to happen in well under a second directly from the surveillance/processing chain, response latency is worth trending after any processing system update, not just occupancy accuracy. A system that eventually reaches the right answer but too slowly has failed its purpose just as surely as one that gets the wrong answer.

Shared Sensor Layer Means Shared Failure Modes
Surveillance

THL shares its surveillance and processing layer with the REL and RIL functions, so a coverage gap or calibration drift in the radar/ADS-B/multilateration feed can degrade all three simultaneously even though only THL is being watched during a routine check. Treat a THL anomaly as a prompt to verify REL/RIL behaviour too.

Diagnose Faults With a Fail-Safe Mindset
Failure Mode

The system's entire safety case rests on illuminating when a hazard is present, so any reported fault should be characterised by which way it fails — a fixture stuck lit is an operational nuisance, one that can't illuminate on command is a runway-incursion risk requiring immediate action, and the two shouldn't be triaged the same way.

Wheel-Load Rating at Rotation Speed, Not Taxi Speed
Mechanical

The THL fixture sits in the same in-pavement environment as any inset fixture, but sees rotation and rejected-takeoff speeds rather than taxi speeds. A seating tolerance that would be a minor issue on a taxiway centreline light is a higher-consequence FOD and wheel-impact risk here.

Functional Tests Need Their Own Safety Coordination
Operational

A test illumination of the THL fixture or algorithm can be mistaken for a genuine hold indication by a crew monitoring the frequency or a controller glancing at the runway if it isn't clearly flagged in advance. Build test coordination into the work plan as its own safety item, not an afterthought to the electrical work.

Health Feedback and Command Are Separate Paths
Architecture

Fixture health is reported to the tower display over a separate path from the illuminate/extinguish command itself, so a healthy status readout doesn't guarantee the command path is functioning, and vice versa. Verify both paths independently after any processing system or cabinet work, not just the one you touched.

Corrective Action: Restore the processing-to-control-subsystem link if the command is not reaching the array, or repair/replace the control and monitoring subsystem interface; escalate a persistent algorithm-side fault to RWSL system maintenance.
