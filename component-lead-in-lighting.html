Lead‑in Lighting

A specialized approach lighting system that guides aircraft from the en‑route environment onto the final approach path. Lead‑in lighting is typically used at airports with offset approach paths, where the runway is not aligned with the standard approach corridor, or where terrain/obstacle constraints require a non‑straight‑in approach.

Overview

What Is It?

Lead‑in Lighting (also known as approach path guidance lighting) is a series of lights that provide a visual track for pilots to follow when the runway is not aligned with the standard straight‑in approach. These lights are typically installed on a curved or angled path that guides the aircraft from a point in the en‑route airspace to the runway threshold. Lead‑in lighting is essential at airports where terrain, obstacles, noise abatement, or airspace constraints prevent a conventional straight‑in approach.

The system consists of a row of lights (often omni‑directional or uni‑directional) that are spaced at regular intervals along the desired flight path. They may be steady‑burning or flashing, and are often supplemented with sequenced flashing lights to indicate direction. Lead‑in lighting is a cost‑effective alternative to full precision approach systems for airports with non‑standard approach geometries.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Lead-in lighting exists to make an otherwise unattainable approach flyable at night or in marginal visibility, at airports where terrain, obstacles, noise restrictions, or airspace conflicts rule out a standard straight-in approach altogether. Without a visual path to follow, a pilot flying a curved or offset approach in the dark has no reliable way to judge whether they're tracking the safe corridor toward the runway, so the lighting substitutes for the straight-line visual cues a conventional approach would otherwise provide. Its purpose is therefore not to refine an already-standard approach but to enable a non-standard one to be flown safely at all, offering airports with awkward geometry a workable alternative to the far more expensive option of re-routing traffic or restricting operations to daylight visual conditions only.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Lead‑in lighting is deployed at airports and heliports where approach paths are not straight‑in:

  • Airports with offset approaches – where the runway is not aligned with the prevailing approach direction due to terrain or obstacles.
  • Heliports – where helicopters approach from varying directions and require visual guidance.
  • Water aerodromes – where floatplanes require a marked approach path over water.
  • Noise‑sensitive airports – where approaches are designed to avoid residential areas.
  • Military airfields – where tactical approaches require non‑standard flight paths.
  • Temporary or remote airstrips – where a simple visual guidance system is needed.
Placement

Installation & Location

Lead-in fixtures are set out along the curved offset path itself rather than on a straight pier line, each fixture mounted on its own frangible pole and foundation positioned to trace the surveyed curve from the point where the approach deviates from straight-in through to where it merges into the standard approach lighting centreline. Pole spacing follows the curvature rather than a fixed interval, tightening through the sharpest part of the bend so the row reads as a continuous curved line to the pilot. Cabling follows the same curved alignment in duct or direct-buried cable back to the approach lighting control cabinet, with each fixture's isolating transformer at its pole base; the whole layout is set out and re-surveyed against the offset-approach procedure design rather than the runway centreline extension.

Function

How Does It Work?

Lead‑in lighting provides a visual path that the pilot follows from the approach start to the runway threshold:

  • Configuration: A row of lights is installed along the desired approach path, typically starting several kilometres from the runway and ending at the threshold. The lights are spaced at intervals (e.g., 100–300 m) and may be elevated or flush‑mounted.
  • Lighting Characteristics: The lights are typically white, steady‑burning or flashing, with intensity controlled by a CCR. In some systems, they flash in sequence to indicate the direction of travel (like a “chase” effect).
  • Guidance: The pilot visually aligns the aircraft with the row of lights, maintaining the path. The spacing and colour provide distance and alignment cues. When the lead‑in lights merge with the approach lighting system, the pilot transitions to the standard approach lights for final alignment.
  • Integration: Lead‑in lighting is often integrated with the runway approach lighting system, with the final lights merging into the approach lighting structure (e.g., ALSF‑1/2, SALS, etc.). They are controlled by the same CCR and ALCMS.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Because a lead-in row can run for several kilometres before it ever reaches the approach lighting system, its control architecture has to solve a distance problem that a compact runway or taxiway loop does not. Brightness is still set by the ALCMS selecting a CCR step, but a single series circuit run over that distance would be impractical to regulate and fault-find, so the path is typically fed from one or more intermediate control points along its length rather than a single CCR at the runway end — keeping voltage drop and fault-loop length within workable limits. Where the lights flash in a "chase" sequence rather than burning steady, a dedicated sequence controller drives that timing layer independently of the CCR brightness layer, the same separation used on sequenced flashing lights. At the point where the lead-in row physically merges into the approach lighting structure, control also merges: the final lead-in fixtures are typically absorbed into the approach lighting circuit and ALCMS command set, so from that point onward the lead-in system is switched and monitored as part of the approach lighting system rather than as a separate circuit.

Construction

Physical Construction

Lead-in lighting fixtures follow the same elevated or flush-mounted construction as standard approach lights, adapted for installation over varied and often uneven terrain along the curved or angled path. Elevated units use a die-cast aluminium housing on a frangible coupling, seated on an L-867 base can whose mounting stake or footing is engineered for the specific ground conditions encountered along the lead-in route, which may include soft ground, embankments, or shallow water crossings; flush units instead sit in an L-868 base can where the terrain permits pavement or hard-standing installation. The optical assembly is gasket-sealed behind a polycarbonate or glass lens to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating, since fixtures along a multi-kilometre lead-in route are frequently far from routine access and must tolerate prolonged exposure to moisture and weather. Because the lead-in path can run for a kilometre or more, cable runs between fixtures are longer than in compact approach arrays, so L-823 connectors and the associated isolating transformer per light are sized and routed with attention to voltage drop and cable protection along the full length. Stainless-steel fasteners and corrosion-resistant coatings are standard, given the extended, often exposed, siting of these fixtures away from the immediate airfield.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Curve Set-Out & Survey
Civil

Set out each pole or base can position against the surveyed offset-approach curve rather than a straight pier line, re-checking spacing through the tightest part of the bend before excavation begins.

Foundation Engineering for Variable Terrain
Civil

Engineer each pole footing or base can to the specific ground conditions encountered along the route, including soft ground, embankments, or shallow water crossings, rather than a single standard foundation design.

Sightline & Vegetation Clearance
Civil

Clear vegetation and obstructions along the full curved path before fixtures are set, since the lead-in only works if the row is visible as a continuous line from the offset entry point onward.

Long-Run Cable & Connector Termination
Electrical

Route and terminate L-823 connectors along the multi-kilometre run with voltage drop and cable protection accounted for over the full length, sealing each splice to a submersible rating before backfill.

Per-Light Isolating Transformer Matching
Electrical

Confirm the isolating transformer at each fixture matches the series circuit's rated current step, since voltage drop across a long lead-in run makes mismatches harder to diagnose after energisation.

Frangible Coupling / Base Can Fixing
Mechanical

Torque the frangible coupling or fixture fixing bolts to the specified value on elevated units, checking the fitting shears as designed under lateral load and does not bind.

Aim Setting Along the Approach Track
Mechanical

Aim each fixture toward the approaching aircraft along the design approach track, verifying alignment against the curve diagram rather than a single fixed bearing.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record pole/base can position, cable route, connector seal, and torque values as-built before backfill, since these are not inspectable once the site is closed.

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify beam intensity against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer at representative points along the lead-in line; degraded output usually points to lens haze or an aging light source.

Site Survey & Vegetation Clearance
Semi-annual

Walk or fly the full length of the lead-in line to confirm no trees, vegetation, or new structures have encroached on the visual path since the last check.

Alignment Verification
Annual

Check each light's position against the intended track using GPS or a theodolite, since ground settlement or cumulative survey drift can pull the line off course over long distances.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Wipe down lenses to remove dust, pollen, and weathering film so beam output stays consistent along the entire lead-in run.

Cable Route Inspection
Annual

Inspect the buried cable route for signs of excavation damage, animal intrusion, or erosion exposure, since lead-in circuits often run long distances through varied terrain.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test the isolation transformers and series circuit to confirm insulation resistance stays above the minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a series-circuit fault.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Obstruction and Vegetation Growth

Lead‑in lights are often installed over long distances across varied terrain. Trees, vegetation, or new structures can obscure the lights over time. Regular site surveys and vegetation clearance are essential to maintain the visual path.

Light Spacing and Alignment Drift

Long rows of lights can suffer from cumulative alignment errors. Ground settlement or inaccurate initial surveying can cause the lights to deviate from the intended path. Periodic alignment checks using GPS or theodolites are recommended.

Power Supply and Cable Integrity

Lead‑in lighting systems often cover long distances, making them vulnerable to cable damage from excavation, animals, or weather. Regular insulation testing and visual inspection of the cable route are necessary.

Flashing Synchronisation (if used)

If the lead‑in lights flash in sequence, synchronisation errors can occur, confusing pilots. Routine testing of the flash pattern and timing is critical, especially before periods of low visibility.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Single Fixture Dark, Rest of Row Normal
Likely Cause
Failed LED module / lamp, or an open isolating transformer (blown primary fuse-wire link or failed secondary winding).
Diagnostic Steps
Confirm series loop continuity through the fixture's transformer primary; megger the transformer; check secondary voltage at the connector with the CCR energised.
Corrective Action
Replace the LED module/lamp if the transformer checks healthy; otherwise swap the isolating transformer and re-seal the L-823 connectors before backfill.
Entire Row Dark / CCR Will Not Regulate
Likely Cause
Open-circuit fault somewhere along the long series cable run (severed cable, corroded splice, excavation damage, or a failed transformer acting as an open point).
Diagnostic Steps
Check the CCR fault/open-circuit indication first; loop-resistance test from the CCR output to isolate the faulted segment; sectionalise and megger the long cable run to narrow down the break.
Corrective Action
Excavate and repair or replace the faulted cable section and connector; re-test insulation resistance before reinstating the circuit.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
Moisture ingress at a fixture or connector along the route has degraded insulation resistance, creating a short or a partial ground fault on the series loop.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the full circuit to identify the low-resistance segment; visually inspect suspect fixtures for cracked lenses, condensation, or damaged secondary cable jackets.
Corrective Action
Dry out or replace the affected fixture/connector, renew O-rings and gaskets, and confirm insulation resistance is back above the acceptable minimum before re-energising.
Flashing Sequence Out of Sync (Where Fitted)
Likely Cause
Timing drift or fault in the sequencer/flasher controller, or a signal-cable fault between the controller and a downstream fixture.
Diagnostic Steps
Observe the flash pattern against the specified timing; check controller diagnostics and firmware/timing settings; test signal continuity to the affected fixture.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or replace the sequencer/flasher controller, and repair or replace the faulted signal cable.
Reduced or Obscured Light Output
Likely Cause
Vegetation growth, new structures, or a hazy lens has partially obstructed the visual path along the lead-in line.
Diagnostic Steps
Walk or fly the approach path to confirm the obstruction; run a photometric check on affected fixtures; inspect and clean lenses.
Corrective Action
Clear vegetation or flag the obstruction for removal, clean or replace degraded lenses, and re-verify with a follow-up photometric reading.
Cumulative Alignment Drift Along the Row
Likely Cause
Ground settlement or an inaccurate initial survey has caused individual fixtures to deviate from the intended approach path over the long run.
Diagnostic Steps
Re-survey the fixture line using GPS or a theodolite; compare against the design centreline; check individual fixture aim.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-aim drifted fixtures, and address any localised subgrade settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

LED Module / Lamp Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Solder-joint fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, moisture ingress through a compromised LED board seal, or ordinary end-of-life lumen depreciation.
Effect
Single fixture goes dark or drops below minimum intensity while the rest of the row continues to operate normally, creating a gap in the guidance line toward the runway.
Detection Method
Nighttime visual patrol, periodic photometric intensity survey, and continuity/secondary-voltage checks at the fixture connector.
Isolating Transformer Winding Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress leading to insulation breakdown, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge.
Effect
Open-circuit point in the series loop; can darken the single fixture or, if the open extends to the loop itself, take down the entire lead-in line.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
L-823 Connector / Splice Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating or crimping during installation, gasket failure allowing groundwater ingress, or corrosion building up on the pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent operation, flickering, or a partial ground fault that degrades circuit insulation resistance along the long feeder run.
Detection Method
Insulation resistance (megger) testing of the circuit and visual inspection of connectors at each fixture base during maintenance.
Long-Run Series Cable Insulation Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Cable jacket damage from nearby excavation, age-related insulation degradation, or prolonged standing water in the duct run over the extended cable length feeding the approach.
Effect
Ground fault or short circuit that trips the CCR or darkens the entire lead-in line, which can extend well beyond the aerodrome boundary.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger test to flag the low-resistance segment, followed by sectionalised testing or time-domain reflectometry to pinpoint the fault location.
Sequencer / Flasher Controller Fault (Where Fitted)
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Timing drift or an internal fault in the sequencer/flasher controller, or a signal-cable fault between the controller and a downstream fixture.
Effect
Flashing sequence stops, runs out of sync, or a fixture fails to fire in turn, weakening the moving-light cue that draws the pilot's eye along the approach.
Detection Method
Observation of the flash pattern against specified timing, controller diagnostics, and signal-continuity testing to the affected fixture.
Frangible Coupling Fatigue / Fracture
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cyclic loading from wind or incidental contact, material fatigue over the service interval, or corrosion at the coupling threads on elevated units.
Effect
Fixture leans, loosens, or separates entirely from its base, taking the light out of tolerance or out of service.
Detection Method
Physical inspection for cracking or play in the coupling, and periodic fixture-aim/level checks against tolerance.
CCR Regulation Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal component failure (control board or output stage), a load mismatch after circuit modification, or a firmware/setpoint fault.
Effect
Incorrect brightness step, failure to hold regulated current across the loop, or nuisance tripping on healthy circuits.
Detection Method
CCR self-diagnostic/fault indication, output current verification, and a load-bank test to confirm regulation across all intensity steps.
Cumulative Fixture-Line Alignment Drift
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ground settlement or an inaccurate initial survey causing individual fixtures to deviate from the intended approach path over the long run.
Effect
The guidance line no longer tracks a true extension of the runway centreline, degrading the visual cue on approach.
Detection Method
Re-survey of the fixture line using GPS or a theodolite compared against the design centreline, with individual fixture aim checks.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lead-in fixtures sit on a constant-current series circuit that can carry several kV across the loop. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the pole base before climbing or handling connectors.

Elevated Pole Work in Remote Terrain
Personnel Safety

Because the curved offset path often crosses terrain, obstacles, or difficult ground that a straight approach avoids, confirm safe access and use fall protection appropriate to pole height before working at any individual fixture along the route.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute a frangible pole with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; each pole must still shear cleanly if struck by an aircraft following the offset approach path.

Path Continuity Is Safety-Critical
Compliance

Because lead-in lighting substitutes for a straight-in visual reference on a non-standard approach geometry, a single dark or misaligned fixture can break the pilot's only guidance through the curve; verify the fixture's position against the offset-approach survey, not just against its neighbours, before closing out.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or darken a section of the curved path.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture dark, misaligned, or at reduced intensity after maintenance; verify photometric conformance and re-survey position before closing out and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Curve Geometry Drives Spacing, Not a Standard Table
Design

Because lead-in lighting follows a curved or angled path rather than a straight centreline, fixture spacing needs tightening on the tighter-radius sections to preserve the same apparent guidance density a pilot gets on a straight approach; don't apply a single straight-line spacing table to the whole curve.

Verify the Track Still Matches the Published Procedure
Compliance

Lead-in lighting guides aircraft along a specific charted path; any change to the published approach procedure or the associated obstacle clearance surface needs a corresponding check that the physical lighting track still matches what's charted, not just that the fixtures still work.

Terrain-Following Fixtures See Uneven Settlement
Mechanical

Lead-in lighting often crosses terrain that a straight runway approach wouldn't, including cut-and-fill sections with different soil types; expect uneven foundation settlement along the run and resurvey fixture elevation and aim periodically rather than assuming uniform settlement across the whole path.

Match Intensity Steps to the Standard Approach Lighting
Retrofit

Where lead-in lighting joins a standard approach lighting system near the threshold, an LED retrofit's brightness-step curve needs validating against the transition point specifically; a visible brightness jump where the two systems meet undercuts the smooth guidance the lead-in path exists to provide.

Remote Sections Can Silently Fall Onto a Longer Interval
Operational

Lead-in lighting sections furthest from the runway are often the least convenient to reach and can silently fall onto a longer inspection interval than the rest of the system; schedule inspection by circuit criticality rather than by physical accessibility.

Trend Loop Readings Across the Full Run
Troubleshooting

A long lead-in lighting circuit accumulates more total splice and connector points than a short runway feeder of the same design; trend insulation resistance for the whole run rather than assuming a fault will always show up near the end closest to the regulator.
