Taxiway Lighting Systems

The visual guidance system for safe aircraft movement on the ground. Taxiway lighting provides pilots with continuous visual guidance while aircraft taxi between runways, aprons, terminals and other operational areas — particularly during darkness and reduced-visibility conditions. It's not a row of lights along a pavement; it's an integrated system of geometry, fixtures, series circuits, CCRs, isolation transformers, cables, connectors, signs, control, monitoring and photometric performance.

Overview

What Is a Taxiway Lighting System?

A taxiway lighting system is the coordinated arrangement of visual aids used to identify taxiways, provide directional guidance and assist aircraft movement on the aerodrome surface.

Depending on the airport configuration and applicable operational requirements, a taxiway system may include taxiway edge lights, taxiway centreline lights, stop bars, runway guard lights, centreline lead-on lights, centreline lead-off lights, illuminated taxiway signs, runway designation signs, information signs and other associated visual aids. The exact configuration depends on the taxiway layout, operational requirements, visibility conditions and applicable standards.

Not Just a Row of Lights

A taxiway lighting system is an integrated system involving geometry, fixtures, series circuits, CCRs, isolation transformers, cables, connectors, signs, and control & monitoring.

Continuous Guidance

Once an aircraft leaves the runway, pilots still need continuous visual information to navigate the airport surface safely.

Verified, Not Assumed

A light being ON does not automatically mean it is performing correctly — electrical testing, inspection and photometry confirm actual performance.

Purpose

Why Taxiway Lighting Is Important

Once an aircraft leaves the runway, pilots still need continuous visual information to navigate the airport surface. Taxiway lighting helps pilots understand where they are, where they should go, and where they must stop.

Where is the taxiway?

Edge and centreline lighting define the usable pavement and the intended taxi path.

Where are its edges?

Edge lights mark the lateral boundary of the taxiway, particularly in darkness and low visibility.

Which direction to follow?

Centreline and lead-on/lead-off lighting give directional continuity through the route.

Where does it meet a runway?

Intersection lighting coordinates taxiway, holding position and runway protection.

Where must it stop?

Stop bars provide a defined, controlled stopping point ahead of a runway or restricted area.

Which route to take?

Signs and lighting work together to communicate the correct taxi route to the pilot.

Core Elements

Main Taxiway Lighting Elements

Each element of a taxiway lighting system serves a distinct guidance function. Together they form a continuous visual language for ground movement.

Taxiway Edge Lights

Identify the lateral boundaries of a taxiway, giving pilots visual information about the usable taxiway area — particularly during darkness and reduced visibility. Colour and placement depend on the applicable requirements.

Taxiway Centreline Lights

Provide guidance along the intended aircraft taxi path and are particularly important where enhanced visual guidance is required. Colour characteristics can vary by location and function.

Centreline lighting provides directional guidance, not simply pavement illumination.

Centreline Lead-On Lighting

Guides an aircraft from one taxiway route toward another operational route — particularly useful where geometry requires clear directional guidance. Design considers geometry, intersections, sequence, colour and control.

Centreline Lead-Off Lighting

Assists aircraft leaving a runway and transitioning onto a taxiway, creating a visual connection from runway → runway exit → taxiway through the transition.

Stop Bar Lighting

Provides a control point at taxiway/runway intersections and other designated locations, typically using red lighting to indicate where aircraft should stop. Part of runway-incursion prevention, not just another taxiway light.

Runway Guard Lights

Provide additional visual warning at runway/taxiway intersections, drawing attention to the runway holding position. May use elevated fixtures, inset fixtures, or other configurations depending on the system.

Stop Bars & Guard Lights

Operational Control at the Runway Boundary

Stop Bar Coordination

A stop bar's design must be coordinated with taxiway centreline lighting, runway protection, the control system, ATC operational procedures, monitoring and switching logic.

Runway Guard Purpose

Runway guard lighting serves a purpose fundamentally different from ordinary taxiway edge lighting — it exists specifically to draw pilot attention to the holding position.

The Runway Holding Position — Plan View

Taxiway centreline leads to the stop bar; runway guard lights flank the holding position just before the runway edge

The stop bar and runway guard lights work together with taxiway centreline and control logic — an aircraft should not cross an illuminated stop bar without ATC clearance.

Quick Answers

Taxiway edge light color: blueTaxiway centerline color: greenStop bar light colors: redRunway guard light colors: yellow

What is the difference between runway edge and taxiway edge lights?

Runway edge lights are white, turning yellow near the end of an instrument runway, and define the runway limits. Taxiway edge lights are blue and define the taxiway limits. The two also differ in typical intensity and the standards that apply, so one must never be substituted for the other.

What is the difference between runway centerline and taxiway centerline lights?

Runway centerline lights are white, then alternate red and white, then red toward the runway end. Taxiway centerline lights are green, with alternating green and yellow (first light green, last light yellow) where the line lies inside the ILS/MLS critical or sensitive area on a runway exit. The colours are different, so the pilot always knows which kind of surface they are on.

Holding Position Systems

Stop Bar Lighting System & Runway Guard Light System: Colours & Installation

How stop bars and runway guard lights are arranged, coloured and installed at a runway holding position. See also Runway Guard Lights & Stop Bars.

Stop Bar Lighting System

A stop bar lighting system is a row of red lights across the taxiway at the holding position, switched on to stop traffic and off to let it proceed. It is controlled together with the taxiway centreline lights beyond it.

Stop Bar and Runway Guard Light Colors

Stop bar lights are red, shown toward the approaching aircraft. Runway guard lights are yellow and flash alternately, so that the two systems are not confused with each other.

Runway Guard Light System

A runway guard light system is installed at a runway holding position, either as a pair of elevated flashing lights at the sides of the taxiway or as a row of in-pavement lights across it.

Runway Guard Light Installation

Runway guard light installation and stop bar installation both depend on correct base position, aiming toward the approaching aircraft, cable routing and connection to the control system. Mistakes in any of these show up as poor visibility or wrong switching.

Switching & Control

Stop bars are normally switched in sections through the control and monitoring system, with the logic coordinated with the centreline lights and ATC procedures.

Verification

Stop bar and guard light installations are checked for colour, aiming, intensity and correct switching during commissioning and periodically after that.

Layout & Intersections

Taxiway Lighting Layout

Taxiway lighting must follow the physical geometry of the airport: centreline, edges, curves, intersections, holding positions, runway intersections, apron connections, aircraft stand routes, taxiway widths and associated signs.

A fixture may be electrically perfect but still provide poor guidance if it is incorrectly located or aligned.

Intersections require special attention because multiple aircraft routes converge. The design coordinates taxiway edge, taxiway centreline, runway holding position, stop bar, runway guard lights, taxiway signs and control logic. A poorly coordinated intersection can create confusing visual information even when every individual light is operating correctly.

Taxiway Intersection — Lead-On / Lead-Off Guidance

Lead-off guidance curves an aircraft off the runway onto the taxiway; lead-on guidance directs it onward to the next route

The portion of exit centreline lighting inside the ILS/MLS critical or sensitive area (or the inner transitional surface limit, whichever the standard applies) is alternating green and yellow, beginning with green and ending with yellow; the continuing taxiway centreline (lead-on) is green. Exact colour, extent and applicability depend on the runway/taxiway category and the applicable ICAO/FAA/project requirement.
Layout Details

Intersections, Holding Positions, Signs, Bases & Aiming

The detail that turns a taxiway lighting layout into usable guidance.

Taxiway Runway Intersection Lights

Taxiway runway intersection lights are the lead-on and lead-off centreline lights, holding position lights and stop bars where a taxiway meets a runway. Coordinate them with the signs and the control logic.

Taxiway Holding Position Lights

Taxiway holding position lights include the stop bar and runway guard lights at a runway holding position, and intermediate holding position lights elsewhere. Their colour and switching must be consistent with the signs.

Taxiway Straight Section Lighting

Taxiway straight section lighting uses wider centreline spacing than curves, where lights are placed closer together to follow the turn. Exact spacing comes from the applicable standard.

Taxiway Lighting Signs

Taxiway lighting signs are illuminated location, direction and destination signs that work with the lights to guide the pilot. See taxiway guidance signs.

Runway Lighting Signs

Runway lighting signs, such as runway designation and holding position signs, are the signs found at runway entry points. They are signs, not lights, but they are designed together with the runway-related lighting.

Taxiway Light Aiming & Alignment

Taxiway light aiming and taxiway light alignment matter most for unidirectional lights such as stop bars and some centreline fixtures. Blue edge lights are omnidirectional and mainly need correct position and height.

Taxiway Light Base

The taxiway light base is the in-ground base or can that carries the fixture and receives the cable connection. Correct base position, depth and orientation make correct aiming and sealing possible.

Design

Taxiway Lighting Requirements, Design & Circuit Design

Requirements and design decisions that come before any fixture is installed.

Taxiway Lighting Requirements

Taxiway lighting requirements depend on the operations the taxiway serves, such as night or low-visibility movements, the taxiway type and the applicable ICAO, FAA or national standard. See Standards Companion.

Taxiway Lighting Design

Taxiway lighting design sets the fixture types, spacing, colours, intersections, holding positions and signs for the airport layout. It is coordinated with the control logic and the circuit design.

Airport Lighting Circuit Design

Airport lighting circuit design groups lights into series circuits, sizes the CCR and cable, and decides how circuits are split so one fault does not blank a whole taxiway. See AGL Design & Electrical Engineering.

AGL Design Basics

The AGL design basics are layout, colour coding, series circuit design, control and the checks that confirm the result. The same principles apply across taxiway, runway and approach systems.

Halogen Taxiway Lights

Halogen taxiway lights use lamps that need regular replacement and draw more power than LED fixtures. LED conversion is a common upgrade, with the circuit load and CCR compatibility (minimum load, power factor, waveform) rechecked afterwards.

Coordination

Requirements, design and operations have to agree, so the lighting is coordinated with markings, signs, the stop bar logic and the ATC procedures.

Circuit Components

CCR, Isolation Transformers & Connectors

Constant Current Regulator

Provides controlled current to the series circuit. The system may use different intensity levels depending on operational requirements. CCR design should consider fixture count and load, transformer characteristics, circuit length, total circuit load, required intensity steps, and control & monitoring requirements — not simply fixture count.

Isolation Transformers

Provide the electrical interface between the primary series circuit and individual fixtures (Series Circuit → Transformer Primary → Transformer Secondary → Fixture). Selection should be coordinated with fixture rating, circuit current, secondary requirements, installation conditions and the applicable specification.

Connectors

Interface between series-circuit cable, isolation transformer and fixture (primary connector, secondary connector, plug, receptacle, connector kits). A poor connector causes high resistance → heating → intermittent operation → fixture failure → circuit problems, so inspection is a key part of maintenance.

Taxiway Lighting Remote Control & ALCMS

How taxiway lighting is selected, switched and monitored from a distance.

Taxiway Lighting Remote Control

Taxiway lighting remote control lets the tower or a control room switch taxiway circuits, select routes and change intensity without anyone going to the substation. It is the normal way taxiway lighting is operated.

Verification

Photometry & Electrical Testing

A taxiway light being ON does not automatically mean that it is performing correctly. Electrical testing, visual inspection and photometry together provide a complete picture of taxiway lighting performance.

Photometry

Evaluates intensity, distribution, colour, alignment, fixture-to-fixture variation and overall system performance. A fixture can appear normal on visual inspection yet produce significantly lower intensity than expected.

Electrical Testing

Series-circuit continuity, insulation resistance, cable testing, isolation transformer testing, connector inspection, CCR testing, circuit current verification, control-system testing, monitoring/fault verification, and earthing & bonding checks where applicable.

Mechanical Inspection

Fixture housing, lens, optical surfaces, mounting, alignment, fasteners, frangible components, water ingress, corrosion, cable entry and connector condition. Taxiway fixtures are exposed to aircraft, vehicle, weather and maintenance activity.

Taxiway Lighting Acceptance Test & Taxiway Lighting Test Report

The taxiway lighting acceptance test is the formal check of the installation against the specification, for acceptance by the owner. It is witnessed and recorded. The taxiway lighting test report then records all results, the instruments used and the outcome, and is signed and kept with the as-built record.

Taxiway Lighting Electrical Test

The taxiway lighting electrical test confirms the electrical condition of the circuits and equipment. It includes the insulation and continuity tests, and it does not replace photometry, which checks the optical performance of the same lights.

Taxiway Lighting Insulation Test

The taxiway lighting insulation test checks the insulation of cables and equipment, for example with a megger. Acceptable values come from the applicable specification.

Taxiway Lighting Continuity Test

The taxiway lighting continuity test confirms that the series circuit is continuous and correctly connected. It is run before the circuit is energised.

Troubleshooting

A Practical Troubleshooting Approach

Symptom: one taxiway light is dark. Work through the checks in sequence, and for multiple failures move the investigation upstream.

1

Is the series circuit operating?

Confirm the circuit itself is energized and carrying the correct current.

2

Is the isolation transformer healthy?

Check the transformer's primary and secondary performance.

3

Are the connectors secure?

Inspect primary and secondary connectors for resistance, heating or damage.

4

Is the fixture receiving the correct secondary supply?

Verify voltage/current reaching the fixture itself.

5

Is the fixture itself healthy?

Check the fixture's internal condition, LED/lamp and optics.

6

Verify after correction

Confirm operation and optical performance once the fault is corrected.

For multiple failures, investigation should move upstream toward: Fixture → Transformer → Connector → Cable → Series Circuit → CCR → Control.

Failures

What Happens When Taxiway Lighting Fails?

Failures range from one lamp to a whole circuit, and the response depends on how much guidance is lost.

What Happens When Taxiway Lighting Fails?

The effect depends on how much is out. Isolated failures are managed within the aerodrome's serviceability limits. A larger failure, or one affecting a stop bar or a holding position, can restrict taxiing, require a NOTAM and call for follow-me or procedural control until it is restored.

Taxiway Light Not Working

A taxiway light not working may be a lamp, transformer, connector, cable or CCR problem. Fault finding starts from the circuit status and current. See the troubleshooting engine.

AAI Taxiway Lighting

The Airports Authority of India (AAI) operates a large share of Indian airports. AAI taxiway lighting is specified and maintained in line with ICAO Annex 14 as implemented through DGCA regulation and AAI's own technical specifications.

Maintenance

Taxiway Lighting Maintenance

A practical maintenance program covers four areas, moving beyond "replace the light when it fails" toward a structured inspect–measure–diagnose–correct–verify cycle.

Electrical

Check circuit, CCR, transformers, cables and connections.

Mechanical

Inspect fixture condition, mounting, seals and physical damage.

Optical

Inspect lenses, LEDs and optical assemblies.

Photometric

Measure lighting performance where required.

Inspect → Measure → Diagnose → Correct → Verify — not simply "replace the light when it fails."

Principle & Design

Taxiway Lighting Working Principle, Functions, Design & Operations

Taxiway Lighting Working Principle

Taxiway lighting working principle: like the rest of AGL, taxiway lights run on series circuits fed by constant current regulators, each light on an isolating transformer. Circuits can be split so segments are switched separately.

Taxiway Lighting Functions

Taxiway lighting functions are to mark the edges and centreline of taxiways, guide aircraft between runway and apron, and mark holding positions and intersections so pilots stay on the correct route.

Taxiway Lighting Design

Taxiway lighting design covers edge and centreline layout, spacing (closer on curves), colour, circuit split for selective switching, stop bar and guard light integration, and the control interface.

Taxiway Lighting at Different Airports

Taxiway lighting for airports ranges from edge lights alone at small aerodromes to centreline, stop bar and guidance systems at large airports with low-visibility operations. Choose the level the operations and standards demand.

Taxiway Lighting Operations

Taxiway lighting operations cover switching circuits, selecting intensity and, where installed, switching centreline segments or stop bars on and off to guide aircraft, normally from the tower through the ALCMS.

Taxiway Lighting Selection Guide

Taxiway lighting selection guide: decide edge or centreline guidance from operations and visibility, then choose elevated or inset fixtures, LED or halogen sources and colours that meet the standard.

Taxiway Lighting Technical Guide

Taxiway lighting technical guide: use this page with the aerodrome layout, circuit drawings and manufacturer data to check positions, colours, spacing and electrical details.

Edge & Centreline

Taxiway Edge Lights, Centreline Lighting Requirements & Fixture Types

Taxiway Edge Lights Working Principle

Taxiway edge lights working principle: each blue fixture is fed by an isolating transformer on the series circuit, so a failed lamp does not break the loop, and brightness follows the CCR step.

Taxiway Edge Lights Types

Taxiway edge lights types are elevated fixtures on frangible couplings, and inset fixtures where elevated lights would be a hazard or obstruction, in LED or halogen versions.

Taxiway Edge Lights Functions

Taxiway edge lights functions are to show the lateral limits of the taxiway, especially on curves and intersections, so aircraft stay on the paved surface at night.

Taxiway Edge Lights Components

Taxiway edge lights components are the blue lens, light source, fixture body, frangible coupling or inset housing, isolating transformer, base and connectors.

Taxiway Centreline Lighting Requirements

Taxiway centreline lighting requirements come from ICAO Annex 14 and national rules: green lights along the taxiway centreline, spaced closer on curves, provided where operations in low visibility or complex layouts call for them.

Why Taxiway Lights Are Blue and Green

Taxiway lights blue and green: blue marks the taxiway edges and green marks the centreline. The colour difference lets pilots tell taxiway guidance from runway lighting.

Taxiway Light Fixture Types

Taxiway light fixture types include elevated edge lights, inset centreline lights, stop bar lights, guard lights, and illuminated signs. Each has its own colour, beam pattern and mounting.

Holding & Standards

Holding Position Lights, Rapid Exit Lighting, Signs, ICAO Requirements & Testing

Taxiway Holding Position Lights

Taxiway holding position lights include red stop bars, flashing yellow runway guard lights and intermediate holding position lights. They mark where aircraft must stop and are controlled from the tower.

Taxiway Rapid Exit Lighting

Taxiway rapid exit lighting guides aircraft off the runway at higher speed, using green centreline lights on the exit and yellow rapid exit taxiway indicator lights that show how far the exit is.

Information Signs on Taxiways

Information signs on taxiways show location and direction: direction and destination signs are yellow with black inscription, and location signs are black with yellow inscription. Signs must be lit or reflective as required.

ICAO Requirements for Taxiway Lighting

Taxiway lighting ICAO requirements are in Annex 14 Volume I, Chapter 5, covering taxiway edge, centreline, stop bar and holding position lights, with their colour, intensity and spacing. Check the current edition.

Taxiway Lighting Testing Procedure

Taxiway lighting testing procedure covers insulation and continuity tests, current at every step, aim and colour checks, and control tests for stop bars and selectively switched circuits, with results recorded.

Taxiway Lighting Preventive Maintenance

Taxiway lighting preventive maintenance is a scheduled routine of cleaning, inspection, electrical tests and replacement of worn parts, with extra attention to inset lights on busy taxiways.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is a taxiway lighting system?

It is the coordinated arrangement of visual aids used to identify taxiways, provide directional guidance and assist aircraft movement on the aerodrome surface. It may include taxiway edge and centreline lights, stop bars, runway guard lights, centreline lead-on and lead-off lights, and illuminated signs, depending on layout, operations, visibility and standards.

Q2Why is taxiway lighting important?

Once an aircraft leaves the runway, pilots still need continuous visual information to navigate the surface. Taxiway lighting helps them understand where the taxiway and its edges are, which direction to follow, where it meets a runway, where they must stop and which route to take.

Q3What are the main taxiway lighting elements?

Taxiway edge lights identify lateral boundaries, centreline lights guide along the intended taxi path, lead-on lights guide from one route toward another, lead-off lights help aircraft leave a runway onto a taxiway, stop bars give a controlled stopping point, and runway guard lights add warning at holding positions.

Q4What is the difference between lead-on and lead-off lighting?

Centreline lead-on lighting guides an aircraft from one taxiway route toward another operational route. Lead-off lighting assists aircraft leaving a runway and transitioning onto a taxiway, creating a visual connection from runway to runway exit to taxiway.

Q5How do stop bars and runway guard lights work at a holding position?

The taxiway centreline leads to the stop bar, with runway guard lights flanking the holding position just before the runway edge. They work together with taxiway centreline and control logic, and an aircraft should not cross an illuminated stop bar without ATC clearance.

Q6Why is stop bar design coordinated with other systems?

A stop bar's design must be coordinated with taxiway centreline lighting, runway protection, the control system, ATC operational procedures, monitoring and switching logic. It is part of runway-incursion prevention, not just another taxiway light.

Q7How is runway guard lighting different from taxiway edge lighting?

Runway guard lighting serves a fundamentally different purpose: it exists specifically to draw pilot attention to the holding position, and may use elevated fixtures, inset fixtures or other configurations depending on the system.

Q8What do taxiway lighting colours mean?

Colour is part of the visual coding system, and different colours may identify different functions or locations. Colour selection should always be based on the specific lighting function and applicable ICAO, FAA, national and project requirements.

Q9Does a taxiway light being ON mean it is working correctly?

No. A light being on does not automatically mean it is performing correctly. Electrical testing, inspection and photometry confirm actual performance.

Q10What is the electrical chain behind taxiway lighting?

A simplified chain runs from the airport power supply and AGL control system through the CCR, series circuit and isolation transformers to the taxiway light fixtures, their optical output and the pilot's visual guidance. Systems with stop-bar switching or route-based centreline selection may add dedicated control, switching and monitoring interfaces.

Q11What happens when taxiway lighting fails?

The effect depends on how much of the lighting is out. Isolated failures are managed within the aerodrome's serviceability limits. A larger failure, or one that affects a stop bar or holding position, can restrict taxiing, require a NOTAM and call for procedural control until the lighting is restored.

Q12What is the difference between runway edge and taxiway edge lights?

Runway edge lights are white, turning yellow near the end of an instrument runway, and mark the limits of the runway. Taxiway edge lights are blue and mark the limits of the taxiway. They also differ in typical intensity and in the standards that apply, so the colour tells the pilot which surface they are on.

Q13What is the difference between runway centerline and taxiway centerline lights?

Runway centerline lights are white, then alternate red and white, then red toward the runway end. Taxiway centerline lights are green, with alternating green and yellow (first light green, last light yellow) where the line lies inside the ILS/MLS critical or sensitive area on a runway exit. The different colours let the pilot recognise the surface.

Q14What color are taxiway edge lights?

Taxiway edge lights are blue. They are omnidirectional fixtures that mark the edges of the taxiway.

Q15What is the taxiway centerline color?

The taxiway centerline color is green. Where the centreline lies inside the ILS/MLS critical or sensitive area on a runway exit, the lights alternate green and yellow, starting with green and ending with yellow.

Q16What are the stop bar light colors?

Stop bar lights are red and are shown toward the aircraft approaching the holding position. They are switched on to stop traffic and off to allow it to proceed.

Q17What are the runway guard light colors?

Runway guard lights are yellow. They flash alternately, either as a pair of elevated lights beside the taxiway or as a row of in-pavement lights across it, to warn of an approaching runway holding position.
