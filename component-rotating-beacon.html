Rotating Beacon

The mechanical rotating assembly that forms the core of airport identification beacons. Consists of a motor‑driven turntable, lamp(s) or LED modules, coloured filters, and a parabolic reflector system that produces the characteristic alternating flashes. Used in airport beacons, heliport beacons, and other aviation warning systems where a rotating light pattern is required.

Overview

What Is It?

A Rotating Beacon is the electro‑mechanical assembly that produces the sweeping light beam used in airport and heliport identification beacons. It consists of a sturdy base, an electric motor, a rotating turntable, one or more high‑intensity lamps (or LED modules), colour filters (e.g., green, white, yellow), and a parabolic or ellipsoidal reflector to collimate the light into a narrow, intense beam. As the turntable rotates, the beam sweeps across the sky, creating a flashing effect for observers. The rotation speed is precisely controlled to produce the required flash rate (typically 12–15 RPM, resulting in 24–30 flashes per minute). The rotating beacon is the core component that differentiates a rotating beacon system from a static flashing light.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The rotating beacon assembly exists to make an airport or heliport findable from far outside the terminal area, at night or in conditions where the field itself isn't yet visually distinguishable from surrounding terrain and lights. Rotation is the specific mechanism chosen to achieve wide-area visibility from a single fixed installation: rather than requiring multiple omnidirectional fixtures to cover all approach directions, a single rotating beam sweeps the full horizon, making the airfield's general location and identity legible to a pilot approaching from any direction. The precisely controlled rotation speed, and the resulting flash rate, is itself part of the identification purpose — a beacon's flash rate is a recognizable, standardized signature that helps a pilot distinguish it from other lights on the ground well before the airfield's runway lighting comes into view.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Used as the central element of airport beacons (green/white), heliport beacons (white/yellow or white/red), and water airport beacons (yellow/white). Also used in some obstruction warning systems where a rotating beam is preferred. Typically installed on control towers, hangar roofs, or dedicated masts at airports, heliports, and seaports. The rotating beacon is also found in some marine and coastal warning systems, though aviation applications are the most common.

Placement

Installation & Location

The assembly is installed inside the beacon's weatherproof housing, mounted on the base structure atop a control tower, hangar roof, or dedicated mast at the highest practical point with an unobstructed 360° sweep. The base is bolted to a rigid mounting platform sized to carry the assembly's weight and the dynamic loading from the rotating turntable, with a service platform or access hatch built in for lamp, motor, and lens maintenance. Alignment between the motor, turntable, and lens assembly is set during installation so the beam sweeps evenly without wobble, and the completed assembly is wired to the beacon controller for speed, photocell, and fault-monitoring control. Where the beacon is one of several on a site (for example, a primary and secondary beacon), each assembly is installed and aligned independently but wired into the same controller for consistent operation.

Function

How Does It Work?

The rotating beacon operates by continuously rotating a lamp assembly around a vertical axis. A motor (AC synchronous or DC brushless) drives the turntable at a constant speed via a gearbox or direct drive. The lamp(s) – typically high‑intensity halogen or LED arrays – are mounted on the turntable along with colour filters that alternate between two colours (e.g., green and white). A parabolic reflector behind the lamp focuses the light into a narrow horizontal beam (typically 3–5° wide). As the assembly rotates, the beam sweeps through 360°, and the alternating filters produce distinct colour flashes in sequence. The entire assembly is enclosed in a weatherproof housing with a transparent dome. Power is supplied through slip rings or a rotary transformer to avoid twisting wires.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The rotating beacon runs on its own dedicated circuit — usually a fixed AC supply from the airfield's general lighting feed rather than the constant-current series loop used for edge and centreline lights — with a photocell and/or the ALCMS providing the ON/OFF command based on daylight level, scheduled hours of operation, or (at controlled aerodromes) a manual switch from the tower. That command energises the beacon's drive circuit, which supplies the synchronous or brushless DC motor through a dedicated motor controller that holds rotation at a constant speed, typically 12–15 RPM. Power for the lamp itself crosses the rotating joint through slip rings or a rotary transformer, so the turntable, lamp, and colour filters can spin continuously without twisting the supply cable. The motor controller, or an integral monitoring board, watches rotation speed and lamp current; a motor that slows or stalls, or a lamp that fails to strike, is reported as a beacon fault to the ALCMS or a local alarm panel rather than isolated to a specific part, since the beacon is a single non-redundant rotating unit that either functions as a whole or does not.

Assembly

Inside the Rotating Assembly

Motor & Slip Rings

AC synchronous or DC brushless motor drives the turntable at a constant 12–15 RPM; slip rings feed power across the rotating joint without twisting the cable.

Turntable

Carries the lamp(s) or LED modules and colour filters as a single rotating unit around the vertical axis.

Lamp + Alternating Colour Filters

High-intensity halogen or LED source, paired with two filters (e.g. green and white) that alternate as the assembly turns.

Parabolic Reflector

Collimates the light into a narrow 3–5° horizontal beam behind the lamp before it leaves the housing.

Beam sweeps a full 360° with each rotation — an observer sees a flash each time the beam points at them, twice per revolution if two colours alternate

This mechanical assembly is the shared "engine" behind airport, heliport, and other rotating beacons — swap the filter colours and mounting, and the same rotating optics become a land airport, water airport, or heliport identifier.

Construction

Physical Construction

The assembly is built on a sturdy cast aluminium or steel base that anchors the drive motor, slip-ring assembly, and control electronics beneath the rotating optical head. An AC synchronous or DC brushless motor turns a gear-driven or direct-drive turntable at the precise speed needed to produce 24-30 flashes per minute, with slip rings or rotary transformers used to pass power to the rotating lamp or LED module without twisting the supply cable. The lamp or LED array sits at the focal point of a parabolic or ellipsoidal reflector that collimates the light into a narrow, high-intensity beam, with coloured dichroic or glass filters (green, white, yellow) fixed in the optical path to produce the required colour sequence as the beam sweeps. The entire optical assembly is enclosed within a weatherproof, optically clear dome, typically borosilicate glass or UV-stabilised polycarbonate, sealed to the base housing to achieve an IP66/IP67 rating against rain and dust while withstanding the centrifugal and vibrational loads of continuous rotation. The base bolts to its supporting mast or beacon tower via a flanged mounting plate, and bearings, gears, and slip rings are specified for continuous-duty operation with scheduled lubrication and inspection access built into the housing design.

Prerequisites

Installation Requirements

Base Levelling
Mechanical

Set the flanged mounting plate plumb and level on the mast or tower before bolting, so the turntable tracks true and rotation loads are evenly distributed on the bearings.

Bearing & Gear Lubrication
Mechanical

Confirm bearings, gears, and slip rings are lubricated to specification before first run, since these components are specified for continuous-duty operation.

Colour Filter Fitment
Configuration

Confirm the correct dichroic or glass colour filters (green, white, yellow) are fixed in the optical path in the sequence required for the site.

Slip Ring Wiring
Electrical

Terminate power and control wiring at the slip ring or rotary transformer per manufacturer procedure, checking the supply cable is not twisted or fouled by rotation.

Power Supply Termination
Electrical

Terminate the 230V AC or 24V DC supply, confirming the inverter (where fitted) is correctly rated and wired before energisation.

Dome Seal Check
Mechanical

Confirm the weatherproof dome seats correctly against the base housing to achieve the rated IP66/IP67 protection before commissioning.

Manual Rotation Check
Mechanical

Rotate the assembly by hand before power-up to confirm free movement through a full revolution with no binding or fouled cabling.

Pre-Energisation Sign-off
Documentation

Record base levelling, lubrication check, colour filter fitment, and wiring termination as-built before the assembly is closed out and energised.

Verification

Testing & Commissioning

Insulation Resistance Test
Pre-Energisation

Megger-test the motor windings and slip-ring circuit before energisation, confirming insulation resistance clears the acceptable minimum.

Slip Ring & Brush Contact Check
Pre-Energisation

Inspect slip rings and brush contacts for wear, pitting, or misalignment, since this is the point where power crosses from the fixed base to the rotating turntable.

No-Load Rotation Direction & Speed Check
Pre-Energisation

Bump-start the assembly uncoupled from its optical load to confirm correct rotational direction before final assembly.

Colour Filter Alternation & Flash Rate Verification
Energisation

Confirm the fitted colour filters alternate correctly as the turntable sweeps, and that the resulting flash rate matches the design pattern.

Rotation Speed Verification Under Load
Energisation

With the full optical assembly fitted, confirm turntable speed holds at the design 12–15 RPM under normal running load.

Photometric Intensity Test
Final Acceptance

Measure beam intensity and colour with a calibrated photometer against ICAO/FAA thresholds prior to operational handover.

Vibration & Bearing Run Check
Final Acceptance

Run the assembly continuously and check for abnormal vibration or noise that could indicate bearing damage, imbalance, or gear wear.

Documentation & As-Built Sign-off
Final Acceptance

Record insulation, rotation speed, colour sequence, and photometric readings alongside assembly serial number in the commissioning dossier for handover.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Bearing & Gear Lubrication
Semi-annual

Lubricate motor bearings and gears, and plan replacement every 5–7 years to prevent uneven rotation, vibration, or stoppage.

Slip Ring Inspection
Annual

Clean and inspect slip ring contacts for wear or oxidation, which can cause intermittent power loss or arcing to the rotating lamp assembly.

Dome & Filter Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean dust, bird droppings, and moisture from the rotating dome and colour filters with approved optics-safe solutions to preserve light output and colour fidelity.

Rotation Speed Check
Annual

Verify rotation speed matches the specified flash rate, since motor or gear wear can slow rotation below the required signal frequency.

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Measure beam intensity and colour with a calibrated photometer against applicable obstruction or aerodrome beacon standards.

Mounting & Housing Inspection
Annual

Check the base mount and housing seals for wear or looseness to keep the rotating assembly stable and weatherproof.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Motor Bearing and Gear Wear

Continuous rotation leads to bearing and gear wear, causing uneven rotation, vibration, or stoppage. Regular lubrication and periodic bearing replacement (every 5–7 years) is essential.

Slip Ring Contact Failure

Slip rings transmit power to the rotating lamp assembly. Over time, contacts can wear or oxidize, causing intermittent power loss or arcing. Periodic cleaning and inspection of slip rings is critical.

Filter and Lens Contamination

Dust, bird droppings, and moisture can accumulate on the rotating dome and filters, reducing light output and colour fidelity. Regular cleaning with approved optics‑safe solutions is required.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Beacon Reported as Fault to ALCMS / Local Alarm Panel
Likely Cause
A stalled or slowed motor, or a lamp that has failed to strike — the beacon is a single non-redundant unit, so the fault flag alone does not indicate which.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the motor controller/monitoring board for rotation speed and lamp current readings to isolate the cause; visually confirm rotation and lamp status at the beacon.
Corrective Action
Address the specific fault found — motor or lamp — using the relevant item below, then confirm the fault clears on the ALCMS or alarm panel.
Beacon Completely Dark
Likely Cause
Loss of supply to the dedicated AC circuit, a failed lamp/LED array, or a slip ring contact failure interrupting power across the rotating joint.
Diagnostic Steps
Check incoming supply at the beacon circuit; test lamp continuity; inspect and clean slip ring contacts for oxidation or wear.
Corrective Action
Repair the supply feed, replace the lamp/array, or clean or replace worn slip rings.
Rotation Stalled or Running Slow/Uneven
Likely Cause
Worn or seized motor bearings, a degraded gearbox, or a motor controller fault failing to hold the constant 12–15 RPM speed.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for binding or resistance when the turntable is turned by hand; measure motor current against the rated draw; test the motor controller output.
Corrective Action
Lubricate or replace bearings and gearing, or replace the motor controller if it is not holding constant speed.
Intermittent Power Loss or Arcing at the Rotating Joint
Likely Cause
Worn, oxidised, or misaligned slip ring contacts, or a fault in the rotary transformer where fitted.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the slip ring surface and brush contacts for wear, oxidation, or arcing marks; check rotary transformer output if fitted.
Corrective Action
Clean and re-seat slip ring contacts, replace worn brushes or rings, or replace a faulted rotary transformer.
Beacon Fails to Switch On/Off at the Correct Time
Likely Cause
Photocell fault, an ALCMS scheduling/command fault, or a manual tower switch left in the wrong position at a controlled aerodrome.
Diagnostic Steps
Test the photocell's response; check the ALCMS command log for the beacon; confirm the manual switch position where fitted.
Corrective Action
Replace the photocell, correct the ALCMS schedule/command, or reset the manual switch.
Reduced Light Output or Colour Fidelity
Likely Cause
Dust, bird droppings, or moisture contamination on the dome and colour filters, or filter degradation.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect and photometrically check output against specification; inspect filters for fading or damage.
Corrective Action
Clean the dome and filters with an optics-safe solution, and replace degraded filters.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Motor Stall or Slowdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Worn or seized motor bearings, a degraded gearbox, or a motor controller fault failing to hold the rated rotation speed.
Effect
Rotation stalls or runs slow/uneven, and is reported as a fault to the ALCMS or local alarm panel since the beacon is a single non-redundant unit.
Detection Method
Binding/resistance checks when turning the turntable by hand, motor current measurement against rated draw, and motor controller output testing.
Lamp Failure to Strike
High Severity
Root Cause
A lamp/LED array that fails to strike, or loss of supply to the dedicated AC circuit.
Effect
Beacon reported as faulted or goes completely dark, since the ALCMS fault flag alone does not distinguish motor from lamp faults.
Detection Method
Motor controller/monitoring board review for rotation speed and lamp current readings, and visual confirmation at the beacon.
Slip Ring Contact Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Worn, oxidised, or misaligned slip ring contacts, or a fault in the rotary transformer where fitted.
Effect
Intermittent power loss or arcing at the rotating joint, which can also cause complete beacon darkness if contact is fully lost.
Detection Method
Inspection of the slip ring surface and brush contacts for wear, oxidation, or arcing marks, and rotary transformer output testing.
Control Logic / Switch Fault
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Photocell fault, an ALCMS scheduling/command fault, or a manual tower switch left in the wrong position.
Effect
Beacon fails to switch on or off at the correct time relative to operational requirements.
Detection Method
Photocell response testing, ALCMS command-log review, and manual-switch position verification.
Dome / Filter Contamination
Low Severity
Root Cause
Dust, bird droppings, or moisture contamination on the dome and colour filters, or filter degradation.
Effect
Reduced light output or colour fidelity affecting the beacon's identification pattern.
Detection Method
Visual and photometric checks against specification, and filter inspection for fading or damage.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Rotating Assembly Pinch Points
Mechanical

Keep hands, tools, and loose clothing clear of the turntable, motor, and gear train until the assembly is confirmed stationary and electrically isolated.

Electrical Isolation
Electrical Safety

Lock out the beacon supply and verify zero energy before opening the lens housing or servicing the lamp, reflector, or drive assembly.

Hot Lamp / Reflector Handling
Personnel Safety

Allow the lamp and reflector assembly to cool after operation before handling; high-intensity lamps retain significant heat and can cause burns during replacement.

Optics / Filter Handling
Mechanical

Handle colour filters, lenses, and reflectors with clean gloves; fingerprints and scratches degrade optical output and colour accuracy.

Working-Height Access
Fall Protection

Use fall-protection PPE appropriate to the mounting location, since the rotating beacon assembly is typically installed on an elevated structure.

PPE
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools for electrical work and cut-resistant gloves when handling glass or metal optical components.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm correct rotation speed, flash rate, and colour sequence before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Slip Ring Wear Announces Itself as Intermittent Flicker
Troubleshooting

A worn or dirty slip ring/brush contact often causes an intermittent flicker or momentary dropout that comes and goes with rotation, rather than a clean total failure, which makes it easy to misattribute to the lamp or driver electronics. Check slip ring and brush contact condition early when troubleshooting a rotating beacon with an inconsistent rather than steady fault.

Verify Rotation Speed Under Load, Not Just No-Load
Commissioning

A drive assembly that spins at the correct RPM with the lamp assembly and reflector removed can still slow under the actual rotating mass once fully built up, if the motor is running close to its torque margin. Confirm rotation speed with the complete optical assembly fitted, not only during the no-load check earlier in commissioning.

Base Levelling Errors Compound Into Uneven Beam Coverage
Installation

A base that's slightly out of level tilts the parabolic reflector's sweep, giving reduced effective range on one side of the rotation without any single reading at the beacon itself showing an obvious fault. Re-check base levelling as part of any beacon assembly overhaul, not just at initial installation.

Filter Fitment Affects Beam Symmetry Between Colours
Optical

A colour filter refitted slightly off its original registration against the reflector can leave one colour's beam narrower or dimmer than the other even though the lamp itself is healthy. Check filter alignment against the reflector geometry after any filter replacement, not just filter condition alone.

Vibration Readings Taken Cold Miss the Real Running Condition
Diagnostics

Bearing wear that produces vibration under sustained running heat and load can be much less apparent on a beacon that's just been switched on for a brief test. Take vibration and bearing run checks after the assembly has been running long enough to reach normal operating temperature, not immediately at power-up.

Lubrication and Slip Ring Servicing Are Not Interchangeable Tasks
Maintenance

Bearing and gear lubrication addresses mechanical wear, while slip ring and brush contact servicing addresses electrical continuity across the rotating joint; a fixture serviced only for one can still fail from the other. Keep both items as distinct line entries on the maintenance schedule rather than folding slip ring servicing into a general lubrication task.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about rotating beacons: how the assembly works, colours, flash rate, slip rings, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a rotating beacon?

A rotating beacon is the electro-mechanical assembly that produces the sweeping light beam used in airport and heliport identification beacons. It combines a base, an electric motor, a rotating turntable, high-intensity lamps or LED modules, colour filters and a reflector that collimates the light into a narrow beam.

Q2How does a rotating beacon work?

A motor drives the turntable at constant speed through a gearbox or direct drive. A parabolic reflector focuses the light into a narrow horizontal beam of about 3°–5°, and as the assembly turns the beam sweeps through 360° while alternating colour filters create distinct coloured flashes.

Q3What is the rotation speed and flash rate of a rotating beacon?

Rotation is typically 12–15 RPM with two flashes per revolution, giving 24–30 flashes per minute. Drift from the rated speed is flagged as a loss-of-sync fault.

Q4What colours does a rotating beacon show?

Colour filters alternate in pairs, such as green/white for airports, white/yellow or white/red for heliports and yellow/white for water airports. Filter output must fall within the ICAO Annex 14 chromaticity boundaries for the assigned colours.

Q5How bright is a rotating beacon?

Effective intensity is up to 50,000 cd depending on the lamp and optics, with peak beam intensity of at least 2,000 cd per colour per ICAO Annex 14 and FAA AC 150/5345-12. The vertical beam is narrow, about 2°–3°, for maximum horizon range.

Q6What are slip rings in a rotating beacon?

Slip rings carry motor and lamp or LED power across the rotating joint to the turning assembly. Their contacts can wear or oxidise, causing intermittent power loss or arcing, so they are a recognised wear point that needs periodic cleaning and inspection.

Q7Where is a rotating beacon installed?

Inside a weatherproof housing on a control tower, hangar roof or dedicated mast, at the highest practical point with an unobstructed 360° sweep. The base bolts to a rigid platform sized for the assembly's weight and the dynamic loading from the rotating turntable.

Q8Which standards apply to rotating beacons?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (rotation speed, colour sequence and intensity), FAA AC 150/5345-12 (airport rotating beacons) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 for LED beacon performance. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a rotating beacon be maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for dome and filter cleaning, semi-annual for bearing and gear lubrication, and annual for slip ring inspection, rotation speed and photometric checks and mounting and housing inspection. Bearings are typically replaced every 5–7 years. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why is a rotating beacon dark or reported as faulty?

A fault flag alone does not show whether a motor has stalled or a lamp has failed, since the beacon is a single non-redundant unit. A completely dark beacon is often loss of supply, a failed lamp or LED array, or a slip ring contact failure. Check rotation speed and lamp current at the controller, then check supply, lamp continuity and slip ring condition.
