TAXIWAY LIGHTING SYSTEMS EXPLAINED FOR STUDENTS

Taxiways provide routes for aircraft movement between runways, aprons, terminals, maintenance areas and other locations on an aerodrome. Taxiway lighting uses different colours, configurations and functions to guide pilots and communicate runway-related information. For easy learning, connect:

FAA guidance (AIM Chapter 2, Section 1; AC 150/5340-30J) confirms blue taxiway-edge lighting, green taxiway-centreline lighting, alternating green/yellow lead-on and lead-off lighting, and flashing-yellow runway guard lights in the applicable configurations.

1

TAXIWAY EDGE LIGHTS

Taxiway edge lights outline the edges of taxiways and help pilots identify the lateral limits of the taxiway route. Their primary purpose is visual identification of the taxiway edge. They are blue and normally omnidirectional, so do not confuse them with the white runway edge lights.

Exam memory
  • Taxiway Edge = BLUE
  • Blue → Taxiway Boundary
2

TAXIWAY CENTRELINE LIGHTS

Centreline lights provide visual guidance along the taxiway route, helping pilots follow the intended movement path between different areas of the aerodrome.

Exam memory
  • Taxiway Centreline = GREEN
  • Green → Taxiway Guidance
3

LEAD-OFF LIGHTS

Lead-off lights provide visual guidance for aircraft exiting a runway onto a taxiway. FAA guidance (AIM Chapter 2, Section 1) uses alternating green and yellow lights beginning with green, extending from the runway centreline to one light position beyond the runway holding position or ILS critical-area holding position.

Think
  • Leaving the runway → Lead-Off
  • Lead-Off = Runway → Taxiway
4

LEAD-ON LIGHTS

Lead-on lights provide visual guidance for aircraft moving from a taxiway toward the runway. FAA guidance describes the applicable system using the same alternating green and yellow pattern as lead-off lights, to warn pilots and vehicle drivers that they are within the runway environment or ILS critical area. The fixtures are bidirectional: one side shows the lead-on function and the other the lead-off function, so any fixture that shows yellow for lead-off also shows yellow for lead-on.

Terminology check. The alternating green/yellow lead-on pattern is FAA usage. In ICAO and UK usage, “lead-on lights” usually means the steady green centreline lights that lead an aircraft from a stop bar onto the runway, switched in step with the stop bar (see Note 17). ICAO and UK practice also uses alternating green and yellow centreline lights where the centreline lies inside an ILS-sensitive area or close to a runway, with no stopping there without ATC approval. Confirm which standard a question or specification follows.
Think
  • Going toward the runway → Lead-On
  • Lead-On = Taxiway → Runway
5

STOP BARS

Stop bars use red lights to identify a stopping position, giving a clear visual indication that an aircraft is required to stop at the designated holding position. Stop bars are normally switched by ATC, and where centreline lead-on lights are provided they are interlocked so that STOP and PROCEED are never shown together. ICAO Annex 14 calls for stop bars at runway-holding positions on runways used in RVR below 550 m (see Note 17).

Exam memory
  • STOP BAR = RED
  • Stop bar lit = do not cross. Cross only when the bar is switched off; if a lit bar and a crossing clearance conflict, stop and query ATC.
Do not confuse Stop Bar → Red with Runway Guard Lights → Flashing Yellow. They are different lighting systems with different functions.
6

RUNWAY GUARD LIGHTS

Runway guard lights help identify a runway holding position. FAA guidance specifies flashing yellow lights, which flash alternately and may be provided in elevated or in-pavement arrangements. In the usual layouts, Configuration A is a pair of elevated lights on each side of the taxiway, and Configuration B is a row of in-pavement lights across it. Guard lights warn that a runway is ahead; unlike a stop bar they do not by themselves order the aircraft to stop. Where RVR is below 550 m and no stop bar is installed, ICAO requires them at the intersection.

Exam memory
  • Runway Guard = Flashing Yellow
  • Runway Guard → Runway Holding Position
  • The key distinction: flashing yellow = runway guard, not red
7

RUNWAY ENTRANCE LIGHTS (REL)

REL = Runway Entrance Lights. They form part of the FAA Runway Status Lights (RWSL) system. FAA describes RELs as red in-pavement fixtures arranged along the taxiway centreline from the holding position to the runway edge. When activated, they indicate that it is unsafe to enter or cross the runway because of runway traffic or an aircraft on final approach. RELs are driven automatically by surveillance data, not switched by ATC, and the FAA instruction to pilots is never to cross illuminated red lights. RWSL is an FAA system, not an ICAO standard.

Important. Do not confuse Runway Guard Lights (flashing yellow) with Runway Entrance Lights (red). These are different systems.
Easy memory
  • REL → Red → Runway Entry Warning
Also Seen

INTERMEDIATE HOLDING POSITION LIGHTS

ICAO intermediate holding position lights are at least three steady yellow lights placed symmetrically about the taxiway centreline. They mark a holding position at a taxiway intersection, where aircraft should give way unless ATC clears them. FAA flashing-yellow runway guard lights replaced the older steady-yellow clearance bars at runway holding positions, so do not mix the two.

Exam Questions

TAXIWAY REVISION QUESTIONS

1. What colour is a standard taxiway edge light?

Blue

2. What colour is the taxiway centreline?

Green

3. What colour is a stop bar?

Red

4. What colour are runway guard lights?

Flashing Yellow

5. What colours are associated with lead-on/lead-off lighting in FAA guidance?

Alternating Green and Yellow

6. What does REL stand for?

Runway Entrance Lights

7. What colour are RELs in the FAA Runway Status Lights system?

Red

8. What is the difference between lead-on and lead-off lighting?

Lead-off provides guidance when leaving the runway, while lead-on provides guidance when moving toward the runway from a taxiway.

9. What are the two runway guard light configurations?

Configuration A: a pair of elevated flashing yellow lights on each side of the taxiway. Configuration B: a row of in-pavement flashing yellow lights across the taxiway.

10. What should a pilot do when RELs are illuminated?

Never cross the illuminated red lights; it is unsafe to enter or cross the runway. Stop and contact ATC.

Final Study Takeaway

FINAL STUDY TAKEAWAY

Do not memorise taxiway lighting colours without understanding their purpose. Study each system through Location → Colour → Function → Operational Meaning.

A strong AGL student should identify not only the colour, but also the location, movement relationship, operational purpose and applicable system.

The key question is not “What colour is this light?” It is “What does this light communicate to the pilot, where is it used, and what is its operational purpose?”
