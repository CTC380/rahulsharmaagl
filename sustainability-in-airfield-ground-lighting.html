Overview

What Is Sustainable Airfield Ground Lighting?

Sustainability in Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) is the practice of reducing the environmental and energy impact of airport visual-aid systems while preserving the safety, optical performance, reliability and operational availability the aerodrome requires. AGL is only one part of an airport’s footprint, but it is a real engineering opportunity because lighting runs across large areas: runway, taxiway and approach lighting, PAPI, stop bars, guard lights, signs, CCRs and control systems.

A sustainable AGL strategy goes beyond swapping lamps for LEDs. It weighs energy consumption, carbon, equipment efficiency, control, maintenance, asset life, materials, renewable energy, resilience and end-of-life management together.

Efficiency

LED, System Efficiency and Losses

What LED can offer

Lower electrical consumption, longer service life, less lamp replacement, controllability and consistent optics when correctly designed. The FAA’s review of LEDs in airfield lighting notes energy and life-cycle cost advantages but stresses evaluating airfield-specific operational and safety needs.

LED is not automatically sustainable

A fitting sold as efficient can still give a poor lifecycle outcome with weak thermal design, poor optics, short-life electronics, hard-to-replace drivers, proprietary parts, poor sealing, high standby power or no repair strategy.

Compare useful photometric performance per unit of energy, not watts per fixture. A 50 W fixture with better optical efficiency and distribution can beat a 45 W fixture with poor distribution, depending on the design.

Control

Smart Control, Dimming and Operating Modes

Operationally responsive lighting

Instead of all taxiway lighting on, an approved system can light only what the current operational state requires. Inputs can include the runway in use, taxiway route, traffic, operational category, low-visibility procedures, maintenance state and closures, through ALCMS, programmable control, route-specific lighting, Follow the Greens or A-SMGCS integration, where permitted.

Dimming and brightness stages

Many AGL systems already have several brightness stages. The question is whether they are used appropriately. Where the applicable requirements permit a lower intensity, the lower setting saves energy, but never reduce brightness only to save energy without confirming compliance with conditions, standards, visibility, runway category and airport procedures.

Follow the Greens and A-SMGCS

Selected green centreline segments communicate a route rather than holding every possible route in the same state. A-SMGCS guidance can include automated switching of centreline lights and stop bars. The benefit is the right lighting at the right time, not just turning lights off.

Standby and idle energy

Equipment left energized when not needed adds up: control electronics, communications, CCR standby losses, UPS losses, auxiliary supplies and sign electronics. A detailed energy audit can find these loads.

Power

Renewable Energy, Storage and Sustainable Emergency Power

Renewable energy can support the airport’s electricity supply through solar PV, renewable electricity procurement, power purchase agreements, battery storage and microgrids. The airport-level opportunity is real, but the engineering question for AGL is not “can solar power the runway lights?” It is “can the renewable system provide the required availability, power quality, backup and switch-over performance for this AGL load?”

Solar for auxiliary lighting

Can suit some obstruction lighting, remote signs, isolated facilities or perimeter applications, depending on equipment and requirement. Treat primary runway and precision approach systems separately because their continuity requirements are far stricter.

Battery energy storage

May support peak-load management, renewable integration, microgrids, backup and reduced generator runtime. Evaluate availability, fire protection, thermal management, autonomy, degradation, monitoring and fault response.

Backup power mix

Optimize utility, UPS, battery storage, DG and renewables to cut generator runtime, fuel use and inefficient part-load operation, while keeping required availability.

Generators, UPS and batteries

Right-size generators, avoid long low-load running, monitor fuel and maintain them. Use high-efficiency, appropriately sized UPS with modular architecture and condition monitoring. Track battery age, capacity, health and temperature, and plan replacement and recycling.

Redundancy without waste

Aim for required redundancy, efficient equipment and appropriate loading, not indiscriminate duplication. Well-designed redundancy can still help lifecycle sustainability by allowing maintenance without full shutdown and avoiding premature replacement.

Sustainability cannot compromise resilience. Emergency power must remain available, with the required switch-over performance, when it is needed.

Total cost of ownership

TCO = capital cost + energy cost + maintenance cost + spare parts + replacement cost + disposal cost. A broader view adds carbon, material impact and operational risk. A dearer fitting that lasts longer, uses less power and is easier to repair can have the lower lifecycle impact.

Embodied and operational carbon

Embodied carbon arises from aluminium, steel, copper, electronics, plastics, concrete foundations, cable, packaging and transport. A programme can consider embodied plus operational carbon, not electricity alone.

Rehabilitation vs full replacement

Before replacing everything, ask whether cable, light bases, transformer housings, ducts, chambers and mounting structures can be retained. If technically sound and compliant, reuse reduces material use, construction waste, transport, civil works and project carbon.

Sustainable installation

Minimize unnecessary excavation and pavement cuts, reuse suitable ducts, optimize cable routes, use efficient logistics, segregate waste, recycle removed cable and metals, and restore disturbed areas.

Maintenance

Photometry, Condition-Based Maintenance and Digital Asset Data

Maintenance protects efficiency

Dirty optics, misaligned fixtures and repeated failures reduce photometric performance, drive premature replacement and can lead to unnecessarily high settings. Cleaning, alignment checks and replacing only the defective component keep output efficient and extend life.

Photometry as a sustainability tool

If a fitting under-performs, the reaction can be replace the whole fixture, or clean, realign, repair or swap one component. Measured photometry shows which. That supports condition-based maintenance instead of replace-by-age.

A trend gives early warning (illustrative)

Year 1: normal
Year 2: normal
Year 3: slight output drop
Year 4: significant drop
Year 5: maintenance required
Trend data helps schedule intervention before complete failure: longer asset life, fewer emergency replacements, lower spare use and less waste.

Repairable and modular equipment

Prefer, where appropriate, replaceable LED modules, drivers, connectors, lenses and gaskets, with local spares and documentation. Modularity must not compromise optical alignment, ingress protection, reliability, safety or approval requirements.

Digital asset database

Record fixture age, power rating, manufacturer, serial number, failure history, maintenance, photometry and energy use. Then target upgrades at the highest-energy, highest-failure and highest-maintenance-cost assets.

Materials and recycling

Materials include aluminium, copper, steel, glass, polycarbonate, electronics, cable and batteries. LED equipment contains electronics and should not go to ordinary scrap: plan e-waste segregation, metal recovery, cable and battery recycling, and ask suppliers for end-of-life information.

Packaging, transport and sourcing

Encourage reusable crates, recyclable materials, less plastic, consolidated shipments, standardized packaging and supplier take-back. Optimize spares and avoid urgent shipping. Domestic supply can reduce transport and help service and spares, but judge the whole supply chain rather than assuming proximity means lower carbon.

Resilience

Climate Resilience and Surge Protection

Climate and environment

Design for high temperature, intense rainfall, flooding, dust, coastal or saline air, extreme weather, lightning and heat stress on electronics. Select for ingress protection, corrosion resistance, thermal management, UV, moisture, dust and local climate. A low-power fitting that fails often can have a worse lifecycle outcome.

Protection reduces waste

Repeated surges destroy LED drivers, controllers, transformers and field equipment, driving replacements and e-waste. Protection means longer asset life, less waste and lower lifecycle impact. Drainage around light bases also shortens or extends equipment life.

Pitfalls

Common Mistakes in Sustainable AGL Projects

Treating LED as the whole strategy

LED is one measure, not the complete solution.

Measuring only fixture wattage

CCR, transformer and control-system losses also matter.

Ignoring operating hours

A low-power fitting running continuously may use more than a higher-power one used only when needed.

Ignoring photometric performance

Energy reduction must never compromise required optical output.

Replacing healthy infrastructure

Complete replacement can create unnecessary material and construction impact.

No energy baseline

Without baseline data savings cannot be reliably shown.

No post-project measurement

Estimated savings should be verified against actual operation.

Ignoring end of life

LED conversion can create e-waste if old equipment is not responsibly managed.

Keyword Reference

Airport Lighting Energy Efficiency & Sustainability

Airport lighting energy efficiency

Airport lighting energy efficiency comes mainly from LED fixtures, efficient CCRs, brightness control that matches operating conditions and good maintenance. Savings should be measured against a baseline, not assumed, and must never reduce the light output or reliability the standard requires.

Airport lighting sustainability

Airport lighting sustainability looks at the whole life of the system: energy use, carbon, materials, maintenance, spare parts and end-of-life recycling. Choosing durable equipment and planning replacements by condition lowers both cost and environmental impact over the life of the airfield.

FAQ

Sustainable AGL FAQ

Q1What is sustainability in Airfield Ground Lighting?

It is the practice of reducing the energy, carbon, material and lifecycle impacts of AGL while maintaining the required safety, photometric performance, reliability and operational availability.

Q2Is LED the most important sustainable AGL technology?

LED is one of the most important measures because it can reduce energy use and maintenance, but sustainable AGL also needs efficient CCRs and transformers, intelligent control, asset management, renewable integration and lifecycle planning. Benefits must be weighed with airfield-specific operational and safety requirements.

Q3How can AGL reduce airport carbon emissions?

Through LED conversion, efficient power equipment, intelligent control, less unnecessary runtime, renewable electricity, better maintenance and longer equipment life.

Q4Can Follow the Greens reduce AGL energy consumption?

Potentially, because dynamic route guidance can control selected segments instead of continuously operating all possible routes. The lighting state must always follow approved operational and safety requirements.

Q5Does smart AGL control save energy?

It can. The benefit depends on operating hours, lighting modes, traffic, control philosophy and which circuits can safely be controlled.

Q6Should all AGL lights be dimmed to save energy?

No. Lighting levels must stay within the applicable operational and photometric requirements. Energy optimization cannot compromise safety.

Q7Is solar power suitable for runway lighting?

Renewable electricity can support airport energy demand, but critical runway lighting needs careful power-system engineering for availability, backup and switch-over. Direct stand-alone solar supply should not be assumed suitable for a safety-critical AGL function without a project-specific design.

Q8How can photometry support sustainability?

Photometry can show when a fitting needs cleaning, alignment, repair or replacement, supporting condition-based maintenance and reducing unnecessary replacement of serviceable equipment.

Q9Does LED AGL require less maintenance?

It can reduce routine maintenance associated with traditional lamps, but LED systems still need optical inspection, cleaning, aiming checks, electrical inspection, photometry and electronic-component maintenance.

Q10Can existing AGL infrastructure be reused during an LED upgrade?

Potentially. Cables, light bases, transformer housings and ducts can sometimes be retained where their condition and the new design permit, reducing civil work, material use and disruption.

Q11What is the best sustainability KPI for AGL?

There is no single KPI. A good programme normally tracks kWh, tCO₂e, fixture efficiency, runtime, failure rates, maintenance, asset life and photometric performance.

Q12How do you calculate AGL energy savings?

Energy savings = baseline consumption minus post-upgrade consumption. For reliable reporting, both should come from comparable operating conditions and measured data.

Q13Does a sustainable AGL system need renewable energy?

No. Renewable energy is one possible component. Efficiency, control, reliability, maintenance and lifecycle management can deliver substantial benefits even where the electricity source is not yet decarbonized.
