Stop Bar Control Module

An electronic control unit that manages the operation of stop bar lights, receiving commands from the ATC or ALCMS and switching the stop bar circuit on/off, often with intensity control and interlock features to ensure safe sequencing with other AGL systems. The module ensures that stop bars are correctly illuminated only when the runway is occupied, preventing runway incursions.

Overview

What Is It?

A Stop Bar Control Module is an electronic control unit that manages the operation of stop bar lights, receiving commands from the ATC or ALCMS and switching the stop bar circuit on/off, often with intensity control and interlock features to ensure safe sequencing with other AGL systems. The module ensures that stop bars are correctly illuminated only when the runway is occupied, preventing runway incursions. It typically interfaces with the constant current regulator (CCR), the ALCMS, and the stop bar monitoring unit to provide a complete stop bar control and supervision solution.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The stop bar control module exists to make sure a stop bar's on/off state is never left to chance or a fixture-level fault — it's the component that translates an actual ATC clearance decision into the physical illumination state of the barrier a pilot sees. Because a stop bar's entire safety function depends on it accurately reflecting whether the runway is actually clear, this module's interlocking logic is what prevents the bar from being switched off, and an aircraft cleared to cross, while a genuine conflict still exists.

Its coordination with other AGL systems is just as important as the switching itself: sequencing stop bar state correctly relative to runway lighting and other holding indicators is what stops mixed signals from ever reaching a pilot, which is exactly the kind of ambiguity that runway incursion defences are designed to eliminate entirely.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Stop bar control modules are installed at runway holding positions on taxiways, particularly where precise control and sequencing are required. They are used on:

  • Precision approach runways (CAT II and III) where stop bar control is critical
  • Busy airports with high traffic volumes where automated control is beneficial
  • Low visibility operations where reliable stop bar operation is essential
  • Complex taxiway intersections with multiple stop bars requiring coordination
  • Airports with ALCMS where remote and automated control is implemented
  • Military airfields requiring robust and secure control systems
Placement

Installation & Location

The module is installed in an equipment shelter, CCR room, or dedicated control cabinet close to the stop bar circuit it serves, wired into the constant current regulator, the ALCMS, and the stop bar monitoring unit so switching commands, brightness settings, and status feedback can all pass through a single, centrally located point. Mounting is typically on an equipment rack or panel alongside related AGL control equipment, with clear labelling of the specific holding position or stop bar circuit each module serves, since a control room may house several modules covering different taxiway intersections. Control and monitoring cabling runs from the module out to the field circuit and to the stop bar monitoring unit over dedicated, clearly identified cable routes, kept separate from unrelated control circuits to avoid cross-connection errors during installation or later maintenance. Because the module's interlock logic is safety-critical, its wiring and configuration are verified against the site's specific stop bar sequencing requirements during commissioning, rather than installed to a generic default configuration.

Function

How Does It Work?

Stop bar control modules operate as intelligent switching and control devices:

  • Command Reception: The module receives on/off commands from the ATC via a control panel or from the ALCMS via digital communication (e.g., Modbus, Ethernet).
  • Switching: It uses relays or solid-state switches to energize or de-energize the stop bar circuit, turning the lights on or off.
  • Intensity Control: Some modules can adjust the intensity of the stop bar lights by controlling the CCR current step, matching the prevailing visibility conditions.
  • Interlocks: The module may include interlock logic to prevent conflicting commands, such as ensuring that a stop bar is not turned on while a clearance bar is active, or that the stop bar is deactivated before the runway entrance light is turned on.
  • Status Feedback: The module reports its status (on/off, intensity step, fault) to the ALCMS, providing real-time visibility for the controller.
Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The control module is the layer that turns a stop bar from a passive series-circuit fixture into an addressable safety device. Above it sits the ALCMS and, ultimately, the tower controller who issues the clearance decision; below it sits the CCR and the physical stop bar circuit. What makes the module's logic safety-critical rather than a simple relay is the interlocking it enforces between adjacent controlled elements: a stop bar cannot typically be switched off while the corresponding runway entrance lights or a downstream clearance bar are commanded on, and at CAT II/III-protected intersections the module may additionally require confirmation that the associated taxiway centreline lead-on lights are correctly sequenced before releasing the stop bar. At larger or more complex airports, the module is one node in a wider Advanced Surface Movement Guidance and Control System (A-SMGCS), where "follow-the-greens" routing software commands stop bars in and out of service automatically as part of a planned taxi route rather than through a single ATC button press — the module still performs the same switching and interlock function, just on commands originating from route logic instead of a human operator.

Construction

Physical Construction

The module is built as an electronic control unit rather than an electromechanical one: a DIN-rail or panel-mounted PCB assembly carries the relay contacts or solid-state switches (rated up to 10A) that actually break and make the stop bar circuit, alongside the logic that interprets ALCMS commands and interlock inputs. Housing ranges from a simple IP20 enclosure for equipment-room installation up to an IP66 weatherproof housing where the module sits closer to the field, and status LEDs on the front panel — power, circuit status, communication, fault — give a technician an at-a-glance read without opening a cover or connecting test equipment. Terminal blocks bring in supply power (120/230V AC or 24V DC), interlock inputs from adjacent systems such as the clearance bar or runway entrance, and the communication bus (Modbus RTU or Ethernet) linking the module back to the ALCMS, keeping field wiring, logic, and switching contained in a single serviceable unit.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Relay Contact Wear

Electromechanical relays used for switching can wear out over time, especially with frequent switching cycles. Regular inspection and replacement of relay modules is recommended to ensure reliable operation.

Communication Intermittency

Noise or poor connections on communication lines can cause intermittent loss of control or status feedback. Proper shielding, termination, and grounding are essential for reliable communication.

Interlock Logic Misconfiguration

Incorrect interlock settings can lead to unsafe conditions, such as allowing a stop bar to be deactivated while an aircraft is on the runway. Thorough testing and validation of interlock logic is critical during commissioning.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Stop Bar Fails to Switch or Responds Sluggishly
Likely Cause
The electromechanical relay handling the switching function has worn from frequent switching cycles.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the relay module for pitting or arcing on the contacts, and check switching response time against specification.
Corrective Action
Replace the worn relay module and re-verify switching response before returning the module to service.
Intermittent Loss of Control or Status Feedback
Likely Cause
Noise or a poor connection on the communication line is disrupting signal integrity.
Diagnostic Steps
Check communication line shielding, termination, and grounding; monitor for intermittent dropouts correlated with nearby equipment operation.
Corrective Action
Correct shielding, termination, or grounding deficiencies found, and re-test communication stability under normal operating conditions.
Unsafe Condition Identified During Interlock Testing
Likely Cause
The interlock logic has been misconfigured, potentially allowing an unsafe state such as stop bar deactivation while an aircraft is on the runway.
Diagnostic Steps
Review the interlock configuration against the design logic, and run a full commissioning-style validation test of all interlock conditions.
Corrective Action
Correct the interlock configuration to match the validated design logic, and re-run the full validation test before returning the module to service.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Interlock Logic Fault Allowing the Bar to Switch Off Prematurely
High Severity
Root Cause
A software or logic fault in the module's interlocking function, the core mechanism that's meant to prevent the bar being switched off while a genuine conflict still exists.
Effect
The stop bar can be switched off and an aircraft cleared to cross while a runway conflict actually remains, defeating the entire safety purpose of the interlock.
Detection Method
Interlock logic validation against known conflict scenarios during scheduled functional testing, not just a simple on/off check.
Loss of Sequencing Coordination With Runway Lighting or Other Holding Indicators
High Severity
Root Cause
A fault in the module's coordination logic with adjacent AGL systems, so stop bar state is no longer sequenced correctly relative to runway lighting or other holding indicators.
Effect
Mixed signals reach a pilot — exactly the kind of ambiguity runway incursion defences exist to eliminate — even though the stop bar's own state may be technically correct in isolation.
Detection Method
Cross-system sequencing check between the stop bar control module and the AGL systems it's meant to coordinate with, during scheduled testing.
Communication Fault Between Module and ALCMS
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Failure or degradation of the communication link between the stop bar control module and the ALCMS.
Effect
ALCMS loses the ability to command or confirm the stop bar's state, undermining tower oversight of a safety-critical barrier.
Detection Method
Communication link health check and command/status round-trip test during scheduled maintenance.
Firmware / Configuration Fault Producing an Incorrect State Output
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Outdated firmware or a misconfigured parameter causing the module to output a state that doesn't match the actual ATC clearance decision.
Effect
The physical illumination state a pilot sees no longer reliably reflects the real clearance decision the module exists to translate.
Detection Method
Firmware version audit against the manufacturer's supported release, and configuration parameter review during scheduled maintenance.
Power Supply Failure to the Module
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Loss of primary power or a failed backup supply feeding the control module itself.
Effect
Loss of stop bar control at that location, with behaviour on power loss depending on the module's fail-safe design rather than continued normal operation.
Detection Method
Power supply and backup source verification during scheduled maintenance, including a simulated outage test of the backup path.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Electrical Isolation Before Servicing
Electrical Safety

De-energise the module's 120/230V AC or 24V DC supply, and separately the stop bar circuit it switches, before opening the housing or working on wiring; verify zero energy at both, since the module sits between two independent supplies.

Safety-Critical Interlock — Coordinate Before Isolating
Operational

The module's interlock logic prevents a stop bar from being incorrectly switched off while a runway is occupied; taking it out of service removes that protection, so coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before isolation, and confirm an interim procedure is in place for the affected stop bar.

Physical Lockout Over Software Disable
Electrical Safety

Where the module is ALCMS-networked, use a physical lockout on the stop bar circuit during hands-on work rather than relying on a software-disabled or local-only mode, which a remote command could still override.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Megger test the stop bar circuit before restoring power after any cable, connector, or relay work, since a residual fault can produce a false or stuck state at re-energisation.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work affecting stop bar operation requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM for the affected holding position, with personnel and vehicles kept clear per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Verify Interlock Sequencing Before Closing Out
Compliance

Confirm the module correctly sequences with the CCR, ALCMS, and stop bar monitoring unit — including fail-safe behaviour on a fault — before returning it to unattended operation; do not rely on a single successful on/off cycle as proof the interlock logic is sound.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Interlock Logic Is the Feature, Not a Formality
Compliance

Never bypass or defeat the module's interlock logic to force a stop bar on or off faster — the interlocking between stop bars, clearance bars, and entrance lights exists specifically to prevent the conflicting-light-state scenarios that cause incursions.

Confirm Which Layer Issued the Command Before Troubleshooting
Troubleshooting

At an A-SMGCS-equipped airport, a stop bar command may originate from follow-the-greens routing logic rather than a direct ATC button press. Confirm the command source before assuming a “stuck” stop bar is a hardware fault rather than a routing-software issue upstream.

Status Feedback Confirms Commanded State, Not Actual Illumination
Troubleshooting

The control module reports what it commanded, not necessarily what happened at the fixture. Cross-check against the separate monitoring unit's feedback before concluding a stop bar is working correctly.

Physical Lockout, Not a Panel Disable, for Any Hands-On Work
Electrical Safety

A software or panel-level disable can be overridden by a subsequent command arriving mid-task. Physically lock out the circuit instead of relying on a soft disable while working on it.

Re-Verify Interlock Sequencing After Any Firmware Update
Commissioning

A firmware or logic update to the control module should trigger a full re-verification of interlock sequencing with adjacent stop bars and entrance lights — not just a check that the module itself powers up correctly.

Confirm CAT II/III Sequencing Is Actually Configured, Not Just Available
Compliance

At precision-approach-protected intersections, confirm the module's requirement for taxiway centreline lead-on sequencing is genuinely configured and tested — not merely present as a feature the module supports.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about stop bar control modules: switching, interlocks, ratings, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a stop bar control module?

It is an electronic control unit that manages stop bar lights, receiving commands from ATC or the ALCMS and switching the stop bar circuit on or off. It often provides intensity control and interlock features, and typically interfaces with the CCR, the ALCMS and the stop bar monitoring unit.

Q2What is the purpose of the stop bar control module?

It translates an actual ATC clearance decision into the physical state of the stop bar a pilot sees. Its interlock logic prevents the bar from being switched off, and an aircraft cleared to cross, while a genuine conflict still exists, and it sequences stop bar state correctly with other lighting so pilots never receive mixed signals.

Q3How does a stop bar control module work?

It receives on/off commands from an ATC control panel or from the ALCMS over Modbus or Ethernet, switches the circuit using relays or solid-state switches, can adjust intensity through the CCR current step, applies interlock logic, and reports on/off state, intensity step and faults back to the ALCMS.

Q4What interlocks does it provide?

Interlock logic prevents conflicting commands, such as turning a stop bar on while a clearance bar is active, or ensuring the stop bar is deactivated before the runway entrance light is turned on. Digital inputs take signals from clearance bar, runway entrance and other interlock sources.

Q5Where are stop bar control modules used?

At runway holding positions on taxiways, particularly on precision approach runways (CAT II and III), busy airports, low visibility operations, complex intersections with several stop bars, ALCMS-controlled airports and military airfields.

Q6What are typical specifications?

Supply is 120/230 V AC or 24 V DC, consuming under 10 W. Outputs are relay contacts (2× changeover) or solid-state switches up to 10 A, controlling 1 to 4 circuits. Communication is Modbus RTU (RS-485), Ethernet or discrete I/O, with a 5-step CCR interface (2.8 A to 6.6 A). Protection ranges from IP20 to IP66.

Q7Where is the module installed?

In an equipment shelter, CCR room or dedicated control cabinet close to the stop bar circuit it serves, on DIN rail or panel mount. It is wired to the CCR, ALCMS and stop bar monitoring unit so switching commands, brightness settings and status feedback pass through one central point.

Q8Which standards apply to stop bar control modules?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (stop bar lighting and holding position requirements), FAA AC 150/5345-56 (L-890 ALCMS), IEC 61508 (functional safety, applicable to the interlock logic) and ICAO Doc 9830 (A-SMGCS Manual). Always verify the current edition.

Q9How often should a stop bar control module be tested?

Typical intervals are quarterly for status indicator tests, semi-annual for relay contact inspection, communication link tests and supply voltage checks, and annual for interlock logic verification, terminal tightness, enclosure seals and firmware and configuration review. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10What are the common problems and safety precautions?

Common issues are relay contact wear, intermittent communication from noise or poor connections, and interlock logic misconfiguration that could allow a stop bar to be deactivated while an aircraft is on the runway. Coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM before isolating, use a physical lockout on the stop bar circuit rather than a software disable, and verify interlock sequencing before closing out.
