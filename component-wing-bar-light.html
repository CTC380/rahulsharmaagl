Wing Bar Light

A specialized visual guidance fixture installed as a row of lights perpendicular to the runway centreline. Wing bar lights serve multiple purposes: identifying runway threshold locations (threshold wing bars), marking stop bars, or indicating clearance bars on taxiways.

Overview

What Is It?

A Wing Bar Light is a versatile AGL fixture consisting of a row of lights installed perpendicular to the taxiway or runway centreline. These lights serve multiple critical functions across the airfield: they can be configured as threshold wing bars (green, marking runway thresholds on precision approach runways), stop bars (red, indicating runway holding positions), or clearance bars (yellow, providing guidance at taxiway intersections). The wing bar configuration provides a continuous visual line across the pavement, clearly defining boundaries and holding positions.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The wing bar's core purpose is to widen a single-point visual cue into a clear, unmistakable line across the pavement at exactly the location where a pilot or vehicle operator must make a decision. As a threshold wing bar, it extends the green threshold reference laterally beyond the runway edge lights, giving the pilot an unambiguous, wide visual marker of where the landing surface begins — reinforcing the touchdown judgement that runway edge and threshold lights alone can leave uncertain in reduced visibility. As a stop bar, the same physical format is repurposed as a hard visual stop signal: an illuminated red line spanning the taxiway that a pilot or vehicle driver must not cross without ATC clearance, making runway holding positions unmistakable and reducing the risk of runway incursion. As a clearance bar, the yellow configuration resolves directional ambiguity at complex taxiway intersections, confirming the correct route rather than merely marking an edge. In every configuration, the wing bar's role is the same: convert a point of critical decision-making into a full-width, colour-coded line that cannot be misread.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Wing bar lights are used in multiple applications across the airfield:

  • Threshold Wing Bars: Installed on CAT II/III precision approach runways, extending outward from the runway edge lights (typically ~9–10 m beyond the edge light line, per the applicable wing bar geometry) — not measured from the runway centreline.
  • Stop Bars: Installed at runway holding positions on taxiways, providing a red visual barrier to prevent unauthorized runway entry.
  • Clearance Bars: Installed at taxiway intersections and holding positions, providing yellow guidance for aircraft movement.
Placement

Installation & Location

Wing bar fixtures extend outward from the runway edge light row at the threshold, set out at 9 m either side of the extended runway edge line per the standard wing bar geometry for CAT II/III precision approach runways, using the same base can and frangible/inset mounting convention as the adjacent threshold and edge lights. Because the wing bar's role is to widen the visual cue at the threshold rather than mark a separate location, its foundations are surveyed directly off the threshold and edge light setout rather than measured independently, keeping the bar square to the runway centreline; cabling and isolating transformer arrangements follow the same series-circuit convention as the threshold lighting it extends.

Function

How Does It Work?

Each wing bar light is powered from the series circuit via an isolating transformer. The lights project a uni-directional or bi-directional beam depending on the application, using precision optics. The colour is determined by the application: green for threshold wing bars, red for stop bars, and yellow for clearance bars. The intensity is controlled by the CCR's current steps. Wing bar lights are typically flush-mounted (inset) in the pavement to provide a low-profile installation that doesn't interfere with aircraft operations.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Wing bar lights sit on the same kind of constant current series circuit as any other AGL fixture, and the ALCMS controls them at the loop level exactly the same way — a commanded brightness step sets the current for the whole circuit, and every fixture on that loop, via its own isolating transformer, changes intensity together. What makes a wing bar a threshold marker, a stop bar, or a clearance bar is the colour filter and beam configuration built into that specific fixture, fixed at manufacture and installation — the ALCMS has no way to change a fixture's colour or beam pattern, only the shared current step for its loop. Where wing bars form part of a stop bar circuit, the on/off command itself may originate from RWSL or ground-movement logic rather than a simple ALCMS brightness schedule, but the underlying series-circuit control mechanism is unchanged.

A wing bar's colour and beam are fixed hardware properties — only its brightness step and on/off state are under ALCMS control.

System Integration

Circuit & Beam Overview

A wing bar light isn't a single fixed-purpose fixture — it's the same series-circuit-fed, inset light-bar hardware, given a different colour and optical configuration depending on whether it's marking a threshold, a stop bar, or a taxiway clearance point.

Construction

Physical Construction

Each fixture within the wing bar is a compact die-cast aluminium housing, machined and anodised or powder-coated for corrosion resistance, with a toughened, coloured prismatic lens (green, red, or yellow depending on the bar's function) gasket-sealed to the housing to achieve an IP67/IP68 rating. Flush-mounted units seat into an L-852 base can recessed into the pavement, load-rated for aircraft wheel loading, while elevated units bolt to a frangible coupling engineered to shear cleanly under impact, seating on a base can set flush with the surrounding grade. Because wing bar fixtures are installed in tight, closely spaced groups of three to five units, their base cans and mounting hardware are precision-positioned along a shared baseline to maintain the specified spacing and alignment across the bar. Internal wiring from each fixture terminates at L-823 sealed connectors, mating to the secondary leads of a shared or individual isolating transformer housed in a nearby base can, with stainless-steel fasteners and drainage provisions used throughout to resist corrosion and prevent water pooling around the cable entries.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Photometric Intensity Check
Annual

Verify beam intensity and colour (green, red, or yellow depending on application) against ICAO / FAA thresholds using a calibrated photometer.

Lens Cleaning
Quarterly

Clean the coloured lens or filter of dust, rubber deposits, and film build-up so beam output and colour stay within spec.

Seal & Gasket Inspection
Annual

Check housing seals and gaskets for cracking or compression set and re-torque fasteners to keep the inset fixture watertight under pavement loading.

CCR Intensity Step Verification
Semi-annual

Confirm the fixture responds correctly across all 5 CCR intensity steps (2.8A–6.6A), since a light that fails at higher steps can mask a developing series-circuit fault.

Insulation Resistance Test
Annual

Megger-test the isolating transformer and series circuit connection to confirm insulation resistance stays above minimum, catching moisture ingress early.

Alignment Check
Semi-annual

Confirm bar spacing and beam direction (uni- or bi-directional) remain correct for the threshold, stop bar, or clearance bar function being served.

Cable & Connector Inspection
Annual

Examine primary and secondary cable connections and isolating transformer terminals for corrosion or loose crimps, a common source of intermittent faults.

Housing & Pavement Interface Check
Annual

Inspect the flush-mounted housing and surrounding pavement for settlement, spalling, or debris that could affect drainage or create a trip/FOD hazard.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Colour Filter Degradation

Wing bar lights use colour filters (green, red, yellow) that are susceptible to UV degradation and thermal stress. Over time, colours can fade or shift, compromising the visual cue. Regular photometric testing and filter replacement is essential.

Lens Contamination

Wing bar lights are exposed to rubber deposits, tyre debris, and de-icing fluids. Regular cleaning with approved solvents and soft brushes is required to maintain light output and colour fidelity.

Misalignment from Pavement Settlement

Pavement settlement can cause wing bar lights to tilt, altering the beam pattern and reducing visibility. Regular alignment checks using a digital level or specialized alignment jig are recommended.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Colour Appears Faded or Shifted
Likely Cause
UV degradation and thermal stress on the colour filter (green, red, or yellow) over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Run a photometric colour check against the applicable ICAO/FAA requirement; compare filter appearance to a new reference filter of the same type.
Corrective Action
Replace the degraded colour filter and re-verify colour compliance with a follow-up photometric test.
Reduced Light Output or Dull Appearance
Likely Cause
Rubber deposits, tyre debris, or de-icing fluid residue contaminating the lens.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the lens for contamination; take a photometric reading before and after a test clean.
Corrective Action
Clean the lens with approved solvents and soft brushes, and adjust cleaning frequency based on the site's traffic and de-icing activity.
Beam Pattern Off-Target or Reduced Visibility
Likely Cause
Pavement settlement has tilted the fixture, altering its beam orientation.
Diagnostic Steps
Check fixture alignment with a digital level or alignment jig against the specified tolerance; inspect surrounding pavement for settlement or heave.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-aim the fixture, and address the underlying pavement settlement to prevent recurrence.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Colour Filter Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged UV exposure and repeated thermal cycling break down the polymer or glass colour filter, fading or shifting its transmitted colour.
Effect
Displayed colour drifts out of the ICAO/FAA chromaticity requirement, weakening the visual cue the wing bar is meant to provide.
Detection Method
Scheduled photometric colour testing and visual comparison against a new reference filter of the same type.
Lens Contamination Build-Up
Low Severity
Root Cause
Rubber deposits, tyre debris, and de-icing fluid residue accumulate on the flush-mounted lens, which sits directly in the wheel path.
Effect
Reduced light output and dulled colour appearance, potentially masking the fixture's true condition until a photometric check is run.
Detection Method
Visual inspection for surface contamination and a before/after photometric reading taken during a test clean.
Isolating Transformer Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Moisture ingress through a compromised housing seal leading to insulation breakdown, or a blown primary fuse-wire link from a transient surge on the series circuit.
Effect
Loss of the single fixture, or an open-circuit point that can take down the entire series loop if the open extends to the loop itself.
Detection Method
Megger test of the transformer, secondary voltage check with the CCR energised, and loop-resistance testing from the CCR output.
Housing Seal / Gasket Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Compression set in the gasket material, fastener loosening under repeated wheel loading, or physical damage from vehicle or aircraft traffic over the inset fixture.
Effect
Water enters the housing, accelerating corrosion of internal contacts and degrading insulation resistance across the fixture.
Detection Method
Annual seal and gasket inspection with fastener re-torque, and insulation resistance testing to catch early moisture ingress.
Pavement Settlement / Misalignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Sub-grade settlement, frost heave, or repeated heavy wheel loading around the flush-mounted housing shifts the fixture out of its installed position.
Effect
Beam pattern tilts off-target, reducing the fixture's effective visibility to approaching or taxiing aircraft.
Detection Method
Alignment checks with a digital level or alignment jig, and inspection of surrounding pavement for settlement or heave.
Series Cable / Connector Fault
High Severity
Root Cause
Loose or corroded crimp connections at the transformer terminals, or cable jacket damage from pavement work or excavation near the loop.
Effect
Intermittent operation or flickering at the fixture, or a full open/ground fault that trips the CCR or darkens the entire series loop.
Detection Method
Full-circuit megger testing to flag a low-resistance segment, and visual inspection of connectors at the fixture during annual maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

Wing bar circuits run on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the fixture before opening a base can or handling cable connectors.

Interlock Logic Before Bypassing
Operational

Where the fixture is configured as a stop or clearance bar wired into ALCMS/runway incursion protection logic, never bypass or manually force the circuit on/off without airside/ATC coordination, since an incorrect state can give a false hold or false clearance to pilots or vehicles.

Frangibility Not to Be Defeated
Mechanical

Never substitute the frangible coupling with a rigid fitting or over-torque it during installation or repair; the coupling must still shear cleanly on aircraft impact to limit damage under the frangibility requirement.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any work on or near an active runway or taxiway requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the fixture will be dark or out of tolerance, with vehicle and personnel movement kept clear of the movement area per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

FOD Control at the Base Can
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and removed gaskets accounted for at all times when the base can is open, and secure or remove any loose material before reinstating the fixture, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any cable, connector, or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the fixture and base are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a fixture out of aim tolerance, at reduced intensity, or otherwise non-compliant after maintenance; verify photometric and positional conformance, and correct colour/function per its role, before closing out the work and cancelling the associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Confirm Interlock Logic Before Any Bench Test
Compliance

If the wing bar is wired into ALCMS or stop-bar interlock logic, bench-testing a fixture by forcing it on or off outside the control system can leave the interlock in a state it never expected; test through the control logic, or fully isolate and tag the fixture from it first.

Bar Geometry Sets the Threshold's Perceived Width
Design

Wing bar spacing and aim collectively frame how wide the runway appears at the threshold; a fixture reinstalled slightly off its designed offset or aim distorts that perceived width for approaching pilots even though it measures fine in isolation.

LED Retrofit Step Matching
Retrofit

When retrofitting LED fixtures onto a legacy series circuit, check that the fixture's brightness-step curve actually tracks the CCR's current steps the way the original incandescent lamp did; a mismatched curve leaves the runway looking uneven between "steps" even though every fixture measures within spec individually.

Frangible Couplings Age, Even Without Impact
Mechanical

UV and thermal cycling can shift the shear characteristics of a composite or aluminium frangible coupling over years of service. A coupling that looks visually fine can no longer shear at its rated load, so periodic torque/shear verification belongs in the overhaul cycle, not just the post-impact inspection.

Size the Transformer to the Load, Not the Legacy Lamp
Electrical

Reusing an isolating transformer sized for the old incandescent wattage on a much lower-draw LED fixture is a common source of premature transformer failure and nuisance loop trips; re-select VA rating against the actual LED load rather than assuming the existing transformer is "close enough."

Balance Loops Sharing One CCR
Design

Where multiple series loops run off a single CCR, unequal loop lengths or lamp counts between loops can produce visibly uneven brightness runway-to-runway even though each individual fixture tests within photometric tolerance. Balance loop impedance at the design stage, not after commissioning complaints.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about wing bar lights: threshold, stop bar and clearance bar roles, colour, spacing, control, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a wing bar light?

A wing bar light is an AGL fixture group installed as a row of lights perpendicular to the runway or taxiway centreline. It turns a single-point cue into a continuous, colour-coded line across the pavement and can be configured as a threshold wing bar (green), a stop bar (red) or a clearance bar (yellow).

Q2What colour are wing bar lights?

The colour depends on the function. Threshold wing bars are green, stop bars are red and clearance bars are yellow. The colour is fixed by the lens or filter fitted at manufacture and installation, so a fixture cannot be switched from one colour to another.

Q3What is the difference between a threshold wing bar, a stop bar and a clearance bar?

They use the same row-of-lights hardware but serve different roles. A threshold wing bar (green) widens the threshold marking on CAT II/III precision approach runways. A stop bar (red) marks a runway holding position that must not be crossed without ATC clearance. A clearance bar (yellow) confirms the correct route at taxiway intersections and holding positions.

Q4Where are threshold wing bars installed?

On CAT II/III precision approach runways, threshold wing bars extend outward from the runway edge light row at the threshold, typically about 9–10 m beyond the edge light line. The distance is measured from the runway edge line, not from the centreline, and the bar is set out square to the runway centreline.

Q5How many lights are in a wing bar and how far apart are they?

A wing bar typically has three to five lights spaced at 1.5 m to 3 m intervals along a shared baseline. Base cans are precision-positioned to hold the specified spacing and alignment across the bar.

Q6How is the brightness of wing bar lights controlled?

Brightness is set at loop level by the Constant Current Regulator (CCR), which steps the series circuit through five settings: 2.8 A, 3.4 A, 4.1 A, 5.2 A and 6.6 A. The ALCMS commands the step, and every fixture on the loop changes intensity together. The ALCMS controls brightness and on/off state only; it cannot change a fixture's colour or beam pattern. Where wing bars form part of a stop bar circuit, the on/off command may come from runway incursion protection or ground-movement logic.

Q7Do wing bar lights need an isolating transformer?

Yes. Each fixture is fed from the constant current series circuit through an isolating transformer, typically 45 W or 65 W, matched to the lamp or LED load. The transformer taps the fixed loop current for the fixture, with L-823 connectors used for the primary and secondary connections. When retrofitting LEDs, re-select the transformer rating against the actual LED load.

Q8Which standards apply to wing bar lights?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (wing bar requirements for thresholds, stop bars and clearance bars), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (specification for runway, taxiway, heliport and vertiport light fixtures), IEC/TS 61827 (inset and elevated aerodrome luminaires) and FAA Engineering Brief #67 (light sources other than incandescent and xenon). Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q9What are the photometric requirements for wing bar lights?

Typical on-axis minimums are 8,000 cd for green threshold units, 150 cd for red stop bar units and 50 cd for yellow clearance bar units. Adjacent units in a bar should be matched within ±25% of each other, and field intensity tolerance is ±25% of rated value at each CCR step. Chromaticity must stay within the ICAO Annex 14 boundary for the applicable colour.

Q10How often should wing bar lights be inspected and maintained?

Typical intervals are quarterly for lens cleaning, semi-annual for CCR intensity step verification and alignment checks, and annual for photometric intensity and colour checks, seal and gasket inspection and insulation resistance testing. Your airport's approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q11Why does a wing bar light look faded, dull or off-target?

A faded or shifted colour usually points to UV and thermal degradation of the colour filter, which needs a photometric colour check and filter replacement. A dull light is typically rubber, tyre debris or de-icing residue on the lens, corrected by cleaning. An off-target beam is usually pavement settlement tilting the fixture, corrected by re-levelling and re-aiming it.

Q12What causes a wing bar circuit to trip the CCR?

The most common causes are moisture ingress at a fixture, connector or isolating transformer, or damaged cable jackets or corroded connections, which lower insulation resistance and create a ground or open-circuit fault on the series loop. Megger the full circuit to find the low-resistance segment and confirm insulation resistance has recovered before re-energising.
