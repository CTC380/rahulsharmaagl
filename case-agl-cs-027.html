AGL-CS-027 — Transformer Failure After Lightning Surge

The AGL Knowledge Hub | Engineering Case Study Series. A lightning-surge investigation where a nearby strike caused sudden primary winding insulation puncture — from CCR alarm confirmation through insulation testing and internal examination to surge-protection review.

Primary Skill

Important: This is an engineering simulation created for training. It is not presented as an actual airport incident. All readings, values, dates and outcomes are illustrative, and no airport, organisation, person or manufacturer is identified or implied.

1Case Study Overview

Following a severe thunderstorm, one runway edge light circuit became unavailable immediately after a nearby lightning event.

The Constant Current Regulator (CCR) indicated abnormal insulation conditions, and one isolating transformer was found completely inoperative.

Initial field inspection showed no significant mechanical damage to the transformer or pit assembly.

A detailed investigation identified lightning-induced surge damage to the transformer insulation system, where a high-energy transient caused primary winding insulation puncture and partial winding failure.

This case demonstrates that lightning surges can destroy transformer insulation even when no external physical damage is visible.

2Learning Objectives

After completing this case study, the learner should be able to:

  • Understand lightning surge effects on AGL isolating transformers.
  • Recognize post-lightning transformer failure patterns.
  • Differentiate surge damage from aging and moisture-related failures.
  • Perform transformer surge-damage investigations.
  • Evaluate insulation and winding integrity after lightning events.
  • Assess connector and cable damage associated with surge events.
  • Develop post-lightning inspection procedures.

3System Background

An AGL isolating transformer is connected between:

Primary Series Cable → Transformer Primary → Magnetic Coupling → Transformer Secondary → Field Fixture

Lightning surges may reach the transformer through:

  • Direct lightning strikes.
  • Induced surges.
  • Ground potential rise.
  • Electromagnetic coupling.
  • Surge propagation through underground cables.

The transformer insulation system is designed for normal operating voltage, not for extreme transient overvoltages.

4Problem Statement

The reported condition was:

  • Transformer failed immediately after a thunderstorm.
  • CCR insulation alarm active.
  • Associated runway edge light OFF.
  • Adjacent transformers operational.
  • No visible pit flooding.
  • Lightning activity confirmed near the runway.
Engineering Question

Has the transformer failed because of lightning-induced surge stress, or is the failure coincidental?

7Lightning Surge Mechanism

A typical transformer surge sequence is:

Lightning event → High-voltage transient → Surge enters primary cable → Primary winding overvoltage → Insulation puncture → Leakage current → Partial winding failure → Transformer outage

The failure is often sudden rather than progressive.

9Investigation

Step 1: Verify Primary Circuit

Measurements confirmed:

CCR current Normal
Primary cable continuity Normal
Adjacent transformers Healthy

The primary cable remained operational.

Step 2: Transformer Continuity Testing

Results:

Primary winding Abnormal
Secondary winding Normal
Winding resistance Increased

The primary winding had been damaged.

Step 3: Insulation Resistance Testing

Results:

Primary to Earth 2 MΩ
Secondary to Earth Greater than 1000 MΩ
Primary to Secondary 3 MΩ

The primary insulation system had deteriorated severely.

Step 4: Connector Inspection

Inspection revealed:

  • Minor arc marks.
  • Surface carbonization.
  • Contact pitting.
  • No major connector failure.

The connector had experienced surge stress but remained functional.

Step 5: Internal Transformer Examination

The failed transformer showed:

  • Primary winding insulation puncture.
  • Carbonized insulation.
  • Localized winding damage.
  • Resin cracking.
  • Evidence of transient overvoltage stress.

The damage pattern was characteristic of lightning-surge insulation failure.

10Root Cause

Training-Case Finding

A nearby lightning strike induced a high-energy surge into the primary cable circuit, causing primary winding insulation puncture and partial winding damage inside the isolating transformer.

The surge exceeded the dielectric strength of the transformer insulation system.

The transformer failed due to transient overvoltage stress, not aging or moisture deterioration.

11Corrective Action

The maintenance team:

  1. Isolated the circuit.
  2. Removed the surge-damaged transformer.
  3. Tested adjacent transformers.
  4. Inspected associated connectors.
  5. Verified primary cable insulation.
  6. Installed a new approved transformer.
  7. Performed insulation resistance testing.
  8. Conducted winding verification.
  9. Restored the circuit.
  10. Reviewed lightning protection measures.

14Why This Case Was Important

Lightning damage differs from normal transformer failure because it is:

  • Sudden.
  • High-energy.
  • Often localized.
  • Frequently accompanied by insulation puncture.
  • Sometimes invisible externally.

A transformer may appear physically intact while suffering catastrophic internal insulation damage.

16Engineer's Decision Point

You observe: Transformer failed immediately after a thunderstorm. CCR insulation alarm active. Primary cable healthy. Adjacent transformers operational. What should you investigate first?

A. Replace the CCR.
B. Replace the secondary cable.
C. Perform transformer insulation and winding testing for surge damage.
D. Increase CCR current.
Correct answer: C. With the CCR and primary cable healthy, the transformer is the primary suspect. Insulation and winding testing will confirm surge-related internal damage.

19Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about transformer failure after a lightning surge, based on this case study.

Q1What is AGL-CS-027?

AGL-CS-027 is an engineering training case study on an isolating transformer that failed after a nearby lightning surge. It is an engineering simulation created for training, not a report of an actual airport incident.

Q2What were the initial symptoms?

The circuit became unavailable immediately after a severe thunderstorm, the CCR showed an insulation alarm and the associated light was OFF. Adjacent transformers were operational, there was no visible pit flooding and lightning activity was confirmed near the runway.

Q3What was the root cause?

A nearby lightning strike induced a high-energy surge into the primary cable circuit, causing primary winding insulation puncture and partial winding damage inside the transformer. It failed from transient overvoltage stress, not ageing or moisture deterioration.

Q4How do you confirm surge damage in a transformer?

Perform transformer insulation and winding testing. With the CCR and primary cable healthy, the transformer is the primary suspect. The team used a 5 kV insulation resistance tester, winding resistance meter and turns-ratio verification equipment.

Q5Why were adjacent transformers and connectors also checked?

Corrective action included testing adjacent transformers, inspecting associated connectors and verifying primary cable insulation, since a surge can affect more than the one visibly failed unit.

Q6How was the circuit repaired?

The team isolated the circuit, removed the surge-damaged transformer and installed a new approved transformer. They performed insulation resistance and winding verification, restored the circuit and reviewed lightning protection measures.

Q7How was the repair verified?

Primary and secondary insulation were both greater than 1000 MΩ, transformer operation was normal and the light was restored.

Q8What should you investigate first if a transformer fails right after a thunderstorm with an insulation alarm?

Perform transformer insulation and winding testing for surge damage. Replacing the CCR or secondary cable, or increasing CCR current, is not the right first step.

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist

Test Transformers Near a Strike Even If They Look Fine

Post-Event

Internal insulation damage may not be visible. Include insulation and winding checks across the affected area.

Inspect Neighbours of the Failed Unit

Scope

A surge travels along the circuit. Check adjacent transformers and connectors that carried the same event.

Review Surge Protection After the Event

Protection

Check devices, bonding and earthing for damage and consider whether the arrangement still suits the exposure.

Retest After a Short Interval

Follow-Up

Weakened insulation may fail later. Plan a repeat check after the first return to service.

20Case Study Conclusion

Key Engineering Principle

Lightning surges can cause sudden internal insulation failure of AGL isolating transformers even when external physical damage is minimal or absent.

Systematic insulation testing, winding resistance measurement, connector inspection, cable verification, and post-lightning transformer evaluation are essential for identifying surge-related damage and restoring long-term system reliability.

In AGL engineering, a transformer that survives the thunderstorm externally may still have suffered a fatal internal insulation failure from the lightning surge.
