Cover Plate

A robust, flush‑mounted steel or aluminium plate that seals the top of a base can, junction box, or other AGL enclosure. It provides a watertight barrier, protects internal components from debris and moisture, and offers a safe, level surface for pavement operations, while allowing easy access for maintenance and inspection.

Overview

What Is It?

A Cover Plate (also known as a lid, access cover, or closure plate) is a flat, rigid plate that is bolted or secured over the opening of a base can, junction box, or other below‑grade AGL enclosure. Its primary purpose is to seal the enclosure, preventing water, de‑icing fluids, debris, and contaminants from entering and damaging the isolating transformer, cable connections, or other internal components. At the same time, it provides a flush surface designed for the same traffic loading as the base can it covers — the cover's load rating must match its can's class, since a light-duty L-867 cover is not rated for aircraft traffic even where a heavier L-868 installation nearby is.

Key characteristics of cover plates include:

  • Flush Design: The plate is designed to sit flush with or slightly below the surrounding pavement surface, minimising the risk of damage from snowploughs or aircraft tyres.
  • Watertight Seal: A neoprene or silicone gasket is fitted between the plate and the enclosure flange to provide a watertight seal, preventing moisture ingress.
  • Load‑Bearing Capacity: Made from high‑strength steel or aluminium, the plate is designed to withstand heavy aircraft wheel loads (rated ≥50–100 kN vertical) without deformation or failure.
  • Corrosion Protection: Hot‑dip galvanised, powder‑coated, or stainless steel construction ensures long‑term resistance to the harsh airfield environment.

Cover plates are used in the following applications:

  • Base Cans: Sealing the top of base cans that do not have a fixture mounted directly on them (e.g., spare cans, junction points).
  • Junction Boxes: Providing access to underground cable splices and terminations while protecting them from the environment.
  • Distribution Boxes: Covering above‑ground or flush‑mounted distribution panels and enclosures.
  • Earth Pits: Providing access to earthing connections while protecting them from debris and moisture.
  • Test Points: Covering test access points for circuit testing and troubleshooting.

The cover plate is secured with bolts or a quick‑release mechanism, allowing for easy removal during maintenance. The plate is typically marked with the enclosure type or circuit identification for easy reference.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Any enclosure with no active fixture mounted on it — a spare base can, a junction box, an earth pit — is still an opening in the pavement, and an unsealed or poorly sealed opening is exactly as dangerous as a damaged fixture: it lets water and contaminants reach the electrical connections below, and it leaves a surface irregularity that aircraft wheels, snowploughs, or foreign object debris (FOD) can catch on. The cover plate's purpose is to give these unfixtured openings the same environmental sealing and load-bearing protection that an installed fixture would otherwise provide, restoring a flush, watertight, traffic-rated surface so that a maintenance access point or a cable splice never has to compromise on airfield safety just because there is no light or transformer sitting on top of it.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Cover plates are used throughout the airfield wherever underground or below‑grade enclosures require a protective, flush‑mounted cover, including:

  • Runway Lighting Circuits: Sealing base cans at junction points, spare cans, or locations where elevated fixtures are not installed.
  • Taxiway Lighting Circuits: Covering junction boxes and base cans along taxiway lighting circuits.
  • Approach Lighting Systems: Sealing base cans and junction boxes in approach lighting systems.
  • Earth Pits and Earthing Systems: Providing access to earth pits while keeping them sealed and protected.
  • Distribution and Control Panels: Covering flush‑mounted distribution boxes and control panels in the airfield.
  • Test Points and Monitoring Points: Sealing test access points for circuit diagnostics and maintenance.

The plate is available in various diameters (150mm to 300mm) to match the base can or junction box size, and in thicknesses ranging from 10mm to 25mm depending on the load rating. The plate is typically recessed into the pavement so that the top surface is flush with the surrounding pavement, ensuring a smooth surface for vehicle and aircraft operations.

Placement

Installation & Location

The cover plate sits directly on the base can or junction box rim, set flush with the surrounding pavement or shoulder surface so it doesn't become a FOD or tripping hazard once installed. Fastener pattern and gasket seating are checked during fitting so the cover seals against water ingress into the empty or spare can beneath it; where the plate covers a spare (currently unused) can, its position and load rating are still set to the same pavement-load standard as an active fixture location, since the surface must handle the same aircraft or vehicle traffic regardless of what's installed beneath it.

Function

How Does It Work?

The cover plate functions as a protective barrier and access point for below‑grade AGL enclosures. Its design ensures that the enclosure remains sealed and protected while providing a safe, level surface for pavement operations:

  • Sealing: The plate is bolted onto the enclosure flange with a gasket between the two surfaces. When the bolts are torqued, the gasket is compressed, creating a watertight seal that prevents moisture, de‑icing fluids, and debris from entering the enclosure.
  • Load Distribution: The plate distributes the load from aircraft, vehicles, and snowploughs over the full area of the enclosure flange, preventing localised stress and damage to the enclosure.
  • Accessibility: The plate is secured with bolts that can be easily removed, allowing maintenance personnel to access the enclosure for inspection, testing, or repair of the internal components.
  • Safety: The flush‑mounted design eliminates trip hazards and reduces the risk of tyre damage from protruding edges.

The installation process typically involves:

  • Cleaning: The enclosure flange and the underside of the cover plate are cleaned to ensure a good seal.
  • Gasket Placement: A gasket is placed on the enclosure flange, aligning with the bolt holes.
  • Plate Placement: The cover plate is positioned over the enclosure, and the bolts are inserted.
  • Torquing: The bolts are torqued to the specified value in a cross‑pattern to ensure even compression of the gasket.
  • Level Check: The plate is checked to ensure it is level and flush with the surrounding pavement. If necessary, shims are used to achieve the correct height.

The cover plate is designed to provide a service life of 20+ years, with regular inspection of the gasket, bolts, and plate condition. The plate should be checked for corrosion, and the bolts should be re‑torqued periodically to maintain the seal.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The cover plate carries no electronic function — it is the load-bearing, sealed access point sitting directly above a below-grade enclosure such as a base can or junction box. Its role in the architecture is to let that enclosure remain buried and protected while still being reachable for maintenance, and to do so without becoming a hazard or an obstruction to the aircraft and vehicle traffic passing over it. It is rated for the traffic load of its specific location (a taxiway cover plate and an apron cover plate can have different load ratings) and sealed against surface water intrusion, but it has no status the ALCMS can see — its condition is confirmed the same way as the enclosure beneath it, by opening it during inspection.

Rated for the traffic load of its specific location — its condition is checked by opening it, not by any ALCMS signal.

Construction

Physical Construction

The cover plate is a flat, load-rated disc of structural steel, stainless steel, or aluminium, sized to span and seal the open top of a base can or junction box while its perimeter bolt pattern matches the can's mounting flange. A continuous gasket, typically neoprene, EPDM, or silicone, is compressed between the plate and the can rim as the bolts are torqued down, forming the seal that gives the assembly its IP67/IP68 rating against water and dust ingress at the pavement surface. Plate thickness is selected to suit the expected aircraft wheel loading, with heavier-gauge plates or additional stiffening ribs used where higher load ratings are required. Surface finishing, hot-dip galvanising, powder-coating, or a plain/polished stainless finish, protects the plate from de-icing fluid and fuel exposure, and cable or conduit pass-through holes, where fitted, are individually sealed with grommets or glands to preserve the overall ingress protection.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Gasket Seal Inspection
Annual

Check the neoprene, EPDM, or silicone gasket for compression set, cracking, or fuel degradation that could break the IP67/IP68 seal against the base can.

Bolt Torque Verification
Annual

Re-torque mounting bolts to spec so the plate maintains its rated ≥50 kN vertical load capacity and even gasket compression.

Surface Corrosion Check
Annual

Inspect the plate surface and edges for coating breakdown, pitting, or galvanising loss, especially on trafficked or de-icing-fluid-exposed pavement.

Load Rating / Deflection Check
Annual

Look for deformation, cracking, or excessive deflection under wheel or vehicle loading that could indicate the plate is undersized or fatigued.

Pavement Interface Inspection
Annual

Check the surrounding pavement or turf for settlement, gaps, or debris accumulation that could compromise the flush fit and drainage around the plate.

Skid Resistance Check
Annual

Verify the plate's surface finish still provides adequate skid resistance for foot and vehicle traffic passing over it.

Fastener Condition Check
Annual

Inspect bolt heads and recessed fasteners for stripping, corrosion, or loosening that could complicate future removal for base can access.

Standing Water Check
Annual

Confirm no water pools on or around the plate after rain, which can indicate a failed seal or blocked drainage beneath the fixture.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Gasket Deterioration

The gasket can degrade over time due to UV exposure, temperature cycling, and chemical attack from de‑icing fluids and fuels. A deteriorated gasket allows water and contaminants to enter the enclosure. Replace the gasket at regular intervals (typically every 5‑10 years) and inspect it during every maintenance visit.

Corrosion of Bolts and Plate

Bolts and the plate surface can corrode, especially in coastal or chemically aggressive environments. Use stainless steel hardware, apply anti‑seize compound, and regularly inspect for rust. Touch up damaged galvanising or powder coating.

Loose Bolts

Vibration from aircraft and thermal cycling can cause the bolts to loosen, compromising the seal and creating a trip hazard. Use lock washers, thread‑locking compound, and schedule regular bolt torque checks as part of the maintenance program.

Plate Misalignment or Protrusion

If the cover plate is not installed flush with the pavement, it can be struck by snowploughs or aircraft tyres, causing damage to the plate, the enclosure, or the tyre. Ensure the plate is properly recessed and level, and use shims if necessary.

Stripped Bolt Threads

Repeated removal and installation of the bolts can strip the threads in the enclosure flange. Use thread inserts (Heli‑coils) to repair damaged threads, and apply anti‑seize compound to prevent galling.

Debris Accumulation Around Plate

Dirt, rubber deposits, and debris can accumulate around the edge of the cover plate, making it difficult to remove and potentially affecting the seal. Regularly clean the area around the plate and ensure the edge clearance is maintained.

Incorrect Gasket Compression

Over‑torquing or under‑torquing the bolts can result in incorrect gasket compression, leading to leaks or damage to the gasket. Always use a torque wrench and follow the manufacturer's specified torque values.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Water or Contaminants Found Inside the Enclosure
Likely Cause
The gasket has degraded from UV exposure, temperature cycling, or chemical attack from de-icing fluids and fuels, breaking the seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Remove the cover plate and inspect the gasket for hardening, cracking, or flattening; check its age against the 5-10 year replacement interval.
Corrective Action
Replace the gasket with the correct size and material, and re-torque the cover plate evenly to the manufacturer's specification.
Rust or Corrosion Found on Bolts or Plate Surface
Likely Cause
Coastal or chemically aggressive site conditions have corroded the bolts or plate, especially where the protective coating has been damaged.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect bolts and the plate surface for rust, and check whether stainless steel hardware and anti-seize compound were used at installation.
Corrective Action
Replace corroded bolts with stainless steel hardware, apply anti-seize compound, and touch up any damaged galvanising or powder coating.
Cover Plate Loose or Seal Compromised
Likely Cause
Vibration from aircraft operations and thermal cycling have loosened the retaining bolts over time.
Diagnostic Steps
Check bolt torque against the manufacturer's specification, and inspect for lock washers or thread-locking compound at the joint.
Corrective Action
Re-torque bolts to specification with lock washers or thread-locking compound applied, and add the plate to the routine torque-check schedule.
Plate Struck by Snowplough, Vehicle, or Aircraft Tyre
Likely Cause
The cover plate was not installed flush and recessed with the pavement, leaving it proud enough to be struck.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the plate's level and recess depth against the pavement surface using a straightedge.
Corrective Action
Re-level and re-shim the plate to sit flush and properly recessed, and repair any damage to the plate, enclosure, or surrounding pavement found.
Bolts Will Not Tighten Securely
Likely Cause
Repeated removal and installation of the bolts has stripped the threads in the enclosure flange.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the flange threads for stripping or damage when the plate is removed.
Corrective Action
Repair damaged threads with thread inserts (Heli-coils) and apply anti-seize compound to prevent future galling.
Plate Difficult to Remove During Maintenance
Likely Cause
Dirt, rubber deposits, or debris have accumulated around the edge of the cover plate, affecting clearance and the seal.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the area around the plate edge for debris buildup, and check that edge clearance is maintained.
Corrective Action
Clean the area around the plate regularly, and re-establish proper edge clearance before reinstalling.
Leak or Gasket Damage Despite a New Gasket Being Fitted
Likely Cause
Bolts were over-torqued or under-torqued during installation, resulting in incorrect gasket compression.
Diagnostic Steps
Check bolt torque values against the manufacturer's specification using a calibrated torque wrench.
Corrective Action
Re-torque all bolts evenly to the correct specified value, replacing the gasket if it has already been damaged by incorrect compression.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Assume the Enclosure Below Is Live
Electrical Safety

Treat the base can or junction box beneath the plate as energised until the associated series circuit has been de-energised, locked out, and verified zero energy at the CCR, even if the plate itself carries no current.

Manual Handling
Personnel Safety

Cover plates are load-rated for aircraft and vehicle traffic and can be heavy for their size; use correct lifting technique or a lifting tool where provided, and avoid working alone on larger plates.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Removing a cover plate creates an open enclosure and a surface irregularity on or near the movement area; coordinate with ATC and raise a NOTAM where the location falls within the runway or taxiway strip, and never leave a plate removed and unattended.

FOD Control
Operational

Keep fasteners, gaskets, and the plate itself accounted for at all times once lifted, and never leave a cover plate or its hardware loose on the pavement, since a dropped fastener or plate is a direct FOD hazard to aircraft.

Flush Fit on Reinstatement
Compliance

Reinstate the plate flush with, or slightly below, the surrounding pavement or shoulder surface and torque fasteners evenly so the surface doesn't become a tripping or FOD hazard, regardless of whether the can beneath is active or spare.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Confirm the gasket seats correctly and the plate sits flush before closing out the work, since a poorly reinstated plate allows water ingress into the enclosure below even if it looks secured.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

By Rahul Sharma

Corroded Fasteners Complicate Future Access
Maintenance

Galvanic or salt-driven corrosion can seize cover plate bolts over time, risking stripped heads on the next access attempt; use an anti-seize compound compatible with the plate and fastener materials at every reinstall, not just when corrosion is already visible.

Inspect the Seal Separately From the Plate Itself
Durability

A cover plate can look structurally sound while its gasket has degraded from repeated freeze-thaw cycling or de-icing fluid exposure, allowing water ingress the plate appears to be preventing; check gasket condition as its own line item rather than inferring it from the plate's visual state.

Re-Verify Load Rating After Any Visible Repair
Compliance

A cover plate that has been welded or otherwise repaired after cracking needs its load capacity re-verified before it goes back into a trafficked area; a visually sound repair is not the same as a confirmed load-rated one.

Confirm Electrical Continuity, Not Just Mechanical Seating
Electrical

Where a cover plate forms part of the bonding path for an enclosure near powered equipment, a plate that seats and seals correctly can still fail to maintain electrical continuity; verify bonding continuity across the plate to the enclosure, especially after any repaint or corrosion treatment.

Track Anti-Skid Surface Wear Separately From Structural Condition
Inspection

Repeated snowplough and vehicle traffic wears the anti-skid surface texture faster than it degrades the underlying plate structure; inspect surface texture on its own rather than assuming a structurally sound plate still provides adequate traction.

Verify Replacement Plates Match the Original Specification
Compliance

In field emergencies it's common for a mismatched or generic plate to go in as a temporary fix and then stay in place; check part number and load rating against the original specification during any post-repair audit rather than assuming an installed plate is the correct one.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about AGL cover plates: function, materials, load rating, standards and maintenance.

Q1What is a cover plate in airfield ground lighting?

A cover plate, also called a lid or access cover, is a flat rigid plate bolted over the opening of a base can, junction box or other below-grade AGL enclosure to seal it and give a flush, load-bearing surface.

Q2Where are cover plates used?

On base cans, spare cans and junction boxes in runway, taxiway and approach lighting circuits, on earth pits, and on flush-mounted distribution and control boxes.

Q3How does a cover plate keep an enclosure watertight?

It is bolted to the enclosure flange with a gasket between the surfaces. Torquing the bolts compresses the gasket, giving an IP67/IP68 seal against moisture, de-icing fluids and debris.

Q4What materials and sizes are cover plates made from?

Typically structural steel (S235JR or S355JR), hot-dip galvanised to at least 85 µm, or 316L stainless steel, in diameters from 150 mm to 300 mm and thicknesses of 10 mm to 25 mm depending on load rating.

Q5What load can a cover plate carry?

It must match its base can's class. The page gives light vehicular loading only for L-867 covers and at least 50 kN to 100 kN for aircraft-rated covers, so check the rating against the location.

Q6Which standards apply to cover plates?

The main references are FAA AC 150/5345-42 (light bases, transformer housings, junction boxes and accessories), ICAO Annex 14, EN 10025 for the steel and ASTM A123 for galvanising.

Q7How often should cover plates be inspected?

Annually. Check the gasket for compression set or fuel damage, re-torque the bolts and inspect the surface and edges for coating breakdown or corrosion.

Q8What are the common problems with cover plates?

Gasket deterioration, corroded bolts and plate, loose bolts from vibration, a plate that sits proud of the pavement and can be struck by snowploughs, and stripped bolt threads from repeated removal.
