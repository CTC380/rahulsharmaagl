PAPI Alignment/Calibration Tool

A precision instrument used to set and verify the beam angle of PAPI light units during installation and periodic maintenance. These tools—ranging from mechanical jigs and optical theodolites to electronic inclinometers—ensure that each unit projects its red/white transition at the exact angle required for accurate glide slope guidance.

Overview

What Is It?

The PAPI Alignment/Calibration Tool is a specialised instrument used by airfield maintenance teams to set the elevation (vertical angle) and azimuth (horizontal orientation) of individual PAPI light units. The tool ensures that the red/white transition occurs at the exact glide slope angle required—typically 3° for fixed‑wing runways or 4°–6° for helicopter PAPI—and that the beam is aligned with the runway centreline.

Several types of tools are used, depending on the accuracy required and the installation environment:

Mechanical Jig

A physical template or fixture that mounts directly onto the PAPI unit, providing a reference surface for setting the angle using a spirit level or protractor.

Optical Theodolite

A precision optical instrument that measures horizontal and vertical angles with high accuracy. Used to set the PAPI beam relative to a surveyed reference point on the runway.

Electronic Inclinometer

A digital level with high‑resolution angle sensors, often with Bluetooth or display readout. Provides quick, accurate angle measurements and can store calibration data.

Modern airfields often use a combination of these tools, with the electronic inclinometer becoming the preferred choice for its speed, accuracy, and data‑logging capabilities.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The calibration tool exists to bridge the gap between the elevation and azimuth adjustment mechanisms being physically capable of fine positioning, and a technician actually achieving the exact angle those mechanisms were built for. A tolerance measured in arc-minutes cannot be judged by eye or by feel, no matter how experienced the technician — without a precision reference instrument, the adjustment mechanism's fine threads and locking hardware are simply unusable at the accuracy PAPI requires. The tool's real function is repeatability as much as accuracy: at commissioning, after a pole is disturbed by a storm or ground settlement, or during periodic re-verification, it lets the same exact angle be re-established with confidence, and — for the electronic inclinometer especially — with a logged record proving it was done. In effect, the calibration tool is what turns a mechanically adjustable fixture into one that reliably meets its designed glide slope angle in practice, not just on paper.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The alignment/calibration tool is an essential part of every PAPI installation and maintenance program:

  • Initial installation – setting the beam angles of all four PAPI units during system commissioning.
  • Periodic recalibration – verifying and adjusting angles as part of routine maintenance (typically every 6–12 months).
  • After maintenance or replacement – re‑aligning a unit after lamp/LED replacement, optical assembly servicing, or frangible coupling replacement.
  • After impact or storm damage – checking alignment after any event that could have shifted the unit.
  • Runway surveys – used in conjunction with surveying equipment to establish reference points for PAPI placement.
  • Quality assurance – verifying that the PAPI system meets ICAO/FAA photometric requirements.
Care

Storage & Handling

Between calibration sessions, the tool is returned to its padded case with the jig, theodolite optics, or inclinometer sensor protected from knocks, since the sub-degree accuracy this tool provides depends on internal reference surfaces and sensors that a hard bump can throw out of true. Mechanical jigs are checked for flatness and squareness against a reference surface before each use, and any jig showing wear at its mounting or reference points is set aside for recalibration or replacement rather than used on the assumption it's still accurate. The electronic inclinometer's zero calibration is verified against a known flat reference periodically, and its battery is checked before a commissioning or recalibration session where a mid-sequence power loss would mean re-measuring units already completed. All tool types are kept dry and within their rated storage temperature range, and any stored calibration data on the electronic inclinometer is backed up or transferred before the unit is reused for a different runway's PAPI array, so records from one site aren't inadvertently mixed with another's.

Function

How Does It Work?

The alignment process varies depending on the tool type, but the fundamental steps are similar:

  • Setup: The tool is mounted or positioned relative to the PAPI unit. For mechanical jigs, the jig is clamped onto the unit’s optical window or housing. For theodolites, the instrument is set up on a tripod at a known distance from the PAPI. Electronic inclinometers are placed on a flat reference surface on the unit.
  • Reference Setting: A reference point or angle is established. For example, the theodolite may be aimed at a survey marker on the runway threshold to set azimuth. The inclinometer may be zeroed on a known horizontal surface.
  • Angle Measurement: The tool reads the current elevation (vertical angle) and azimuth of the PAPI unit. The measured angle is compared with the required glide slope angle (e.g., 3°). The difference is noted.
  • Adjustment: The PAPI unit’s elevation and azimuth adjustments (on the mounting base) are loosened, and the unit is rotated or tilted until the tool indicates the correct angles. This is done incrementally, with re‑checking after each adjustment.
  • Locking & Verification: Once the correct angles are achieved, the adjustment mechanisms are locked. The tool is used again to verify that the angles have not shifted during locking.
  • Documentation: The final measured angles are recorded (often automatically by electronic tools) for maintenance logs and future reference.

For a complete PAPI system, each of the four units is aligned individually, with the elevation angles set to produce the characteristic red/white transition pattern (e.g., 2°30′, 2°50′, 3°10′, 3°30′ for a 3° glide path).

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The calibration tool sits outside the airfield's electrical control system entirely — it has no place in the ALCMS/CCR command chain that runs the PAPI's lamps day to day. Its "architecture" is instead a manual measure-adjust-verify loop, with the technician acting as the controller and the tool providing the reference measurement at each step: read the current angle, compare it against the required glide-path value, adjust, and re-measure, repeating until the reading holds within tolerance before locking the adjustment down.

This is a fundamentally different kind of control loop from the ones elsewhere in the AGL system — there is no continuous automatic feedback, no ALCMS visibility into it, and no fault reporting; the loop closes only for as long as the technician is actively cycling through it, and its result (the locked mechanical angle) is what every subsequent electrical control function in the PAPI unit then depends on.

Reference Measurement → Deviation Calculated → Technician Adjusts → Re-Measure (manual loop, technician-closed)

Construction

Physical Construction

Build varies by tool type but each is designed to mount directly on, or reference against, the PAPI unit being set. A mechanical jig is a rigid metal template or fixture, machined to seat squarely against the PAPI unit's mounting face, with a flat reference surface and an integral bubble level or protractor scale that the technician reads directly. An optical theodolite is a precision instrument on its own levelling tripod, with a rotating telescope on a graduated horizontal and vertical circle, allowing the operator to sight a surveyed reference point and read off the angle to fine resolution independent of contact with the PAPI unit itself. An electronic inclinometer is a compact, sealed housing containing a solid-state angle sensor, digital display or Bluetooth output, and battery, designed to sit flat against a machined reference face on the PAPI unit and provide a direct digital angle reading; some versions log or transmit the reading for calibration records. All three are built for repeated field use and are stored and transported in a protective case to preserve their calibration accuracy between uses.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Tool Calibration Drift

Electronic inclinometers and theodolites require periodic calibration to maintain accuracy. A tool that has been dropped or subjected to extreme temperatures may give false readings. Always check calibration before use and send for service annually.

Improper Tool Mounting

If the alignment tool is not securely mounted or placed on an unclean or uneven surface, the measurement will be inaccurate. Ensure the mounting surface is clean and use the manufacturer’s recommended mounting procedure.

Operator Error

Incorrect reading of the tool (e.g., misreading a vernier scale, not accounting for zero offset, or using the wrong reference point) is a common cause of misalignment. Always double‑check readings and, where possible, have a second operator verify.

Environmental Interference

Wind, vibration, and thermal expansion can affect the stability of the tool during measurement. Use a sturdy tripod, perform measurements in calm conditions, and allow the PAPI unit to stabilise to ambient temperature before aligning.

Lack of Reference Data

If the runway survey data or the required glide slope angle is not accurately known, the alignment will be incorrect. Always have up‑to‑date survey data and verify the required angle from the airfield’s AGL documentation.

Battery Depletion

Electronic tools can run out of battery mid‑alignment, leading to lost settings or interruptions. Always start with a fully charged battery and have a spare on hand.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Tool Gives Inconsistent or Suspect Readings
Likely Cause
Calibration drift in the electronic inclinometer/theodolite from a drop, temperature extremes, or an overdue service interval.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the tool against a known reference angle before use, review the last calibration date/certificate, and inspect the tool for physical damage.
Corrective Action
Recalibrate or send the tool for service, replace it if damage is confirmed, and do not use it until calibration is verified.
Measurement Varies Between Repeated Readings at the Same Point
Likely Cause
Improper tool mounting on an unclean, uneven, or unstable surface.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the mounting surface and interface, verify the tool is seated per the manufacturer's mounting procedure, and repeat the reading after re-seating.
Corrective Action
Clean and level the mounting surface, re-mount per procedure, and confirm repeatable readings before proceeding.
Alignment Result Doesn't Match a Second Check
Likely Cause
Operator error — a misread scale, an unaccounted zero offset, or the wrong reference point used.
Diagnostic Steps
Have a second operator independently verify the reading, and re-check the zero offset and reference point against procedure.
Corrective Action
Correct the reading procedure, re-zero the instrument, and repeat the measurement with verification.
Unstable Readings in the Field
Likely Cause
Environmental interference — wind, vibration, or thermal expansion affecting the tool during measurement.
Diagnostic Steps
Check for wind or vibration at the time of measurement and confirm the PAPI unit and tool have stabilised to ambient temperature.
Corrective Action
Use a sturdier tripod or mount, perform the measurement in calmer conditions, and allow thermal stabilisation before re-measuring.
Tool Powers Down or Loses Settings Mid-Alignment
Likely Cause
Battery depletion during the alignment procedure.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the battery charge level and review whether a spare battery was available on site.
Corrective Action
Replace with a fully charged battery, restart the alignment procedure, and carry a spare battery for future work.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Optical Calibration Drift
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal optical component ageing, or a shock/impact event during transport that shifted the tool's internal reference alignment.
Effect
Tool gives inconsistent or suspect readings, undermining confidence in every PAPI alignment set with it until corrected.
Detection Method
Periodic calibration check against a certified reference standard, at the interval specified by the manufacturer.
Mounting Interface Wear
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Repeated mounting and dismounting on the PAPI unit wearing the tool's mounting interface or locating pins.
Effect
Measurement varies between repeated readings at the same point, even when the underlying PAPI alignment has not changed.
Detection Method
Repeatability test by taking multiple readings at a known reference point, and inspection of the mounting interface for wear.
Level Sensor Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Ageing of the internal level/inclinometer sensor, or condensation forming inside the sensor housing over repeated temperature cycling.
Effect
Alignment result doesn't match a second check with an independent instrument, indicating the tool's own reference has drifted.
Detection Method
Cross-check against a second calibration tool or independent survey instrument during scheduled verification.
Vibration Sensitivity in the Field
Low Severity
Root Cause
Wind-induced vibration, ground traffic nearby, or the tool resting on an unstable temporary mount during a field reading.
Effect
Unstable readings in the field that fluctuate beyond the tool's rated accuracy, without an underlying tool fault.
Detection Method
Repeat readings under calmer conditions, and comparison against readings taken on a more stable mount.
Battery / Power Failure Mid-Alignment
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Battery ageing beyond its service life, or a loose battery contact that causes an intermittent power interruption.
Effect
Tool powers down or loses settings mid-alignment, forcing the alignment procedure to restart from the beginning.
Detection Method
Battery voltage check before each field use, and inspection of battery contacts during scheduled maintenance.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Glide Path Safety Impact
Operational

A tool reading that has drifted can silently propagate into a PAPI's aim, directly affecting the visual glide path pilots fly on approach; treat any calibration doubt as a stop-work condition until it is resolved.

Verify Calibration Before Use
Verification

Check the tool against a certified reference standard at the manufacturer's specified interval, and after any shock or impact event during transport, before trusting it to set or verify a PAPI unit's aim.

Cross-Check Against an Independent Method
Verification

Where practical, confirm a critical alignment reading with a second independent instrument or method before accepting it, since the tool's own mounting interface or level sensor can drift without an obvious external sign.

Confirm Fixture State Before Mounting
Personnel Safety

Confirm the PAPI unit is de-energised or has cooled, as applicable, before mounting the tool on the fixture, and follow the aerodrome's electrical safety procedure for any work at the base can.

Working at Height / Near the Movement Area
Personnel Safety

Use appropriate fall protection and stable footing when mounting the tool on an elevated PAPI unit, and keep vehicles and personnel clear of the runway/taxiway strip per the aerodrome's safety management procedures.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Alignment or calibration work on an in-service PAPI requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the unit will be off-aim or out of service during the check.

Handling & Storage
Mechanical

Store and transport the tool in its case with the optical and mounting interface protected, since the mounting pins and internal optics are the components most sensitive to accumulated wear and shock.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not sign off an alignment check on a reading you can't corroborate; confirm the resulting elevation/azimuth setting against the approved glide path and centreline reference before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Level Sensor Can Drift Without Any External Sign
Verification

An electronic level or inclinometer inside the tool can shift its zero point from repeated shock over its service life without any visible damage; recalibration on schedule is the only way to catch this, not periodic visual inspection.

Independent Cross-Check Catches Tool-Specific, Not Just Random, Error
Verification

A single reliable-looking reading from one instrument can still carry a systematic error unique to that tool's mounting interface or sensor; a second independent method doesn't just add confidence, it specifically catches errors a repeat reading with the same tool never would.

Case Damage Predicts Reading Errors Before the Numbers Do
Mechanical

A dented case or a mounting pin that no longer seats smoothly is often the first sign that a tool has taken a shock capable of shifting its internal calibration, even while it still produces plausible-looking readings; treat visible case damage as a reason to recalibrate immediately, not as a cosmetic issue.

Log Which Reference Standard Verified Each Calibration
Compliance

Recording which certified reference standard was used, and its own certification date, at each calibration check creates a traceable chain; without it, a future audit or fault investigation has no way to establish how reliable a past PAPI setting actually was.

Temperature Extremes at the Time of Use Affect Accuracy
Environmental

Some calibration tools have a rated operating temperature range narrower than typical airfield ambient extremes; a reading taken at the edge of or beyond that range carries more uncertainty than the tool's nominal accuracy spec suggests, even if it appears to function normally.

Standardise the Mounting Procedure Across Technicians
Workmanship

Small variations in how different technicians seat or clamp the tool on the fixture's mounting interface can introduce reading differences larger than the tool's own rated accuracy; a documented, consistent mounting procedure removes this variable from the measurement.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about PAPI alignment and calibration tools: tool types, accuracy, alignment steps, standards, maintenance and common measurement problems.

Q1What is a PAPI alignment/calibration tool?

It is a specialised instrument used by airfield maintenance teams to set the elevation (vertical angle) and azimuth (horizontal orientation) of individual PAPI light units. It ensures the red/white transition occurs at the exact glide slope angle required, typically 3° for fixed-wing runways or 4° to 6° for helicopter PAPI, and that the beam is aligned with the runway centreline.

Q2What types of PAPI alignment tools are there and how accurate are they?

There are three main types. A mechanical jig, read with a spirit level or protractor, has a typical accuracy of ±0.05°. An optical theodolite sighting a surveyed reference point is accurate to about ±5 arc-seconds. An electronic inclinometer with a digital readout is accurate to about ±0.01° and can log readings. Modern airfields often combine them, with the electronic inclinometer increasingly preferred for its speed, accuracy and data logging.

Q3What elevation angles are the four PAPI units set to?

Each of the four units is aligned individually so that together they produce the characteristic red/white transition pattern. For a 3° glide path the elevation angles are 2°30′, 2°50′, 3°10′ and 3°30′.

Q4How is a PAPI unit aligned with the tool?

The tool is mounted or positioned relative to the unit and a reference point or angle is established, for example zeroing an inclinometer on a known horizontal surface or sighting a survey marker for azimuth. The current elevation and azimuth are measured and compared with the required values, then the mounting base adjustments are loosened and moved incrementally with re-checking after each change. Once correct, the adjustments are locked, the angle is verified again to confirm nothing shifted, and the readings are documented.

Q5When does a PAPI need to be aligned or recalibrated?

Alignment is set at initial installation and verified during periodic recalibration, typically every 6 to 12 months. It is also checked after lamp or LED replacement, optical assembly servicing or frangible coupling replacement, and after any impact or storm damage that could have shifted the unit.

Q6Which standards apply to PAPI alignment and calibration?

The main references are ICAO Annex 14 (PAPI alignment tolerances and verification procedures), FAA AC 150/5345-28 (PAPI alignment and calibration requirements), FAA AC 150/5345-46 (photometric performance, including alignment verification), ISO 17123 for theodolite testing and calibration, and IEC 61010-1 for safety of electrical test and measurement equipment. Always verify the current edition and your aerodrome's approved requirements.

Q7What checks should be made on the tool before using it on a live PAPI?

Confirm it has a current calibration certificate traceable to a national standard and that its calibration interval has not lapsed. Check mechanical jigs for flatness and squareness, verify theodolite optics and tripod levelling, and for electronic inclinometers check battery charge and zero against a known flat reference. It is also good practice to compare a sample reading against a second independent instrument or certified angle block, and confirm repeatable readings.

Q8How often should an alignment tool itself be calibrated and maintained?

The tool's own angular reference should be checked against a certified standard annually and recalibrated if it has drifted. The mounting interface, battery condition and loaded reference data should be checked before each use. Tools should be stored in a padded case, since a hard knock can shift internal calibration.

Q9Why are my alignment tool readings inconsistent?

Common causes are calibration drift from a drop, temperature extremes or an overdue service, an unclean or uneven mounting surface, operator error such as a misread scale or unaccounted zero offset, wind or vibration, and battery depletion. Check the tool against a known reference angle, re-seat it on a clean, level surface, have a second operator verify the reading, and repeat in calmer conditions. Do not use the tool until its calibration is verified.

Q10Why should a critical alignment reading be cross-checked with a second method?

A single reliable-looking reading can still carry a systematic error specific to that tool, such as a worn mounting interface or a drifted level sensor, and repeating the measurement with the same tool will not reveal it. A second independent instrument or method specifically catches those errors. Do not sign off an alignment on a reading that cannot be corroborated.

Q11What safety and operational precautions apply when aligning an in-service PAPI?

Alignment work on an in-service PAPI requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the unit will be off-aim or out of service. Confirm the fixture is de-energised or has cooled, use fall protection when working on an elevated unit, and keep vehicles and personnel clear of the runway or taxiway strip. Any calibration doubt is a stop-work condition, since a drifted tool reading directly affects the glide path pilots fly.
