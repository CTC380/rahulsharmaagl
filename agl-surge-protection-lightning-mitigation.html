AGL SURGE PROTECTION & LIGHTNING MITIGATION IN AIRPORT LIGHTING DESIGN

AGL systems operate in an environment where electrical equipment, metallic structures, underground cables, light bases, signs, PAPI units and control systems are all exposed to lightning and transient overvoltage. Lightning protection in AGL is not simply the installation of a surge protective device — it is a system-level engineering approach involving lightning-current management, counterpoise, grounding, bonding, equipotentialization, isolation, surge protective devices, cable routing, equipment protection, inspection, testing and post-lightning restoration.

Protect → Bond → Ground → Divert → Isolate → Verify → Restore

01 · Definition

What Is AGL Lightning Mitigation?

AGL lightning mitigation is the coordinated engineering of grounding, bonding, counterpoise, isolation and surge protection to reduce the effects of lightning and transient overvoltage on Airfield Ground Lighting equipment.

The objective is not to claim that an AGL system can be made completely immune to lightning. The objective is to provide a controlled path for electrical energy, reduce damaging potential differences, protect sensitive equipment and maintain the required serviceability of visual aids. The exact arrangement must always follow the approved airport design and applicable requirements.

02 · System Exposure

Why Lightning Protection Matters in AGL

AGL equipment is distributed over large outdoor areas — long underground series-circuit cables, runway and taxiway fixtures, approach lighting structures, PAPI units, airfield signs, CCRs, control panels, communication equipment, metallic bases, transformer housings and junction boxes. This creates a large physical system exposed to environmental electrical disturbances.

03 · Terminology

Lightning vs Surge

These terms should not be treated as identical.

Lightning

A high-energy atmospheric electrical discharge.

Surge

A transient overvoltage or overcurrent event that travels through an electrical system.

Surge protection addresses the electrical consequences of transient events, not simply the physical lightning strike itself.
04 · Effects

Direct & Indirect Lightning Effects

Lightning can affect AGL equipment through different mechanisms.

Direct Effects

A lightning current path interacts directly with a structure, conductor or equipment.

Indirect Effects

A nearby lightning event induces or couples transient energy into electrical systems — this can occur even when the equipment itself is not directly struck.

Surge mitigation must consider the complete electrical environment, not only the point of direct strike.
05 · Protection Philosophy

Lightning Protection Philosophy

Provide a Preferred Path for Energy

Instead of allowing lightning-related current or transient energy to find uncontrolled paths through sensitive equipment, the protection system should provide an intentional, controlled path for that energy.

Control the path → reduce damaging voltage differences → protect equipment → maintain system availability.

06 · Counterpoise

Counterpoise & Configuration

A counterpoise is an important component of airfield lighting lightning protection — a conductive system associated with the airfield lighting installation that helps manage lightning current and potential differences. FAA airport visual-aid guidance identifies counterpoise as a dedicated lightning-protection element in airfield lighting installations.

The exact conductor size, configuration, routing, bonding and installation method must be taken from the applicable design and project requirements. Counterpoise design should be treated as part of the original AGL electrical design, not an afterthought.

08 · Grounding vs Bonding

Grounding vs Bonding — and Why It Matters

These terms are related but different. In lightning mitigation, both may be required — a grounding electrode alone does not automatically create an effective bonding system.

Grounding

Provides an intentional connection to earth.

Bonding

Provides an intentional electrical connection between conductive parts.

Why Bonding Matters

During a lightning-related transient, two separate metallic structures — Structure A at Potential A and Structure B at Potential B — can develop a large voltage difference between them (Potential A ≠ Potential B). Bonding provides a low-impedance connection intended to reduce dangerous potential differences between the bonded structures.

Unbonded vs Bonded Structures During a Transient

Grounding alone connects each structure to earth — it does not guarantee the two structures stay at the same potential as each other

09 · Isolation

Lightning Protection & Isolation

AGL systems have a special engineering characteristic: the series circuit is electrically separated from individual light fixtures through isolation transformers. This isolation is an important part of the AGL architecture. However, isolation does not mean that the equipment is automatically protected from every transient event.

Protection must consider transformer insulation, primary-side transients, secondary-side transients, grounding, bonding, fixture construction, connector paths and cable coupling.

Airfield lighting lightning protection can involve different design philosophies — isolation-based and equipotential-based. FAA construction guidance describes both approaches, with selection dependent on site conditions and engineering requirements. The engineer should not automatically apply one method to every airport; the selected method must follow the approved project design and applicable standards.
10 · Surge Protective Devices

SPD Principle, Selection & Coordination

A Surge Protective Device (SPD) is designed to limit transient overvoltage and divert surge energy away from protected equipment. An SPD may be used on appropriate power circuits, control circuits, communication circuits and signal circuits — it must be correctly selected for the circuit. Installing an SPD without considering system voltage, grounding arrangement, short-circuit capability, discharge characteristics and coordination can compromise the intended protection.

Operating Principle

Under normal conditions an SPD presents high impedance. During a transient, its impedance decreases and it provides a path that limits transient voltage and diverts energy according to its design. After the transient, the system returns toward normal operation — exact behaviour depends on SPD technology.

Selection Criteria

System voltage, frequency, earthing configuration, maximum continuous operating voltage, surge current capability, protection level, response characteristics, short-circuit rating, coordination, environmental conditions, manufacturer requirements and applicable standards.

Do not select an SPD based only on its nominal voltage.
SPD Coordination

Multiple SPDs may exist in a system and the protection stages should be coordinated.

Main Power SPD
Distribution SPD
Equipment-Level SPD
Poorly coordinated SPDs may result in inadequate protection, unnecessary operation, premature failure or improper energy distribution.
SPD Operating Principle — Diverting the Surge

Under normal conditions the SPD is effectively invisible to the circuit; during a transient its impedance collapses and it opens a low-impedance path to earth

11 · CCR Protection

AGL CCR Surge Protection & Lightning Events

CCR Surge Protection

CCRs contain electrical and electronic equipment that can be affected by transient events. Protection may include appropriately designed input protection, output protection, control-line protection, monitoring-line protection, internal surge protection, and grounding and bonding. FAA airport lighting design guidance identifies lightning arresters and other protective devices in CCR-related installations.

CCR After a Lightning Event

After a lightning event, a CCR may appear operational while hidden damage exists — electronic component degradation, control-board damage, sensor failure, insulation stress, protection-device operation or communication failure. CCR powers ON ≠ CCR fully verified. Post-lightning testing should be appropriate to the equipment and event.

12 · ALCMS & Communication Protection

ALCMS & Communication Surge Protection

ALCMS Surge Protection

ALCMS can contain sensitive PLCs, communication modules, I/O modules, network switches, servers, workstations, remote I/O and control interfaces. Surge protection should therefore address both power paths and signal / communication paths.

Communication Surge Protection

Communication circuits can be affected by transient events. Possible protection methods — appropriate signal SPDs, fibre-optic isolation, shielding, bonding and proper cable routing — depend on the technology and architecture. Fibre-optic communication can provide galvanic isolation across the communication path, but associated powered equipment and termination points still require protection.

13 · Cable & Connector Protection

AGL Cable, Isolation Transformer & Connector Protection

AGL Cable & Lightning

Long underground cables can provide conductive and coupling paths for transient energy. Important considerations: cable routing, separation, shielding where applicable, counterpoise arrangement, grounding, bonding, termination and connector protection.

Isolation Transformer Protection

An isolation transformer can experience electrical stress during a transient — primary or secondary insulation stress, winding stress, flashover, connector damage or fixture-side damage. The transformer forms part of the lightning protection chain.

Primary & Secondary Connectors

Primary connectors operate within the series-circuit environment; transients can stress connector insulation, sealing systems, cable insulation and connection interfaces. Secondary connectors (transformer-to-fixture) should be inspected for correct mating, sealing, physical damage, contamination, moisture and cable condition.

14 · Fixture & Structure Protection

Fixture, PAPI, Sign & Structure Lightning Protection

Light Fixture Bonding

Metallic fixture structures may require bonding according to the applicable design. FAA airport visual-aid guidance specifically identifies light fixture bonding as a design area, managing potential differences and providing the intended protection path.

PAPI Lightning Protection

PAPI contains optical, electrical and control equipment, metallic structures, foundations and power connections. Mitigation should consider structure, grounding, bonding, power supply, control circuits, surge protection, cable entry and enclosure — without interfering with optical alignment or frangible design requirements.

Airfield Sign Protection

Illuminated signs can contain LED assemblies, drivers, power supplies, control electronics and metallic structures. Protection should consider both the electrical supply and control architecture.

Approach Lighting Structures

Approach lighting systems can include long runs of elevated structures, conductors, light fixtures and metallic components. Their lightning protection must be coordinated with structural design, grounding, bonding, counterpoise and equipment protection.

Lightning Protection at Light Bases

Light bases provide important interfaces between fixture, transformer, cable, the grounding/bonding system and counterpoise. Metallic bases require appropriate bonding arrangements according to the applicable design.

In-Pavement Fixtures

Inset fixtures are exposed to water, moisture, mechanical loads, cable transients and ground potential changes. Protection depends on the complete system: fixture + transformer + connector + cable + bonding + grounding + protection.

15 · Surge Paths & Impedance

Surge Paths, Impedance & Cable Routing

Surge Paths

A useful engineering question is: if a transient enters the system, where will the energy go? Possible paths include power conductors, control cables, communication cables, grounding conductors, bonding conductors, cable shields, metallic structures and transformer windings. The protection design should provide an intentional and controlled path.

Low-Impedance Protection Paths & Inductance

Lightning protection is not simply about having a conductor with low DC resistance. Lightning contains very fast transient components, so the impedance of the protection path matters — a path that appears low-resistance at DC may behave differently during a fast transient because of inductance. A conductor has inductance, and during a rapidly changing current, V = L(di/dt) — so even a low-resistance conductor can develop substantial transient voltage when current changes rapidly. This is one of the most important physical principles behind lightning protection.

This is why short + direct + appropriately bonded is generally preferable to unnecessarily long, looping protection connections. Protection conductors should avoid unnecessary loops, excessive length, sharp routing, poor bonding and parallel arrangements that create unwanted coupling — the physical installation is part of the electrical design.

16 · Ground Potential Rise & Grounding Systems

Ground Potential Rise, Electrodes & Impedance

Ground Potential Rise

A lightning event can cause a local rise in ground potential, so two physically separated grounding points may temporarily be at significantly different potentials. This is why bonding and equipotentialization can be important — the objective is to reduce damaging potential differences between connected equipment.

Grounding Electrode Systems

Grounding electrodes provide intentional connections to earth. Their performance depends on soil characteristics, electrode configuration, installation, connections, corrosion and environmental conditions. A single ground resistance measurement does not describe every aspect of lightning protection performance.

Ground resistance ≠ complete lightning protection performance. A grounding system may have an acceptable low-frequency or DC resistance while still presenting significant impedance to very fast transient currents. Lightning protection requires consideration of the complete current path and transient behaviour.
Ground Potential Rise During a Strike

Lightning current entering one electrode raises local earth potential near it — nearby earth can be measurably higher than earth further away for the duration of the transient

18 · Fixture Technology

Isolation ≠ Immunity — LED vs Incandescent AGL

Isolation Transformers

The isolation transformer provides galvanic separation between the primary series circuit and the fixture-side circuit, reducing direct electrical coupling. However, isolation ≠ immunity — transformer insulation and connected equipment can still be subjected to transient stress.

Lightning & LED AGL

LED systems may contain electronic drivers and control circuits that can be more sensitive to transient events than simple resistive lamp loads — LED driver, power electronics, control board, monitoring circuit and internal protection components can be affected. Fixture selection should consider manufacturer transient and environmental specifications.

Lightning & Incandescent AGL

Incandescent systems are not immune to lightning — transformers, connectors, cables, insulation, CCRs and control equipment can still be damaged. The vulnerability profile may differ from LED equipment, but the overall protection architecture remains important.

19 · Post-Lightning Response

Lightning Event Response & Post-Lightning Inspection

Lightning Event Response

After a significant lightning event, the first objective is personnel safety. Do not immediately open or disconnect energized AGL equipment. Airport maintenance guidance warns that airport visual-aid equipment can present electrical hazards following lightning and that series circuits can operate at high voltage. Follow the airport's approved electrical safety and lightning-weather procedures.

20 · Damage Patterns & SPD Health

Delayed Damage, Failure Patterns & SPD Maintenance

Damage Isn't Always Obvious

A system may remain operational after a lightning event while individual components have been stressed — intermittent electronics, repeated CCR alarms, communication failures, insulation degradation, premature LED-driver or transformer failure, and repeated connector faults. Post-event monitoring can be important.

Damage Pattern Analysis

Repeated failures after storms can reveal protection weaknesses — e.g. storm → CCR communication failure, storm → same field controller failure, storm → repeated sign-driver failures. This pattern may indicate a protection or bonding problem rather than unrelated component failures.

SPD Failure & Maintenance

An SPD can itself fail after absorbing a surge — its status may indicate protection active, degraded, disconnected or failed, and must be inspected per the manufacturer's requirements. Maintenance should consider visual condition, status indicator, connections, wiring, earthing/bonding, environmental condition, replacement history and surge-event history. An SPD should not simply be installed and forgotten.

Lightning damage can produce optical degradation without an obvious electrical failure — LED driver degradation, partial fixture failure, changed intensity, colour shift or intermittent operation. Repeated CCR Electronic Failure

Storm → CCR control board failure, repeatedly. Do not immediately conclude "bad CCR" — investigate utility-side surge protection, CCR input/control-line protection, grounding, bonding, communication interface, counterpoise, nearby equipment and event history. Repeated component failure can indicate a system-level protection problem.

Repeated LED Fixture Failures

If several fixtures repeatedly fail after thunderstorms, investigate the full chain: fixture → secondary connector → isolation transformer → primary connector → series cable → counterpoise/bonding → CCR → protection system. The repeated pattern may reveal the actual entry or coupling path.

ALCMS Communication Failure After Lightning

Check network switch, fibre equipment, field communication interface, power supply, surge protection, grounding/bonding, remote I/O and the CCR communication interface. Do not assume the CCR itself is defective.

26 · Testing, Commissioning & Documentation

Testing, Commissioning & Serviceability

Lightning Protection Testing

Testing should follow the applicable design and equipment requirements. Verification areas can include grounding continuity, bonding continuity, counterpoise connections, SPD status, equipment grounding, insulation resistance, functional operation, communication, and CCR/ALCMS operation, using appropriate instruments and procedures.

Protection vs Electrical Testing

Lightning protection verification confirms the protection system has been installed and maintained correctly. Electrical testing confirms electrical characteristics; functional testing confirms equipment operates correctly; photometry confirms optical performance. A complete post-event assessment may require more than one of these.

Commissioning

Lightning protection should be verified during commissioning, not only after the first lightning event. Commissioning should establish a baseline for grounding, bonding, counterpoise, SPDs, equipment protection and control-system protection — this baseline becomes valuable during future maintenance.

Documentation

Maintain lightning protection, counterpoise, grounding and bonding drawings; SPD schedules and datasheets; grounding and bonding test records; inspection records; lightning-event reports; replacement records; post-event test reports; and as-built documentation.

Lightning Protection & Serviceability

Lightning protection should ultimately support AGL serviceability: Lightning Protection → Reduced Equipment Damage → Higher Equipment Availability → Higher AGL Serviceability → Better Operational Resilience. ICAO Annex 14 includes serviceability provisions for aerodrome visual aids, so lightning mitigation should ultimately be viewed in relation to these requirements.

Keyword Reference

Airfield Lighting Earthing & Surge Protection

Airfield lighting earthing

Airfield lighting earthing connects the lighting equipment, cabinets, structures and the counterpoise to a controlled earth system. Good earthing and bonding limit dangerous voltage differences during faults and lightning. Earth resistance targets and conductor sizes come from the project design and the standard, and the connections should be tested and recorded.

Airfield lighting surge protection

Airfield lighting surge protection limits the damage from lightning and switching surges on series circuits, power supplies and control cables. It uses surge protective devices, isolation and correct cable routing, coordinated with earthing at the CCR, the ALCMS and the field equipment. Devices should be inspected and replaced after a significant strike.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is AGL lightning mitigation?

AGL lightning mitigation is the coordinated engineering of grounding, bonding, counterpoise, isolation and surge protection to reduce the effects of lightning and transient overvoltage on Airfield Ground Lighting equipment. It is a system-level approach, not simply the installation of a surge protective device.

Q2Can an AGL system be made completely immune to lightning?

No. The objective is not complete immunity. It is to provide a controlled path for electrical energy, reduce damaging potential differences, protect sensitive equipment and maintain the required serviceability of visual aids. The exact arrangement must follow the approved airport design and applicable requirements.

Q3What is the difference between lightning and a surge?

Lightning is a high-energy atmospheric electrical discharge. A surge is a transient overvoltage or overcurrent event that travels through an electrical system. A lightning event can generate surges through direct connection, nearby coupling, ground potential changes, conductive paths and inductive or capacitive coupling. Surge protection addresses the electrical consequences, not simply the strike itself.

Q4What are the direct and indirect effects of lightning on AGL?

Direct effects occur when a lightning current path interacts directly with a structure, conductor or equipment. Indirect effects occur when a nearby lightning event induces or couples transient energy into electrical systems, which can happen even when the equipment itself is not struck.

Q5What is a counterpoise in airfield lighting?

A counterpoise is a conductive system associated with the airfield lighting installation that helps manage lightning current and potential differences. FAA airport visual-aid guidance identifies it as a dedicated lightning-protection element. Its conductor size, configuration, routing and installation method must come from the applicable design, and it should be part of the original AGL electrical design, not an afterthought.

Q6What is equipotential bonding in AGL?

Equipotential bonding keeps interconnected metallic parts at substantially similar electrical potential during transient events. Typical targets include metallic light bases, fixture mounting structures, transformer housings, junction boxes, sign structures and PAPI structures.

Q7What is the difference between grounding and bonding?

Grounding provides an intentional connection to earth, while bonding provides an intentional electrical connection between conductive parts. Both may be required in lightning mitigation, because a grounding electrode alone does not guarantee that two separate metallic structures stay at the same potential as each other.

Q8Does an isolating transformer protect AGL equipment from lightning?

Not automatically. The series circuit is electrically separated from the fixtures by isolation transformers, but isolation does not mean protection from every transient event. Protection must consider transformer insulation, primary-side and secondary-side transients, grounding, bonding, fixture construction, connector paths and cable coupling.

Q9What AGL equipment can lightning damage?

Equipment at risk includes CCR electronics, control equipment, ALCMS interfaces, field monitoring equipment, LED drivers, PAPI electronics, sign electronics, transformers, connectors, cable insulation and communication equipment.

Q10Which lightning protection philosophy should an AGL design use?

FAA construction guidance describes both isolation-based and equipotential-based approaches, with selection dependent on site conditions and engineering requirements. An engineer should not automatically apply one method to every airport; the selected method must follow the approved project design and applicable standards.

Bonding Quality Decides Protection Performance
Bonding

A surge device is only as good as its connection to the reference. Keep leads short and straight, and verify the bond with measurement, not appearance.

Coordinate Protection Stages
Coordination

Devices at different points must work together, not against each other. Check ratings and spacing against the manufacturer's coordination guidance.

Protect the Signal Paths Too
Control

Control and communication cables can carry damaging surges into ALCMS equipment. Treat them with the same discipline as power.

Inspect Surge Devices After Events
Maintenance

Devices can degrade after absorbing surges. Check status indicators and test or replace according to the manufacturer's guidance after storms.

Record Storm-Related Faults
Records

Log failures after lightning with their location and cause. Patterns show where protection needs strengthening.

Do Not Mix Earthing Philosophies Without a Design
Earthing

Connecting separate earthing systems informally can create hazards or reduce protection. Agree and document the earthing concept.
