Plug‑in Isolating Transformer

A specialised isolating transformer designed for rapid installation and maintenance, featuring quick‑connect terminals that allow plug‑and‑play connection to the primary series circuit and secondary fixture cabling. This innovation significantly reduces installation time, simplifies troubleshooting, and enables swift component replacement without specialised tools.

Overview

What Is It?

The Plug‑in Isolating Transformer is a variant of the standard AGL isolating transformer that incorporates quick‑connect terminals—typically in the form of heavy‑duty plugs and sockets—on both the primary and secondary sides. This design eliminates the need for traditional screw‑terminal connections, allowing the transformer to be rapidly installed, disconnected, or replaced with minimal labour and without the need for specialised crimping or soldering tools.

The transformer retains all the essential characteristics of a conventional isolating transformer: galvanic isolation between the primary series circuit and the secondary fixture circuit, constant‑current transfer, and robust encapsulation for IP68 environmental protection. The plug‑in interface is typically designed to meet the same ruggedness and reliability standards as hard‑wired connections, with locking mechanisms to prevent accidental disconnection under vibration or thermal cycling.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The plug-in isolating transformer exists to cut the time and skill required to install, disconnect, or replace a transformer in the field, replacing screw terminals and crimped or soldered joints with a connector interface that can be mated or separated in seconds. This matters most during large-scale installations or time-critical fault repairs, where every fixture on a runway or taxiway loop may need attention and minimising per-unit labour time directly affects how quickly a circuit can be restored to full service.

The locking connectors are what keep this convenience from becoming a reliability trade-off: without a secure mechanical lock, a plug-in connection exposed to aircraft-induced vibration and thermal cycling could work loose over time, so the design has to match the ruggedness of a hard-wired joint even while offering the speed of a quick-connect.

Application

Where Is It Used?

The plug‑in isolating transformer is ideally suited for applications where rapid installation, maintenance, or reconfiguration is a priority. Typical use cases include:

  • Rapid Deployment Airfields: Temporary or expeditionary airfields where speed of installation is critical, and connections must be made quickly without specialised tools.
  • Maintenance‑Focused Facilities: Airfields with high fixture turnover or frequent lamp replacements, where plug‑in transformers allow maintenance crews to swap faulty units in seconds, minimising runway downtime.
  • Modular AGL Systems: Pre‑assembled AGL kits where plug‑in transformers simplify on‑site assembly and reduce wiring errors.
  • Test and Calibration Rigs: Workshop or laboratory setups where transformers are frequently swapped for testing different fixture types or power ratings.
  • Retrofit Projects: Upgrading older hard‑wired systems to plug‑in configuration to improve maintainability and reduce future labour costs.

The transformer is typically installed in standard handholes or concrete pits, with the plug‑in connectors accessible for easy disconnection. It is available in a range of power ratings (15W to 100W) and secondary voltages to suit most common AGL fixtures.

Placement

Installation & Location

The plug-in transformer is installed in a standard handhole or concrete pit adjacent to the fixture, positioned so the primary and secondary plug connectors remain accessible from above without needing to disturb the surrounding cable. Rather than splicing the primary directly into the loop, the cable ends are fitted with mating plug connectors that the transformer clips into, allowing the whole unit to be lifted out and swapped in seconds during maintenance. This installation style is especially suited to expeditionary airfields and modular AGL kits, where pits are laid out with the connectors near the surface of the enclosure specifically to make the plug-in swap quick and tool-free.

Function

How Does It Work?

The plug‑in isolating transformer operates on the same electromagnetic induction principle as all AGL transformers, with the primary winding connected in series with the constant‑current loop. The secondary winding delivers the appropriate voltage to the connected fixture. The key differentiator is the connection method:

  • Primary Side: The primary winding is terminated with a robust plug (often an L‑823 compatible or proprietary connector) that mates with a corresponding socket on the primary cable. This connection carries the 6.6A series current and provides galvanic isolation.
  • Secondary Side: The secondary winding is terminated with a similar plug/socket arrangement, allowing the fixture cable to be connected quickly. The secondary voltage and current are thus transferred without hard‑wired splices.
  • Locking Mechanism: Both connectors incorporate positive locking (e.g., bayonet, threaded collar, or snap‑fit) to ensure secure mating and prevent disconnection due to vibration or thermal expansion.
  • Environmental Sealing: The connectors are designed to maintain IP68 rating when mated, with O‑rings and gaskets preventing moisture ingress.

The plug‑in design does not compromise the transformer's electrical performance, efficiency, or reliability. It is fully compatible with standard CCR dimming profiles and meets the same insulation, dielectric, and thermal requirements as conventional transformers.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

The plug-in transformer occupies the same position in the control chain as any hard-wired isolating transformer: an Airfield Lighting Control and Monitoring System (ALCMS) selects the required brightness step, the CCR regulates the whole loop to the corresponding fixed current, and the transformer carries that current with no addressable identity of its own — the plug-and-socket connection is a mechanical convenience for installation and maintenance, not a control interface, and it changes nothing about how the loop is commanded. It does change one thing about fault behaviour worth noting: because the primary and secondary connections are separable rather than spliced, a connector left unmated, partially seated, or degraded by corrosion presents to the CCR exactly like any other open-circuit or high-impedance fault on the loop — flagged as a loop fault by the same voltage-monitoring logic — so a mis-seated plug is one of the first things checked during sectionalised fault-finding on a loop using this variant, precisely because it's easier to disturb in the field than a permanent splice.

Construction

Physical Construction

The distinguishing feature of this variant is mechanical rather than electrical: both the primary and secondary connections are made through separable plug-and-socket connectors — L-823 compatible or a proprietary high-current design — rather than through screw-terminal splices, so the transformer itself is otherwise built and potted the same way as a standard fixed-lead unit, with windings around a laminated core fully encapsulated in epoxy or polyurethane. Each connector uses a bayonet, threaded collar, or snap-fit locking mechanism with O-ring seals at the mating face, and the IP68 rating only holds when that lock is fully engaged — a connector that is seated but not locked is a moisture path the potted body itself does not have. Complete units are comparatively light, around 1.5–3.0 kg, since replacement is meant to be quick: a failed unit is unplugged and swapped rather than spliced out, which is the main reason this construction is specified for high-fixture-count sections of a loop.

Confirm primary and secondary plugs seat fully and the bayonet, threaded collar, or snap-fit lock engages positively, preventing vibration-induced disconnection.

Upkeep

Inspection & Maintenance

Insulation Resistance (Megger) Test
Annual

Megger-test primary-to-secondary and primary-to-earth insulation to confirm it stays comfortably above minimum, flagging moisture ingress before it causes a series-circuit fault.

Connector Mating & Lock Engagement Check
Semi-annual

Confirm the bayonet, threaded collar, or snap-fit connectors mate fully and lock positively, since a partially seated connector is a common source of intermittent faults.

Connector Seal & O-ring Inspection
Annual

Check L-823 or proprietary connector O-rings and seals for cracking or compression set to keep the mated IP68 rating intact.

Connector Pin & Contact Corrosion Check
Annual

Inspect connector pins and contacts for corrosion, pitting, or discolouration, since this directly increases connection resistance and heat at the joint.

Encapsulation Integrity Inspection
Annual

Check the transformer body encapsulation for cracking, swelling, or breach, since a compromised seal is the most common path to insulation failure.

Secondary Voltage Verification
Semi-annual

Measure secondary output voltage at rated current and confirm it matches the transformer's rated value for the connected fixture.

Cable Strain Relief Inspection
Annual

Check cable strain relief at both connector ends for cracking or pull-out, since repeated plug/unplug cycles during maintenance stress this point.

Load Current Verification
Semi-annual

Confirm primary current draw tracks the CCR's commanded intensity step correctly across the dimming range.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Connector Corrosion & Contact Resistance

Plug‑in connectors are exposed to the same harsh environment as any AGL component. Corrosion of the contacts can increase resistance, leading to localised heating and voltage drop. Regular inspection, cleaning with contact cleaner, and application of dielectric grease are recommended. Use connectors with gold‑plated or corrosion‑resistant finishes.

Incomplete Mating & Locking

If the plug is not fully inserted and locked, the connection may be intermittent or have high resistance. This can cause arcing, overheating, or intermittent lamp flicker. Ensure that the locking mechanism is fully engaged and that the connector is properly seated. Training for maintenance crews is essential.

Seal Deterioration

The O‑rings and gaskets that provide IP68 sealing can degrade over time due to UV exposure, ozone, or temperature cycling. This can allow moisture ingress into the connector, causing corrosion and insulation breakdown. Replace seals periodically and ensure they are properly lubricated with silicone grease.

Mechanical Damage to Connectors

Plug‑in connectors are more exposed than hard‑wired terminals and can be damaged by impact, heavy vehicle loads, or improper handling. Use protective covers when connectors are not in use, and ensure handholes are properly sealed to prevent debris ingress.

Incorrect Transformer Sizing

As with all isolating transformers, selecting the wrong power rating or secondary voltage can cause premature lamp failure or reduced light output. Verify the fixture's requirements before ordering and ensure the plug‑in transformer matches these specifications.

Retrofit Compatibility

When replacing hard‑wired transformers with plug‑in versions, ensure that the primary and secondary cables are equipped with the matching connectors. This may require retrofitting cable ends, which should be done with care to maintain the integrity of the series circuit.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Intermittent Lamp Flicker or Arcing at the Connector
Likely Cause
Corrosion on the plug-in connector's contacts has increased resistance at the joint, causing localised heating and voltage drop.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the connector contacts for corrosion, pitting, or discolouration; measure voltage drop across the mated connector under load if possible.
Corrective Action
Clean contacts with appropriate contact cleaner and apply dielectric grease, or replace the connector with a corrosion-resistant (e.g., gold-plated) type if corrosion is advanced.
No Output Despite Apparently Connected Plug
Likely Cause
The connector is not fully inserted and locked, leaving the connection open or intermittent.
Diagnostic Steps
Check that the locking mechanism is fully engaged and the plug is fully seated; wiggle-test the connection (with power isolated) to check for intermittent contact.
Corrective Action
Fully re-seat and lock the connector; reinforce maintenance crew training on correct mating procedure to prevent recurrence.
CCR Trips on Overcurrent or Ground Fault
Likely Cause
O-rings or gaskets providing the connector's IP68 seal have degraded from UV exposure, ozone, or temperature cycling, allowing moisture ingress.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger the transformer and connector assembly (target ≥ 50 MΩ at 500V); inspect seals for cracking, hardening, or compression set.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded seals with new O-rings lubricated with silicone grease, or replace the connector assembly if damage is extensive.
Connector Physically Damaged
Likely Cause
The exposed plug-in connector has been struck by equipment, vehicle loads, or improper handling, since it is more exposed than a hard-wired terminal.
Diagnostic Steps
Visually inspect the connector for cracking, bent pins, or housing damage; check that the surrounding handhole is properly sealed.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged connector, and fit protective covers over connectors when not in use to reduce future damage.
Fixture Over- or Under-Voltage
Likely Cause
The plug-in transformer's power rating or secondary voltage doesn't match the fixture's requirement.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the fixture's rated power and voltage against the transformer's nameplate.
Corrective Action
Replace with a plug-in transformer correctly matched to the fixture's specification.
Mismatched Connectors After Retrofit
Likely Cause
A hard-wired transformer was replaced with a plug-in version, but the primary or secondary cable ends were not properly retrofitted with matching connectors.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect cable ends for connector type and compatibility with the new plug-in transformer.
Corrective Action
Retrofit cable ends with matching connectors, carried out carefully to maintain the integrity and sealing of the series circuit.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Primary Winding Open Circuit
High Severity
Root Cause
Wire fatigue from repeated thermal cycling, a manufacturing defect in the winding, or a blown internal fuse-wire link from a lightning-induced transient.
Effect
Opens the series loop, darkening every fixture downstream of the transformer on that circuit.
Detection Method
CCR loop-continuity/open-circuit indication, and megger or continuity testing at the transformer primary.
Insulation Breakdown from Moisture Ingress
High Severity
Root Cause
Cracked or degraded encapsulation, a failed cable gland or seal, or prolonged submersion in a flooded handhole or pit.
Effect
Reduced insulation resistance leading to a partial or full ground fault that can trip the CCR.
Detection Method
Megger testing against the ≥50 MΩ minimum, and visual inspection of the encapsulation and cable entries.
Secondary Winding Short or Breakdown
High Severity
Root Cause
Internal insulation failure between secondary turns, often accelerated by sustained overtemperature operation.
Effect
Loss of secondary voltage regulation, fixture dimming or failure, and possible primary overcurrent.
Detection Method
Secondary voltage measurement under load, compared against the transformer's rated output.
Overload / Core Saturation from Rating Mismatch
Medium Severity
Root Cause
A CCR step or connected fixture load exceeding the transformer's rated primary current or secondary power.
Effect
Increased core losses, overheating, and accelerated insulation ageing.
Detection Method
Verification of the CCR step current against the transformer's rating plate, and thermal inspection where accessible.
L-823 Connector / Termination Corrosion
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Improper seating during installation, gasket failure allowing moisture in, or galvanic corrosion building up at the connector pins over time.
Effect
Intermittent contact, flicker, or a high-resistance joint that generates localised heat.
Detection Method
Visual inspection at scheduled maintenance, and continuity or resistance checks across the joint.
Encapsulation Cracking / Case Damage
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Freeze-thaw cycling, mechanical impact during installation or maintenance, or age-related degradation of the potting compound.
Effect
Exposes the winding to moisture, accelerating insulation breakdown.
Detection Method
Visual inspection during handhole or pit access, and a drop in insulation resistance on megger testing.
Thermal Degradation from Sustained Overcurrent
High Severity
Root Cause
Repeated or prolonged operation above rated current, often from an incorrectly set CCR step or a transformer undersized for the installed load.
Effect
Progressive insulation embrittlement that shortens service life and raises the risk of an eventual winding failure.
Detection Method
Rating verification against actual loop current, and insulation resistance trending across successive maintenance cycles.
Output Voltage Drift / Regulation Loss
Low Severity
Root Cause
Gradual winding degradation or core ageing that shifts the transformer's turns-ratio performance away from its original specification.
Effect
Fixture brightness drifts slightly from the intended intensity step without an outright fault.
Detection Method
Photometric intensity survey and secondary voltage measurement compared against commissioning records.
Quick-Connect Terminal Wear / Contact Fretting
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Repeated connect/disconnect cycles during rapid field swaps wearing down spring-contact plating, or arcing from mating the connector on an energised loop.
Effect
Rising contact resistance, localised heating at the terminal, or an intermittent connection.
Detection Method
Contact resistance measurement at the quick-connect terminals, and visual inspection for pitting or discolouration during each swap.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Series Circuit Isolation
Electrical Safety

The plug-in transformer sits on a constant-current series loop that can carry several kV. De-energise and lock out the CCR, then verify zero energy at the transformer before disconnecting or handling its plug-in connectors.

No Hot-Swapping Connectors
Electrical Safety

Never mate or unmate the primary or secondary plug-in connectors while the series loop is energised; disconnecting under load can draw an arc at the connector pins and damage both the transformer and the connector.

Full Seating & Locking Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

Confirm the plug-in connector is fully seated and any locking collar or latch is engaged before restoring power; a partially mated connector can run hot, arc, or work loose under vibration.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any connector or transformer work, megger test the affected series loop before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can trip the CCR or damage healthy fixtures on the same circuit.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working on series-circuit connections, and confirm the transformer case and connector shells are bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting the loop.

FOD Control at the Connector Housing
Operational

Keep tools, fasteners, and connector dust caps accounted for at all times when the housing is open, and secure or remove any loose material before closing it, to prevent foreign object debris on the movement area.

Hot Surface Contact
Personnel Safety

Allow the transformer case and connector shells to cool before handling after an active period, since sustained loop current can leave exposed surfaces hot enough to burn.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a connector unlocked, a cover unsealed, or an insulation reading below the acceptable minimum after maintenance; verify a compliant, secure connection before closing out the work.

Engineering & Expert Notes

By Rahul Sharma

Quick-Connect Speed Doesn't Reduce the Need for Full Seating Verification
Installation

A connector that feels seated can still be making incomplete contact. Confirm full seating and locking mechanism engagement, not just that it “clicked,” before re-energising.

No Hot-Swapping, Despite the Convenience
Electrical Safety

The quick-connect design is built for fast replacement during a scheduled outage, not for swapping components while the circuit is live. Treat isolation requirements identically to a hard-wired unit.

Connector Wear From Repeated Swaps Is Its Own Failure Mode
Reliability

A connector designed for quick replacement will see more mating cycles over its life than a hard-wired termination. Inspect connector pins and contacts for wear specifically, not just the transformer body.

Fast Replacement Can Outpace Documentation Updates
Maintenance

Because swapping a plug-in unit takes less time than a hard-wired replacement, it's easy to skip updating the as-built record. Make the paperwork part of the same workflow as the physical swap, not a follow-up step.

Confirm the Replacement Unit's Connector Generation Matches
Compliance

Connector designs can change across product generations. Verify a replacement plug-in transformer's connector is compatible with the installed cabling before assuming any plug-in unit will fit.

Insulation Testing Still Applies Despite the Tool-Free Install
Inspection

The ease of installation doesn't reduce the need for a post-installation insulation resistance test. Treat it with the same rigor as a hard-wired unit even though no specialised tools were needed to fit it.

Quick Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about plug-in isolating transformers: how they differ from standard ones, ratings, connectors, standards, maintenance and common faults.

Q1What is a plug-in isolating transformer?

It is a variant of the standard AGL isolating transformer with quick-connect plugs and sockets on both the primary and secondary sides instead of screw terminals. It can be installed, disconnected or replaced quickly without specialised crimping or soldering tools.

Q2What stays the same compared with a conventional isolating transformer?

It keeps the same essentials: galvanic isolation between the primary series circuit and the secondary fixture circuit, constant-current transfer and robust encapsulation. Only the connection method changes.

Q3Why use a plug-in transformer?

It cuts the time and skill needed to install, disconnect or replace a transformer, which matters most in large-scale installations and time-critical fault repairs. A faulty unit can be swapped in seconds, minimising runway downtime.

Q4Where are plug-in isolating transformers used?

Rapid deployment airfields such as temporary or expeditionary sites, maintenance-focused facilities with high fixture turnover, and modular pre-assembled AGL kits.

Q5How does the plug-in connection work?

The primary winding ends in a robust plug, often L-823 compatible or a proprietary connector, that mates with a socket on the primary cable and carries the 6.6 A series current. The secondary side uses a plug in the same way. Connectors lock by bayonet, threaded collar or snap-fit, and are IP68 once mated.

Q6What are the typical ratings?

6.6 A nominal primary current with a 5-step range of 2.8–6.6 A, rated power of 15, 25, 45, 65 or 100 W at 50/60 Hz, and Class F (155 °C) or Class H (180 °C) insulation. Operating temperature is −40 °C to +55 °C and weight roughly 1.5–3.0 kg.

Q7Where is a plug-in transformer installed?

In a standard handhole or concrete pit beside the fixture, positioned so both plug connectors are accessible from above. The cable ends are fitted with mating plugs the transformer clips into, so the whole unit can be lifted out without disturbing the surrounding cable.

Q8Which standards apply to plug-in isolating transformers?

The main references are IEC 61823, FAA AC 150/5345-47 (L-830, L-831, L-832 series isolating transformers, including plug-in variants), SAE ARP 5553 (LED transformer performance) and ICAO Annex 14. Always verify the current edition and your approved requirements.

Q9How often should a plug-in transformer be inspected?

Typical intervals are semi-annual for connector mating and lock engagement checks, and annual for insulation resistance (megger) tests, connector seal and O-ring inspection, pin and contact corrosion checks and encapsulation integrity inspection. Your approved maintenance programme takes precedence.

Q10Why does a lamp flicker or arc at the connector?

Corrosion on the plug-in connector's contacts has increased resistance at the joint, causing localised heating and voltage drop. Inspect for corrosion or pitting, clean the contacts and apply dielectric grease, or replace the connector with a corrosion-resistant (for example gold-plated) type if corrosion is advanced.

Q11Why is there no output when the plug looks connected?

The connector is not fully inserted and locked, leaving the connection open or intermittent. With power isolated, check the locking mechanism is fully engaged and the plug is fully seated, and wiggle-test the connection for intermittent contact.
