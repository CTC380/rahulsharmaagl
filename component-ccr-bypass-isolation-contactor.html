CCR Bypass/Isolation Contactor

A specialised switching assembly that provides a safe means of bypassing a Constant Current Regulator (CCR) for maintenance or in the event of a fault, while also offering a visible isolation point for the series lighting circuit. Enables hot‑swapping of CCRs and ensures system continuity during servicing.

Overview

What Is It?

A CCR Bypass/Isolation Contactor is a dual‑purpose switching device used in AGL power distribution systems to provide both a bypass path around a CCR and a visible isolation point for maintenance. It typically comprises a set of mechanically and electrically interlocked contactors that can connect the series circuit directly to an alternate power source (such as a second CCR or a spare regulator) or completely isolate the CCR from the circuit, allowing safe servicing.

The bypass function is critical for maintaining airfield lighting continuity during CCR maintenance or failure. When a CCR needs to be taken out of service, the bypass contactor closes, routing the series circuit to the standby or backup CCR, while the isolation contactor opens to disconnect the faulty CCR. The isolation contactor also provides a visible break (when open) to ensure the circuit is de‑energised for safe maintenance work. This assembly is often integrated into the CCR cabinet or installed as a separate panel adjacent to the CCR bank.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

This assembly exists to solve a conflict that would otherwise be unavoidable: a CCR that needs maintenance must be safely de-energised for the technician's protection, but the series circuit it feeds may need to stay lit for continued airfield operations. By pairing a bypass path to a standby regulator with a true isolating break on the faulty unit, the device lets both needs be met at once — the circuit keeps running on the alternate CCR while the original is genuinely disconnected, not just switched off at the control level. The isolation contactor's visible break specifically exists for technician safety, giving unambiguous physical confirmation that the CCR is de-energised, since a technician working on a device believed to be off but still electrically connected faces a serious risk that a control-level shutoff alone cannot fully rule out.

Application

Where Is It Used?

CCR Bypass/Isolation Contactors are installed in airfield electrical rooms, equipment shelters, and AGL power centres where redundancy and maintenance flexibility are required. They are used in:

  • Redundant CCR Configurations: Where a standby CCR is available to take over the load if the primary CCR fails or requires maintenance.
  • Hot‑Swap Maintenance: Allowing a CCR to be replaced or serviced without interrupting the lighting circuit (provided a standby CCR is available).
  • Precision Approach Runways (CAT I, II, III): Where high availability is mandated, and bypass capability is essential to minimise downtime.
  • Critical Lighting Circuits: Runway edge, centreline, threshold, and approach lighting systems that must remain operational at all times.
  • Integration with ALCMS: Enabling remote bypass and isolation control, with status feedback to the central control system.
  • Testing and Commissioning: Allowing new CCRs to be tested while the existing regulator remains in service, using the bypass to switch between them.
Placement

Installation & Location

The assembly is installed in the equipment shelter as part of the CCR bank, either integrated into the CCR's own cabinet or mounted as a separate panel immediately adjacent to it, with the bypass and isolation contactors wired between the primary CCR, the standby CCR, and the outgoing series circuit. Positioning keeps the visible isolation break within clear sight of anyone working on the de-energised CCR, since the whole purpose of that break is to give unambiguous physical confirmation that the unit is disconnected before maintenance begins. Control wiring to the ALCMS or a local control panel is run alongside other CCR control cabling, allowing remote bypass switching, with the contactor's status feedback clearly labelled to the specific CCR pairing it manages in an equipment room that may house several redundant regulator sets. Because switching a fault onto a standby CCR needs to happen quickly and correctly, the interlocking between bypass and isolation contactors is verified during commissioning by testing an actual changeover, rather than assumed correct from the wiring diagram alone.

Function

How Does It Work?

The bypass/isolation contactor assembly consists of three main contactor groups:

  • Isolation Contactor (Normal Path): Connects the CCR's output to the series circuit. When closed, the CCR feeds the circuit. When open, the CCR is isolated from the circuit.
  • Bypass Contactor: Connects an alternate power source (e.g., a standby CCR or a direct mains feed, though the latter is rare in AGL) to the same series circuit. When closed, the circuit is powered by the alternate source.
  • Interlocking: Mechanical and electrical interlocks ensure that the isolation and bypass contactors are never closed simultaneously, preventing two CCRs from being paralleled (which would cause circulating currents and overloads).

The normal operating state has the isolation contactor closed and the bypass contactor open. When a transfer is required (e.g., for maintenance), the control system (or manual operator) first closes the bypass contactor to connect the standby CCR, then opens the isolation contactor to disconnect the primary CCR. This "make‑before‑break" sequence ensures no interruption to the lighting circuit. Conversely, when returning to normal operation, the isolation contactor is closed first, then the bypass contactor is opened. The assembly also includes auxiliary contacts for status indication (position feedback) to the ALCMS.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

This assembly sits above the loop-level control described for individual fixtures — it decides which CCR feeds the circuit, not what current the circuit runs at. A transfer can originate two ways: a technician initiating a manual changeover for maintenance, or an automatic transfer triggered when the CCR regulator monitor (L-827) reports a regulator failure that the ALCMS has been configured to act on without waiting for manual intervention. Either way, the make-before-break sequencing and mechanical interlocking are what make the swap safe to leave to automation — the ALCMS never has to time the transfer itself, only issue the "transfer to standby" command and read back the resulting contactor positions from the auxiliary contacts. Because the standby CCR is a physically separate regulator, this level of redundancy protects against a CCR failure that would otherwise take the entire circuit dark, which is a different failure class from the individual fixture or transformer faults that ordinary loop-voltage monitoring is designed to catch.

Construction

Physical Construction

The bypass and isolation contactors are built as a matched pair, each with silver-alloy or silver-tungsten contacts sized to interrupt the CCR's rated series-circuit current and withstand the open-circuit voltage without excessive arcing, mounted on a common frame or DIN rail within a shared enclosure so their mechanical interlock can be built directly into the frame geometry. The mechanical interlock is a rigid linkage or interposed lever between the two contactor bodies that makes it physically impossible for both to be closed at once, backed by an electrical interlock using each contactor's auxiliary contacts wired into the other's coil circuit as a second, independent line of defence. Indoor installations use an IP20 open-frame or ventilated panel enclosure sized for the airflow the contactors need under frequent switching, while outdoor or unconditioned equipment shelters use a weatherproof IP54 enclosure with a viewing window so the visible isolation gap can be confirmed without opening the cabinet. Control wiring terminates at a separate, clearly labelled terminal block from the high-current power connections, and auxiliary contact wiring for remote status feedback is routed through its own gland or conduit entry to keep low-voltage control signals segregated from the switched series-circuit conductors.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Interlock Failure

Mechanical or electrical interlocks may fail, allowing both the isolation and bypass contactors to close simultaneously. This can cause paralleling of CCRs, leading to severe overcurrent and potential damage. Regular testing of interlocks is critical.

Contact Wear on Bypass Contactor

The bypass contactor may be operated infrequently but must be capable of carrying full load. Over time, contact surfaces can oxidise or pit, increasing resistance and causing overheating. Periodic inspection and exercising are recommended.

Coil Burnout

Prolonged energisation or voltage spikes can cause coil failure. Surge suppression across coils and proper control logic (e.g., not holding coils for extended periods) can extend life.

Position Indication Errors

Auxiliary contacts may fail, leading to incorrect status reporting to the ALCMS. This can prevent automated transfer sequences from executing correctly. Regular verification of auxiliary contacts is essential.

Arc Chute Degradation

The arc chutes on the bypass contactor (and isolation contactor) can degrade over time, especially if the contactor interrupts load current. Visual inspection and timely replacement of arc chutes are necessary to maintain safe interruption capability.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

Both Contactors Show Closed Simultaneously
Likely Cause
Mechanical or electrical interlock failure has allowed the isolation and bypass contactors to close together, risking CCR paralleling.
Diagnostic Steps
Immediately de-energise and manually verify contactor positions; test the mechanical and electrical interlocks through a full operating cycle.
Corrective Action
Repair or replace the failed interlock mechanism, and re-verify correct interlocking before returning the assembly to automatic operation.
Bypass Contactor Runs Hot or Trips on Transfer
Likely Cause
Contact oxidation or pitting on the infrequently operated bypass contactor has increased resistance under full load.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise and inspect bypass contact surfaces for oxidation or pitting; measure contact resistance across the closed contactor.
Corrective Action
Clean or replace worn contacts, and add the bypass contactor to a periodic exercise schedule to prevent oxidation buildup.
Contactor Fails to Energise / Coil Dead
Likely Cause
Coil burnout from prolonged energisation or a voltage spike without adequate surge suppression.
Diagnostic Steps
Check coil continuity and resistance against the manufacturer's specification; confirm control voltage is present at the coil terminals; check for missing or failed surge suppression.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed coil, add or replace surge suppression across the coil, and review control logic to avoid unnecessarily prolonged energisation.
ALCMS Shows Incorrect Contactor Status
Likely Cause
A failed auxiliary contact is reporting incorrect position status, which can block automated transfer sequences.
Diagnostic Steps
Manually verify the actual contactor position against the ALCMS-reported status; test the auxiliary contact for correct operation through a full cycle.
Corrective Action
Replace the failed auxiliary contact, and re-verify status reporting matches actual position before re-enabling automated transfer.
Visible Arcing or Damage at Contact Opening
Likely Cause
Degraded arc chutes on the bypass or isolation contactor have lost their ability to safely interrupt load current.
Diagnostic Steps
De-energise and visually inspect the arc chutes for erosion, cracking, or carbon tracking.
Corrective Action
Replace degraded arc chutes promptly, and schedule regular visual inspection to catch wear before it affects interruption capability.
Reliability

Failure Modes & Root Causes

Interlock Mechanism Failure
High Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical linkage wear, a broken interlock bar, or a failed electrical interlock relay allows both contactors to be commanded closed together.
Effect
Risk of paralleling the CCR output with the bypass path, potentially damaging the regulator or creating a hazardous fault current.
Detection Method
Manual position verification, full-cycle interlock testing, and auxiliary contact status cross-checking through the ALCMS.
Bypass Contact Oxidation / Pitting
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Long dwell times in the open position leave the bypass contactor's contacts exposed to atmospheric oxidation, and infrequent operation prevents natural contact wiping.
Effect
Increased contact resistance leads to localized heating, nuisance thermal tripping, or contact welding under full transfer load.
Detection Method
Periodic contact resistance measurement and thermal imaging during scheduled exercise cycles.
Coil Burnout
High Severity
Root Cause
Prolonged continuous energisation beyond duty rating, a voltage transient without adequate surge suppression, or a manufacturing defect in the winding insulation.
Effect
Contactor fails to energise on command, preventing the required transfer between isolation and bypass states.
Detection Method
Coil continuity and resistance testing, control voltage verification at the coil terminals, and inspection of surge suppression components.
Auxiliary Contact Failure
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Mechanical wear or misalignment of the auxiliary contact block, or contamination on the auxiliary contact surfaces.
Effect
ALCMS receives incorrect contactor position status, which can block or falsely trigger automated transfer sequences.
Detection Method
Cross-check of reported status against manually verified physical position, and cyclic testing of the auxiliary contact block.
Arc Chute Degradation
Medium Severity
Root Cause
Cumulative erosion from repeated arc interruption, carbon tracking build-up, or thermal stress beyond the arc chute's rated interruption life.
Effect
Reduced ability to safely quench the arc on opening, increasing the risk of sustained arcing, contact damage, or a failure to clear the fault.
Detection Method
Scheduled visual inspection for erosion, cracking, or carbon tracking, tracked against the arc chute's rated operation count.
Control Circuit / Sequencing Fault
Low Severity
Root Cause
A logic error in the ALCMS transfer sequence, a wiring fault in the control circuit, or a timing mismatch between contactor open and close commands.
Effect
Delayed, out-of-sequence, or stalled transfer between isolation and bypass states, potentially leaving the circuit in an undefined condition.
Detection Method
Control circuit continuity checks, review of ALCMS sequence logs, and end-to-end functional testing of the transfer sequence.
Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Both Sources Before Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out both the primary CCR and the bypass/alternate source before working inside the enclosure, since the unit is specifically designed to connect two live sources and either can carry the series circuit voltage.

Verify Interlock Before Manual Operation
Electrical Safety

Confirm the mechanical and electrical interlock is intact before manually cycling either contactor; a defeated or worn interlock can allow both paths to close together and parallel the CCR output with the bypass, risking fault current or regulator damage.

Confirm Position Before Re-Energising
Operational

Physically verify contactor position matches the intended state (bypass or normal) before restoring power, rather than relying solely on ALCMS auxiliary contact status, since a stuck or welded contact can misreport its own position.

Airside Work Coordination
Operational

Any transfer to bypass or maintenance state that takes a lighting circuit off its normal regulation requires ATC/airside coordination and a NOTAM where the affected fixtures will be out of tolerance or dark.

Insulation Test Before Re-Energisation
Electrical Safety

After any internal work, megger test the affected circuit paths before restoring power, since a residual fault at re-energisation can damage the regulator or trip healthy circuits sharing the same board.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when working near series-circuit connections, and confirm the enclosure is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before reconnecting either path.

FOD & Enclosure Integrity
Operational

Account for all tools and fasteners before closing the enclosure, and confirm the door/lid seal is properly reinstated to keep moisture and debris out of the contactor assembly.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave the unit in bypass, with the interlock defeated, or in an unverified position after maintenance; confirm normal-path operation and full interlock function before closing out the work and cancelling any associated NOTAM.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Interlock Integrity Is the Whole Point — Test It, Don't Assume It
Safety

The mechanical/electrical interlock preventing simultaneous closure of bypass and isolation contactors is the single component this device exists for; verify it functionally at every maintenance visit rather than relying on the assembly's original factory test, since a worn or defeated interlock can leave both paths closed under fault conditions with no visible warning.

Confirm the Visible Break Is Actually Visible
Verification

An isolation contactor's visible-break feature only provides genuine safety assurance if the open contacts can actually be observed or independently verified from outside the enclosure; a viewing window obscured by dust or a contactor mounted where the gap can't be seen defeats the purpose even though the device itself is functioning correctly.

Bypass Switching Under Load Stresses Contacts Differently Than Normal Operation
Maintenance

Contactors that only operate during a CCR swap-over see infrequent but high-stress switching events rather than routine cycling; contact wear from these events can be more severe per-operation than a contactor cycling normally, so inspect contact condition based on switching severity, not just operation count.

A Standby Path That's Never Exercised Is Unproven
Reliability

An assembly that hasn't performed an actual bypass operation in months or years provides no real assurance it will work correctly during a genuine CCR failure; schedule a planned exercise of the full bypass sequence rather than discovering a stuck contactor for the first time during an actual emergency changeover.

Verify Control Logic Sequencing, Not Just the Contactors Themselves
Controls

The contactors can be mechanically and electrically sound while the ALCMS or PLC sequencing that commands them has a timing fault, causing a brief make-before-break or break-before-make condition outside the intended sequence; periodically verify the actual switching sequence and timing, not just that each contactor operates when commanded individually.

Log Every Bypass Event With Its Reason
Documentation

A bypass/isolation assembly that switches unexpectedly or more frequently than planned is often an early symptom of an intermittent CCR fault rather than a problem with the contactor assembly itself; keeping a log of every bypass event and its cause makes that pattern visible instead of each event being treated as an isolated occurrence.
