Airfield Ground Lighting Design & Electrical Engineering

Designing the electrical system behind safe and reliable Airfield Ground Lighting. AGL design is more than selecting fixtures — it's a coordinated engineering process spanning runway and taxiway layout, series circuits, Constant Current Regulators, isolating transformers, cable, connectors, earthing, control, monitoring, loading and reliability.

Start Learning Review AGL Fundamentals
Overview

What Is AGL Design?

AGL design is the engineering process of converting an airport's operational and lighting requirements into a complete, coordinated airfield lighting system.

A typical design considers the aerodrome layout, runway and taxiway configuration, approach requirements, the lighting systems required, fixture locations, circuit configuration, CCR selection, series-circuit cable, isolating transformers, connectors, control and monitoring, earthing and bonding, electrical loading, installation requirements, and testing and commissioning.

Not a Drawing Exercise

AGL design should not be treated as producing a set of drawings — it is a complete engineering process from layout through commissioning.

Electrically Coordinated

The objective is a system that is technically appropriate, electrically coordinated, maintainable and compliant with the applicable requirements and standards.

Serves the Pilot

Every design decision ultimately supports the same purpose: giving pilots reliable, correctly performing visual guidance.

Component Selection

CCR, Transformer & Cable Engineering

These three components sit at the electrical heart of the series circuit and must be selected together, not independently.

CCR Design

Selection requires consideration of required circuit current, number of fixtures, transformer ratings, circuit load, CCR capacity, intensity steps, cable characteristics, circuit length, control and monitoring requirements.

Isolating Transformers

Selection may involve fixture wattage, primary current, secondary requirements, transformer rating, connector configuration, installation environment, applicable specification and system compatibility.

Series-Circuit Cable

Considerations include cable voltage rating, conductor size, circuit length, installation method, insulation characteristics, environmental conditions, cable joints, connector compatibility and insulation resistance.

Electrical Checks

Voltage Drop & Circuit Performance

Electrical calculations should consider the complete circuit rather than only individual components. Depending on circuit arrangement and design methodology, checks may include:

Cable Losses

Conductor resistance and circuit length effects.

Transformer Characteristics

Losses and performance under load.

Circuit Loading

Total connected load versus rated capacity.

CCR Operating Conditions

Output current, voltage headroom and step behaviour.

The objective is to ensure that the complete system operates within the required electrical and operational parameters — not simply that each component individually meets its own rating.

Why Loop Voltage Rises With Every Transformer

In a constant-current loop, current stays fixed — so the CCR must raise its output voltage to overcome the accumulated impedance of cable and transformers

This is why CCR sizing considers rated output voltage, not just current — enough headroom must exist to drive the fully-loaded loop, including cable and every transformer on it.
Capacity Planning

AGL Circuit Loading

A series circuit may contain a significant number of lighting fixtures. The design needs to establish clear answers to a small set of recurring questions — particularly important when adding or replacing fixtures during airport modifications.

Questions the Design Must Answer

  • How many fixtures?
  • What is the load of each fixture?
  • What transformer is required?
  • What is the total circuit requirement?
  • What CCR capacity is required?
  • What happens if the circuit configuration changes?

A Change Cascades

A seemingly simple change — such as adding fixtures — can ripple through the entire circuit design:

  • Circuit load
  • Transformer requirement
  • CCR capacity
  • Cable performance
  • Control configuration
Beyond the Series Circuit

Control, Earthing & Protection

A complete electrical design also has to coordinate the control architecture, the earthing and bonding arrangement, and the protection philosophy with the series circuit and airport electrical system.

Control & Monitoring

CCR control, intensity selection, local and remote control, airport lighting control systems, fault monitoring, circuit status, CCR alarms, emergency operation and interface with airport systems.

Earthing & Bonding

Equipment earthing, metallic structure bonding, electrical equipment bonding, transformer and CCR installation, cable-related considerations and lightning protection interfaces where applicable.

Protection

Overcurrent protection, surge protection, equipment protection, lightning-related protection, insulation monitoring, earth-fault monitoring, emergency isolation and equipment interlocking.

The exact arrangement must be designed according to the applicable electrical standards, airport requirements and project specifications — protection philosophy should always be coordinated with the CCR, series circuit and airport electrical system as a whole.

Keyword Reference

Airfield Lighting Design Basics & Layout Design

The starting principles of airfield lighting design and how the layout is set out.

Airfield lighting design

Airfield lighting design is the process of deciding what lighting a runway, taxiway or apron needs and how it will be powered, controlled and maintained. It covers the lighting layout, series circuits, CCR sizing, transformers, cables, control and documentation, all checked against the standard the aerodrome follows. See The AGL Design Process section above.

Airfield lighting design basics

The basics are few. Lighting is fed from constant-current series circuits, each light has its own isolating transformer, a CCR sets the circuit brightness, and the layout, intensity and colour of each lighting system follow ICAO Annex 14 or the national equivalent. Everything else in the design builds on these points. See Understanding the AGL Series Circuit above.

Airfield lighting layout design

Airfield lighting layout design places each light in the correct position, spacing and orientation relative to the runway, taxiway or approach geometry. It also splits the lights into circuits so the failure of one circuit does not leave the airfield without guidance. Spacings and patterns come from the standard, so none are given here. See Lighting Layout, Circuit Zoning & Segmentation above.

Keyword Reference

Design Drawings, Bill of Quantities, Design Review & Rehabilitation Design

The documents that carry the design and the checks that prove it, including work on existing airfields.

Airfield lighting design drawings

Airfield lighting design drawings show light positions, circuits, cable routes, ducts, CCR and equipment room layouts, and the single line diagram. They are the main reference for construction, testing and later maintenance, so they must be complete, consistent and kept as-built. See AGL Drawings & Engineering Documentation above.

AGL bill of quantities

An AGL bill of quantities lists the fixtures, transformers, cables, connectors, CCRs, civil items and other work in a project, with descriptions, units and quantities. It is derived from the drawings and equipment schedule and should be cross-checked against design calculations. A clear bill of quantities helps tendering, cost control and ordering. See AGL BOQ, Equipment Schedule & Design Calculations above.

Airfield lighting design review

An airfield lighting design review checks the design before it is built. A typical review tests the layout against the standard, the circuit loading and voltage drop calculations, CCR and transformer selection, earthing, control, drawing consistency and maintainability. Reviews are often done at set stages, such as concept, detailed design and pre-construction. See the AGL Design Checklist below.

Airfield lighting rehabilitation design

Airfield lighting rehabilitation design covers upgrading or replacing lighting on an existing airfield. It starts with a survey of existing cables, ducts, circuits and equipment, then decides what can be reused and what must be replaced. It must also plan how work will be phased so the airfield stays operational, and how new equipment will work with existing CCRs and circuits.

Connected Disciplines

AGL Design, Photometry, Commissioning & Maintainability

Design & Photometry

The designer establishes where the light is installed, what type of light is installed, how it is powered, how it is controlled and how it is aligned. Photometry then verifies how the installed light actually performs.

Design & Commissioning

A good design should make commissioning easier — the documentation should provide enough information to verify correct fixture installation, transformer selection, circuit connection, cable identification, CCR assignment, control configuration and intensity operation.

Design defines the intended system. Photometry verifies the optical result.

Design for Maintainability

  • Equipment accessibility
  • Cable identification
  • Circuit identification
  • Transformer identification
  • Spare capacity
  • Replacement compatibility
  • Maintenance isolation
  • Test points

The Engineering Cycle

Design for operation. Design for maintenance. Design for the future. A functional design that is difficult to maintain is an incomplete design.

Common AGL Design Mistakes

Recognising these recurring mistakes early in a project is far cheaper than correcting them after installation.

01

Selecting Independently

Choosing equipment component-by-component rather than as a coordinated system.

02

Ignoring Total Loading

Not establishing the total circuit loading across the full series circuit.

03

Layout / Circuit Mismatch

Not coordinating the electrical circuit with the physical lighting layout.

04

Poor Cable Routing

Insufficient attention to cable routing and identification.

05

Poor Documentation

Weak documentation of transformer and fixture locations.

06

Ignoring the Future

Not allowing for future modifications or expansion.

07

Photometry as an Afterthought

Treating photometry as completely separate from the design process.

08

Generic Calculations

Using generic calculations without checking actual project conditions.

09

Uncoordinated Deliverables

Failing to coordinate drawings, schedules and the BOQ.

10

No Final Verification

Not verifying the final installed configuration against the design.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1What is AGL design?

AGL design is the engineering process of converting an airport's operational and lighting requirements into a complete, coordinated airfield lighting system. It covers aerodrome layout, lighting systems, fixture locations, circuit configuration, CCR selection, series-circuit cable, isolating transformers, connectors, control and monitoring, earthing and bonding, electrical loading, installation, and testing and commissioning.

Q2What are the stages of the AGL design process?

The sequence runs from airport layout and operational requirements through lighting system selection, fixture layout, circuit planning, electrical load calculation, CCR selection, transformer and connector selection, cable design, earthing and protection, control and monitoring, drawings and BOQ, installation, testing and commissioning, and finally photometric verification of the installed system.

Q3Why do AGL systems use a series circuit?

The series circuit is designed so that the required current is maintained through the circuit while individual fixtures stay electrically isolated from the high-voltage primary circuit. A constant-current loop runs from the CCR through each isolating transformer and fixture and back to the CCR.

Q4How is a CCR selected for an AGL circuit?

CCR selection should be based on the actual system design rather than simply the number of lights. Factors include required circuit current, number of fixtures, transformer ratings, circuit load, CCR capacity, intensity steps, cable characteristics, circuit length, and control and monitoring requirements.

Q5Why does CCR output voltage rise with every transformer in the loop?

In a constant-current loop the current stays fixed, so the CCR must raise its output voltage to overcome the accumulated impedance of the cable and every transformer on the loop. This is why CCR sizing considers rated output voltage, not just current.

Q6What happens to the design when fixtures are added to a circuit?

A seemingly simple change such as adding fixtures can cascade through the design, affecting circuit load, transformer requirement, CCR capacity, cable performance and control configuration. This is particularly important when adding or replacing fixtures during airport modifications.

Q7What does a voltage drop and circuit performance check involve?

It considers the complete circuit rather than individual components: cable losses, transformer characteristics, circuit loading and CCR operating conditions such as output current, voltage headroom and step behaviour. The aim is that the whole system operates within the required parameters, not merely that each component meets its own rating.

Q8What do earthing, bonding and protection cover in AGL design?

Earthing and bonding cover equipment earthing, metallic structure bonding, transformer and CCR installation, and lightning protection interfaces where applicable. Protection covers overcurrent and surge protection, equipment protection, insulation and earth-fault monitoring, emergency isolation and interlocking. The arrangement must follow the applicable standards, airport requirements and project specifications.

Q9Why is circuit zoning important in AGL design?

Large systems often need multiple circuits or functional zones, and good zoning makes troubleshooting and maintenance significantly easier. Zoning considers runway and taxiway sections, approach systems, operational and maintenance requirements, CCR allocation, redundancy and future expansion.

Q10What drawings and schedules make up an AGL design package?

A complete package can include an AGL layout drawing showing equipment locations, a series circuit diagram, a CCR schematic, a transformer schedule and a cable schedule, among other engineering drawings and documentation, each serving a distinct purpose.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
Design the Circuit Around Serviceability
Design

Position access points, spare capacity and sectioning so that faults can be isolated and repaired without taking the whole airfield out of service.

Leave Margin in CCR Loading
Loading

A circuit designed exactly to capacity leaves no room for later additions, cable ageing or higher losses. Agree and record the margin policy early.

Voltage Drop and Insulation Are Both Constraints
Cables

Check cable length against the CCR's available output voltage and verify the insulation rating for the primary voltage. A design can fail on either one.

Earthing and Surge Protection Belong in the First Layout
Protection

Adding protection late tends to produce compromises. Integrate earthing, bonding and surge devices into the original drawings and schedules.

Drawings and Schedules Must Agree
Documentation

Mismatches between single-line diagrams, layout drawings and bills of quantities cause site errors. Cross-check them before issue.

Run a Design Review With Operations and Maintenance
Review

People who will run the system often see access, control and spares issues the designer cannot. Invite them before construction starts.
