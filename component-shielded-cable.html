Shielded Cable

A specialised cable with a metallic screen (braid, foil, or combination) that provides electromagnetic interference (EMI) protection for sensitive AGL circuits. Used in control, monitoring, and communication links where signal integrity is critical and interference must be minimised.

Overview

What Is It?

A Shielded Cable (also known as a screened cable or EMI‑shielded cable) is a cable that incorporates a metallic layer—either a braided copper mesh, a spirally wound copper tape, or a combination—to protect the internal conductors from external electromagnetic interference (EMI) and to prevent the cable itself from radiating interference. In airfield ground lighting (AGL) systems, shielded cables are essential for maintaining signal integrity in control, monitoring, and communication circuits that are susceptible to interference from high‑power transmitters, radar systems, and other electrical equipment.

Key characteristics of shielded cables include:

  • Metallic Screen: A conductive layer that surrounds the insulated conductors, providing a barrier against electromagnetic fields.
  • EMI Suppression: The screen reduces both radiated emissions (from the cable) and conducted susceptibility (interference picked up by the cable).
  • Earthing/Bonding: The screen must be properly terminated and earthed to be effective, providing a low‑impedance path for interference currents.
  • Variety of Types: Available as single‑pair, multi‑pair, or multi‑core cables, with various screen constructions (braid, foil, or combined).

Shielded cables are used in AGL systems for:

  • Control circuits: Between CCRs and field equipment, such as brightness control and switching signals.
  • Monitoring and feedback: From ALCMS sensors and status indicators.
  • Communication links: For data transmission between AGL equipment and the ALCMS, often with RS‑485, Ethernet, or other protocols.
  • Sensor cables: For weather sensors, RVR sensors, and other sensitive instrumentation.

Proper selection, installation, and termination of shielded cables are critical to achieving the required EMC performance and ensuring the reliable operation of AGL systems.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

Shielded cable exists to keep low-level control, monitoring, and communication signals trustworthy in an environment saturated with sources of electrical noise, from high-power CCR switching to radar and transmitter equipment nearby. Without the metallic screen, induced interference on these circuits could corrupt sensor readings, garble ALCMS data links, or trigger false control signals, all of which would undermine confidence in the monitoring system the AGL infrastructure depends on. The screen only delivers this protection when it is correctly terminated to earth at every point along the run, which is why the cable's shielding performance is inseparable from the quality of its screen termination rather than a property of the cable alone.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Shielded cables are used throughout the airfield wherever electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) is a concern, including:

  • AGL Control and Monitoring Systems: Connecting CCRs, ALCMS workstations, and field controllers to transmit control signals and receive status updates.
  • Data Communication Networks: Ethernet cables (e.g., CAT‑5/6 with FTP or STP) for ALCMS network infrastructure.
  • Sensor Wiring: Connecting wind sensors, visibility sensors, RVR sensors, and pavement temperature sensors to data acquisition units.
  • ILS and Navigation Aid Cables: Interconnecting ILS localizer and glide slope equipment, where signal integrity is paramount.
  • Fibre Optic Cable Metallic Members: Shielded cables are sometimes used for the metallic strength members of fibre optic cables to provide lightning protection and EMI shielding.
  • Retrofit and Upgrades: Replacing unshielded cables in existing installations to improve EMC performance, especially when new radar or communication equipment is added.

The cable type is selected based on the application—single pair for simple signals, multi‑pair for multiple channels, and overall screen (with or without individual screens) depending on the required level of shielding. The screen material (copper, tinned copper, or aluminium) and coverage (often ≥ 80% for braid, 100% for foil) are chosen to meet the specific EMC requirements.

Placement

Installation & Location

The cable is routed separately from primary and secondary power cables wherever practicable, typically in its own duct or a dedicated section of a shared trench, to limit the electromagnetic coupling between high-current power circuits and the lower-level control, monitoring, or communication signals the shielded cable carries. Where it must cross a power cable route, the crossing is made at as close to a right angle as possible to minimise the length of parallel run between the two. At each termination — whether at a CCR, ALCMS controller, sensor, or junction box — the cable's screen is bonded to earth using a screen connector, since the shielding is only effective when properly earthed at its termination points. Runs are kept as short and direct as the site layout allows, with excess coiled cable avoided, as a coiled or looped run can itself act as an inductive element that undermines the very interference suppression the shielding is intended to provide.

Function

How Does It Work?

A shielded cable works by providing a conductive barrier between the internal signal conductors and the external environment. The screen intercepts electromagnetic fields and diverts interference currents to earth, preventing them from reaching the signal conductors. The functional principles are based on electromagnetic theory:

  • Reflection and Absorption: The metallic screen reflects a portion of the incident electromagnetic wave and absorbs the remainder, reducing the field strength inside the cable.
  • Low‑Impedance Path: When the screen is connected to earth at one or both ends, it provides a low‑impedance path for induced currents, preventing them from propagating along the screen and coupling into the conductors.
  • Transfer Impedance: The effectiveness of the screen is characterised by its transfer impedance—the lower the transfer impedance, the better the shielding. Braided screens have higher transfer impedance at high frequencies than solid or foil screens.
  • Termination: The screen must be terminated to earth using a screen connector or gland to be effective. A poorly terminated screen can act as an antenna, worsening EMI.

In AGL systems, shielded cables are used for both analog and digital signals. For analog signals (e.g., 4‑20 mA current loops, 0‑10 V voltage signals), shielding prevents noise pickup that could cause measurement errors. For digital signals (e.g., RS‑485, Ethernet), shielding reduces bit errors and ensures reliable communication in noisy environments.

Construction

Physical Construction

Shielded cable adds one construction layer on top of an otherwise conventional multi‑core design. Each internal conductor — annealed copper, tinned or bare, single‑pair, multi‑pair, or multi‑core depending on the circuit — is individually insulated and, where multiple pairs are present, laid up (twisted together) to cancel out mutual interference before the whole core bundle is wrapped. The screen itself is applied over this core as a braided copper mesh, a helically wound (spiral) copper tape, an aluminium‑polyester foil, or a combination of foil‑plus‑drain‑wire, chosen according to the frequency range and level of EMI rejection the circuit needs, with braid giving the most robust, flexible coverage and foil giving the most complete high‑frequency shielding at lower cost. An overall outer jacket of PVC, PE, or a similarly tough polymer is extruded over the screen to protect it mechanically and environmentally. The screen is only effective once terminated: at each cable end it is drawn out, twisted into a pigtail or connected via a screen connector, and bonded to earth, so the finished installation's shielding performance depends as much on that termination as on the cable's own construction.

Test screen continuity end-to-end and measure resistance, since damage from cable pulling or crushing can silently reduce shielding effectiveness.

Termination & Earth Bond Check
Annual

Confirm the screen is terminated with 360° contact, kept short (ideally under 50mm), and earthed per the equipment manufacturer's single- or dual-end recommendation.

Annual

Verify the screen is not inadvertently earthed at both ends of a long run in a way that creates a ground loop and induces noise.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Improper Screen Termination

One of the most common issues is a poorly terminated screen—either not connected to earth or connected with a long pigtail that increases impedance. Use a screen connector or gland that provides 360° contact, and keep the screen termination short (ideally < 50mm) and direct to an earth bus.

Ground Loops

Connecting the screen at both ends in a long run can create a ground loop, inducing noise. Typically, the screen should be earthed at one end (usually the source or panel end). For high‑frequency applications, earthing at both ends with low‑impedance connections may be required. Follow the equipment manufacturer's recommendations.

Screen Damage During Installation

The screen can be damaged (cut, frayed, or crushed) during cable pulling, reducing shielding effectiveness. Handle cables carefully, use cable rollers, and avoid sharp bends. After installation, test the screen continuity and resistance.

Moisture Ingress

Moisture can enter the cable through damaged sheath or terminations, causing corrosion of the screen and conductors. Use moisture‑blocking materials, proper sealing glands, and inspect cables for sheath damage.

EMC Failure Due to Incorrect Cable Type

Using a cable with insufficient shielding (e.g., a braid with low coverage) for a high‑interference environment can lead to EMC problems. Ensure the cable's screen type and coverage meet the required EMC level for the application.

Screen Not Connected to Earth at All

In some installations, the screen is left floating, which renders the shielding ineffective. Always connect the screen to a proper earth point. Use a dedicated earth bar for screen terminations.

Crosstalk Between Pairs

In multi‑pair cables, crosstalk between pairs can occur if the pairs are not individually shielded or if the overall shield is insufficient. Use individually shielded pairs (e.g., twisted pairs with foil screens) for sensitive signals.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

EMI/Noise on Signal Despite Shielded Cable
Likely Cause
The screen is poorly terminated — not connected to earth, or connected through a long pigtail that increases high-frequency impedance.
Diagnostic Steps
Inspect the screen termination for pigtail length and earth bond; test screen continuity end to end; check the noise symptom against known interference sources nearby.
Corrective Action
Re-terminate with a screen connector or gland providing 360° contact, keeping the termination short and direct to an earth bus.
Noise Consistent with a Ground Loop
Likely Cause
The screen has been earthed at both ends of a long run, creating a ground loop that induces noise onto the signal.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the earthing configuration at both ends of the cable run; measure for circulating current on the screen if a loop is suspected.
Corrective Action
Earth the screen at one end only (typically the source or panel end) per the equipment manufacturer's recommendation, unless high-frequency low-impedance earthing at both ends is specifically called for.
Reduced Shielding Effectiveness After Installation
Likely Cause
The screen was damaged (cut, frayed, or crushed) during cable pulling, most often from a sharp bend or an unprotected pull.
Diagnostic Steps
Test screen continuity and resistance along the full run; visually inspect accessible sections for crushing or fraying, especially near bends and pull points.
Corrective Action
Replace the damaged cable section, using cable rollers and avoiding sharp bends during re-installation, then re-test continuity.
Insulation or Screen Resistance Failure
Likely Cause
Moisture has entered through a damaged sheath or an unsealed termination, corroding the screen and conductors.
Diagnostic Steps
Megger test the cable and compare against the acceptable minimum; inspect the sheath along the run and check terminations for intact sealing.
Corrective Action
Replace the affected cable section, seal terminations with proper moisture-blocking glands, and re-verify insulation resistance.
EMC Failure at Commissioning or Retest
Likely Cause
The installed cable's screen type or coverage is insufficient for the level of electromagnetic interference at the site.
Diagnostic Steps
Compare the cable's screen specification (braid coverage, foil/braid combination) against the EMC requirement for the application; measure actual interference levels if an EMC test is available.
Corrective Action
Replace the cable with a type providing adequate screen coverage for the required EMC level.
Crosstalk Between Pairs in a Multi-Pair Cable
Likely Cause
Individual pairs are not separately shielded, or the overall shield is insufficient to isolate them from each other in a multi-pair cable.
Diagnostic Steps
Test signal quality on affected pairs while cycling adjacent pairs on and off to confirm crosstalk; compare cable construction against the application's sensitivity requirements.
Corrective Action
Replace with a cable using individually shielded pairs (e.g., foil-screened twisted pairs) where signals are sensitive to crosstalk.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Isolate Before Termination Work
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the associated circuit before terminating, splicing, or reworking shielded cable, since control and monitoring cables can share routes or ducts with higher-energy circuits.

Single-Point Earth the Screen
Operational

Terminate the cable screen at a single point per the system design, typically at the control end, to avoid creating a ground loop; earthing the screen at both ends is a common and hard-to-diagnose source of interference.

Handle the Screen Carefully During Installation
Operational

Avoid nicking, crushing, or excessively bending the cable during pulling and termination, since damage to the screen or jacket compromises both shielding effectiveness and weatherproofing.

Verify Screen Continuity Before Closing Out
Compliance

Test screen continuity and insulation resistance after termination and before commissioning, so a damaged or poorly terminated screen is caught before it manifests as intermittent interference.

Seal Terminations Against Moisture
Operational

Properly seal glands, boots, and enclosure entries after termination to keep moisture out of the cable end and connector, preventing insulation breakdown over time.

Confirm Signal Integrity After Work
Compliance

After any cable or termination work, confirm the associated control or monitoring signal is clean and stable before closing out the task.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Shield Grounded at Both Ends Can Create a Ground Loop
Design

Bonding a cable shield to earth at both ends, rather than following the specified single-point grounding scheme, can introduce a ground loop that shows up as noise on the signal rather than an obvious fault. Verify the shield termination scheme matches the design intent whenever a cable is re-terminated.

Intermittent Noise Often Traces to a Damaged Shield, Not the Conductors
Diagnostics

A signal that picks up noise only when nearby equipment cycles on and off frequently points to a compromised or poorly terminated shield rather than a fault in the core conductors themselves. Check shield continuity and termination before assuming the conductors are at fault.

Tight Bend Radii Damage Shield Coverage Invisibly
Installation

Pulling a shielded cable around a bend tighter than its rated minimum radius can gap or thin the shield's braid coverage without any visible damage to the outer jacket, degrading its shielding effectiveness. Respect the manufacturer's minimum bend radius during installation and re-routing alike.

Moisture at an Unsealed Termination Corrodes the Shield First
Durability

A shield braid or foil is often more susceptible to corrosion from moisture ingress than the insulated core conductors, so a termination that looks fine electrically on the core can still have a degraded shield. Inspect shield condition specifically at every termination check, not just core conductor continuity.

Don't Run Shielded and Unshielded Circuits in the Same Duct Without Checking Separation
Design

Routing a shielded signal cable in close proximity to a high-current power cable in the same duct can still induce noise if separation distance falls short of the design guidance, even with the shield correctly terminated. Verify duct separation against the applicable standard, not just shield presence.

Record Shield Continuity Separately From Core Continuity
Process

Logging shield continuity as its own line item, rather than folding it into a general cable pass/fail result, makes it possible to spot a shield-specific degradation trend on a particular run that a combined result would mask.
