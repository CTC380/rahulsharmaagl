Predictive Maintenance Sensor

An intelligent, AI-powered sensing device that continuously monitors AGL equipment health and predicts potential failures before they occur. By analyzing vibration, temperature, current, and other critical parameters, this sensor enables proactive maintenance strategies that maximize uptime, reduce costs, and enhance airfield safety.

Overview

What Is It?

A Predictive Maintenance Sensor is a sophisticated edge device that combines high-fidelity sensing, local analytics, and machine learning to predict equipment failures before they happen. Unlike traditional monitoring sensors that simply report current values, this device:

  • Monitors multiple parameters – vibration, temperature, current, voltage, humidity, and acoustic emissions
  • Performs edge-based AI inference – using pre-trained models to detect anomaly patterns
  • Predicts remaining useful life (RUL) of critical components
  • Provides actionable insights – recommending specific maintenance actions
  • Learns and adapts – continuously improving prediction accuracy over time

This sensor represents the pinnacle of the Smart Airport paradigm, transforming maintenance from reactive "run-to-failure" to proactive "predict-and-prevent" strategies.

Purpose

Purpose & Function

The predictive maintenance sensor's purpose is to shift AGL maintenance from reacting to failures after they happen to acting on early warning signs while a fault is still developing, at the specific point where the highest-consequence equipment — CCRs, isolating transformers, LED drivers — is most likely to fail unexpectedly. By tracking subtle changes in vibration, temperature, or current that precede a breakdown, it gives maintenance teams the lead time to schedule a repair on their own terms rather than responding to an unplanned outage that could interrupt a runway or taxiway circuit during operations. Its value is therefore measured less in the data it collects and more in the maintenance visits it prevents from becoming emergencies.

Application

Where Is It Used?

Predictive Maintenance Sensors are deployed on critical AGL equipment where early failure detection is essential:

  • Constant Current Regulators (CCRs): Monitoring transformers, cooling fans, and power electronics for early signs of degradation.
  • Isolating Transformers: Detecting winding insulation breakdown, core saturation, and thermal stress.
  • LED Drivers and Fixtures: Predicting driver failures and LED lumen depreciation.
  • Motors and Actuators: Monitoring bearing wear, misalignment, and imbalance in beacon drives and moving parts.
  • Switchgear and Circuit Breakers: Detecting contact wear, arcing, and insulation degradation.
  • UPS and Battery Systems: Predicting battery end-of-life and inverter failures.

They are deployed as part of comprehensive condition monitoring programs, typically integrated with IoT Gateways and cloud-based analytics platforms.

Placement

Installation & Location

The sensor is fixed directly to the component it monitors, typically bolted, magnetically clamped, or adhesive-mounted onto the transformer casing, motor housing, or driver enclosure so vibration and thermal readings are taken from the source rather than through an intervening structure that would damp or delay the signal. Mounting position and orientation follow the manufacturer's guidance for the specific measurement (an accelerometer needs a rigid, flat mounting face aligned to the axis being monitored), and the unit is sited so its antenna or communication module has a clear path to the nearest gateway. Power is drawn from the host equipment's local supply or a self-contained battery, and cabling or wireless commissioning is completed and range-tested at installation before the sensor is added to the monitoring platform.

Function

How Does It Work?

The Predictive Maintenance Sensor operates through a sophisticated pipeline of sensing, signal processing, and AI inference:

  • Multi-Modal Sensing: Continuously captures vibration (accelerometer, 3-axis), temperature (thermistor/RTD), current (Hall effect), and acoustic emission (ultrasonic microphone) data at high sampling rates (up to 25 kHz).
  • Signal Conditioning and Pre-processing: Filters noise, performs FFT for frequency domain analysis, and extracts key features (RMS, crest factor, spectral energy).
  • Edge AI Inference: Runs a lightweight neural network (TinyML) or statistical model directly on the device to detect anomalies and predict failure probability.
  • Self-Learning: Uses online learning techniques to adapt to changing equipment conditions and improve prediction accuracy over time.
  • Reporting: Transmits health scores, anomaly alerts, and recommended actions to the ALCMS or cloud platform.

The sensor is typically powered by energy harvesting (vibration or thermal) or long-life batteries, with a wireless communication interface (LoRa, Wi-Fi, or NB-IoT) for data transmission.

Architecture

System Architecture & Control Logic

Control logic here runs almost entirely at the edge rather than centrally: sampling, FFT and inference happen on the sensor itself, and only a health score or anomaly flag is escalated to the ALCMS or cloud platform when a threshold is crossed, rather than streaming raw waveform data continuously — this keeps bandwidth low over LoRa/NB-IoT links. The sensor's "control" is advisory, not command-and-control: it triggers a maintenance workflow or alert, it does not switch a circuit. Where it monitors a fixture on a series circuit already watched at loop level by the CCR, the sensor's per-unit diagnosis complements that loop-level fault detection, narrowing a generic "loop fault" alarm down to a specific fixture or transformer before a technician is dispatched.

Construction

Physical Construction

The sensor is a compact, hermetically sealed unit combining a MEMS 3-axis accelerometer, an RTD or thermistor, and (where fitted) a Hall-effect current sensor on a single PCB, potted or conformal-coated against moisture and vibration to reach an IP67/IP68 submersible rating. A magnetic or bolt-on mounting base clamps it directly to the monitored equipment — a CCR contactor, a transformer, or a fixture housing — so the accelerometer picks up vibration through solid contact rather than through the enclosure alone, since any gap between sensor and equipment would damp the very signal the unit is trying to read. Onboard flash holds the quantized AI model that runs the edge prediction, and a small internal battery (or energy-harvesting module) powers the sensor through the -40°C to +85°C range for a rated 5–10 years without a wired power feed.

Field Notes

Common Field Issues

Insufficient Training Data

The AI models require extensive historical data (including failure examples) to achieve high prediction accuracy. Without sufficient training, the sensor may generate false positives or miss early warning signs. Collecting and labeling data from actual failures is essential.

Environmental Interference

Vibration sensors can be affected by nearby machinery, wind, or ground movement. Proper mounting (using magnetic, adhesive, or bolt-on bases) and filtering algorithms are essential to isolate equipment-specific signals.

Data Privacy and Cybersecurity

Predictive maintenance data is sensitive operational information. Ensuring end-to-end encryption, secure data storage, and compliance with local cybersecurity regulations is critical to protect the airfield's operational integrity.

Diagnostics

Troubleshooting Guide

High Rate of False Positive Alerts
Likely Cause
The AI model has insufficient or unrepresentative training data, particularly a lack of labelled failure examples for the monitored equipment.
Diagnostic Steps
Review recent alerts against actual equipment condition to confirm they are false positives; check how much labelled failure data the model was trained on for this asset class.
Corrective Action
Feed confirmed false positives back into the training set as negative examples, and prioritise collecting and labelling real failure data to improve model accuracy over time.
Sensor Readings Noisy or Inconsistent with Equipment Condition
Likely Cause
Environmental interference — nearby machinery vibration, wind, or ground movement — is being picked up alongside the equipment's own signature.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the sensor's mounting method (magnetic, adhesive, or bolt-on) for a secure, vibration-isolated fit; compare readings against a known-good baseline; review whether filtering algorithms are correctly configured for this installation.
Corrective Action
Re-mount the sensor using a more rigid or better-isolated method if mounting is the cause, and tune filtering algorithms to isolate the equipment-specific signal from ambient interference.
Sensor Data Not Reaching the Monitoring Platform, or Security Concern Raised
Likely Cause
A connectivity fault, or a gap in encryption/access-control configuration for the sensor's data path.
Diagnostic Steps
Check the sensor's communication link and last-reported timestamp; review encryption settings and access controls against the required cybersecurity baseline.
Corrective Action
Restore connectivity and confirm data resumes flowing, or correct encryption/access-control configuration and re-audit compliance with local cybersecurity regulations before returning the sensor to full service.

Precautions

Safety & Operational Notes

Circuit Isolation Before Installation or Removal
Electrical Safety

De-energise and lock out the monitored circuit before installing, removing, or wiring the sensor onto a fixture, transformer, or panel, and verify zero energy before making connections.

Treat AI Predictions as Advisory, Not a Substitute for Testing
Operational

Use the sensor's remaining-useful-life estimates and anomaly alerts to prioritise and plan maintenance, but confirm the underlying condition with calibrated test equipment (megger, clamp meter, etc.) before making a run-to-failure or immediate-replacement decision.

PPE & Grounding
Personnel Safety

Use insulated tools and appropriate PPE when mounting the sensor on or near live-capable equipment, and confirm the sensor housing is bonded/grounded per the electrical design before energising the monitored circuit.

ESD Precautions
Personnel Safety

Use anti-static handling precautions when connecting or replacing the sensor's electronics, since its onboard inference hardware and sensing elements can be damaged by electrostatic discharge.

Verify Sensor Isolation on the Monitored Circuit
Electrical Safety

Confirm the sensor's own power and signal connections are properly isolated (opto-coupled, transformer-isolated, or otherwise rated) from the higher-voltage circuit it is monitoring before energising either side.

Verify Data Reporting Before Return to Service
Compliance

After installation or maintenance, confirm the sensor is reporting valid data to the monitoring platform before relying on it, since a sensor that appears physically installed but is not communicating provides a false sense of coverage.

FOD Control at the Mounting Point
Operational

Keep tools and fasteners accounted for at all times when mounting or servicing the sensor, and secure or remove any loose material before closing up the fixture or panel.

Restore to Compliant State
Compliance

Do not leave a sensor unmounted, unpowered, or reporting a communications fault after maintenance; confirm it is reporting current, valid data before closing out the work.

Field Insight

Engineering & Expert Notes

RS
By Rahul Sharma
Airfield Ground Lighting Specialist
A Model Is Only as Good as Its Training Baseline
Design

A predictive model trained on a short commissioning-period baseline can flag entirely normal seasonal variation as an anomaly, or miss a genuine slow degradation that looks similar to the baseline noise. Let the model accumulate at least a full seasonal cycle of data before treating its alerts as reliable.

False Confidence Is the Real Risk, Not False Alarms
Operational

Too many false-positive alerts get the system ignored, but the more dangerous failure mode is a team that stops doing periodic manual checks because "the sensor would have flagged it." Keep a baseline manual inspection cadence in place regardless of how well the predictive system has been performing.

Investigate the Underlying Parameter, Not Just the Alert
Troubleshooting

An anomaly flag is a starting point, not a diagnosis; two very different root causes can produce a similar-looking vibration or current signature. Pull the underlying trend data behind the alert before dispatching a crew, so the visit is targeted rather than exploratory.

Sensor Placement Affects What the Model Can Actually See
Installation

A sensor mounted at a point that's mechanically or electrically isolated from the actual failure mode it's meant to catch will never generate a useful early warning, no matter how good the underlying model is. Validate sensor placement against the specific failure modes it's expected to detect, not just against the mounting point's convenience.

Retrofitting Onto Live Circuits Needs Extra Discipline
Electrical Safety

Adding a monitoring sensor to an existing energised circuit is inherently higher-risk than installing on new equipment, since the surrounding conductors are already live and the working space is often more constrained. Treat a retrofit installation with the same isolation and verification discipline as any other live-circuit work, not as a minor add-on task.

A Silent Sensor Is Worse Than a No-Data Alert
Reliability

A sensor that stops reporting due to a communications fault provides no protection at all, but if the system doesn't clearly distinguish "no data" from "normal," that gap can go unnoticed for a long time. Confirm the monitoring platform actively alerts on missing data, not just on out-of-range readings.
